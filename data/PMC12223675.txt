Objectives This study explored how the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model can explain and address addictive behaviors in East Asian contexts. Methods Over the past decades, articles published in peer-reviewed journals were identified and reviewed by searching academic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. These articles were related to the I-PACE model and considered data from other Asian jurisdictions. Results Asian cultural factors within the I-PACE model may include 1) personal factors (academic stress, family expectations, and collectivistic values), 2) affective factors (shame, fear of failure, and social comparison), 3) cognitive factors (cultural beliefs about technology use and success), and 4) execution factors (parental control, technological restrictions, and school policies). Conclusion We propose specifying the I-PACE model to include cultural factors relevant to the East Asian environment. Such considerations could promote investigations into how addiction pathways involve Asian familial structures, societal expectations, and cultural norms. This model describes the theorized relationships between four primary components: personal factors, affective responses, cognitive processes, and execution of behaviors [ 3 , 4 ]. Recent studies have used the I-PACE model to investigate specific psychological mechanisms underlying addiction. For instance, a study focusing on smartphone addiction among international students in China used the I-PACE framework to examine specific personal and cognitive elements [ 5 ]. Another study from South Korea investigated the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and internet gaming disorder (IGD) among adolescents and found that stress mediated the effects of ACEs on IGD, thereby supporting the applicability of the model in this cultural context [ 6 ]. These studies suggest the framework’s utility in examining how diverse factors, ranging from individual personality features to specific environmental influences, may converge to shape addiction processes [ 3 ]. This research may be bolstered by integrating neuroimaging techniques and psychological assessments. Such approaches could help identify specific neural and cognitive processes involved in addictive behaviors, including technology-driven addictive disorders [ 7 , 8 ]. Although the I-PACE model has demonstrated utility in understanding global addictive behaviors, its application in the Asian context requires further examination [ 9 ]. Cultural values, such as collectivism, academic pressure, and societal conformity, may shape the manifestation of addiction-related behaviors [ 10 , 11 ]. In countries such as South Korea, China, and Japan, the emphasis on academic success and technological integration in daily life may create unique environments that may amplify certain risk factors relevant to the I-PACE model [ 5 , 12 ]. For instance, South Korea has one of the highest rates of Internet and smartphone use among adolescents, with significant concerns regarding IGD and social media addiction [ 13 - 15 ]. These concerns are often intertwined with academic stress, peer pressure and familial expectations [ 16 ]. However, to date, few reviews and studies exist that have applied the IPACE model to Asian contexts. A recent viewpoint focusing on the I-PACE model proposed incorporating cultural specifications [ 17 ]. Therefore, considering the background, this study aimed to explore how the I-PACE model may be specified to better consider addictive behaviors in East Asian contexts. Understanding the cultural factors within the I-PACE framework may help policymakers and clinicians design more effective prevention and intervention strategies tailored to specific jurisdictions. METHODS To conduct a literature review, we searched MeSH keywords such as “I-PACE model,” “addiction,” “Asian context,” “smartphone addiction,” “Internet addiction,” and “behavioral addiction.” We used academic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, for searches and concentrated on articles from peer-reviewed journals published within the last decade, as the I-PACE model was first published in 2016. We implemented Boolean operators for searches with keyword combinations such as “I-PACE AND addiction AND Asia” to effectively identify relevant articles. After removing duplicate articles, the screening phase commenced by evaluating abstracts based on predefined inclusion criteria, including relevance to applying the I-PACE model in Asian contexts and methodological robustness. The selection criteria were as follows: 1) studies applying the I-PACE model or related to potential risk factors; 2) empirical studies on the mechanisms of smartphone and Internet addiction; 3) studies conducted in Asian cultures, such as South Korea, China, and Japan; and 4) studies published between 2016 and 2025. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) duplicate manuscripts in research databases; 2) meta-analyses, systematic reviews, or review manuscripts; 3) manuscripts written in languages other than English; and 4) manuscripts with unclear research methodology. After searching, our research team conducted collaborative discussions to evaluate the key articles. We focused on studies that addressed cultural, emotional, and cognitive dimensions influencing addiction processes in Asian populations. Through the iterative process illustrated in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) diagram, six articles were identified ( Fig. 1 ). The identified articles were considered integral to contemplating culturally appropriate interventions guided by the IPACE model and applicable to Asian populations. We focused on methodological approaches and key findings, and considered existing research gaps to suggest specific regional dynamics and propose directions for future exploration. RESULTS Empirical evidence from Asian studies Recent studies in Asia have provided empirical support for the I-PACE model, with potentially culturally specific findings. The key findings and implications of the research articles that have considered the I-PACE model with respect to addiction processes in Asian jurisdictions are described below. In this process, we considered the year of publication, subjects of the study, key results, conclusions drawn by the researchers, general implications of the study, and specific components of the I-PACE model that were involved in or supported by the findings ( Table 1 ). Li [ 18 ] used the I-PACE model to explore the predictive capacity of emotional factors such as depression and anxiety for Internet addiction among Chinese adolescents. This study utilized a hierarchical linear model to assess adolescents’ Internet addiction over time, considering sex differences. Boys exhibited a higher initial level of addiction and a more rapid decline than did girls. These results suggest the importance of addressing depression and anxiety, and fostering positive coping strategies to mitigate concerns related to Internet addiction, particularly in sex-sensitive contexts [ 18 ]. Zhang and Zeng [ 19 ] applied the I-PACE model to investigate how academic anxiety mediates the effect of smartphone addiction on academic achievement among Chinese college students. Their findings suggested that smartphone addiction may negatively impact academic performance through anxiety, suggesting that educational strategies should focus on managing academic pressure and enhancing students’ control over their academic environment [ 19 ]. Zhao et al. [ 20 ] further extended this model by focusing on the interaction between cognition and affect, demonstrating how anxiety mediates the relationship between Internet addiction and depression moderated by social support. Their work highlights the importance of social interventions in alleviating the psychological impact of addiction [ 20 ]. In addition, Jhone et al. [ 6 ] utilized the I-PACE model to examine the influence of ACEs on IGD and identified stress as an important mediator. The study concluded that addressing childhood stress is vital for managing IGD, suggesting that effective stress management skills may be particularly helpful for individuals with a history of ACEs [ 6 ]. Hong et al. [ 21 ] examined smartphone addiction in a cohort of South Korean middle school students and identified several statistical predictors. Hierarchical regression analysis indicated that both personal predispositions (evidenced by sex differences) and affective factors (notably elevated stress levels and interpersonal problems) were related to smartphone addiction. Specific smartphone usage types were related differently; while using the device for social networking and consuming music/videos was linked to addictive engagement, academic usage appeared to be protective. Additionally, suggested influences of symptoms of depression and attentiondeficit/hyperactivity disorder align with the I‑PACE model’s proposal regarding how emotional dysregulation and impaired control may predispose individuals to addictive behaviors. Overall, these findings suggest the relevance of intervention strategies that target interactions between affective, cognitive, and behavioral factors when addressing Internetuse concerns, as reflected in the I‑PACE framework. Doo and Kim [ 22 ] demonstrated how parental smartphone addiction may act as an initial vulnerability (personal factor) that shapes the family environment by engendering negative parenting attitudes (affective and cognitive factors), which may elevate adolescent aggression (perhaps reflected in executive functioning deficits). A potential cascade, a sequence in which parental addictive behaviors influence parenting style that may lead to increased aggression in adolescents, provides insight into adolescent smartphone addiction. In this framework, possible interplays between parental predispositions, maladaptive emotional responses, and impaired regulatory control may contribute to problematic smartphone use, consistent with processes described in the I‑PACE model and suggesting a possible mechanism for intergenerational behaviors. Cultural considerations regarding the I-PACE model in Asian contexts The I-PACE provides a comprehensive theoretical framework for hypothesis-driven investigations into addiction, suggesting a complex interplay of emotional, cognitive, and behavioral factors. We propose that the I-PACE model be further modified to consider cultural factors in the East Asian context. Such considerations may relate to cultural norms, societal expectations, familial structures, and how they may shape addiction pathways. Table 2 and Fig. 2 consider specific factors pertinent to the East Asian context in the I-PACE framework. Personal factors (academic stress, family expectations, and collectivistic values) As described in the initial I-PACE model, personal factors include personality features and genetic propensities that may predispose individuals to addictions [ 23 ]. Personal factors such as personality features may be shaped by cultural identity, familial expectations, game preferences, and other factors, and these may contribute to potential risk factors such as high neuroticism and low conscientiousness [ 24 , 25 ]. In many Asian countries, academic success is viewed as a pathway to social mobility, potentially leading to intense pressure on students. Potential protective factors may include having supportive family environments and strong cultural identities [ 12 , 15 , 26 ]. Affective factors (shame, fear of failure, and social comparison) Affective responses may promote motivation to engage in addictive behaviors [ 2 , 27 ]. Affective factors and emotion regulation processes may differ between Eastern and Western cultures. Within Eastern collectivistic societies, societal norms may be linked in nuanced manners to emotional dysregulation, stress, and depression risks [ 10 , 12 , 20 ]. Emotions may be amplified in complex ways in collectivistic societies, where individual success or failure may, for example, reflect on family units. As such, protective factors may involve family and community support, which aid in addressing potential stressors linked to affective considerations in collectivist cultures [ 20 , 26 ]. Cognitive factors (cultural beliefs about technology use and success) Cognitive factors, including attention and decision-making processes, may affect how individuals respond to addictionrelated cues [ 4 ]. Cognitive factors may be influenced by cultural elements, with potential risks stemming from cognitive distortion amid academic and societal pressures [ 28 ]. For example, in South Korea, technology is often viewed as both a tool for success and a potential threat to academic achievement. Protective strategies such as mindfulness and balanced academic pursuits may help address these concerns [ 29 ]. Execution factors (parental control, technological restrictions, and school policies) Execution involves behavioral engagement related to control and impulsivity and may contribute to the development and maintenance of addictive behaviors. Execution may be influenced by societal expectations, and impulse control may be a particular challenge with respect to technology use in Asian contexts [ 6 , 28 ]. Authoritative parenting and strict school environments may both protect against and contribute to addictive behaviors. Potential protective measures include discipline and cultural engagement activities, which may help individuals cope with peer and societal pressures [ 30 - 32 ]. Such moderators may influence the pathways from vulnerability to addictive behaviors, suggesting that culturally tailored interventions may be particularly effective. Better understanding of I-PACE factors in specific cultural contexts may allow the development of culturally nuanced interventions to effectively address addiction risks. Such approaches can potentially leverage cultural norms and community strengths to foster resilience. DISCUSSION This study suggests that the I-PACE model may be further specified within East Asian contexts. Western studies traditionally emphasize individualistic cultural dimensions such as personal decision-making and cognitive aspects such as impulsivity that reflect environments in which personal agency is paramount. Affective factors like anxiety and depression have been linked to addictions across diverse cultural landscapes [ 1 , 2 ]. Here, we propose that the I-PACE model should be further specified when applied to understanding addictive processes in Asian jurisdictions. As Asian societies witness rapid technological integration, issues like Internet and gaming disorders are becoming more prevalent [ 30 , 33 ]. Cultural attributes such as collectivism, intense educational pressures, and societal expectations may modify how individuals experience and respond to engagement with digital technologies. All I-PACE model domains (person, affect, cognition, and execution) may warrant specification in cultural contexts [ 3 , 6 , 16 ]. Previous studies [ 19 , 20 ] have suggested that sociocultural factors in Asia, such as familial and interpersonal relationships, may influence addiction dynamics. Recent Korean studies [ 6 , 34 ] have suggested the importance of considering socio-environmental factors relevant to Asian contexts. Culturally sensitive research aligns with applications of the I-PACE model. Diverse sociocultural factors may be relevant to I-PACE factors. Eastern and Western cultures differ, and Eastern cultures involve collective societal factors. Such factors may be integral to understanding and addressing addictive behaviors in Asian jurisdictions, suggesting the importance of developing culturally nuanced intervention strategies [ 19 ]. Cultural values, family dynamics, and social structures may influence addiction pathways in ways that differ between Eastern and Western contexts. Findings from existing studies suggest that cultural factors may influence the development and maintenance of addictive behaviors in ways that can be further specified in the I-PACE model [ 23 ]. Existing studies suggest cultural and contextual nuances in understanding addiction behaviors in Asian settings and the relevance of customizing interventions that effectively address cultural and emotional factors [ 1 , 2 ]. Applying the I-PACE model to Asian contexts may benefit from specifying specific culturally relevant factors, including parenting styles, educational systems, and technology use tendencies [ 1 , 6 ]. Variations in digital access and usage may impact addiction severity and type across different demographic segments, such as Asian adolescents or university students [ 19 ]. By considering factors relevant to Asian environments within the I-PACE model, researchers can conduct hypothesis-driven investigations that incorporate sociocultural constructs [ 31 ]. The implications and suggestions for preventing, assessing, and intervening in addiction may stem from considering specific cultural factors. School-based interventions addressing digital addiction should consider factors such as academic stress and peer dynamics. Schools should implement and assess stress management programs, provide counseling services, and promote healthy technology use. Considering the hierarchical family structures common in Asia, family therapeutic interventions can engage parents. Parenting programs can promote balanced approaches that combine guidance and emotional support. Public health campaigns can be culturally sensitive and can address the stigma surrounding mental health and addiction. Campaigns could highlight how addiction is a complex issue influenced by environmental factors rather than a personal failing. Teaching adolescents how to responsibly navigate digital environments may reduce their addiction risk. Digital literacy programs emphasize critical thinking, self-regulation, and healthy technological habits. Assessing relationships within culturally specified I-PACE frameworks could provide insight into relationships and inform policymaking and intervention efforts. However, the limitations of this study warrant further investigation. First, Asia comprises multiple cultural environments with unique sociocultural norms and practices. This diversity may complicate the generalization of the findings across different Asian populations, because the personal, affective, cognitive, and execution factors outlined in the IPACE model may manifest differently in various cultural settings. Consequently, future research should provide further specifications for culturally informed frameworks that consider unique cultural contexts. Second, this study primarily examines research conducted in East Asian countries, specifically China, South Korea, and Japan. Limited data specific to individual Asian jurisdictions are available, which may hinder the accurate assessment and interpretation of how the interacting factors of the I-PACE model may operate within these contexts. A more comprehensive approach involving region-specific data collection and analysis may enhance the validity of the findings in specific contexts. Therefore, future research should include South and Southeast Asian samples with measures related to cultural features to improve our understanding of and provide insight into the complexities within Asia. Third, societal stigma surrounding addiction in Asian jurisdictions may inhibit open discussions and honest self-reporting among participants. This may lead to underreporting of addictive behaviors or influence participants’ willingness to engage in research studies, potentially negatively affecting data reliability. These limitations highlight the importance of conducting more culturally sensitive research that could promote further specification of the I-PACE model in different cultural contexts. Despite these limitations, this study suggests that by considering specific aspects of the I-PACE model, the cultural contexts of Asian societies, public health policies and interventions may be more effectively developed and tested [ 33 ]. Studies that consider the cultural specifications of the I-PACE model may inform the development of more effective and culturally adapted interventions, thereby enhancing the prevention and treatment strategies used in specific jurisdictions. CONCLUSION Research into the application of the I-PACE model within the East Asian context is important, as there may be specific cultural and socioeconomic factors that influence technology-related addictive behaviors. By considering cultural factors within the I-PACE framework, researchers and clinicians can develop and test more effective interventions to address the specific challenges faced by individuals in these societies. Future studies should focus on longitudinal studies, cross-cultural comparisons, and culturally tailored interventions. Additionally, collaboration among policymakers, educators, and mental health professionals may ensure that interventions are implemented effectively and reach those most in need.
