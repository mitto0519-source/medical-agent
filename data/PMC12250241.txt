Background: Bipolar disorder (BD) is associated with a high risk of lifetime suicide attempts, but relatively few neuroimaging studies have investigated suicidality in the context of BD. Aim: The study aimed to assess the white matter (WM) integrity and gray matter (GM) volume associated with suicide attempts in BD. Method: This observational study recruited 32 patients with DSM-5 bipolar I disorder. Group I had multiple (≥2) lifetime suicide attempts, with at least one attempt in the past year. Group II comprised nonattempters/nonideators who screened negative on the C-SSRS-lifetime version. All patients were euthymic (≤8 on 17-item HAM-D; ≤4 on YMRS). Study assessments included a clinical proforma, NIMH-Life Chart Method, and Barratt’s Impulsivity Scale (BIS). MR imaging was performed using a 32‐channel head coil on a 3T-MR scanner. All participants underwent DTI, and images were processed using tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS). Results: The study groups were comparable for age, gender, total BIS, and illness variables. The mean age was 34.3 years (SD 10.4), with 62.5% males in group I. Group comparisons for FA value revealed a lower WM anisotropy for five clusters (threshold of FA 0.2; P &lt; 0.001; GRF corrected) in the suicide attempters’ group. These included the left and right inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), left superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), cingulate, and forceps minor. Voxel-wise analysis of GMV did not show any significant group differences. Conclusion: The findings indicate white matter disruptions associated with lifetime suicide attempts in BD, particularly in the bilateral IFOF, left SLF, cingulate, and forceps minor. BD is a significant contributor toward suicide-related deaths, with a lifetime risk of completed suicide varying between 3.4 and 14% in BD.[ 6 7 ] BD with significant suicidality may represent a distinct subtype that warrants closer examination. Several clinical correlates have been associated with suicide attempts and suicides in BD.[ 7 8 9 ] However, there is a need to better understand the biological correlates and work toward an early detection of suicidality in BD patients. The MRI-based structural brain studies of suicidal behavior in BD report the involvement of corpus callosum in the pathophysiology of impulsive and suicidal behaviors, while other MRI studies showed abnormalities of right anterior cingulate cortex in patients of BD with suicide attempts.[ 10 11 12 ] There is a need to better understand the pathogenesis of suicidal behaviors specifically in the context of bipolar illness in order to predict and mitigate the suicidal risk over the course of BD. Very few published DTI studies could be found for suicide attempters with BD.[ 13 14 15 16 17 18 19 20 ] These studies differed in age group, selection criteria, and brain regions being studied. Only adolescent or young adults were included in some studies.[ 14 15 16 ] Some researchers focused on ideators rather than attempters.[ 20 ] The diagnosis was seldom restricted to BD I, often including BD II.[ 17 18 19 ] Two of these papers focused only on corpus callosum as the region of interest.[ 13 20 ] Overall, findings from these few existing studies indicate an abnormal WM integrity in the frontal cortex or frontolimbic connections may underlie suicidal behaviors in BD. Available literature has no clear consensus on gray matter (GM) volume changes associated with suicidality, with some studies indicating regional reductions in prefrontal and fronto-limbic regions,[ 15 21 22 ] while other studies reported contradictory results with no reduction or even an increase in GMV.[ 12 23 24 ] The present study aimed to assess the GM volume and white matter integrity, using DTI tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS), in suicide attempters with BD compared to those without lifetime suicidality in BD. METHODS Institutional ethics clearance was obtained prior to study initiation. We used the STROBE case control checklist when writing our report. The study was conducted at the Department of Psychiatry and Department of Nuclear Magnetic Resonance of a tertiary care institute. Selection criteria Inclusion criteria for patient groups (Group I and II) comprised adult patients (18–65 years) who met DSM-5 diagnosis of BD type I,[ 25 ] along with total duration of illness ≥3 years and a history of at least two lifetime depressive episodes. Participants in an early course of illness were excluded to allow for adequate duration and episodes to determine lifetime suicidality. Patients were recruited if they were in clinical remission and currently euthymic with a score of ≤8 on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating scale[ 26 ] and ≤4 on the Young’s Mania Rating Scale.[ 27 ] Group I (suicide attempters) comprised patients with more than one lifetime suicide attempt across discrete mood episodes, with at least one suicide attempt in the recent year to minimize recall bias. Group II (nonattempters/nonideators) comprised patients with the absence of lifetime suicidality, a negative screen on the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)-lifetime version.[ 28 ] The exclusion criteria for both patient groups were co-occurring psychiatric disorder, apart from BD and nicotine dependence, known neurological (e.g., seizure disorder, cerebrovascular disease, head injury, meningoencephalitis, etc.) or medical conditions that may potentially affect brain (e.g., autoimmune diseases, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic liver or kidney disease, etc.), pregnancy, or lactation. Right-handedness was confirmed for all study participants using Edinburgh’s handedness inventory.[ 29 ] Standard MRI contraindications such as metallic implants were ruled out. Clinical assessments A semistructured proforma was used to gather relevant information about sociodemographic and relevant clinical details. The NIMH-Life Chart Method (retrospective clinician version) was used for gathering illness details. Suicide severity in group I was assessed using the C-SSRS lifetime version. The Barratt’s impulsiveness scale (BIS) has 30 items that are scored in order to give six first-order factors and three second-order factors (attentional, motor, and nonplanning impulsiveness) along with a total score.[ 30 ] MRI and DTI image acquisition MR imaging was performed using a 32‐channel head coil on a 3T-MR scanner (Ingenia; M/s Philips HealthCare, The Netherlands). High-resolution structural brain images were collected using three-dimensional MRI sequences with a sagittal Fast Spoiled Gradient Echo sequence [field of vision (FOV) of 240 × 240 × 175 mm; voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm; matrix size of 240 × 240 × 350 slices; default gap of -0.5 mm; echo time (TE) = 3.7 ms; repetition time (TR) = 8.1 ms]. DTI was performed by using a single‐shot echo‐planar sequence with the array sensitivity–encoding technique, with 32 directions, two b values 0, 1000 s/mm 2 (TR/TE, 8,000/83.3 ms; bandwidth, 143 kHz; matrix size, 128×128; slice thickness, 2 mm without interslice gap; FOV, 30×30 cm; and NEX, 2). Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) preprocessing All the high-resolution structural brain images were processed using a VBM analysis using CAT12 toolbox and SPM12 software package running on MATLAB R2019a (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). The T1-weighted image data were normalized to a template space and subsequently segmented into GM, WM, and CSF. The modulated warped GM images were then normalized to MNI-152 standard space with an isotropic voxel resolution of 1.5 mm × 1.5 mm × 1.5 mm. The images of all participants were averaged to generate a study population-specific template. The GM map of each individual was smoothened, and total intracranial volume (TIV) was estimated using the “Estimate TIV” module. The intergroup comparisons were done using SPM12 software by defining contrasts for each group, which resulted in the MNI coordinates for the areas with difference along with the voxel size. The significance level was corrected using false discovery rate (FDR) method, with FDR-adjusted significance &lt; 0.05. Coordinates that were reported in the stereotactic space of the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) were converted to Talairach coordinates[ 31 ] using the Lancaster transform in GingerALE. DTI image processing The processing of images was done using TBSS method of FSL (Oxford Centre for Functional MRI of Brain, Oxford, UK).[ 32 ] Each DTI image was run through a series of quality assurance tests for common artifacts, including ghosting, ringing, slice-wise intensity, venetian blind, and gradient-wise motion artifacts.[ 33 ] The raw FA images were then aligned into MNI152 space using FNIRT, which is a nonlinear tool of FSL software. The aligned FA images were combined to form mean FA ‘skeletons’, which creates a mask using set of voxels representing the center of each white matter tract from the participants. The mean FA ‘skeletons’ of each participant were used for comparison. The data of each participant were projected onto each skeleton mask and fed into voxel-wise statistical analysis. We created pairwise contrasts using independent samples t-tests with the following input parameters: number of permutations = 5000; statistical significance was defined as P &lt; 0.001 corrected by using Gaussian Random Field (GFR) correction. Study procedure Patients with BD seeking treatment at the psychiatry outpatient department were screened using predefined selection criteria and screening tools and enrolled into the study between July 2018 and December 2019. The diagnosis was reconfirmed using DSM-5 in an interview with a psychiatrist. Clinical assessments were performed, followed by DTI within a week with prior appointment. Statistical analysis Statistical analysis for demographic and illness variables was conducted using IBM SPSS statistical software (version 22.0, IBM, USA). Continuous data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were estimated by Mann–Whitney U-test and Chi-square test/Fisher’s exact test. Analysis for VBM and DTI was conducted as previously described, with FDR correction applied to voxel-wise GM analysis, with adjusted significance level &lt; 0.05, and GRF correction applied to FA values (cluster-level P &lt; 0.001, GRF-corrected). RESULTS A total of 122 patients with BD I attending psychiatry outpatient services were referred for study participation and were further screened, as depicted in the flowchart [ Supplementary Figure 1s ]. Of these, 63 were excluded due to reasons as follows: age below 18 or above 60 years (n = 6), illness less than 3 years (n = 22), HAM-D &gt;8 (n = 13), comorbid alcohol dependence (n = 3), concomitant cannabis use disorder (n = 6), significant neurological and medical conditions with propensity to cause brain changes (n = 5), logistics hindering participation (n = 8), refusal to provide consent (n = 14), and missed appointment after consent (n = 3). A total of 42 patients fulfilling all the predefined selection criteria underwent DTI scans, but the procedure had to be aborted for four due to subjective discomfort inside the machine. In 38 patients who successfully completed DTI, data from three scans could not be transferred from the main console to the workstation, with missing files found in scan data of two patients and data file corrupted for one patient, leaving a total of 32 patients with BD across group I (n = 16) and group II (n = 16). The demographic and clinical variables were comparable across the study groups, as shown in Table 1 . Compared to group II, the suicide attempters group scored higher in motor impulsivity subscale; however, total BIS score was not significantly different. Among suicide attempters (group I), all (100%) had two or more actual suicide attempts as part of selection criteria. The number of lifetime suicide attempts ranged from two to four. In C-SSRS lifetime, 56.2% and 31.2% of study group I also reported interrupted and aborted attempts, respectively, besides actual suicide attempts. In the C-SSRS item for medical damage resulting from most lethal attempt, 25% had minor damage, 25% had moderate damage, and 12.5% sustained moderately severe damage from the attempt, as per C-SSRS. Table 2 shows the voxel-wise brain ROI volume comparisons across study groups; however, none of those comparisons met the threshold for significance. The total intracranial volume and total GM volume did not differ across the study groups (not tabulated). The DTI findings pertaining to white matter integrity are shown in Table 3 . The comparison of FA values across study groups revealed five clusters with significantly lower white matter anisotropy in BD group with suicide attempts (threshold of FA 0.2; P &lt; 0.001; GRF corrected). DISCUSSION The current study provides an insight about the neural correlates of suicidality in patients with BD I, adding to the relatively few available studies. The group of interest consisted of individuals with BD who had a substantive and clinically reliable history of lifetime suicide attempts, compared to individuals with BD without lifetime suicidality. One of the study findings was a significant reduction of FA value in the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF) in suicide attempters with BD. In another recent study on BD with history of suicide attempts (n = 25), a decreased FA was found in bilateral IFOF, bilateral UF, and CC, as well as other white matter bundles, compared to BD without prior suicide attempts and HC.[ 19 ] The IFOF is a long associative bundle that connects the occipital cortex, the temporo-basal areas, the superior parietal lobule, and the precuneus to the frontal lobe.[ 34 ] This wide distribution of cortical terminations serves to integrate several functional networks, thereby playing a role in semantic processing, visuospatial processing and planning, social cognition, and emotional and neuropsychological behaviors.[ 34 35 36 ] The atropy in IFOF can impair the goal-directed behavior. The IFOF in suicide attempters showed significant bilateral white matter disruptions compared to nonattempters. The involvement of IFOF in the pathophysiology of suicidality in context of BD merits further investigation. Another finding from the study pertained to a significant reduction of FA values in left SLF in the suicide attempters’ group, relative to nonattempters. The SLF is an extensive white-matter tract that mainly communicates between frontal and parietal lobes. The dorsal SLF is involved in visuospatial attention and motor control, including motor planning of language especially on the left side, while the ventral and posterior SLF play a role in language-related processing.[ 37 ] Functionally, the SLF appears to be involved in supporting the integration of sensory information and motor planning, finalized to visuospatial attention as well as complex motor behaviors.[ 38 ] An aberrant FA of the SLF has been previously reported in suicide attempters with psychotic disorders[ 39 ] and panic disorder,[ 40 ] alongside other areas of the brain. These findings are inconsistent with the reduced connectivity as reported by Lee et al. ,[ 39 ] while the study by Kim et al. [ 40 ] reported a greater FA value and enhanced connectivity in these regions. Relative to the BD without suicidality, the study found a significant reduction of FA values in the cingulate area and forceps minor in BD suicide attempters. The cingulate area is part of the limbic system and surrounds the corpus callosum at its superior margin. This region is important for emotional regulation and affective and cognitive processing. The forceps minor is situated in the anterior portion of the corpus callosum, connecting the left and right frontal lobes. This connection is important for higher cognitive functions like planning and decision-making. Impaired information transfer between hemispheres has also been implicated in BD and may be mediated by alterations in the structural integrity of callosal structure.[ 41 ] Previous structural MRI studies have also demonstrated alterations in size and shape of corpus callosum in BD.[ 10 11 ] A recent DTI study showed that there is a reduced integrity of corpus callosum in suicide attempters with BD and major depression.[ 19 ] Another study which was done exclusively on female samples reported disruption of the corpus callosum integrity in suicide attempters with BD and MDD independent of their psychiatric status.[ 13 ] The mean FA values in the corpus callosum were lower in suicide attempters with mood disorders compared to HC.[ 13 19 ] Similar studies on suicide attempters within MDD patients also report wide-spread white matter disruptions in those with suicide attempt history, relative to those without suicidality. The patient group of MDD with suicidality had lower FA values in the anterior limb of internal capsule,[ 16 42 ] PFC,[ 43 ] IFOF,[ 19 ] and CC[ 16 ] compared to nonsuicidal MDD group. The voxel-wise analysis did not find any GM volumetric alterations associated with lifetime suicide attempts in BD. Some of the prior studies reported a reduced GM volume in the OFC, hippocampus, and cerebellum[ 15 ] and in prefrontal cortex and fronto-limbic regions[ 44 ] in BD with history of suicide attempt. Available studies are not consistent with respect to GMV reduction with some previous studies reporting no GM reduction in orbitofrontal region,[ 23 ] while others found an increased GM volume in inferior frontal gyrus[ 24 ] and anterior cingulate.[ 12 ] Impulsivity may mediate the relationship between emotional distress and suicidal behavior, increasing the risk of attempted suicide in individuals with trait impulsivity.[ 18 44 ] The total BIS score was comparable across groups, though motor impulsivity was higher among the suicide attempters. The findings from the study have clinical and research implications. The white matter disruption in key tracts may underlie cognitive-affective dysregulation in BD suicide attempters. The IFOF and SLF are known to support the integration of sensory information, motor planning, and cognitive control, while the cingulate and forceps minor are associated with emotional regulation and interhemispheric communication, respectively. Disruptions in these pathways may impair the ability to process and regulate emotions effectively, contributing to maladaptive coping mechanisms and behaviors, which can manifest as suicidal attempts. These insights underscore the importance of investigating white matter integrity as a potential biomarker for suicidality in BD and highlight the need for targeted clinical interventions aimed at enhancing cognitive and emotional resilience in at-risk individuals. Certain limitations must be discussed in the context of interpretation of study findings. Though care was taken to include patients with long-term bipolar illness only, one cannot fully exclude the possibility of future suicidality. The findings for study group with lifetime suicide attempts may not necessarily extrapolate to individuals with BD who died of suicide. Inclusion of another group with healthy controls could have informed the study further. The potential effect of psychotropic medications on the brain WM remains unknown. Certain factors such as hydration could potentially influence WM variables. Although standard software has been used for MR and DTI image processing, their inherent shortcomings cannot be ruled out. As higher stability of FA values is directly related to the threshold value of FA, in our study, the FA values were documented at a threshold value of 0.2 (0 min, 1 max). The sample was modest in size and recruited from a tertiary care hospital, which may limit power and generalizability of the study. The study employed a whole-brain, voxel-wise approach for DTI using TBSS and VBM, without preselecting ROIs. Significant findings in the IFOF, SLF, cingulate, and forceps minor were identified post hoc based on statistical thresholds and multiple comparison corrections. Finally, the cross-sectional design can point to an association rather than causation. It is not possible to comment on trajectory of white matter microstructural changes over the course of the illness. We explored the correlation between clinical variables (i.e., duration of illness and number of depressive episodes) and GMV in select brain regions; however, none reached statistical significance. Further correlations, including those with FA, were not conducted as they were beyond the study’s focus, and the small sample within a single group would not yield meaningful conclusions. To conclude, the clusters within the bilateral IFOF, left SLF, cingulate, and forceps minor had significantly lower white matter anisotropy in suicide attempters with BD, relative to patients without lifetime suicidality. These neuroanatomical alterations may affect the functional networks associated with emotional and behavioral aspects. The findings from the study add to a few existing studies on white matter disruptions associated with suicide attempters in BD.
