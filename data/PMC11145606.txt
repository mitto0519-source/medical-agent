Abstract The ketogenic diet (KD) can have a negative impact on the linear growth and body composition of children. The aims of this study were to review two centers' experience with children who developed height deceleration on the KD and determine if the height deceleration was secondary to growth hormone deficiency (GHD), and if growth hormone therapy (GHT) would be effective and safe (not altering ketosis or seizure frequency). Retrospective chart reviews were performed on patients with KD referred to Endocrinology between 2013 and 2018. Seventeen children were identified. Data reviewed included: demographics, growth velocity, KD ratio, protein/calorie intake, lab results, GH dosage, Tanner stage, and seizure frequency, and endocrine recommendations. Descriptive statistics were performed. Of the 17 children referred to the Endocrine Division, seven children were growth hormone deficient and began GHT. Data were provided for six patients (2 males, 4 females; age 2–7 years at the start of KD) on the KD for &gt;6 years and on GHT for &gt;4 years. Growth for all patients stabilized or increased. IGF‐1 z ‐scores normalized. GHT did not affect seizure frequency or ketosis. GHT in those with GHD can be an appropriate option allowing better growth while still maintaining ketogenic therapy and seizure control. Plain Language Summary The KD can be an effective treatment for difficult‐to‐control epilepsy and some disorders of carbohydrate metabolism. The KD can adversely affect the linear growth (height) of children. This case series reviewed six patients who had slow linear growth. In addition, GH treatment did not affect the level of ketosis for these patients. 4 DISCUSSION Five out of 14 patients with KD were referred to Endocrinology at CHOP and all three referred patients at KAA were diagnosed with GHD. Four of the CHOP patients and two patients from KAA initiated GHT. Five out of these six patients had a faster height velocity while on GHT. For all six patients, while on GHT, seizures remained stable, and IGF‐1 normalized. The prevalence of GHD in children on the KD for &gt;6 months appears to be significantly greater than in the general population. The prevalence in the general population is estimated to be 1:3500. 14 At CHOP, we found at least 1:74 for patients (372 total patients &gt;6 months for 2013–2018) who experienced GHD when on the KD &gt;6 months. Increased risk of GHD has also been well‐documented in patients with neurologic conditions such as cerebral palsy, septo‐optic dysplasia, and holoprosencephaly 5 ; however, our cohort only included children who were ambulating, on oral diets and were of normal to moderate intellectual disability. The KD is known to have a negative impact on linear growth when treatment is &gt;6 months. 15 Peterson et al found that linear growth declined significantly for patients ( n = 34) on the diet for &gt;12 months with “high ketosis” (urinary ketones of 80–120 mg/dL), whereas patients with “moderate ketosis” (urinary ketones of &lt;80 mg/dL) did not have height decelerations. 16 In contrast, growth deceleration was not found in two short‐term studies of 3 and 6 months on the KD, likely due to the brevity of the studies, as it takes time for growth deceleration to be detected. 17 , 18 In a large cohort (133 children), Vining et al found a small amount of height deceleration by 6 months with significantly larger changes at 2 years. 2 There have been some theories that children with epilepsy have increased energy needs; however, Bergqvist et al. in a 15‐month study of 24 subjects on KD found a decline in height status without alteration in resting energy expenditure (REE), instead REE was strongly correlated with fat‐free mass. 19 The connection between the KD and height deceleration is likely multifactorial. Patients on the KD generally have lower insulin levels. Insulin down‐regulates production of the IGF binding protein, IGFBP‐1, while increasing hepatic GH receptor availability. 8 The catabolic effect of the KD may cause an increase in the IGF binding proteins and thereby reduce the bioavailability of IGF‐1. 3 In addition, KD patients often have borderline adequate protein intake, which can affect growth and may affect GH receptor signaling leading to reduced IGF‐I production. 8 Some micronutrient deficiencies, such as zinc, iron, and vitamin A, are also known to be related to GH resistance and reduced IGF‐1 levels. 20 Growth may also be impacted by chronic metabolic acidosis, which can impair growth and inhibit GH and IGF‐1 production. 21 All six of the patients were able to maintain their current level of ketosis and seizure control while receiving GHT, and five had improved growth. The CHOP patients did not suffer from any of the recognized side effects of GHT. Increased GH and IGF‐1 exposure and action can also lead to improved bone density. 6 For the patients with GHD, GHT allowed for better growth while still maintaining long‐term KD therapy and seizure control. The primary limitations of this study were its small cohort size and observational nature. The study was also limited in the accuracy of measurements performed in the clinic, not a research setting. The KD was initially designed as a 3‐year treatment, and children would have the opportunity for catch‐up growth when the diet was discontinued. 1 Now, the KD is often used for a longer time period due to its positive effects on seizures and cognition. In addition to reducing seizures, the KD also allows for minimizing the number of anti‐seizure medications used. Improved changes in behavior, cognition, and quality of life while on KD are well documented. 22 At CHOP, approximately 40% of our current patients have been on the KD for &gt;5 years. 5 CONCLUSION GHT is safe and appropriate for patients who develop GHD while on long‐term KD treatment. As the KD has become a long‐term treatment, the effect of growth deceleration is an important factor to consider when deciding to continue treatment. Just as we monitor for other side effects, kidney stones, and osteoporosis, and treat those that occur, we should monitor for growth deceleration, evaluate for possible GHD, and treat accordingly.
