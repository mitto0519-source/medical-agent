Summary This report describes a case involving a boy in his middle childhood with a history of recurrent iron deficiency anaemia, accompanied by respiratory distress, tachypnoea and cyanosis. The respiratory distress improved with pulse doses of methylprednisolone and respiratory support using high-flow oxygen via heated humidified nasal cannula. During each admission, respiratory distress was resolved after administering pulse methylprednisolone. The child was admitted five times with such episodes in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU). X-rays and high-resolution CT of the chest performed during respiratory distress showed diffuse ground-glass opacities and multiple areas of consolidation. After excluding sepsis, autoimmune diseases and vasculitis, a bronchoscopic alveolar lavage was conducted, revealing haemosiderin-laden macrophages. Having ruled out other causes of diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, this case was identified as idiopathic pulmonary haemosiderosis (IPH). Following discharge from the PICU and resolution of respiratory distress, the child was started on maintenance immunosuppression with prednisolone at 15 mg/day. Background Pulmonary haemosiderosis is a rare condition with incidence ranging from 0.24 to 1.26 cases per million children. 1 2 This unusual presentation of iron deficiency anaemia needs to be diagnosed early. Early diagnosis and starting immunosuppressive agents prevent lung fibrosis, thereby decreasing morbidity and mortality in children. Case presentation A child presented to the outpatient department accompanied by his parents, with complaints of difficulty in breathing for the past 2 days. According to the father, the child’s distress had increased progressively over the past 2 days. There was no history of any consanguinity or any history of illnesses running in the family. The child used to get an adequate and well-balanced diet. On examination, the child had tachypnoea with respiratory rates of around 40 breaths per minute, tachycardia with heart rates around 150–160 beats per minute, fever and cyanosis with saturation on room air being 60%. The child was immediately transferred to paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), where respiratory support was initiated using heated humidified high-flow oxygen via nasal cannula (HHFNC). The child had crepitations and rhonchi in bilateral lower zones of the lung on auscultation, nasal flaring, subcostal and intercostal retractions. The child was conscious and oriented to time, place and person. The child had severe pallor as visible on palms and palpebral conjunctiva. Abdomen was soft with no hepatosplenomegaly, cardiovascular system examination revealed tachycardia with no murmurs. 2D echocardiography of the child was within normal limits with no structural anomaly and no pulmonary hypertension. An X-ray of the chest (CXR) obtained in the PICU revealed diffuse consolidations and ground-glass opacities ( figure 1 ). In view of anaemia, packed red blood cells were transfused. Methylprednisolone was given in view of respiratory distress and rhonchi. Empirical broad antibiotics (piperacillin+tazobactam) were given after obtaining blood cultures. Respiratory distress gradually settled on high-flow nasal cannula within 4 hours. Oxygen saturation gradually improved to around 90–95% on HHFNC, and signs of respiratory distress settled. The child was kept on high-flow oxygen therapy for 36 hours and gradually weaned off the respiratory support. A CXR was repeated after 36 hours, which appeared considerably better than before with considerable clearing of lung parenchyma ( figure 2 ). The child was then shifted to wards and discharged on oral iron supplements. The child was again readmitted with similar signs and symptoms after 2 months. The child appeared pale, and a repeat CXR showed diffuse ground-glass opacities and consolidation. On second admission, while the child was on non-invasive respiratory support, bone marrow aspirate and other relevant blood workup for anaemia were sent. Similar two such episodes of anaemia and respiratory distress occurred after 1 and 2 months, respectively. On every admission, respiratory distress gradually settled after giving corticosteroids. On the last admission, we got a high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest of the child, which revealed multiple areas of consolidation and ground-glass opacities ( figures3 5 ). We also attempted to obtain a bronchoscopic alveolar lavage of the child. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) sample was obtained by wedging the fibreoptic bronchoscope on the right middle lobe and the left lower lobe. Cytology of the BAL sample revealed haemosiderin-laden macrophages. BAL cytology ( figure 6 ) confirmed the diagnosis of pulmonary haemosiderosis, after which the child was started on maintenance immunosuppression with prednisolone at 0.75 mg/kg/day. Differential diagnosis The patient presented with diffuse pulmonary haemorrhage and anaemia. Tests for anti-dsDNA and ANA were negative, thus excluding autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. In addition, c-ANCA and p-ANCA tests were negative, ruling out vasculitis conditions such as Wegener’s granulomatosis, Churg-Strauss syndrome, microscopic polyangiitis, polyarteritis nodosa, Henoch-Schönlein purpura and Behçet’s syndrome. Antiglomerular basement antibodies were also negative, excluding Goodpasture’s syndrome and pulmonary–renal syndrome. The child was not on any medications, such as amiodarone, penicillamine or propylthiouracil. A cardiac examination revealed no abnormalities, ruling out pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary veno-occlusive disease and mitral stenosis. The child had no signs of coagulopathy, heart disease or drug intake history, and there was no evidence of renal pathology. With a negative workup for vasculitis and other causes of pulmonary haemorrhage excluded, this case was diagnosed as idiopathic pulmonary haemosiderosis (IPH). Treatment Non-invasive respiratory support in the form of high-flow oxygen via heated humidified nasal cannula (HHFNC) was given in the PICU at a flow rate of 15 L/min, which was gradually tapered and stopped as the respiratory distress settled. In view of severe anaemia, the patient was also given packed red blood cells at 10 mL/kg. Intravenous fluids, antipyretics and broad-spectrum antibiotics were also given. There are no evidence-based guidelines for the treatment of IPH. Treatment is based on various case reports. Intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg/day is administered for 5 days during the acute phase of respiratory failure. Maintenance therapy was continued with oral prednisolone at a dose of 15 mg/day. Oral steroids at a dose of 15 mg/day were continued at discharge and follow-up. Outcome and follow-up After discharge from the hospital, the child has been symptom free. The patient has been attending monthly follow-up review with me. He is on a balanced diet that is rich in iron. There have been no repeat episodes of iron deficiency anaemia and respiratory distress/failure since discharge. The child has no exertional dyspnoea, and the respiratory system examination has been normal with no abnormal breath sounds on auscultation. The child has been able to rejoin school and is able to carry on his daily physical and scholastic activities without any limitations. The child is also able to do exertional activities such as playing and running without any difficulty. After 2 months of discharge, the child is stable and has had no new episodes of pulmonary haemorrhage. He is still on maintenance prednisolone of 15 mg/day. Discussion IPH is a rare condition of the lung characterised by repeated episodes of pulmonary haemorrhage in the alveoli along with symptomatic presentation of iron deficiency anaemia in children. Incidence as per a Swedish study is 0.24 per million children per year. 1 Another retrospective study from Japan states the incidence to be around 1.23 cases per million children per year. 2 These red blood cells in the alveoli following pulmonary haemorrhage are taken up by the alveolar macrophages. Macrophages degrade the heme to release excess iron. This excess iron is converted to ferritin, which in turn is further processed to haemosiderin. Multiple episodes of haemorrhages in the alveoli lead to the accumulation of haemosiderin in the alveolar macrophages. This form of iron cannot be used by the body, which in turn leads to iron-deficient states in the body. The exact cause of IPH is unknown. Some believe it may be due to autoimmune damage to the pulmonary capillaries, leading to bleeding into pulmonary alveoli. When no cause can be attributed to the pulmonary haemorrhage, it is labelled as idiopathic pulmonary haemorrhage. 3 Aetiology of IPH is multifactorial and uncertain. Predisposing factors include prematurity, exposure to toxic insecticides, secondhand smoke. There has also been some association of toxigenic indoor moulds such as Stachybotrys atra found in the homes of children found suffering from IPH. 4 5 It also has autoimmune mechanisms as it is associated with coeliac disease, rheumatoid arthritis, hyperthyroidism, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, etc. 3 The clinical presentation of the disease can be either acute or chronic. The acute phase typically includes symptoms such as cough, respiratory distress and blood in sputum. Massive pulmonary haemorrhage can also lead to life-threatening asphyxiation. In the chronic phase, patients present with anaemia, organomegaly and failure to thrive. Examination may reveal bilateral pulmonary crackles and clubbing of fingers. Patients with respiratory distress are treated with a pulse dose of methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000–2000 mg per day for 5 days. Patients without respiratory distress may be treated with prednisolone at a dose of 0.75 mg/kg/day, which is continued for around 6–8 weeks until resolution of pulmonary opacities, after which it is gradually tapered by 5 mg every other week to a maintenance dose of 10–15 mg/day. 3 Maintenance dose of steroids might have to be given for prolonged periods of around 18–24 months. Patients who do not respond to prednisolone might have to be started on other immunosuppressive agents along with glucocorticoids such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, hydroxychloroquine and rituximab. 6 A case review published in the European Journal of Medical Research in 2009 by Kamienska et al 7 described a case of a 9-year-old girl who presented with recurrent episodes of pulmonary haemorrhage. Few episodes of anaemia were not accompanied by pulmonary haemorrhage. This case also had coeliac disease with anti-gliadin antibodies positive. Despite giving prednisolone and a gluten-free diet, the child only showed a transient response and developed cushingoid features. Thus, the child had to be started on azathioprine. Despite immunosuppressive therapy, the child showed repeated episodes of pulmonary haemorrhage every 2–3 months. This case review indicates that there are no evidence-based treatment guidelines for this entity, and cases can also have associated coeliac disease. 8 Screening for coeliac disease with serologic testing for IgA anti-TTG antibodies may yield a diagnosis of Lane-Hamilton syndrome. 9 10 Another case review published in the British Medical Journal in 2021 by Iwasaki et al 11 described IPH in an adult man aged 50 years who was suffering from recurrent haemoptysis and chronic respiratory since 6 years. This case was also treated with prednisolone and azathioprine. The case responded well to immunosuppressants; however, there was no improvement in the pulmonary diffusing capacity. This review thus emphasises the fact that early recognition of the disease and early administration of immunosuppressants can prevent lung fibrosis and thus decrease morbidity. The review also emphasises the fact that this disease is also prevalent in adults, although lower as compared with paediatric population. 80% of the cases are childhood onset and only 20% are adult onset. 12 Newer treatment modalities also need to be highlighted for IPH. In a few cases of both adult and paediatric IPH, off-label use of recombinant factor VIIA has been tried. It can be given intravenously or intratracheally via bronchoscope as a single dose or repeated dose if bleeding continues. 13 14 Alternative preparations for delivery of corticosteroids have also been assessed. Liposteroid preparation of dexamethasone palmitate in lipid microspheres at a dose of 0.8 mg/kg per day, administered intravenously for three consecutive days, was effective in a series of nine children with acute presentation of IPH. 15 High-dose inhaled glucocorticoids may be used as an alternative to oral maintenance glucocorticoids. 16 17 Single lung transplants have been tried, but the disease has recurred in the allografts as well. 18 , 20 In our case, the patient presented with recurrent episodes of pulmonary haemorrhage accompanied by iron deficiency anaemia. HRCT of the chest confirmed pulmonary haemorrhage, with no bleeding at other sites. BAL confirmed the presence of haemosiderin-laden macrophages. Other laboratory diagnoses ruled out immunological and autoimmune causes. Thus, in our case, the pulmonary haemorrhage was attributed to IPH. Therapeutic approach with high doses of corticosteroids was successful with subsequent clearance of pulmonary haemorrhages and discharge of the patient. Patient has been on follow-up since then and has had no new episodes of pulmonary haemorrhage since then. The above findings were enough to diagnose this case as a case of IPH. It is important to emphasise that this rare disorder is easy to miss. It is important to diagnose these cases early and start immunosuppressive therapy early to prevent lung fibrosis and achieve optimal results. Patient’s perspective I am the father of this patient and I am highly satisfied with the treatment that my child has received. My child had repeated admissions in PICU for difficulty In breathing. He has been admitted 5 times in the same hospital since the past 9 months. Every time he was admitted to the PICU his chest xray was very bad and was on respiratory support multiple times. I was told by my doctor that this is an unusual cause of anaemia in children. I also went to many other big medical institutes, but my child’s diagnosis could not be made. My doctor convinced me to undergo a bronchoscopic evaluation of my child which helped to reach to the diagnosis. I was a bit hesitant for the bronchoscopy, but I knew that it was important for the diagnosis of disease. I have been in regular follow up with my doctor and since my child has been on prednisolone, my child has not had any repeat episodes of bleeding in the lungs or difficulty in breathing. I am grateful to my doctor for diagnosing this extremely rare disorder in my child and starting prompt treatment with prednisolone. Learning points Pulmonary haemosiderosis is an uncommon cause of diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, causing iron deficiency anaemia. Aetiology is multifactorial and unknown. Idiopathic pulmonary haemosiderosis is a diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out other immunological and autoimmune causes. Management includes supportive care along with the use of glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide, azathioprine and hydroxychloroquine, if required.
