ABSTRACT This study investigated the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet (MD) and new‐onset hypertension (HTN) in a cohort of community‐dwelling, middle‐aged, older Korean individuals with obesity. We used data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study, a population‐based prospective cohort study. The Korean version of the MD Adherence Screener was used to evaluate compliance with the MD. The primary endpoint was the incidence of new‐onset HTN. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval for HTN incidence with increasing MD adherence. Data from 1995 participants with obesity without HTN at baseline were analyzed. After adjusting for confounders, participants with moderate (Group 2) and high adherence (Group 3) to the MD had HRs for new‐onset HTN of 0.72 (0.57–0.90) and 0.73 (0.56–0.95), respectively, compared with those with low adherence (Group 1). Sex and age differences were associated with MD adherence and HTN incidence. The association between MD adherence and HTN incidence was only significant in males and in participants aged 40–64 years. There was no significant association between MD adherence and new‐onset HTN in females or those aged ≥ 65 years. In conclusion, this study found a significant association between adherence to the MD and a reduction in new‐onset HTN in Korean adults with obesity. Considering its value and sustainability, the adoption of the MD may contribute to the prevention of new‐onset HTN in populations with obesity. Introduction Hypertension (HTN) significantly increases the risk of coronary heart disease, stroke, as well as renal and other vascular diseases, and is one of the major causes of premature death [ 1 ]. In 1990, the estimated number of people affected by HTN worldwide was 640 million, which doubled to 1.278 billion in 2019 [ 2 ]. According to statistics from the National Health Insurance Service of Korea, medical expenses for HTN treatment are estimated at 4.3 trillion won, accounting for 4.5% of total medical expenses or 10.9% of medical expenses for chronic diseases [ 3 ]. As of 2020, it is estimated that 29.4% of the Korean adult population, approximately 12.6 million people, have HTN [ 3 ]. The World Obesity Atlas 2023 report stated that 38% of the world's population has a body mass index (BMI) higher than 25 kg/m 2 [ 4 ]. In the United States, based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) III sample, approximately 63% and 55% of adult males and females, respectively, are overweight or obese [ 5 ]. Moreover, the prevalence of HTN among individuals with obesity has been reported to be 40.5% [ 6 ]. Over the past few decades, HTN and obesity rates have rapidly increased in Asia due to lifestyle Westernization. In Korea, the obesity rate has rapidly increased in recent decades due to fast economic growth and the influence of Western culture, with up to 40% of the population classified as having obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m 2 ) [ 7 ]. Individuals with obesity are 3.5 times more likely to have HTN, which increases by 60% with increased fat storage [ 8 ]. According to the NHANES data, the prevalence of HTN among individuals with obesity is 42.5%, while that among individuals without obesity is 15.3% [ 9 ]. Previous studies have shown that weight loss is associated with a significant clinical reduction in the activity of the sympathetic nervous system and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, which can have a substantial impact on lowering the blood pressure (BP) [ 10 , 11 ]. The Mediterranean diet (MD) is characterized by a high intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil, along with a low intake of animal products, and has been proven to have beneficial effects on health [ 12 ]. The MD Adherence Screener (MEDAS) is a well‐validated tool for assessing MD adherence in population‐based studies (internal consistency coefficient: 0.51) [ 13 ]. Dietary habits vary by country and race, and it is difficult to apply the existing MEDAS to other populations. Traditional Korean dietary habits are characterized by a high intake of grains, vegetables, legumes, and fish and a low consumption of red meat. The major differences between the traditional Korean diet and the MD are the high consumption of white rice and the low consumption of fats in the Korean diet [ 14 ]. Therefore, we used the Korean version of the MEDAS (K‐MEDAS), developed by our research team from the original MEDAS, for easy application in the Korean population and validated it in the general and high‐risk populations with obesity [ 15 ]. Therefore, this study investigated the association between MD adherence, measured using the K‐MEDAS, and new‐onset HTN in a community‐dwelling, middle‐aged, and older Korean population with obesity, utilizing data from a large prospective cohort study. 2. Methods 2.1. Study Population This study used data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), a large‐scale, population‐based, prospective cohort study aimed at analyzing the environmental causes and trends of chronic diseases in Korea. The baseline KoGES study was conducted in rural Ansan and urban Ansung, Korea, from 2001 to 2002 and enrolled 10030 adults. Inclusion criteria were 40–69 years of age with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m 2 ( N = 4285). Follow‐up examinations were conducted biennially, with the final follow‐up visit conducted in 2019–2020 (ninth follow‐up visit). A total of 3922 participants had at least one follow‐up. To investigate the development of new‐onset HTN, 1465 participants with HTN at baseline and 462 participants with missing data for confounding factors were excluded. As a result, 1995 participants remained for analysis (Figure 1 ). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Institutional Review Boards of the medical colleges (approval No. 4‐2023‐1360). 2.2. Anthropometric and Laboratory Measurements Trained medical professionals measured the anthropometric variables, including weight, height, and waist circumference (WC). Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg, height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm with participants wearing light clothing and standing without shoes, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m 2 ). WC (cm) was measured at the midpoint between the iliac crest and lowest rib. The participants were seated, and their BP was recorded twice at 1‐min intervals. A mercury sphygmomanometer was used to measure the BP according to a standardized protocol. Mean arterial pressure was calculated from systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) as follows: (SBP + [2 DBP]) / 3. Collected blood samples were sent to a central laboratory (Seoul Clinical Laboratory, Seoul, Korea) for analysis. Blood samples were collected after ≥8 h of fasting. Fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin, creatinine, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and C‐reactive protein levels were enzymatically analyzed using a Chemistry Analyzer (Hitachi 7600; Hitachi Ltd, Tokyo, Japan [until August 2002] and ADVIA 1650; Siemens, Tarrytown, NY, USA [from September 2002]). The self‐reported questionnaires contained information on smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity. Non‐smokers were defined as those who had never smoked during their lifetime. Smokers included participants who reported ever smoking at any point in their lifetime and those who reported current smoking on the questionnaire. Alcohol intake was defined as consumption of &gt; 20 g/day for males and &gt; 10 g/day for females. Physical activity levels for each participant were recorded in metabolic equivalents of tasks using an International Physical Activity Questionnaire, which represents the energy expenditure of an activity compared with that at rest. This was measured as the sum of the average hours of activity per week. Detailed study protocols are available at: https://nih.go.kr/ko/main/contents.do?menuNo=300563 . 2.3. Dietary Assessment Dietary intake was assessed using a semiquantitative 103‐item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) administered by well‐trained nutritionists. Previous studies have confirmed the reproducibility and validity of the FFQ [ 16 ]. The questionnaire assessed how often participants consumed each food (never or rarely, once a month, 2–3 times a month, 2–3 times a week, 3–4 times a week, 5–6 times a week, once a day, twice a day, or 3 times a day) and how much of the food they consumed each time (half a serving, 1 serving, or ≥ 2 servings). Nutrient intake was calculated using CAN‐Pro 5.0, developed by the Korean Nutrition Society ( http://www.kns.or.kr ). The ratio of carbohydrates and proteins was calculated as carbohydrate intake (g) × 4 kcal/total energy intake (kcal/day) and protein intake (g) × 4 kcal/total energy intake (kcal/day). The ratio of fat (total, saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated, omega‐3, and omega‐6) was calculated as fat intake (g) × 9 kcal/total energy intake (kcal/d). 2.4. Definition of Variables 2.4.1. Definition of HTN The primary outcome was the incidence of new‐onset HTN. HTN was defined when at least one of the following criteria was met: (1) SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, (2) DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or (3) treatment with anti‐hypertensive medications [ 17 ]. New‐onset HTN was defined as the diagnosis of HTN during the follow‐up period in individuals with previously normal BP. 2.4.2. Definition of the K‐MEDAS The K‐MEDAS was translated into Korean from the original MEDAS to evaluate MD adherence [ 18 ]. The K‐MEDAS consists of 14 questions, each scored as 0 or 1 point: thus, the total score ranges from 0 to 14 points. The detailed K‐MEDAS questions are presented in Table S1 [ 15 ]. Participants’ adherence to the MD was classified as low (≤ 4), moderate (5–7), and high (≥ 8), according to their K‐MEDAS score [ 15 , 19 , 20 ]. 2.5. Statistical Analysis All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Among‐group comparisons of continuous and categorical variables were performed using analysis of variance and Chi‐squared test, respectively. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for HTN incidence across categories of increasing adherence to the MD. Four models were used in the analysis: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, alcohol intake, smoking, and physical activity; and Model 4 was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, SBP, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), creatinine, LDL‐C, alcohol intake, smoking, and physical activity. Covariates were selected based on statistical and clinical significance, which included variables with p &lt; 0.05 in the baseline comparison across groups and univariate analysis. Subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the association between the K‐MEDAS score and new‐onset HTN across the different subgroups based on age and sex. For all statistical tests, statistical significance was set at p &lt; 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 3. Results 3.1. Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population According to the K‐MEDAS Scores The baseline characteristics of the study population according to the K‐MEDAS scores are presented in Table 1 . Among the total cohort of 1995 participants, 1118 (56%) developed new‐onset HTN during a median follow‐up period of 13.9 years after completing the ninth follow‐up survey. Participants with high adherence to the MD (Group 3) were more likely to be female ( p &lt; 0.001), older ( p &lt; 0.001), and have lower BMI ( p = 0.023), total cholesterol ( p = 0.034), and LDL‐cholesterol levels ( p = 0.015) compared to participants with low and moderate adherence to the MD (Groups 1 and 2, respectively). Moreover, participants in Group 3 were less likely to be smokers ( p &lt; 0.001) and consume alcohol ( p = 0.003) compared to those in Groups 1 and 2. The incidence of HTN was significantly higher in participants in Group 1 than those in Groups 2 and 3. Table S2 shows the incidence of HTN according to the follow‐up period. The incidence of new‐onset HTN was analyzed biennially. A total of 1118 individuals (56.0%) developed new‐onset HTN with the incidence rate per 2 years ranging from 5.6 to 14.1. 3.2. Nutritional Status of the Study Population According to the K‐MEDAS Scores Table 2 shows the nutritional status of the study population according to the K‐MEDAS scores. Participants with high adherence to the MD (Group 3) had a higher intake of total energy ( p = 0.015), protein ( p &lt; 0.001), and carbohydrates ( p = 0.003) than did those with low and moderate adherence to the MD (Groups 1 and 2, respectively). Moreover, participants in Group 3 were more likely to have higher intake levels of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA), including omega‐3 fatty acids ( p &lt; 0.001), and dietary fiber ( p &lt; 0.001) compared to those in Groups 1 and 2. However, participants in Group 3 had lower intake levels of saturated fatty acid ( p &lt; 0.001) and sodium‐potassium ratios ( p &lt; 0.001) compared to those in Groups 1 and 2. Participants in Group 3 were more likely to have higher intake levels of Vitamin A ( p &lt; 0.001), β‐carotene ( p &lt; 0.001), Vitamin C ( p &lt; 0.001), Vitamin D ( p = 0.003), Vitamin E ( p &lt; 0.001), Vitamin K ( p &lt; 0.001), Vitamin B complex; such as thiamine ( p &lt; 0.001), riboflavin ( p &lt; 0.001), niacin ( p &lt; 0.001), Vitamin B6 ( p &lt; 0.001), Vitamin B12 ( p &lt; 0.001), and folic acid ( p &lt; 0.001) compared to those in Groups 1 and 2. 3.3. Association Between MD Adherence and New‐Onset HTN in Adults With Obesity Table 3 shows the univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses for incident new‐onset HTN based on the K‐MEDAS scores. In the univariable analysis, the HRs (95% CI) for new‐onset HTN for the participants with moderate and high adherence to the MD (Groups 2 and 3, respectively), with reference to the participants with low adherence to the MD (Group 1), were 0.77 (0.62–0.97) and 0.78 (0.61–1.01), respectively. In Model 2, the adjusted HRs (95% CI) for new‐onset HTN for participants in Groups 2 and 3, compared with those for participants in Group 1, were 0.77 (0.61–0.96) and 0.75 (0.58–0.97), respectively. In Model 3, the adjusted HRs (95% CI) for new‐onset HTN for participants in Groups 2 and 3, compared with those for participants in Group 1, were 0.76 (0.61–0.95) and 0.76 (0.59–0.98), respectively. In Model 4, the adjusted HRs (95% CI) for new‐onset HTN for participants in Groups 2 and 3, compared with those for participants in Group 1, were 0.72 (0.57–0.90) and 0.73 (0.56–0.95), respectively. 3.4. Association Between the K‐MEDAS Scores and New‐Onset HTN in Adults With Obesity According to Sex and Age Table 4 shows the HRs with 95% CIs for incident new‐onset HTN according to the K‐MEDAS scores in a subgroup analysis by sex and age. In Model 4, after adjusting for age, BMI, SBP, glucose, creatinine, LDL‐C, alcohol intake, smoking, and physical activity, the adjusted HRs (95% CIs) for incident new‐onset HTN among males with moderate and high adherence to the MD (Groups 2 and 3, respectively), compared to those with low adherence to the MD (Group 1), were 0.70 (0.52–0.95) and 0.65 (0.44–0.97), respectively. No significant difference in the risk of incident new‐onset HTN was observed among females according to the K‐MEDAS scores. In the subgroup analysis by age, in Model 4, after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, SBP, glucose, creatinine, LDL‐C, alcohol intake, smoking, and physical activity, the adjusted HRs (95% CIs) for incident new‐onset HTN for participants aged 40–64 years in Groups 2 and 3, compared to those in Group 1, were 0.68 (0.53–0.86) and 0.73 (0.55–0.96), respectively. No significant difference in the risk of incident new‐onset HTN was observed in the age group of ≥65 years according to the K‐MEDAS scores. 4. Discussion In this Korean prospective cohort study, adherence to the MD was significantly associated with the incidence of new‐onset HTN in adults with obesity. Participants with moderate (Group 2) and high adherence (Group 3) to the MD had a lower incidence of new‐onset HTN than did those with low adherence (Group 1). The association between MD adherence and HTN incidence was only significant in males and those aged 40–64 years. There was no significant association between MD adherence and HTN in females or those aged ≥ 65 years. The MD reflects the characteristic food combinations of Mediterranean populations, such as those in Greece, Italy, and Spain [ 21 ]. A large cross‐sectional study conducted in Italy's Tuscan region showed a significant inverse association between the MD, SBP, and DBP values in normotensive adults, and the major clinical trial, the Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea study (PREDIMED), confirmed that adherence to the MD could lower DBP [ 22 ]. This diet is predominantly plant‐based and includes vegetables, legumes, whole grain bread, and other grains, nuts, and seeds, with fresh fruit as a typical dessert and extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) as the main source of fat [ 23 ]. Moderate consumption of low‐ or moderate‐fat dairy products, fish, poultry, and eggs; low consumption of red meat; and moderate wine consumption with meals are also characteristic of the MD [ 23 ]. Our findings are supported by the characteristics of the MD, which is a diet rich in bioactive nutrients that promote health in terms of cardiovascular mortality [ 24 ]. Recent research has shown that the MD has a protective effect against dyslipidemia and CVD, which are related to the progression of atherosclerosis [ 25 ]. The high‐intensity monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and omega‐3 PUFAs obtained from olive oil, nuts, and fish oil in the MD reduce hepatic lipid production, LDL cholesterol, and LDL‐oxidation susceptibility, thereby exerting a lipid‐lowering effect [ 26 ]. Dietary fiber from whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and fruits also significantly lowers plasma LDL cholesterol levels by preventing bile acid reabsorption in the ileum [ 27 ]. EVOO and vegetables reduce BP by increasing nitric oxide (NO) synthesis to relax endothelial muscles through high levels of polyphenols [ 28 ]. Additionally, fish, nuts, and seeds reduce inflammation and promote healthy endothelial function as they are rich in MUFAs and PUFAs [ 29 ]. The fatty acids in EVOO are protected by natural antioxidants, including carotenoids, tocopherols, and phenolic compounds [ 30 ]. Antioxidants neutralize the effects of free radicals and lipid oxidation, which can affect arterial stiffness [ 31 ]. The high content of plant‐based foods, which are rich in minerals that help lower BP, such as potassium, magnesium, and calcium, may serve as a mechanism in this regard [ 32 ]. Sodium intake is regulated through the kidney and hormonal processes and is essential for extracellular fluid balance [ 33 ]. Potassium plays a physiological role in determining plasma volume [ 34 ]. Excessive sodium intake can lead to arterial stiffness and increase HTN by blocking NO, while high dietary potassium intake can reduce arterial stiffness and activate NO release, thereby lowering HTN [ 35 ]. It has been reported that the effect of potassium shows a stronger correlation than that of sodium [ 36 ]. Moreover, the combined effect of the sodium‐to‐potassium ratio is stronger than the individual effects of sodium or potassium alone [ 37 ]. In our study, participants in Group 3 had lower sodium‐potassium ratios compared to those in Groups 1 and 2. The prevalence of obesity is continuously increasing worldwide and contributes to the increasing prevalence of HTN [ 38 ]. The reason for the increase in the number of patients with obesity is related to dietary habits that promote excessive energy intake [ 39 ]. Therefore, HTN treatment should begin with weight loss by improving dietary patterns [ 40 ]. Patients with obesity tend to prefer high‐calorie, low‐nutrition foods and consume relatively fewer unprocessed foods that are rich in vitamins and minerals, such as fruits, vegetables, dairy products, and nuts [ 41 ]. Recommended dietary approaches for weight loss in patients with obesity include diverse intakes of nutrient‐rich foods and limitations on high‐energy‐dense foods. The MD is low in animal fats and sugars, while the moderate incorporation of vegetables, fruits, nuts, and dairy products reduces the overall caloric intake [ 42 ]. The mechanisms of HTN in obesity include increased activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, increased sympathetic nervous system activity, and insulin resistance [ 43 ]. Furthermore, obesity is associated with increased renal sodium reabsorption, impaired pressure natriuresis, and increased BP. In 2012, in their recommendations for obesity complications, the European Association for the Study of Obesity and the European Society of HTN stated that weight loss could contribute to BP control, resulting in an average BP reduction of 1.05/0.92 mm Hg per kilogram of weight loss [ 44 ]. In this study, adherence to the MD was only significantly associated with the incidence of HTN in males. These results are consistent with those of a previous study that reported the differential effects of MD intake on insulin concentration [ 45 ]. The differences in arterial structure and function between males and females provide plausible biological mechanisms that support these results [ 46 ]. This corresponds to differences in arterial structure and hormonal effects on BP. The sex‐specific discrepancies in the effects of the MD on HTN may also arise from the sex‐specific pharmacokinetics of the phenolic compounds contained within foods [ 47 ]. In our study, no significant association was found between adherence to the MD and HTN in patients aged ≥65 years. However, in the 40–64‐year age group, adherence to the MD was significantly associated with a reduced incidence of HTN. These results support those of previous studies and suggest that healthy dietary patterns may be more effective in reducing cardiometabolic risk among younger adults [ 48 ]. As recently evidenced in cross‐sectional studies, adherence to the MD can provide health advantages starting at a young age [ 49 ]. In older adults, factors other than diet, such as physical activity, may be more important in cardiometabolic abnormalities owing to age‐related changes in body composition [ 50 , 51 ]. We have conducted additional analyses on non‐obese individuals, and the results are presented in Tables S3–S5 . Interestingly, we found no significant association between the MD and new‐onset HTN in non‐obese individuals, suggesting that the MD may be particularly effective in preventing HTN through its role in weight reduction. Previous studies on the MD have shown that HTN is closely linked to weight status, and the MD can modify this relationship [ 52 ]. Consistent with our findings, obese participants who adhered well to the MD had a lower likelihood of developing HTN compared to those who did not adhere to the diet. Future research should further explore the role of the MD across different weight categories to better understand its broader implications. Our study had some limitations. First, since this study was conducted in middle‐aged and older adult populations in Korea, the results may not be generalizable to other ethnic groups. Second, since dietary intake data were collected through self‐reporting, the accuracy of the results may have been limited. Third, dietary patterns may have changed over the cohort period; however, this aspect was not considered. Nonetheless, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to confirm the prospective relationship between MD adherence and new‐onset HTN in a Korean adult population with obesity. Few studies have specifically targeted patients with obesity with HTN; however, our findings expand the current body of literature on the association between MD adherence and HTN in a large, non‐Mediterranean population. 5. Conclusions In conclusion, this study found a significant association between MD adherence and a reduction in new‐onset HTN in Korean adults with obesity. Considering its value and sustainability, the adoption of the MD may contribute to the prevention of new‐onset HTN in populations with obesity.
