Background Few studies have longitudinally assessed the prevalence and factors associated with oral healthcare utilisation (OCU) in older adults. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence and factors associated with OCU based on 4-wave national longitudinal data from Thailand. Methods Analysis was done on four waves of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Thailand (HART) investigations carried out in 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022 (analytical baseline sample N = 2775). The number of past 12 months of oral healthcare visits was assessed by self-report. Using the panel data structure of four study waves, the impact of time-dependent regressors on healthcare utilization was estimated using conditional fixed effects (FE) logistic regressions. We used the Andersen model as the theoretical foundation. Results The prevalence of OCU in the past 12 months at baseline assessment was 7.4%. Regressions showed the importance of need factors (poor self-rated physical health, loneliness, number of chronic conditions, weight loss, meal skipping and wearing dentures), predisposing characteristics (decreasing age), and enabling and disabling resources (private health insurance, Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme, Social Security Scheme, or Other Scheme, transitioning to live alone, higher qualify of life or happiness and higher depressive symptoms) in relation to OCU. Conclusions This longitudinal study improves our knowledge of the factors influencing middle-aged and older individuals’ OCU in Thailand. In 2022, 13 million people aged ≥ 60 constitute about 19% of the Thai population, which is expected to increase further to 19 million, accounting for 31.4% in 2042 [ 2 ]. Because life expectancy is now rising, the prevalence of oral illnesses is gradually increasing among older age groups [ 3 ]; oral health has become a significant concern [ 4 ]. In Thailand, 6.2% of people aged 60–74 and 23.1% of people aged 80–85 had edentulism, according to the 9th Thai National Oral Health Survey conducted in 2022–2023. Of those aged 60–74, 74.3% had gingivitis, 48.7% had periodontitis, with 18.7% of cases being severe, 60.0% had untreated coronal dental caries, with an average of 2.1 teeth per person, 35.5% had untreated root caries due to gingival recession, with an average of 0.9 teeth per person, and 3.7% had xerostomia. If these oral health issues are not adequately addressed, they may worsen, resulting in tooth loss and dental pain [ 5 ]. Older people may have a lower quality of life and use dental services less frequently due to complications from oral disorders, such as pain, infection, and loss of function [ 6 ]. Regular usage of oral healthcare services is crucial and required because of its significance for older adults. Frequent dental visits result in oral health promotion, oral health education, early diagnosis and treatment of oral health issues, and maintenance of oral health status [ 7 ]. But older persons are less likely to seek oral healthcare than others [ 8 ]. Even though all Thai citizens are covered by the nation’s publicly administered health insurance programs, which offer comparatively extensive benefits coverage, there are still significant gaps in non-medical expenditures and social support to make it easier, especially for older people, to obtain and use healthcare, including dental care services [ 9 ]. In cross-sectional surveys, including the Thailand Health &amp; Welfare Survey of the older adults aged 60 years and older in 2007, low rates of oral healthcare utilisation (OCU) in the past 12 months were found (8.1%) [ 10 ], and in the 8th and 9th th Thai National Oral Health Surveys in older adults aged 60–74 years in 2017 and 2022/23, 38.6% and 30.6%, respectively, had OCU in the last 12 months [ 5 , 11 ]. However, we have no longitudinal national data on middle-aged and older adults on the prevalence and correlates of OCU in Thailand, which led to this study. According to Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Healthcare Access [ 12 ], factors associated with OCU may include predisposing, enabling, and need factors. Demographics and social structure are examples of predisposing factors; organizational and financial resources are examples of enabling elements; and need factors indicate a felt or assessed need for oral healthcare. Predisposing factors associated with OCU may include decreases with age [ 4 , 13 ], females [ 4 , 14 ], and race or ethnic factors (non-white) [ 4 ]. Enabling or disabling factors associated with OCU include transition to widowed [ 13 ], married [ 4 , 14 ], increased or higher education [ 4 , 11 , 15 ], higher income [ 4 , 10 , 11 ], member of health insurance fund [ 4 , 10 ], urban residence [ 4 , 11 ], family and social support [ 4 ]), social interaction, social participation, and neighborhood cohesion [ 16 ] and dental health literacy [ 4 ]. Need factors associated with OCU include a lower number of chronic conditions [ 4 ], an increasing number of physical illnesses [ 13 ], the transition from normal to overweight [ 13 ], poor mental health (anxiety and depression) [ 17 , 18 ], lower loneliness [ 16 ], non-smoking [ 4 , 11 ], fewer number of teeth [ 4 ], brushing teeth two times or more per day [ 11 ], good perceived quality of dental care and perceived oral problems [ 14 ]. However, only a few longitudinal studies have investigated OCU in national community-dwelling older adults, and these few have been conducted in high-income countries (e.g., in Sweden [ 14 ], and Germany [ 13 ]), while no longitudinal studies have been identified in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Considering the paucity of data on this topic, especially concerning longitudinal studies in LMICs and among older adults, our study aimed to examine, using longitudinal, national community-dwelling data from 2015 to 2022, the factors influencing OCU among people 45 and older. This knowledge is essential to avoid mis- or inappropriate use of OCU. Methods Sample and procedures Four waves of health and retirement in Thailand (HART) investigations conducted by the National Institute of Development Administration in Thailand in 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022 were analyzed. One adult (over 45) was randomly selected each household (as the inclusion criterion) and questioned in the home using a multi-stage national sample method; more information is provided elsewhere [ 19 ]. In a nutshell, Step 1 involved creating six strata from the following regions: South; (2) East; (3) Bangkok and its environs (Pathum Thani, Samut Prakan, and Nonthaburi); (4) North; (5) Northeast; (6) Central, omitting Bangkok and its surroundings; and (5) East. Every province (Changwat) was chosen for the first stratum. Thailand’s capital is Bangkok, and compared to other provinces, the core areas of Nonthaburi, Samut Prakan, and Pathum Thani are home to a disproportionately large number of elderly people. Only seven provinces make up the second stratum; they are all in eastern Thailand and have relatively modest older populations. As a result, one province was chosen at random. “Small-size” provinces are defined as having fewer than 300,000 households, whereas “large-size” provinces are defined as having more than 300,000 households. Two provinces were chosen at random from the group of major provinces and the group of minor provinces for each stratum [ 19 ]. From 5616 participants at baseline in 2015, 887 died, 462 refused, and 1492 were uncontactable, leaving an analytic sample of 2775 participants in 2022 (response rate 58.7%). Participants provided their informed written consent, and the Human Research Ethics Committee of the National Institute for Development Administration gave its approval to the study’s protocol (ECNIDA 2020/00012). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (such as the Declaration of Helsinki). Measures Dependent variable OCU was assessed with the question, “In the past year, have you visited a dentist for examination or treatment of your teeth?” (Yes/No). This is a common way to assess OCU [ 5 , 10 , 11 ]. Furthermore, those who responded with ‘yes’ to the OCU question were asked to report their out-of-pocket expenses for oral healthcare in the past year. Independent variables The “behavioural model of health service use” developed by Andersen et al. [ 12 ] served as the source of the independent variables used in the analysis of OCU. The literature on OCU also served as a guide for the selection (e.g [ 4 , 15 ].,. The potential predictors of OCU were predisposing, enabling or disabling, and need factors. Predisposing characteristics Age, sex, highest educational level (1 = None, 2= ‘Primary school’,3=’Junior secondary school’, or ‘Senior secondary school’, or ‘Vocational Certificate’, or ‘Vocational diploma’, or ‘Bachelor’s Degree’, or ‘Higher than Bachelor’s Degree), marital status (widowed/single/divorced/separated versus married/cohabiting), and work status (not working/working) were included. Although education and sex are time-invariant (i.e., they do most likely not vary within older individuals over time), and residence status is almost time-invariant, they were mentioned in the descriptive results to understand our sample better. Enabling and disabling characteristics Participants were asked if they had purchased a private health insurance. In addition, they were asked if they were member of the following health insurance schemes: ‘Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme’, ‘Social Security Scheme’, ‘Universal Coverage Scheme’ and ‘Other’. “How satisfied are you with your economic situation?” was the question that assess subjective economic status. Higher scores indicated higher economic standing; scores ranged from 0 to 10. People who reported having just one person in their home as opposed to living with one or more other people were considered to be living alone [ 20 ]. Religious involvement was sourced from four items: (1) “Making merit and giving alms according to respected religious principles,” (2) “Prayer in the morning/before going to bed,” (3) “Performing merit-making activities at religious places according to the religions that the interviewees respect on important religious days,” and (4) “Observing important religious days that the interviewee respects.” [ 20 ]. The Cronbach alpha was 0.80, and the response choices were 0 = never to 3 = always, for a total of 0–12. Higher scores indicated stronger religious commitment. Quality of life (QoL)/happiness was assessed with the question, “Overall, how satisfied are you with your quality of life (or how happy do you feel)?” reported on a “0 (= very poor) to 10 (= excellent) scale”. Single-item QoL measures have been found to be valid [ 21 ]. The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) was used to measure depressed symptoms; the loneliness item was not included. Higher scores denoted more depressed symptoms; the total scores ranged from 0 to 27 [ 22 ]; 0.69 was Cronbach’s alpha. Need characteristics Self-rated physical health status was assessed with the question, “In general, how would you rate your physical health status?” reported on a “0 (= very poor) to 10 (= excellent)” scale. Poorer self-rated physical health status was reverse scored, with higher scores indicating poorer self-rated physical health status. Single-item self-rated health measures are valid [ 23 ]. Loneliness was measured from the CES-D-10 item [ 22 ], “In the past week, how often did you experience feeling lonely?” defined as “almost always (5–7 days), often (3–4 days) or sometimes (1–2 days)”=1 and “very rarely (less than one day) or none”=0. Items were reverse scored so that higher scores reflected higher loneliness. Diabetes, high blood pressure, bone disorders, heart disease, mental and emotional disorders, dementia, liver and gastrointestinal illnesses, renal issues, lung diseases, prostrate diseases, uterine or ovarian diseases, cancer, visual impairment, hearing impairment, and self-reported urinary incontinence during the previous month were among the chronic conditions (0–15) identified by healthcare providers. Functional limitations were assessed with a modified 5-item Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale (dress, wash, eat, bathing or showering, and other) [ 24 ]. Response options were 0) ‘Can do every step myself without needing help,’ 1) ‘Need help sometimes with some steps,’ 2) ‘Need help with some steps,’ 3) ‘Need help sometimes with every step,’ and 4) ‘Need help every time with every step.’ Total scores ranged from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating higher ADL, and as a binary variable, any score on the five items = 1 and no score = 0. It has a Cronbach’s α of 0.95. Weight loss was assessed with the question, “Have you gained or lost 5 or more kilograms in the last year?” Responses were coded 1= “Yes, lost more than 5 kg”, and 0=”Yes, the extra weight is more than 5 kg”, or “Yes, the weight increased more than 5 kg, but the current decrease is the same”, or “Yes, lost more than 5 kg, but the current increase is the same, or “No fixed weight (increase/decrease not more than 5 kg).” [ 25 ]. Substance use included current smoking (yes/no). To determine the frequency of physical activity or exercise, participants were asked, “How often do you exercise?” (“1 = 0 days, 2 = 1–2 days, 3 = 3–4 days, 4 = 5–6 days or 5 = 7 days per week”). Meal skipping (0–6) was assessed with the question: “How many meals have you had in the last 2 days?” “Yesterday (breakfast, lunch, dinner; yes/no) and the day before yesterday (breakfast, lunch, dinner; yes/no).” [ 26 ]. The wearing of dentures was assessed by asking the question, ”Do you wear dentures?” (Yes/No). Data analysis The sample was described using descriptive statistics. For each of the four study waves from 2015 to 2022, conditional fixed effects (FE) logistic regressions were used to assess the longitudinal associations between time-varying independent factors and time-varying OCU (the likelihood of a move from a non- OCU state to an OCU state), for the whole sample, and stratified by sex- and age-group. As a result, only those whose OCU status changed during the observation time were added to FE models. Because these people only altered their OCU status within the observation period, only 3098 observations were considered in the FE regression analysis. The findings of the FE regressions can be understood as average treatment effects on the treated because this estimator focuses on individuals with changes [ 27 ]. The conditional FE logistic regression was chosen based on the Hausman specification test between the FE model and the random-effects model [Chi 2 (57.21) p &lt; 0.001]. Based on previous reviews [ 4 , 10 , 11 , 13 – 18 ], time-varying independent variables were included. Sex and education cannot be included as main effects in FE regressions because they are time-invariant variables, meaning that they typically do not change over time among individuals. Consequently, education was used for descriptive purposes and sex, a time-constant component, was used to stratify the regression models. The variance inflation factor (VIF) of each individual predictor variable was used to perform collinearity analysis; values less than 1.82 denoted minimal collinearity in this study, while values of 10 or more denoted excessive collinearity [ 28 ]. P &lt; 0.05 was deemed significant, and only full instances (missing cases were &lt; 4% in study variables) were examined by using listwise deletion. For statistical analysis, StataSE 18.0 (College Station, TX, USA) was utilized. Results Participants The baseline analytical sample consisted of 2775 participants. The prevalence of OCU in the past 12 months at baseline was 7.4%. Time-constant variables (excluded in FE regressions) indicate that 56.2% were females, 93.0% had primary or more education, and 51.2% lived in rural areas. The mean age was 65.2 years (ranging from 45 to 106 years), 62.7% were married or cohabiting, and 51.5% were currently working. Almost one in ten (8.0%) living alone, and 6.7 (0–10) had a subjective economic status. The average number of chronic conditions was 0.94 (SD = 1.1), functional limitations 0.18 (SD = 1.2), 11.9% were current smokers, and 20.1% were wearing dentures. The average poor self-rated physical health state was 2.9 (SD = 1.9), 5.7 (SD = 3.0) was the average level of depression symptoms, and the average level of QoL/happiness was 7.9 (SD = 1.5) (see Table 1 ). Factors associated with dental care utilization FE logistic regressions showed that decreasing age (OR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.87–0.92), an increase in buying health insurance (OR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.02-2.00), transitioning to living alone (OR: 1.35, 95% CI: 1.02–1.79), increases in quality of life or happiness (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.00-1.14), increases in poorer self-rated physical health status (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.12), decreases in depressive symptoms (OR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.98), increases in loneliness (OR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.15–1.66), increases in the number of chronic conditions (OR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.08–1.28), increases in weight loss (OR: 1.95, 95% CI: 1.35–1.81), increases in physical activity (OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.09–1.21), increases in meal skipping (OR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.09–1.21) and increase in wearing dentures (OR: 2.94, 95% CI: 2.14–4.04) increased OCU. Furthermore, transitioning from Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme’, ‘Social Security Scheme’, or ‘Other Scheme’. to universal coverage scheme (OR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.60 to 0.92) decreased OCU (see Table 2 ). In sex-stratified analysis, among women and not men, a decrease in depressive symptoms (OR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.89–0.97), an increase in loneliness (OR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.11–1.71), an increase in the number of chronic conditions (OR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08–1.38), an increase in weight loss (OR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.29–3.24), and an increase in meal skipping (OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.02–1.38) increased OCU, while transitioning from Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme’, ‘Social Security Scheme’, or ‘Other Scheme’ to universal coverage scheme (OR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.52 to 0.93) decreased OCU (see Table 3 ). In age group-stratified analysis, among middle-aged (45–59 years) participants and not among participants 60 years and older, an increase in poorer self-rated physical health status (OR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.04–1.33) increased, and transitioning to non-smoking (OR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.10–0.84) decreased OCU, while among older adults (60 years and older) and not among middle-aged (45–59 years) participants, transitioning to living alone (OR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.07–2.02), an increase in meal skipping (OR: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.09–1.48) increased OCU, while transitioning from Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme’, ‘Social Security Scheme’, or ‘Other Scheme’ to universal coverage scheme (OR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.52 to 0.88) decreased OCU (see Table 4 ). Discussion This study aimed to identify longitudinal predictors of OCU among Thai adults aged 45 and older using national, community-based data from 2015 to 2022. The prevalence of OCU in the past 12 months was 7.4%, which was similar to previous health and welfare survey in Thailand among older adults (8.1% in 2007 [ 10 ], but much lower than in oral health surveys in Thailand (38.6% in 2017, and 30.6% in 2022-23 [ 5 , 11 ]. Being similar survey methodologies across the different studies reported here (nationally representative face-to-face interview household surveys in older adults, and with similar questions on OCU), one explanation for the significantly higher prevalence of OCU in oral health surveys (&gt; 30%) than in general health surveys (&lt; 10%) could be that in oral health surveys also oral health examinations are conducted, which may lead to an increased reporting of OCU. The major reasons for OCU in 2022-23 oral health survey in Thailand were dental problems (48.0%), dental check-ups (19.8%), and follow-up dental appointments (19.8%) [ 5 ]. Nevertheless, the past 12 OCU rates of 7.4-30.6% in Thailand seem low and call for public dental health care interventions. The study found that decreasing age, an increase in buying health insurance, transitioning to living alone, increases in quality of life or happiness, increases in poorer self-rated physical health status, increases in loneliness, decreases in depressive symptoms, increases in the number of chronic conditions, increases in weight loss, increases in physical activity, increases in meal skipping and increase in wearing dentures increased OCU. Furthermore, transitioning from Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme’, ‘Social Security Scheme’, or ‘Other Scheme’ to universal coverage scheme decreased OCU. Regarding predisposing factors, in line with previous cross-sectional and longitudinal studies [ 4 , 13 ], this study found that older adults had lower OCU than younger adults, emphasising the higher risk of oral healthcare underutilisation, especially considering the low prevalence of 7.4% who had OCU in the past 12 months. In terms of enbling or disabling factors, although various studies [ 4 , 10 , 11 ] showed that higher income was associated with OCU, this study did not find a significant association between subjective economic status and OCU. However, meal skipping and weight loss, which were both positively associated with OCU, may be considered as a proxy of decreased economic capacity in the context of Thailand and other societies [ 30 , 31 ]. Among OCU users, 38.0% reported to have out-of-pocket expenses for OCU in the past year. Previous research showed a positive association between members of health insurance fund and OCU [ 4 , 10 ], while in this study, purchase of private health insurance increased the odds of OCU. Being a member of the Universal coverage scheme had a significantly lower OCU, in particular among older women, than being a member of other schemes, which is consistent with a previous study in Thailand [ 10 ]. The reasons for this may lie in the fact that the Universal coverage scheme offers less comprehensive benefits than the other health insurance schemes, such as ‘Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme’, ‘Social Security Scheme’ and private health insurance. Transitioning to living alone increased the odds of OCU, in particular in the older age group (60 years and older). Similar results were found in a cross-sectional study among older adults in São Paulo, Brazil [ 32 ], but most studies found opposite results [ 33 , 34 ]. We would have expected that due to the lack of the enabling factor social support in older adults living alone would have decreased the odds of OCU [ 33 ]. However, little is known about the longitudinal changes in living arrangement and OCU [ 13 ]. One possibility suggested previously [ 13 ] could be that the loss of a spouse leading to living alone may free up increased self-care, including OCU. Previous cross-sectional studies found an association between poor mental health (anxiety and depression) [ 17 , 18 ] with OCU, while our study found the opposite, namely decreasing depressive symptoms increased the odds of OCU. It is possible that people with depressive symptoms are more withdrawn, lack energy, and do not have the motivation to consult dental services. A higher quality of life or happiness increased the odds of OCU. Similarly, in a previous study among aging adults in Mexico [ 35 ], low life satisfaction and past-year OCU in the rural population. The promotion of subjective well-being may also increase OCU. However, social participation in the form of religious involvement did not significantly increase OCU, as found in a previous study [ 16 ]. In terms of need factors, another sub-component of access was the need for therapy [ 4 , 36 ], which is consistent with our findings that those wearing dentures, increases in meal skipping, and had poorer self-rated physical health had a higher OCU. Consistent with a longitudinal study [ 13 ] and a cross-sectional study [ 37 ], this study showed that an increase in the number of chronic conditions was associated with an increase in OCU. It is possible that a rising number of physical diseases generates an increase in illness-related symptoms, indirectly increasing OCU [ 13 ]. Meal skipping increased OCU, which was also found in a large study among young adults [ 38 ]. Among older adults, meal skipping has been found to be associated with poor oral health [ 39 ], which may have triggered increased OCU. Increasing weight loss was found a significant predictor of OCU. It is possible that significant weight loss (&gt; 5 kg in the past year) influences the dentate status negatively [ 40 ] and having fewer teeth [ 41 ]. In a large cohort study, oral problems accounted for a large proportion of digestive causes of unintentional weight loss [ 42 ]. So dental issues may contribute to weight loss which prompts OCU, and the weight loss may simply be a secondary consequence of the underlying dental problem. A previous cross-sectional study found an association between lower loneliness [ 16 ] with OCU, while our study found the opposite, namely increasing loneliness increased the odds of OCU. Similarly, a previous longitudinal study [ 43 ] showed an association between chronic loneliness and physician visits. It is possible that people with loneliness may seek dental services as a way of coping with loneliness. Several studies [ 4 , 11 ] found that smoking decreased OCU, while in our study, transitioning to not smoking marginally significantly decreased OCU. Consistent with previous research [ 44 ], more frequent physical activity increased the odds of OCU, which may be interpreted as pursuing a healthy lifestyle that may include OCU. Study strengths and limitations The study used known measures from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) and the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), and it included a 4-wave longitudinal, national community-dwelling population. Using panel FE regression models, we reduced the problem of unobserved heterogeneity. Potential residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., access to dental clinics, oral health literacy) cannot be ruled out. The final analytic sample size for the models was low ( N = 911 individuals), which reduced the statistical power differences in the sex- and age group-stratified models. The fact that all data were evaluated using self-report is one study drawback. Perhaps we should mention that we do not distinguish between preventive and curative OCU ( e.g., pain treatment vs. professional tooth cleaning). Recall bias may have influenced the time frame of OCU in the past 12 months. However, there is a significant correlation between self-reported healthcare use and medical records data [ 45 ]. The sample excluded residents in assisted living facilities and institutions, which would have produced different OCU rates than those of the oldest-old adults who live in the community [ 46 ]. The dental status, including self-perceived status of oral health and self-perceived importance of dental care, were not assessed, and future studies should assess the perceived need and evaluate it by the DMFT index or the quantity of decaying or missing teeth. Conclusions The study found a low prevalence of OCU in middle-aged and older adults in Thailand. Findings support the importance of need factors (poor self-rated physical health, loneliness, number of chronic conditions, weight loss, meal skipping and wearing dentures), predisposing factors (decreasing age), and enabling and disabling factors (private health insurance, Civil Servants Medical Benefit Scheme, Social Security Scheme, or Other Scheme, transitioning to live alone, higher qualify of life or happiness and higher depressive symptoms) in relation to OCU. Results may assist healthcare planners in targeted measures to manage oral healthcare services in aging adults in Thailand. Health education strategies should promote the importance DCU in ageing adults. Health and welfare care strategies may increase enabling factors (private health insurance and other health insurance schemes) and decrease disabling factors (transitioning to live alone, and higher depressive symptoms).
