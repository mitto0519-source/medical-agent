Chronic stress during life has been considered a risk factor for the development of psychiatric illness in humans. Chronic unpredictable stress battery (CUSB) is an animal model to study depression through stress exposition in rodents. CUSB induces depression and anxiety behavioral markers that could be modulated with fluoxetine an antidepressant treatment. However, it is well known that not all subjects develop depression-like behaviors or do not respond to antidepressant treatments. The way to screen these individual differences in susceptibility to stress or the efficacy of antidepressant therapy in depression models is not well studied. Here we show that saccharine consumption, immobility and time spent in the center of the area could be useful as behavioral markers for screening susceptibility to stress and depression, also we show that fluoxetine treatment had different efficacy depending on the age when the stress occurred. First, we found that after CUSB (during adolescence, adulthood, or both) rodents show depression and anxiety profiles. Second, we found that fluoxetine (10 mg/kg) could recover depression but no anxiety profile in all the groups exposed to stress. Finally, we use the machine learning clustering method (k-means), to classify the subjects in all groups based on the individual effects modulated by stress exposure and antidepressant treatment to find the ratio of stress effects and pharmacological efficacy. Using altered behaviors as classifiers, we found three possible clusters, (1) Without alterations, (2) Moderated anxiety and depression profile (3) Serious anxiety and depression profile, suggesting two groups of susceptible animals with different intensities of altered behavior. Also, fluoxetine could change the ratio of rats previously classified in groups 2 or 3, showing the beneficial effects of antidepressant treatment. Our results demonstrate that CUSB has different consequences even in subjects that experienced the same stress protocol. Also, fluoxetine has different efficacy in recovering behavior associated with the age of exposure to stress. Finally, we suggest k-means as an easy and useful method to apply in susceptible rodent studies of depression and to study the individual efficacy of antidepressant treatment. Highlights • Behavioral consequences of adolescent chronic stress remain to adulthood. • Double-hit stress potentiates anxiety profile in adulthood. • Fluoxetine treatment reduces depression but has no anxiety profile. • The k-means clustering method classifies rodents exposed to CUSB into three groups of altered behavior. • Fluoxetine could change the ratio of rodents previously classified in groups found in the k-means method. This response involves different biological mechanisms that increase survival capacity ( McEwen and Akil, 2020 ). However, sustained exposure to stress (chronic stress) overwhelms the regulatory capacity of these systems and becomes a risk factor for the development of depression and anxiety ( Chaby et al., 2017 , McEwen, 2016 , Willner, 2016 ). This relationship can be explained by the fact that chronic stress has been described as affecting the HPA axis response and the circuits involved in mood regulation (specifically, the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex), in addition to causing alterations in neurogenesis and inflammatory responses (Valencia-Flores, 2023; ( Hassamal, 2023 ). On a clinical level, depression is referred to as major depressive disorder (MDD) and affects more than 300 million people globally ( Aleksandrova et al., 2019 ) . Associated symptomatology according to the DSM-V, includes depressed mood, decreased interest or pleasure (anhedonia), body weight loss, sleep disturbances, agitation or psychomotor retardation, fatigue or loss of energy, feelings of hopelessness or guilt, decreased capability to think, thoughts related to death ( American Psychiatric Association, 2013 ) and according to clinical observations the inability to actively cope with stress ( Commons et al., 2017 ). MDD symptoms are generally modulated with antidepressant treatments, one of the most used and prescribed antidepressive treatments is fluoxetine ( Wenthur et al., 2016 ). Fluoxetine sold under the brand names Prozac and Sarafem among others, is an antidepressant of the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) class ( Alboni et al., 2015 ). Fluoxetine has been validated as a powerful treatment to reduce anxiety ( Levone et al., 2021 ), depression ( Bessa et al., 2009 ), and cognitive impairments in patients with MDD (Song, Che, Min-Wei, Murakami, &amp; Matsumoto, 2006). But even with its efficacy, some patients report do not reach remission after a fluoxetine treatment ( Wang et al., 2011 ). The chronic unpredictable stress battery (CUSB) is a rodent model that induces a human-like depressive phenotype ( Sanchez-Castillo et al., 2015 , Torres-Carrillo et al., 2017 ) and is part of the most widely used models, due to its high validity and reliability ( Willner, 2016 ). However, despite being widely validated ( Bondi et al., 2008 ), several reports have shown wide intersubject behavioral variability (Oitzl, Champagne, Veen, &amp; Kloet, 2010; ( Van Camp et al., 2018 ). This behavioral variability can be explained by the fact that most individuals when experiencing intense stress, can cope with it and recover itself ( Wu et al., 2013 ). This phenomenon has been termed resilience and refers to the ability to adapt to stress and adversity while maintaining normal physical and psychological functioning ( Feder et al., 2011 , Pfau and Russo, 2015 ). However, although most subjects may recover from the stressful experience, other individuals have been referred to as susceptible, being mostly affected by stress and developing altered behavior ( Ebner and Singewald, 2017 ). One of the characteristics that has been related to increased susceptibility to stress is the age at which it is experienced ( Herrera-Pérez et al., 2008 ). Developmental periods such as adolescence have been identified as periods of increased sensitivity to stressors ( McCormick and Hodges, 2017 ) and therefore there is a greater risk of developing a disorder into adulthood ( Hollis et al., 2013 ). Also, the burden of stress we experience has been associated with increased susceptibility to the development of mood disorders, primarily anxiety and depression ( McEwen et al., 2015 ). Double hit models have been proposed to study the stress load that subjects may have throughout their lives and whether it is associated with the onset, development, or maintenance of stress disorder ( Cotella et al., 2014 ). However, in all stress studies, despite being well validated and consistently used, the response of exposed subjects shows wide variability. Because the effects of stress can vary between subjects and antidepressant treatment cannot have the expected efficacy all the time, studying group and individual alterations is important to understand the general and specific effects of stress exposure and pharmacology treatments ( Smeets, 2010 ). For the study of intrasubject stress effects and fluoxetine efficacy, data classification methods can be useful to identify how individuals respond even under similar conditions of stress exposure. In CUSB protocols the only way to identify subjects who develop behavioral alterations (susceptible) and those who do not (resilient) typically relies on a single test of anhedonia ( Prakash et al., 2020 , Cerniauskas et al., 2019 ) or social behavior after stress paradigm (Alves-dos-Santos, Resende, &amp; Chiavegatto, 2020). This method of classification by single assessment could exclude other behavioral alterations, such as impaired motor activity, altered coping behaviors, and anxiety-like behaviors also reported in rats exposed to chronic stress ( Vázquez-León et al., 2023 , Valencia-Flores, 2018 , Herrera-Pérez et al., 2016 ), that could be useful to differentiate both populations, as well as to identify possible degrees of behavioral alteration as occurs in diagnosis with humans ( Carver and Connor-Smith, 2010 ). Considering all the above, it is essential to investigate the behavioral effects of chronic stress exposure and examine how factors such as age of exposure and pharmacological treatment may modulate outcomes in exposed subjects, thereby classifying them as either susceptible or resilient. 2 Material and methods 2.1 Animal manipulation, housing, and ethics approval Animal procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the Psychology School at UNAM (FPSI/422/CEIP/449/2018). We used male Wistar rats of 21 days old at the start of the experiment (N = 120), housed in communal boxes (5 subjects per box) under standard laboratory conditions with a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle, with lights on at 8:00 am. The sample size per group was selected based on literature in the field. Access to food and water was ad libitum during the entering experiment. Each subject was randomly assigned to an experimental group. In the first experiment: control (n = 10), CUSB in adolescence (n = 10), CUSB in adulthood (n = 10), or CUSB double hit (n = 10); in the second experiment saline control (n = 10), saline + CUSB in adolescence (n = 10), saline + CUSB in adulthood (n = 10) and saline + CUSB double hit (n = 10) were compared to Fluoxetine control (n = 10), Fluoxetine + CUSB in adolescence (n = 10), Fluoxetine + CUSB in adulthood (n = 10) and Fluoxetine + CUSB double hit (n = 10). 2.2 Chronic unpredictable stress battery (CUSB) To study the effects of chronic stress, we use a previously described chronic unpredictable stress battery (CUSB) ( Torres-Carrillo et al., 2017 ). In brief, rats were exposed to six different stressors in random order for ten days (Appendices - Table A.1). Four groups were formed, for the stress experiment: (1) control – without stress exposure, (2) CUSB during adolescence (Exposure from Postnatal day, PND, 40–49), (3) CUSB during adulthood (Exposure to CUSB from PND 60–69) and (4) CUSB double hit (Exposure to CUSB in both periods PND 40–49 and PND 60–69). 2.3 Behavioral evaluation 2.3.1 Open field test (OFT) For assessing general motricity and anxiety profile, subjects were placed at the center of the arena as previously described ( Torres-Carrillo et al., 2017 ), and behavior was recorded offline using a camera for posterior evaluation. Anxiety-like behaviors included time spent in central and peripheral areas, total number of crosses, and duration of immobility and grooming were measured ( Migliaro et al., 2020 , Prut and Belzung, 2003 ). 2.3.2 Forced swim test (FST) We use this test to evaluate depression profiles through passive and active coping behaviors ( Commons et al., 2017 ). In brief, subjects were placed in a water-filled cylinder for two sessions, the first session of habituation for fifteen minutes, and the second session of evaluation for five minutes as previously described ( Yankelevitch-Yahav et al., 2015 ). Depression-like behaviors were interpreted by passive coping immobility ( De Kloet and Molendijk, 2016 ). 2.3.3 Saccharine preference test (SPT) We used this test to assess the depression profile through anhedonia induced by stress as previously described ( Antoniuk et al., 2019 , Migliaro et al., 2020 ). In brief, during the test, subjects were housed individually and were allowed access to a bottle of water (250 ml) and a bottle of water with saccharine (3 % / 250 ml). Depression-like behavior was interpreted by total consumption of saccharine ( Stepanichev et al., 2016 ). 2.4 K-means clustering method The K-means clustering method is one of the simplest and most popular unsupervised machine learning algorithms, the idea of k-means clustering is to separate multivariate data into k groups ( Cohen, 2017 ). We used this method to classify susceptible from resilient subjects using behavioral data from OFT, FST, and SPT. We selected these behaviors because they are part of the depression and anxiety profile that we seek to mimic in the animal model of CUSB. Also, we use this method to screen at the individual level the number of subjects that a fluoxetine treatment could recover after stress exposure in different lifetimes. The code is available in the appendices. 2.5 Fluoxetine treatment Fluoxetine hydrochloride was acquired from Sigma Aldrich (now Merck) and was used as a pharmacological treatment for the reduction of stress-induced behavioral disturbances. A dose of 10 mg/kg administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) for 14 days (PND 70 to PND 83 during adulthood) was used. Dosage, temporality, and route of administration were selected based on previous reports of efficacy and to ensure similar blood levels of the drug to those of patients using this antidepressant treatment ( Bessa et al., 2009 , Lu et al., 2019 , Wang et al., 2015 ). For the fluoxetine experiment, four groups were used: (1) control + fluoxetine, (2) CUSB during adolescence + fluoxetine, (3) CUSB during adulthood + Fluoxetine, and (4) CUSB double hit + fluoxetine. 2.6 Statistical analysis Matlab R2019b and GraphPad Prism 8.01 were used to prepare all the graphs and perform all the statistical analyses. For the stress experiments, OFT results were evaluated with one-way ANOVA; FST results were evaluated with two-way ANOVA (stress and behavior as factors); SPT results were evaluated with two-way ANOVA (Stress and liquid type as factors). For the fluoxetine experiments, OFT, FST, and SPT results were evaluated with two-way ANOVA using a first-factor Stress treatment and a second-factor pharmacology treatment. Post-hoc analyses for all the above tests were performed with Tukey's method. For data classification by the k-means clustering method, a code was programmed in MatLab (attached in the appendix). An alpha of 0.05 was established to indicate statistical significance. 3 Results Stress effects on behavior 3.1 Open field test We found that exposure to chronic stress at any life stage generates an anxiety-like profile in adulthood ( Fig. 1 ). One behavioral marker of anxiety in rodents is the decrease in time spent in the center of the open field ( Fig. 1 A); our data showed an effect in all groups exposed to chronic stress (F(3,36)= 19.62; p &lt; 0.0001). Tukey´s multiple comparisons tests showed differences in Control vs. CUSB in adolescence (p &lt; 0.0001), Controls vs. CUSB in adulthood (p &lt; 0.05), Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.0001), CUSB in adolescence vs. CUSB in adulthood (p &lt; 0.05) and CUSB in adulthood vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.005). To complement the evaluation, we included two other markers of the anxiety-type profile in rodents (immobility and grooming). Our data show an effect of CUSB on immobility duration ( Fig. 1 B) (F(3,36)= 6.357, p &lt; 0.01). Tukey´s multiple comparisons tests showed differences in Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.01), CUSB in adolescence vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.01), and CUSB in adulthood vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.05). Also, data show an effect of stress on grooming duration ( Fig. 1 C) (F(3,36)= 9.415, p &lt; 0.0001). Tukey´s multiple comparisons tests showed differences in Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.0001), CUSB in adolescence vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.05), and CUSB in adulthood vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.05). Finally, to evaluate whether chronic stress affects locomotor activity, the number of crossings in the field was measured. Our data show an effect of stress on the number of crosses ( Fig. 1 D) (F(3,36)= 4.921, p &lt; 0.05). Tukey´s multiple comparisons tests showed differences in Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.05) and CUSB in adulthood vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.05). These results indicate that chronic stress exposure generates an anxiety-like profile in rodents, which is intensified when chronic stress is experienced during adolescence and into adulthood. 3.2 Forced swim test Immobility behavior was measured in the forced swim test to assess the depression-like profile of rodents ( Fig. 2 ). Our data do not show a main effect of stress on time (F(3108) = 0.0008, p &gt; 0.05), but we find a main effect of behavior on time (F(2108)= 91.92, p &lt; 0.0001) and a significant interaction effect (F(6108)= 7.972, p &lt; 0.0001). Tukey´s multiple comparisons tests showed differences in Swimming CUSB in adolescence vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.05), Climbing Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.0001), and Immobility Control vs. CUSB in adolescence (p &lt; 0.01) and Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.05). These results suggest that chronic stress in adolescence, or double hit, generates the development of a marker of the depression-type profile in adulthood and that exposure to chronic stress only in adulthood does not promote this change. 3.3 Saccharin preference test Another behavioral marker of the depression-like profile in rodents is the change in saccharin consumption preference ( Fig. 3 ). Our data show a significant main effect of stress on liquid consumption (F(3,72) = 13.70, p &lt; 0.0001). Also, a significant main effect of liquid type on liquid consumption (F(1,72)= 223.2, p &lt; 0.0001) and a significant effect of interaction on liquid consumption (F(3,72)= 31.33, p &lt; 0.001). Tukey´s multiple comparisons tests showed differences in Water Consume Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.01) and Saccharine Consume Control vs. CUSB in adolescence (p &lt; 0.0001), Control vs. CUSB in adulthood (p &lt; 0.0001) and Control vs. CUSB double hit (p &lt; 0.0001). This result shows that exposure to chronic stress, regardless of the period in which stress is experienced, produces an anhedonic state in adulthood. The analysis showed statistically significant main effects of stress (F(3,70)= 13.93, p &lt; 0.0001) and pharmacological treatment (F(1,70)= 5.239, p &lt; 0.05), as well as significant interaction effects (F(3,70)= 3.767, p &lt; 0.05). This data is consistent with forced swim results because fluoxetine showed antidepressant effects when stress exposure occurred in only one life period, adolescence or adulthood, but was not shown to be effective in recovery subjects with double stress exposure (during adolescence and adulthood). 3.7 Classification of subjects considering altered behavior after stress Considering the altered behavior resulting from chronic stress, we proceeded to test whether the k-means grouping method was able to classify susceptible subjects from resilient ones. The anxiety-like behaviors (time in the center) and depression like-behaviors (saccharin consumption and immobility) were taken as data of interest for classification. Once the data were entered into the program, the treatment groups from which they came were identified ( Fig. 7 ). Subsequently, the k-means data clustering method was used. Our data show that the appropriate number of classification groups was 3, this data was calculated by the sum of variance reduced by each cluster (elbow method) (See appendices Fig. B.1). Once the appropriate number of classification groups was calculated, the grouped data showed one grouping of data in green which for the behavior shown, was labeled (1) Without alterations on green dots, (2) Moderated anxiety and depression profile on blue dots and (3) Serious anxiety and depression profile on red dots ( Fig. 8 left side). These data suggest that the k-means clustering method can achieve a visually clear classification of subjects exposed to chronic stress. Once the data were classified, the percentage of subjects in each treatment and the classification group in which the k-means algorithm placed them were calculated ( Fig. 8 right side). The data show that the green group "Without alterations" consisted of the total subjects of the control group. The blue group "Moderated anxiety and depression profile" consisted of 7 subjects from the adolescent stress group, 10 subjects from the adult stress group, and 5 subjects from the double hit group. Finally, the red group "Serious anxiety and depression profile" consisted of 3 subjects from the adolescent stress group and 5 subjects from the double hit group ( Fig. 8 right side). These data show that the effects of chronic stress are not homogeneous, even in rodents undergoing the same experimental manipulation. 3.8 Classification of subjects considering altered behavior after fluoxetine treatment When the behavioral data were obtained after treatment with fluoxetine, the k-means algorithm was applied. The same behavioral markers used for post-stress rodent classification were considered. After applying the clustering algorithm, 3 groups were identified ( Fig. 9 left side). The same that were identified in the previous results. The data show that after a fluoxetine treatment the green group "Without alterations" consisted of 20 subjects (10 from the control group; 6 from CUSB during adulthood and 4 of CUSB during adolescence). The blue group "Moderated anxiety and depression profile" consisted of 15 subjects (5 from CUSB during adolescence; 4 from CUSB during adulthood and 6 from CUSB double hit). Finally, the red group "Serious anxiety and depression profile" consisted of only 3 subjects consisted of CUSB double hit ( Fig. 9 right side). 4 Discussion and conclusion In this study, chronic stress adversely affected hedonic state, exploration, and coping in rats, with saccharin consumption, time spent in the center, and immobility serving as key indicators of susceptibility or resilience. Fluoxetine treatment reversed these stress-induced behaviors during adolescence and adulthood, except in cases of “double-hit” exposure, which remained resistant. Using k-means clustering, three behavioral subgroups were identified (unaffected, moderately altered, and severely altered) demonstrating the graded nature of stress effects. Overall, these findings highlight both the variable impact of stress depending on life stage and the limitations of fluoxetine in treating severe, prolonged stress exposure. 4.1 Chronic stress produces the development of an anxiety-type profile In this study, we report that the period of life in which chronic stress is experienced is relevant for the development of the anxiety-type profile. The data indicate that exposure to chronic stress at any stage generates an anxiety-type profile in adulthood as measured by decreasing time in the center zone of the open field, which is related to avoidance of environments that are evaluated as aversive ( Sestakova et al., 2013 ). We report that stress during adolescence causes disturbances that remain into adulthood as previously described ( Hollis et al., 2013 )a; ( Romeo, 2010 ). We also report that stress exposure during adulthood causes effects that remain even 14 days after the last stressor, the opposite effect to that previously described, where adults exposed to stress recovered after 10 days of rest ( McCormick and Hodges, 2017 ). Furthermore, it is highlighted that exposure to double hit stress (stress during adolescence and adulthood periods) increases the severity of the anxiety-like profile by increasing immobility considered a fear-related behavior ( Blanchard et al., 2001 ), grooming as a coping behavior to stressful conditions ( Kalueff et al., 2016 ) and decreased general motor skills or exploration of the environment ( Dulawa et al., 2004 ). This effect strengthens the cumulative stress hypothesis, which states that stressful events generate alterations that accumulate and that with more stressful events the effects become increasingly severe ( Willner et al., 2013 ). 4.2 Chronic stress produces helplessness and passive coping in stressful situations Clinical observations in patients with depression describe that most of these patients present high levels of anxiety and an inability to cope with highly stressful situations ( Commons et al., 2017 ). The forced swim test has been described as a test to assess the depression-like profile due to the helplessness that animals show to highly stressful conditions ( Holmes, 2003 ), the responsiveness that behaviors have to antidepressant administration ( Willner et al., 2013 ), and the assessment of the coping strategy to stress, with swimming and climbing being active strategies, while immobility is considered a passive coping strategy ( De Kloet and Molendijk, 2016 ). In this research, we report that chronic stress exposure during adolescence and the double hit group produces a depression-like profile measured by increased immobility duration assessing helplessness behavior, as well as a passive coping strategy in the face of highly stressful conditions ( Lovelock and Deak, 2019 ). The stress exposure group during adulthood does not show a significant increase, suggesting a possible post-stress recovery in the depression-like profile and not in the anxiety-like profile as observed in the open field is consistent with previous reports ( McCormick and Hodges, 2017 ), however, given that the double hit group shows an increase, this is consistent with the cumulative stress hypothesis and reports indicating that exposure to stress during adolescence increases susceptibility to the development of a disorder in adulthood ( D'Souza and Sadananda, 2019 , Hammen, 2005 ). 4.3 Chronic stress results in the development of a depression-like profile Anhedonia is the central symptom of the diagnosis of depression ( Larsen et al., 2010 ). Anhedonia is defined as the decreased experience of pleasure from stimuli that normally produce it ( Stanton et al., 2018 ). In this study we report that preference for saccharin consumption was present in all groups, as all groups consumed more saccharin than water, however, groups exposed to chronic stress developed an anhedonic state because saccharin consumption decreased compared to the group without stress exposure. The decrease in saccharin consumption has been previously described in other studies and is considered a behavioral alteration within the depression-like profile ( Harkin et al., 2002 , Migliaro et al., 2020 ). This data is consistent with those of the forced swim test, where the groups exposed to stress show alterations in the depression-like profile in adulthood. Finally, the anhedonic effect resulting from stress has been previously described and is associated with alterations in the neural circuits of the reward pathway, particularly disruptions in the mesolimbic dopaminergic system and nucleus accumbens ( Pizzagalli, 2014 ), as well as changes in neuroplasticity and neuroinflammatory processes ( Boyle et al., 2023 ). 4.4 Chronic fluoxetine has anxiogenic effects on non-stressed group Fluoxetine is a widely and well-known drug successfully used in treating several diseases. Its unique combination of efficacy and its safety profile explains its key role in the history of the pharmacotherapy of several diseases ( Rossi et al., 2004 ). In this experiment, fluoxetine showed not to be efficient in the recovery of anxiety-like behavior in any of the groups, data that go against several studies that report chronic fluoxetine treatments with anxiolytic properties ( Farhan and Haleem, 2016 , Papp et al., 2016 ), on the contrary, in the non-stress group, chronic treatment with fluoxetine showed to have anxiogenic effects, this result has been previously described in the literature as a possible side effect after treatment with fluoxetine ( File et al., 1999 , Gray and Hughes, 2015 ), and could be explained that after chronic treatment could promote the desensitization of 5-HT1A autoreceptors and the consequent reduction in their inhibitory effects on raphe activity ( Silva et al., 1999 ). Other authors, such as Richardson-Jones et al. ( Richardson-Jones et al., 2010 ), report that the response to stress stimuli increases when there is a low response or expression of 5-HT1A autoreceptors (in subjects without stress exposure), suggesting that the anxiety we reported could be due to heightened reactivity to acute stressors such as open field area. On the other hand, authors such as Gross et al. ( Gross et al., 2002 ) report that 5-HT1A receptor agonists promote anxiolytic behaviors when they stimulate hippocampal or prefrontal cortical regions, but not when they act at the level of the raphe nuclei. Although the mechanisms by which various antidepressants exert their therapeutic and side effects remain unclear, these reports point to a distinction between the regulation of presynaptic and postsynaptic 5-HT1A receptors, as well as the brain regions in which they are expressed, as possible modulators. Further research is needed to clarify this aspect. 4.5 Chronic fluoxetine has antidepressant effects on behavior after chronic stress during adolescence or adulthood, but not in double-hit conditions It has been reported that chronic, but not acute, administration of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, such as fluoxetine, induces antidepressant effects in the FST ( Zolfaghari et al., 2021 ) and in saccharine preference test ( Lu et al., 2017 ). Chronic treatment with fluoxetine was shown to have antidepressant properties only in the single stress exposure groups, consistent with the literature reporting reduction of the depression-like profile during adolescence ( Klomp et al., 2014 ) or adulthood ( Song et al., 2019 ). These effects may be associated with increased serotonergic signaling and desensitization of postsynaptic 5HT1A receptors of serotonergic neurons ( Stahl, 1998 ). However, it showed to be ineffective in the reduction of depressive-like behavior in the double hit group, with no changes in immobility time in FST or consumption of saccharine in SPT, this could be because the longer exposure time to glucocorticoids due to the double stress exposure causes the basal amount of serotonin available to be strongly decreased, as well as the increased reuptake effectiveness as showed in reports with non-response rodents to fluoxetine ( Sun et al., 2021 ). Another possible explanation for the lack of effect of fluoxetine in the double-hit group could be due to the dosage and time of administration, since several studies have shown that even at higher doses of fluoxetine (20 mg/kg) for 21 days antidepressant effects can be found in more severe stress conditions (exposure periods of up to 56 days) ( Alboni et al., 2017 , Zlatković et al., 2014 ), however, more research needs to be done to clarify this aspect. 4.6 Chronic stress at different stages of life increases susceptibility to the development of differentially altered behavior within subjects In this research, we tested a paradigm called k-means, coming from computer learning techniques for data classification ( Wallisch et al., 2014 ). It was used in this research to classify subjects into subgroups by behavioral characteristics. The results showed the classification of 3 groups: (1) Green - No behavioral alterations; (2) Blue - Moderate anxiety and depression-type profile; (3) Serious anxiety and depression-type profile. When identifying the treatments of each subject belonging to the 3 classification groups, it was observed that group (1) No behavioral alterations had been formed by the 10 subjects that made up the control group, this behavior without alterations was expected, since this group was not exposed to any stressful condition. Group (2) Moderate anxiety and depression-type profile was formed by the 10 subjects who were part of the stress group in adulthood, 7 subjects of the stress exposure group during adolescence, and 5 subjects of the stress group in both periods (adolescence and adulthood). Finally, group (3) Serious anxiety and depression-type profile consisted of 3 subjects from the exposure group during adolescence and 5 from the stress group in both periods (adolescence and adulthood). These data demonstrate that not all subjects within the same group respond homogeneously to stress exposure, an aspect that has been previously reported ( Oitzl et al., 2010 ), but has been overlooked in understanding the effects of stress. Also, the results show that the stage of life in which chronic stress is experienced is a relevant factor for the development of behavioral disturbances in adulthood ( Breton et al., 2021 , Romeo, 2010 ). Within this reasoning, we can observe that exposure to stress during adolescence promotes alterations that last into adulthood, these results coincide with previously reported data ( Hollis et al., 2013 ); Spear, 2000). When chronic stress is experienced during adolescence according to our data 7 of the 10 subjects could develop a moderate anxiety and depressive profile in adulthood, while 3 of the 10 subjects could develop a severe depressive profile. These data contribute knowledge to the investigation of susceptibility to stress when going through important stages of maturation of the organism, with adolescents being especially susceptible ( Hollis et al., 2013 ). When chronic stress is experienced in adulthood, behavioral alterations appear but develop as a moderate anxiety and depressive profile, which is consistent with the literature mentioning that adults are less susceptible to the effects of stress than adolescents ( McCormick and Hodges, 2017 ). Surprisingly this stress exposure group behaved homogeneously, it is possible that by being less affected by stress they were able to recover more easily than adolescents and not reach the serious anxiety and depression profile cluster. When stress is experienced in both periods (adolescence and adulthood) an interesting phenomenon occurs, starting from the data of the exposure group in adolescence where 3 of the 10 subjects presented severe alterations, with a second exposure to chronic stress in adulthood, this number increases to 5 of the 10, so that these data suggest that the behavioral alterations produced by exposure to stress behave cumulatively as indicated by the cumulative stress hypothesis ( Willner et al., 2013 ). Second, double exposure to stress does not improve the coping of subjects exposed during early stages as suggested by the stress mismatch hypothesis ( Nederhof and Schmidt, 2012 ), therefore according to our results, resilience is not favored by learning from previously experienced stressful events. Surprisingly, in our research no subjects were found to be resilient to the effects of stress; however, this effect could be explained by the characteristics of the CUSB model, given that since stressors are unpredictable and uncontrollable, it is not possible to cope with the stressful situation and reduce the impact it may have on the health of the organism. 4.7 Fluoxetine treatment differentially reduces the number of subjects with anxiety and depressive profiles at the individual level The data present in this research consistently showed that exposure to stress as well as treatment with fluoxetine have non-homogeneous effects at the group level. Therefore, it is important to study the individual responses of the subjects to know the degrees of efficacy of the antidepressant treatments in more detail. When chronic stress is experienced during adolescence and a chronic fluoxetine treatment is provided in adulthood according to our data 4 subjects showed remission of the depression-like behaviors and the other 5 remained classified within the group of moderate anxiety and depression profile. In contrast to the adolescent stress classification only, the rate of subjects showing moderate impairment was reduced and the rate of subjects showing severe impairment became null, these data demonstrate that antidepressant treatment with fluoxetine can reverse the effects of stress in a hierarchical manner considering k-means classification, i.e., subjects with moderate impairment recover and subjects with severe impairment move to moderate impairment. The fact that most of the group remains even within the moderate impairment criteria may be due to the sensitivity of adolescents to stress ( Roberts and Lopez-Duran, 2019 ) and that the behavioral impairments have been reported to be strong and long-lasting into adulthood ( Chaby, Cavigelli, Hirrlinger, Caruso, &amp; Braithwaite, 2015 ). When chronic stress is presented during adulthood and fluoxetine treatment is provided during the same period of life, according to our data, 6 subjects could recover reaching the classification without alterations, while 4 subjects remain within the moderate anxiety and depression classification. In contrast to the classification of stress during adulthood alone, treatment with fluoxetine is shown to be able to increase the rate of subjects who recover and in comparison to stress exposure during adolescence, there is a greater number of subjects with beneficial effects, possibly due to the stronger impact of stress during this period of development compared to adulthood ( Klein and Romeo, 2013 ), where the physiological stress response systems have finished maturing and their modulation is more effective ( Walker and McCormick, 2009 ). These data demonstrate that fluoxetine is more effective in its antidepressant effect when subjects experience stress in adulthood. Finally, when stress occurs in two periods of life (double hit model), and antidepressant treatment is provided during adulthood, according to our data, six subjects remained in the group of moderate anxiety and depression, while the other three were classified in the group of serious disturbances. In contrast to the double hit stress classification alone, the number of subjects exhibiting serious anxiety and depression decreased, and the number of subjects exhibiting moderate anxiety and depression increased. However, unlike the other groups, no subjects who were exposed to the double hit model were classified into the group without alterations. These data support the idea that fluoxetine shows beneficial effects in a hierarchical manner considering the k-means classification. Furthermore, the data suggest that double stress exposure shows more severe impairments that possibly confer resistance to antidepressant treatment. Taking into account the data shown in this research, it can be concluded that the k-means algorithm can be useful in the behavioral classification of stress-induced depression models. On the other hand, its use can also be useful for the evaluation of the individual effects of stress exposure and the effectiveness of antidepressant treatments. Finally, the study of individual effects promotes the extrapolation of the data to a translational context of studying the effects of stress and pharmacological efficacy in patients with depression. 5 Limitations The study has some limitations. First, in this study only male rats were used; therefore, expanding the research to include females is essential to broaden our understanding of the individual effects of stress, antidepressants, and susceptibility and resilience. Second, longitudinal studies are required to investigate how stress affects behavior during life and behavioral outcomes could change with time during and after the Flx treatment. Third, we only use a single regimen of fluoxetine administration to test. Therefore, other effects could be found under other dosage and administration regimens. Last but not least, the causal physiological relationship between the effects of stress, behavior, and fluoxetine presented here needs to be studied to achieve a better understanding of the phenomenon at the neurobiological level.
