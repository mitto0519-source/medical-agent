Abstract Background Due to the lack of comprehensive longitudinal studies examining the impact of long-term volatility on health issues, existing research primarily focused on income levels rather than fluctuation. Thus, we aimed to investigate the relationship between income instability and a range of physical health conditions using an extensive longitudinal dataset. Methods This study utilized data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) Ansan-Anseong cohort (2001–2022), which includes valuable confounding factors such as demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Income volatility was calculated as the intra-individual standard deviation of income changes across study waves. A Cox proportional hazards model was employed to assess correlations between income volatility and the risk of 12 non-communicable diseases (NCDs; hypertension, myocardial infarction [MI], coronary artery disease [CAD], cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, gout, gastric disease, renal disease, arthritis, allergic disease, and asthma). Results A total of 9,185 participants (mean age, 52.21; male, 47.59%) were analyzed. In the fully adjusted model, medium volatility was correlated with a higher disease risk (hazard ratio, 1.14; 95% confidence interval, 1.07–1.21), as similar to high volatility (1.09; 1.03–1.16). The highest risk was observed in individuals experiencing income decreases (1.61; 1.13–2.28). All health conditions examined in this study showed an increased risk correlated with higher income volatility as follows: hypertension (1.13; 1.03–1.25); MI (2.15; 1.33–3.48); CAD (1.37; 1.04–3.48); cerebrovascular disease (1.69; 1.21–2.37); hyperlipidemia (1.14; 1.01–1.28); gout (2.11; 1.12–3.95); gastric disease (1.15; 1.01–1.32); renal disease (2.28; 1.22–4.25); arthritis (1.33; 1.04–1.70); allergic disease (1.54; 1.16–2.03); asthma (1.67; 1.04–1.67). Conclusion Income volatility, particularly income decreases and fluctuations, is significantly correlated with higher risk of chronic NCDs in South Korea. These findings underscore the need to develop interventions to mitigate financial instability, thereby improving public health outcomes. INTRODUCTION The correlation between socioeconomic status and health outcomes has been a central focus of public health and epidemiology research. 1 As income instability, in particular, has a bidirectional effect on health, understanding this relationship is vital for developing targeted public health interventions and policies. 2 Middle-aged adults are especially vulnerable to the health impacts of income volatility due to prolonged workforce exposure and economic pressures. 3 This age group often experiences income changes from job transitions, economic shifts, or retirement. At this aspect, previous studies focused on these age groups to discover the association between low socioeconomic status and adverse health outcomes. 4 Additionally, health conditions also influence income status through various factors. Chronic illnesses or disabilities can limit individuals’ ability to work, reduce productivity, and increase healthcare expenses, all of which contribute to financial strain. Moreover, frequent medical visits or prolonged recovery periods may disrupt stable employment, further exacerbating income insecurity. 5 This bidirectional relationship highlights the importance of addressing health disparities to mitigate their economic impact on vulnerable populations. However, many prior studies have focused on specific diseases or relied on cross-sectional data, limiting their ability to capture the dynamic nature of income changes over time. 2 , 6 , 7 To address this gap, we examined the long-term effects of income volatility on various health outcomes, using data from the Ansan-Anseong cohort (2001–2022). Residents of these key industrial regions in South Korea are particularly vulnerable to income instability, making them an ideal population for studying these correlations. METHODS Study design and population This study utilized a cohort design, analyzing 18 years of longitudinal data from the ongoing Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES). 8 , 9 A total of 10,030 South Korean participants, aged 40 to 69 years and residing in the cities of Ansan and Anseong, were enrolled in the baseline survey conducted between June 2001 and January 2003. Of the initial 10,030 participants, 9,185 were included after applying the following exclusion criteria: 1) incomplete essential questionnaires, 2) participation in fewer than two follow-ups, 3) missing baseline data, and 4) presence of pre-existing underlying diseases. Covariates The covariates were collected at the time of the baseline survey. Specific covariates used for adjustment in this study were sociodemographic variables (age, sex, occupation, education level, and residential years), health behaviors (smoking status, alcohol consumption status, regular exercise status, and body mass index [BMI]), and biomarkers (total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein [HDL], serum creatinine, systolic blood pressure [SBP], and diastolic blood pressure [DBP]). In previous studies, these covariates are well-established and have known associations with various disease conditions. 7 Sociodemographic information was collected in the Ansan-Anseong study using a self-administered questionnaire. Occupation was divided into three groups: homemaker, manual (working for agriculture and factory), and non-manual (self-employment, office workers, sales workers, and professionals). Residential year refers to the total time each participant has lived in either Ansan or Anseong. Smoking status was classified into never smokers and at least once smokers. Alcohol consumption status was ascertained by asking participants whether they had ever consumed alcoholic beverages in their lifetime and classified into never drunken and at least once drunken BMI categories were classified according to the Asian–Pacific guidelines into four groups: underweight (&lt; 18.5 kg/m 2 ), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m 2 ), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m 2 ), and obese (≥ 25.0 kg/m 2 ). 10 Biomarker data such as total cholesterol, HDL, and serum creatinine were collected from the participants’ serum. SBP and DBP were measured with the mean value of left- and right-hand blood pressure while sitting. For precise analysis, continuous biomarker data were categorized using the following cut-off values: total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL; HDL ≥ 40 mg/dL (for males) and ≥ 50 mg/dL (for females); serum creatinine ≥ 1.3 mg/dL (for males) and ≥ 1.1 mg/dL (for females); SBP ≥ 140 mmHg; and DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. 11 , 12 , 13 Primary economic predictors Participants reported their monthly household income within the following categories: less than 500,000 KRW; 500,000 to 999,999 KRW; 1,000,000 to 1,499,999 KRW; 1,500,000 to 1,999,999 KRW; 2,000,000 to 2,999,999 KRW; 3,000,000 to 3,999,999 KRW; 4,000,000 to 5,999,999 KRW; and 6,000,000 KRW or more. Income volatility was defined as the intra-individual standard deviation (SD) of the percent change in income. Specifically, for each participant, we calculated the percent change in inflation-adjusted income between every two consecutive examination waves using the formula (Y t2 − Y t1 )/0.5(Y t1 + Y t2 ) × 100, where Y represents household income, t1 is time for the previous wave, and t2 is time for the subsequent wave. 3 , 14 If a participant was missing an income measurement for a particular examination wave, the income measurement from the next available wave was used. For each participant, we then calculated the SD of these percent changes. Income volatility, expressed in percentage points, was categorized into tertiles: low volatility (SD of 0% to 21% change), medium volatility (SD of 22% to 51% change), and high volatility (SD of 52% to 242% change). 14 Secondary economic predictors To more precisely capture the direction of income volatility (whether positive, negative, or mixed), we developed an income trajectory measure categorizing participants into four distinct groups based on income changes: 1) stable income with no changes, 2) income increased at least once with no decreases, 3) income decreased at least once with no increases, and 4) fluctuating income with both increases and decreases. 3 Subsequently, we merged groups 1 and 4 (no income change and fluctuating income) into a single category, representing non-directional income changes, as opposed to the clear directional shifts observed in groups 2 and 3. 14 Definition of health conditions All outcomes were assessed using the question, “Have you been diagnosed with any of the following diseases by a physician at a hospital or clinic during the past two years (since the last follow-up survey)?” This approach ensured that only new-onset diseases were considered in the analysis. The primary outcome was defined as the occurrence of cardiovascular diseases, endocrine and metabolic diseases, gastrointestinal diseases, genitourinary diseases, musculoskeletal diseases, and immunological and allergic diseases. Secondary outcomes included the following specific conditions: 1) hypertension, 2) myocardial infarction (MI), 3) coronary artery disease (CAD), 4) cerebrovascular disease, 5) diabetes, 6) hyperlipidemia, 7) gout, 8) gastric disease, 9) renal disease, 10) arthritis, 11) allergic disease, and 12) asthma. For clarity, CAD encompassed angina; cerebrovascular diseases included stroke, infarction, and hemorrhage; gastric diseases included gastritis and gastric ulcer; renal diseases included renal failure, renal stones, renal cysts, kidney transplantation, and IgA nephropathy; arthritis included both osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis; and allergic diseases included allergic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis. Statistical analysis General characteristics were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical data and as mean values with SDs for continuous data. For the primary analysis, we employed Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate the risk of various diseases with income volatility. 15 The analysis was adjusted for potential covariates using four multivariable models. Model 1 is unadjusted, while Model 2 is adjusted for sociodemographic factors such as sex, continuous age, occupation, education level, and years of residence. Model 3 included all covariates from Model 2 and adjusted for health behaviors. Model 4 was further adjusted for biomarkers, including total cholesterol, HDL, SBP, DBP, and serum creatinine, in addition to the variables in Model 3. To explore potential variations in the correlation between income volatility and disease incidence, we conducted subgroup analyses based on key demographic and socioeconomic factors: sex, education level, and occupation. These factors were selected due to their recognized importance in health disparities research and their potential interaction with income dynamics. For each subgroup analysis, we applied a stratified Cox proportional hazards model, which allows for different baseline hazards across strata while assuming consistent covariate effects. 16 , 17 This approach enabled us to examine whether the impact of income volatility on disease incidence differs across various subpopulations. Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided P value of less than 0.05 and Bonferroni correction for 12 non-communicable diseases (NCDs) was applied for multiple testing. All analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Ethics statement This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University (KHSIRB-23-085(EA)), and The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency and followed the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All study participants gave their written informed consent. RESULTS A total of 9,185 patients with a mean age ± SD of 52.21 ± 8.92 years were analyzed, among whom 4,371 (47.59%) were male patients. The general characteristics of the participants and explanations are described in Table 1 and Fig. 1 . Participants were categorized into three income volatility groups: low, medium, and high. Among them, 28.85% had low volatility, 26.98% had medium volatility, and 44.17% had high volatility. The correlation between income volatility and disease incidence was analyzed using a HR adjusted for potential covariates. As shown in Supplementary Table 1 , participants with medium- and high-income volatility were discovered to have a significantly higher disease correlation than those with low-income volatility. Specifically, in the fully adjusted model, the HRs of medium and high volatility were 1.14 (95% CI, 1.07–1.21) and 1.09 (95% CI, 1.03–1.16), respectively. Further analysis of income trajectory, as detailed in Fig. 2 and Table 2 , revealed that participants with fluctuating income had higher disease incidence across all volatility groups. Notably, individuals in the high volatility group who experienced income decreases had the highest risk (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.13–2.28). The analysis of risk factors for disease incidence by income trajectory highlights significant variations across sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and biomarkers ( Table 3 ). For instance, females who experienced an increased income had a higher risk of disease than males (HR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.19–2.88). Occupational status also interacts with income trajectories to affect health risks. Homemakers who saw their income increase or decrease showed a higher correlation with disease risk with an HR of 1.81 (95% CI, 1.28–2.57) and 1.65 (95% CI, 1.07–2.54), respectively. Lifestyle factors and biomarkers further delineate these risks. For current smokers, individuals with income increase and fluctuation are correlated with significantly higher disease risk (increase: HR, 2.28; 95% CI, 1.56–3.34; fluctuation: HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.17). Interestingly, elevated HDL levels were linked to a lower disease risk across all income trajectories, particularly with income fluctuations (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.83–0.90). The correlation between income volatility and several disease risks is shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2 , providing a comprehensive view across various conditions. Cardiovascular diseases show a distinct pattern of increasing HRs with higher income volatility. For instance, individuals in the high volatility group have a significantly increased correlation with MI (HR, 2.15; 95% CI, 1.33–3.48) and CAD (HR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.04–1.82) compared to those with lower income volatility, while only MI and cerebrovascular disease were significant after Bonferroni correction. Similarly, the correlation for allergic disease and asthma also increases notably in the high volatility group, having HR with 1.54 (95% CI, 1.16–2.03) and 1.67 (95% CI, 1.04–2.67), respectively. Notably, in high-income volatility, individuals with renal disease showed the highest HR (2.28; 95% CI, 1.22–4.25). Overall, these findings underline the importance of economic stability in mitigating health risks across multiple disease categories. In the context of different patterns of income volatility, Supplementary Table 3 illustrates specific disease risks correlated with the direction of income changes for hypertension, with a fully adjusted model, individuals with decreasing and fluctuating income showed a significantly higher risk, with an HR of 1.51 (95% CI, 1.21–1.90) and 1.49 (95% CI, 1.28–1.74). Our result indicates that females experience a higher correlation between income instability and disease risk compared to male, particularly in the increased income trajectory group (HR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.19–2.88) ( Table 3 ). Additionally, within the same income trajectory group, homemakers showed a higher correlation with disease risk compared to those who are employed (HR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.28–2.57). Furthermore, subgroup analyses indicated that only the high-decreasing income group among males showed a significant correlation with NCD risk in cases of unidirectional income change (HR, 2.28; 95% CI, 1.27–4.09) ( Supplementary Table 4 ). DISCUSSION Based on a large longitudinal cohort from an industrial region, this prospective study revealed several significant findings. First, individuals with unstable socioeconomic status were found to have a higher likelihood of developing cardiovascular, metabolic, and musculoskeletal diseases. Notably, individuals in the medium- and high-volatility income groups demonstrated a stronger correlation with NCD risk compared to those in the low-volatility group. Furthermore, among those with unstable incomes, participants who experienced income declines or fluctuations were significantly more likely to develop NCDs than those with increasing incomes. Lastly, our findings revealed that current smoking, homemaker status, and elevated DBP showed higher probability of NCDs. These results emphasize the need to consider income patterns over time rather than focusing solely on static income levels when evaluating socioeconomic health factors. A key mechanism linking income instability to adverse health outcomes is the effect of financial stress on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Financial stress triggers activation of the HPA axis, resulting in cortisol release, which increases the risk of hypertension, insulin resistance, and visceral fat accumulation. 18 , 19 This persistent stress response also stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, leading to elevated inflammatory markers and a pro-inflammatory state, which are strongly linked to the onset of chronic diseases. 20 , 21 Additionally, financial stress may contribute to unhealthy behaviors such as smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, and poor dietary choices, further compounding the risk of disease. 22 , 23 Furthermore, individuals with fluctuating or declining incomes are more likely to face interruptions in health insurance coverage and financial barriers to healthcare, leading to limited access to essential medications, and poor management of chronic conditions like diabetes and hypertension. 24 Moreover, Social determinants of health further influence the impact of income instability, as these individuals often experience greater social isolation, reduced support networks, and increased exposure to unhealthy environments. 25 These combined factors contribute to the higher prevalence of chronic diseases observed in individuals with unstable income patterns. 26 However, in the opposite way, current health conditions themselves can also influence to the economic instability. Chronic illnesses or severe health conditions often limit an individual’s ability to maintain stable employment due to frequent medical appointments, reduced physical capacity, or prolonged recovery periods. 27 Additionally, the high cost of medical care, including treatments, medications, and hospitalizations, can impose significant financial strain on individuals and families, further destabilizing their economic situation. 28 Beyond direct medical expenses, poor health may also diminish opportunities for career advancement, as individuals with ongoing health challenges may be less likely to take on additional responsibilities or invest in skill development. 29 These combined effects create a vicious cycle in which declining health perpetuates financial instability, which in turn exacerbates stress and limits access to necessary healthcare resources. This bidirectional relationship underscores the complex interplay between health and economic stability, emphasizing the need for policies that address both aspects. Given the strong correlation between income instability and NCD risk, healthcare providers should screen individuals with unstable incomes for early signs of chronic conditions. Regular monitoring and timely interventions could mitigate the disease progression, particularly among vulnerable populations. 30 Furthermore, integrating socioeconomic factors into clinical risk assessments may enhance personalized prevention strategies. From a policy perspective, reducing income volatility through stronger social safety nets, such as unemployment benefits and housing subsidies, can alleviate the health impacts of economic instability. 31 Stable income support programs have been shown to improve long-term health by reducing financial stress. 32 In addition, policies promoting financial literacy and access to affordable healthcare can further empower individuals to manage health risks during economic hardship. Public health efforts should address broader social determinants of health, including education, employment, and neighborhood environments. 33 Collaborative strategies between healthcare systems and policymakers are essential for reducing the burden of chronic diseases linked to economic instability. 34 By prioritizing economic policies that promote stability, governments can improve population health and reduce healthcare costs associated with managing chronic diseases. Chronic health conditions can drive income instability by reducing work capacity and increasing medical costs. Clinically, healthcare providers should address the economic impact of chronic diseases through early interventions, rehabilitation, and vocational support. 35 Policies expanding disability benefits, subsidizing healthcare, and promoting workplace accommodations can alleviate financial strain. Additionally, improving access to affordable healthcare and social support systems can prevent income disruptions. 36 Integrated approaches combining clinical care and policy interventions are essential to enhance financial stability for individuals with chronic illnesses. Finally, our study highlights unique stressors women face, especially homemakers, related to income changes, caregiving responsibilities, and economic uncertainty. These challenges may arise from greater caregiving responsibilities and heightened sensitivity to economic uncertainty due to traditional roles in South Korea. 37 , 38 These findings emphasize the need for sex-sensitive public health policies, promoting women’s workforce participation, and addressing homemakers’ health risks, while encouraging further research on income, sex, and occupation dynamics. This study has some limitations. First, there may still be residual confounding factors, such as unmeasured psychological stress or informal social support, that could influence the association between income volatility and health outcomes. Second, due to the nature of observational study, income data may be subject to recall bias or non-reporting errors, potentially affecting the accuracy of the income volatility measurements. Third, factors like health behaviors, used as confounders in this study, might also be seen as mediators. However, treating them as confounders allows us to isolate the independent effect of income instability on health outcomes. Lastly, our study design limits the ability to completely reflect direct causality between income instability and disease risks due to the averaging of income predictors across time, which may lead some results to reflect reverse causation—where disease onset influences subsequent income instability. However, leveraging 18 years of longitudinal data enables us to capture a unique view of how economic fluctuations and health risks interact over time. Additionally, our detailed modeling of income volatility, including the direction of income changes, enhances our understanding of the subtle ways income instability affects health, allowing us to comprehensively explore its cumulative impact. Despite these limitations, this study provides critical insights that can inform national health policies and broader global discussions on the intersections between economic security and health outcomes. This study highlights the significant correlation between income volatility and NCD risks among middle-aged adults in South Korea. Decreasing and fluctuating income levels are strongly correlated with increased risks for diverse health conditions. These findings highlight economic stability as a key health determinant and the need for targeted policies to address financial insecurity. Further research should investigate these relationships across diverse populations and explore strategies to mitigate the health impacts of economic fluctuations.
