To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Abstract Medically unexplained oral symptoms (MUOS) are frequently associated with psychosocial factors. Although most affected individuals can maintain their usual daily activities, a small subset experience pronounced socioeconomic hardship, and the prevalence and characteristics remain unclear in working-age Japanese public assistance recipients. This retrospective chart review examined 3685 first-visit patients at a tertiary psychosomatic dentistry clinic between April 2016 and March 2023. Of these, 98 (2.7%) received public assistance, and 55 (1.5% of the total sample) were aged 18–64 years. Demographic, clinical, and social determinants of health—including psychiatric diagnoses, living arrangement, and employment status—were analyzed. Results showed that 90.9% of the 55 participants had current or past psychiatric diagnoses, most commonly depression, anxiety, schizophrenia-spectrum, or bipolar disorders; 74.5% experienced polypharmacy. Unemployment and living alone were prevalent, underscoring persistent social isolation and limited support networks. These findings highlight the importance of integrating psychosomatic care with psychiatric support and broader social resources—such as community-based NPO services, and informal family or peer support—to address the complex needs of this underserved population. By situating MUOS within a broader context of social determinants of health, this study offers insights for more holistic, multidisciplinary interventions. Introduction Medically unexplained oral symptoms (MUOS), encompassing conditions like burning mouth syndrome (BMS), persistent idiopathic facial pain (PIFP), oral cenesthopathy (OC), and phantom bite syndrome (PBS), are characterized by chronic oral discomfort without clear pathological causes, presenting significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges 1 . These conditions often involve a complex interplay of neurological, physiological, and psychosocial factors, necessitating a holistic, multidisciplinary management approach that addresses the mind-body connection rather than relying solely on mechanical or surgical interventions 1 . BMS is a prevalent MUOS, presents as chronic oral burning with xerostomia and dysgeusia despite normal mucosa 2 . It occurs predominantly in postmenopausal women and affects approximately 1.7% of the general population 3 , 4 . PIFP including atypical odontalgia (AO), causes persistent tooth pain that arises spontaneously or persists after routine dental care despite normal exams and imaging 5 . PIFP has an estimated lifetime prevalence of 0.03% 6 . OC manifests as persistent, unusual intra-oral sensations—sliminess, excess mucus, or a “coils and wires” feeling—despite no detectable pathology 7 . It often follows dental work and is never improved by standard dental care. Although population data are lacking, Japanese single-center audits detect OC in 0.2–1.9% of psychiatry patients and 27% of psychosomatic dentistry referrals 8 . PBS is a persistent sense of malocclusion without objective evidence, often worsening after repeated occlusal adjustments and increasing patient distress 9 . Its prevalence is unknown, but it accounted for &lt; 10% of outpatients in our earlier survey 10 , 11 . MUOS is highly comorbid with depression and anxiety 7 , 11 , 12 . Effective MUOS care hinges on recognizing its psychosocial drivers, tailoring pharmacologic and psychosomatic interventions to each patient’s profile, and sustaining a supportive therapeutic alliance 2 , 7 , 9 , 11 . Because MUOS is deeply linked to socioeconomic context, examining Japan’s public-assistance recipients clarifies how key social determinants of health (SDOH)—education, employment, and community ties—shape disease outcomes 13 , 14 . In BMS, low educational attainment, unemployment and marital status correlate with higher pain intensity and symptom triggers, while job security appears protective 15 . Limited schooling heightens pain in women, while steady employment lessens it in men 16 . An SDOH lens suggests that economic precarity, limited education, and weak social ties are linked to neuro-sensory dysregulation in MUOS 17 – 19 . Japan’s public assistance program, Seikatsu Hogo, supplies cash stipends, full health coverage, housing aid, and educational support to people with limited means 20 . It covers basic needs, waives healthcare fees to curb poverty-linked health gaps, and encourages self-reliance 21 , 22 . Although only about 1.6% of Japan’s population received public assistance in fiscal year 2021, the program crystallizes for researchers, policymakers, and clinicians the public-spending trade-off—equity gains versus long-term fiscal strain—while serving as a testbed for future safety-net reforms 20 , 23 . Patients with MUOS, particularly those receiving public assistance, often encounter dismissive attitudes in clinical settings, and these individuals remain understudied 21 . This Japanese cohort study seeks to fill this knowledge gap and guide more effective interdisciplinary care worldwide. Accordingly, the primary research question asks: What demographic, clinical, psychiatric, and social features characterize working-age Japanese patients with MUOS who receive public assistance and attend a tertiary psychosomatic dentistry clinic? Two secondary questions follow: (1) What proportion of these patients has a schizophrenia-spectrum or bipolar disorder? and (2) How common is polypharmacy? Clarifying these points will illuminate the group’s complexity and help design targeted, resource-efficient multidisciplinary care. Methods Overall study design We conducted a retrospective chart review of consecutive patients who first attended the Psychosomatic Dentistry Clinic at Tokyo Medical and Dental University Hospital between 1 April 2016 and 31 March 2024. We used a convergent mixed-methods design that paired a quantitative retrospective chart review (QUAN) with qualitative content analysis (QUAL) of narrative data from routine semi-structured first-visit interviews; both strands pursued the same research questions and were merged at interpretation 24 – 26 . Each 2 h intake interview was conducted in person by the patient’s rotating dentist (one of five) together with a certified psychosomatic-dentistry consultant (AT, &gt; 35 years’ MUOS experience). After individual QUAN and QUAL analyses, we built a joint display pairing each theme with its matching metric (e.g., “living alone” vs. an 81.8% single-household rate). The convergences and gaps gleaned there guided the Discussion. Study participants Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–64 years at first visit; (2) receipt of public assistance at that time; (3) primary complaint consistent with MUOS; and (4) completion of the department’s standard semi-structured intake interview. Exclusion criteria were: (1) confirmed organic oral pathology explaining the symptoms (e.g., candidiasis, Sjögren syndrome, oral lichen planus); (2) cognitive impairment that precluded meaningful interview; or (3) withdrawal of consent for research use of the record. Of the 3,721 eligible first-visit out-patients invited to join the study, 3,685 consented and 30 (0.8%) declined to participate and 6 were excluded for incomplete data or other reasons. Within the 36 excluded cases, none were working-age recipients of public assistance. Among 3,685 patients, 98 (2.7%) were receiving public assistance. Of these, 55 (56.1%) aged 18 to 64 years, accounting for 1.5% of the total patient population, met the inclusion criteria. Setting and referral pathway Embedded in a university hospital, our clinic accepts patients solely by referral—mainly from community dentists, psychiatrists, or other providers—and neither advertises nor accepts self-referrals. Every consecutive new patient was prospectively screened for eligibility immediately after the first visit. In addition to the pathway described above, our psychosomatic dentistry clinic operates a joint referral program with Orthodontics. Orthodontic care—including orthognathic surgery—is lengthy, invasive, and stressful, making outcomes hard to predict for patients with mental disorders. Because orthodontists rarely have psychiatric training and psychiatrists seldom have dental expertise, our department bridges the gap. We brief patients on possible risks—such as a flare-up of psychiatric symptoms—and propose practical ways to manage them. Study variables Demographic variables (age, sex), physical and psychiatric history—classified according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)—living status, occupation, education, and current medications were extracted. Because many participants had psychiatric comorbidity, polypharmacy was operationally defined, in line with psychiatric convention, as the concurrent use of three or more agents, confirmed through referral letters and patient interviews 27 , 28 . Social context and stressors were recorded during our routine semi-structured intake, preferred over fully structured formats to preserve clinician flexibility. Standard self-report instruments, including the self-rating depression scale (SDS) and the pain catastrophizing scale (PCS), were offered but fell outside this study’s aims and were therefore not analyzed. Patient journals were omitted, as their burden—particularly for older patients—can reduce clinic attendance. Qualitative data analysis Qualitative data from the semi-structured interviews were analyzed using thematic content analysis 26 , 29 . The analysis followed a systematic, multi-step process. First, two investigators (T.S. and A.T.) independently read the narrative records multiple times to familiarize themselves with the data. During this process, we performed open coding by applying descriptive labels (codes) to segments of text that captured key concepts related to patients’ experiences. Next, these initial codes were collated, and redundant codes were merged. The investigators then collaboratively grouped the codes into potential sub-themes and broader themes. These themes were reviewed and refined by checking them against the original data to ensure they accurately reflected the participants’ narratives. Any discrepancies in coding or theme development were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. This process was facilitated by a coding sheet, which systematically organized the data excerpts, corresponding codes, and final themes to ensure analytical transparency (see Supplementary Table S2 for an example of the coding sheet). Statistical analysis Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Patient demographic characteristics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Human ethics and consent to participate declarations This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and Japan’s Ethical Guidelines. Written informed consent (including permission for publication) is obtained from every new patient under a standing protocol approved by the Tokyo Medical and Dental University Hospital Ethics Committee (D2013-005-04), which also cleared this retrospective, anonymized chart review. Participation is voluntary and non-incentivized; refusal or later withdrawal does not affect care. Records were fully de-identified, with the linkage file stored on an encrypted, offline computer accessible only to the investigators. Patients may withdraw consent at any time through their dentist or the hospital Ethics Support Centre. No additional questionnaires, patient journals, or interventions were introduced solely for research purposes, thereby minimizing burden and incremental risk to this cohort of patients in socio-economic and psychiatric circumstances of particular difficulty. For patients with a current or past psychiatric disorder, the dentist obtained explicit consent before contacting the psychiatrist to verify the diagnosis and active psychotropic therapy and the psychiatrist’s view; the minimum information needed to individualize MUOS treatment while preventing drug-interaction risks. No psychotherapy notes or other narrative records were accessed in line with data-minimization and confidentiality principles. ChatGPT o1 Pro (OpenAI) was used to translate the manuscript from Japanese to English and refine its language; the authors then reviewed and edited the text and bear full responsibility for the final content. Results The background characteristics of all 55 patients are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Among patients aged 18 to 64 years who were receiving public assistance, the mean age was 50.6 ± 9.0 years; there were 15 males and 40 females. The MUOS diagnoses in our department, including duplicate diagnoses, were as follows: OC in 18 patients (32.7%), BMS in 16 patients (29.1%), PIFP including AO in 14 patients (25.5%), and PBS in 10 patients (18.2%). A total of 50 patients (90.9%) had a history of psychiatric disorders, with 10 (18.2%) diagnosed with or suspected of having a schizophrenia spectrum disorder and 5 (9.1%) having a bipolar and related disorder. The rate of polypharmacy was 74.5%. Figure 1 shows the psychiatric diagnoses (including suspected cases and duplicates) among all 55 patients. Figure 2 illustrates the approach implemented in our department. Qualitative insights from intake interviews Content analysis of the 55 interview records yielded two major themes: Chronic social isolation and limited informal support – consistent with the objectively high living-alone rate (81.8%), many participants described estranged family relationships, few confidants and reluctance to “burden others.” Intertwined trajectories of psychiatric instability and oral symptoms—patients frequently linked flare-ups of burning, occlusal or foreign-body sensations to periods of depressed mood or psychotic exacerbation, reinforcing the 90.9% prevalence of psychiatric history found quantitatively. Integration The joint display showed strong concordance between qualitative and quantitative strands: qualitative narratives of isolation, unemployment and medication burden mapped directly onto the measured 76.4% unemployment and 74.5% polypharmacy rates, illustrating how social determinants and treatment complexity intersect in this cohort. Discussion The 55 patients analyzed in this study constitute only 1.5% of the 3,685 new patients seen in our department and—being welfare recipients aged 18–64 years—form a niche subgroup with limited socioeconomic resources within the broader MUOS population. Consequently, the findings presented below should not be extrapolated directly to MUOS in general but should be interpreted only in the context of patients with comparable socioeconomic backgrounds. Prevalence of psychiatric history and the importance of collaboration with psychiatrists In this study, 1.5% (98/3,685) of the total patient population, aged 18 to 64 years, were receiving public assistance. Among these individuals, 90.9% had a previous or current psychiatric history, a markedly higher rate compared with the 48% (43% mental disorders, 14% substance use) reported among Medicaid recipients in the National Comorbidity Survey 30 . A notable proportion of participants had schizophrenia spectrum or bipolar disorders. Such severe mental illnesses can complicate MUOS, as mood instability and cognitive challenges may intensify pain, burning, or occlusal discomfort 31 – 33 . Such complexity complicates the objective assessment of MUOS and the development of standardized treatment protocols. When psychiatric symptoms are inadequately controlled, managing all of a patient’s issues solely through MUOS treatment proves difficult. Thus, the primary focus should be on stabilizing psychiatric care. A collaborative approach—where dentists and psychiatrists exchange information on oral symptoms and jointly refine treatment priorities—is crucial. If patients have discontinued or resist psychiatric follow-up, dentists must emphasize that collaboration with a psychiatrist is essential for symptom improvement and encourage adherence. (Fig. 2 Referred to psychiatry cases) When dental assessments suggest drug-induced effects—such as dry mouth, dyskinesia, or open bite—requesting prescription adjustments from the psychiatrist may alleviate symptoms 34 . Continuous information exchange and joint decision-making with psychiatric services are crucial for delivering truly holistic patient care. Nevertheless, exchange of psychiatric information carries ethical and practical hazards. First, revealing sensitive diagnoses or risk histories may stigmatize the patient or alter the clinician–patient relationship. Second, every transfer of personal health data increases the chance of accidental disclosure. Third, psychiatric details can be misinterpreted by non-specialists, leading to over- or under-treatment. As previously described, our clinic adopts a minimal-yet-sufficient approach to balance therapeutic benefits against potential risks while mitigating both clinical and ethical pitfalls. Polypharmacy In this study, polypharmacy was common. Among public assistance recipients, both physical and mental comorbidities may drive an increase in prescribed drugs. References show that among Medicaid recipients, 19% received antipsychotic polypharmacy, while overall polypharmacy prevalence can reach about 50.9% 35 , 36 . A similar trend appears in Japan: many public assistance recipients also manage chronic conditions (e.g., hypertension) alongside mental disorders, leading to multiple drug regimens 23 . MUOS treatment often involves psychotropics, which further heighten polypharmacy risks 4 . Managing drug interactions and side effects is vital. Therefore, balancing pharmacotherapies to address MUOS, mental health, and physical illnesses is a pressing concern. The high rate of living alone, lack of family support, and limitations of the social security system As this study shows, the living alone rate is strikingly high (81.8%). Even among public assistance recipients, many have severed or never formed family ties, resulting in chronic isolation and heightened stress 37 . Limited family support can compromise everyday tasks—such as medical visits and medication management—increasing the risk of treatment discontinuation, missed doses, and worsening symptoms 38 , 39 . Consequently, MUOS patients living alone need not only in-hospital assistance but also cooperation with community resources (e.g., comprehensive support centers, caseworkers, home-visit nursing services) to stabilize their daily lives, mental health, and employment 40 . However, the current social security system remains underprepared for seamless multidisciplinary coordination, partly due to regional disparities in resources and inconsistent information sharing. Stigma (prejudice) toward public assistance recipients may further limit access to crucial social resources like community centers, caseworkers, and family support 41 . Moving forward, it will be essential to leverage medical coverage under public assistance while establishing a more comprehensive community-based support structure. Achieving this goal requires system redesign, better resource allocation, reduced regional gaps, and concerted efforts to combat stigma. Limitation This study has several limitations. First, the study population was limited to public assistance recipients at a single institution (Department of Psychosomatic Dentistry, Tokyo Medical and Dental University Hospital), which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future multicenter, large-scale studies incorporating regional variations are warranted. Second, the retrospective nature of data collection from medical records, referral letters, and self-reports may introduce recall bias and information gaps. Prospective studies could provide more robust data. Third, self-reported data on medical history, living conditions, and medication, even with partial verification from referral letters, are susceptible to reporting biases. Fourth, the lack of a control group of non-public assistance recipients of similar age precludes determining if the findings are specific to this population or reflect broader trends. Fifth, the limited sample size (n = 55, ages 18–64) may reduce statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses. Larger cohort studies are needed to validate these findings. Finally, while psychiatric histories were noted, detailed assessments of disease severity, stage, and medication were incomplete. Furthermore, psychosocial factors like social isolation and financial strain were evaluated primarily through semi-structured interviews, lacking comprehensive quantitative assessment. Conclusion In this single-center retrospective review of 55 working-age Japanese public-assistance recipients with MUOS, we observed a striking convergence of psychiatric comorbidity, complex polypharmacy, and social vulnerability. Although these exploratory, hypothesis-generating findings derive from a small cohort, our result suggested that 1.5–2.7% (55/3,685; 98/3,685) of the patients with MUOS need more psychiatric stabilization, prudent prescription management, and community-based social support at the heart of care pathways for this socio-economically underserved population.
