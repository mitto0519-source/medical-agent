Abstract Background Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a major global health challenge and occupational status is a critical determinant influencing NCD development, yet many cross-sectional studies have only linked current occupational status with specific diseases, overlooking broader temporal factors. The primary aim of the present study was to comprehensively elucidate the intricate interplay between alterations in working status, NCDs, and physical activity (PA) among Korean middle-aged and older adults, employing a longitudinal panel study design. Methods This study utilized longitudinal data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging, covering the period from 2006 to 2020, with a median follow-up duration of 10 years. ( n = 5101 participants aged 45 years and older). Changes in working status were categorized, focusing on (1) yes–yes (e.g., indicative of sustained employment), (2) yes–no (e.g., denoting a transition from employment to non-employment), (3) no–yes (e.g., representing a shift from non-employment to employment), and (4) no–no (reflecting a continual non-employment status). PA was defined as the total minutes of moderate-to-vigorous PA per week and categorized into two groups: &lt;150 min/week and ≥ 150 min/week. Confounding factors included age, gender, marital status, education level, and body mass index. Relative risk ratios (RRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using random-effect multinomial logistic regression. Results Individuals who were currently employed (adjusted RRR = 0.688, 95% CI = 0.656–0.722, p &lt; 0.001) or who transitioned to employment (adjusted RRR = 0.755, 95% CI = 0.707–0.807, p &lt; 0.001) had a reduced risk of physical inactivity. However, employed individuals had an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases (yes-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.653, 95% CI = 1.547–1.766, p &lt; 0.001; no-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.168, 95% CI = 1.100–1.240, p &lt; 0.001) and diabetes (yes-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.535, 95% CI = 1.464–1.610, p &lt; 0.001; no-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.124, 95% CI = 1.078–1.173, p &lt; 0.001). Additionally, current workers exhibited higher risks of cancer (yes-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.871, 95% CI = 1.721–2.035, p &lt; 0.001; no-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.089, 95% CI = 1.018–1.166, p = 0.014) and chronic respiratory diseases (yes-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.467, 95% CI = 1.307–1.648, p &lt; 0.001; no-yes: adjusted RRR = 1.121, 95% CI = 1.011–1.242, p = 0.029). Conclusion This longitudinal study revealed that individuals engaged in or transitioning to employment displayed a reduced likelihood of regular PA. Moreover, those with work history, transitioning, or consistently working, exhibited increased vulnerability to all NCDs compared to those without work experience. Introduction Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), colloquially referred to as lifestyle diseases, represent a substantial global health concern, especially prevalent among middle-aged and older adults [ 1 , 2 ]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), NCDs, which include heart disease, stroke, chronic lung disease, cancers, and diabetes, contribute to an estimated 41 million annual fatalities, comprising 74% of the overall global mortality [ 3 ]. While diverse factors such as genetic predisposition, physiological attributes, and environmental conditions contribute to the occurrence of NCDs [ 3 ], lifestyle choices and behaviors play a pivotal role. Factors such as physical inactivity, unhealthy dietary patterns, excessive alcohol consumption, and tobacco use significantly influence the onset and progression of these diseases [ 4 – 10 ]. Therefore, elucidating the fundamental determinants of NCDs is imperative for advancing global health initiatives. A pivotal determinant influencing the development of NCDs is the occupational status of individuals. Numerous investigations corroborate the substantial impact of employment on the overall health of individuals [ 11 , 12 ]. Specifically, a study revealed a noteworthy association between occupational stress and the health of workers, particularly in terms of depression, a condition closely linked to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [ 11 ]. Additionally, Kirk and colleagues observed an elevated risk of inactivity among individuals engaged in prolonged work hours with low levels of occupational physical activity (PA) [ 12 ]. Furthermore, retirement emerges as a noteworthy factor influencing health, with some studies suggesting positive effects [ 13 – 15 ]. Chanda’s research indicated that retirement at an appropriate age, coupled with adequate social participation, conferred cognitive well-being benefits [ 16 ]. Similarly, other investigations proposed that post-retirement activities contributed to increased cognitive flexibility [ 14 , 15 ]. Within the spectrum of age demographics, individuals classified as middle-aged and older adults undergo more frequent alterations in their employment status compared to their younger counterparts. This phenomenon can be ascribed to various factors, including retirement, career transitions, or other employment-related factors, all of which have implications for health-related outcomes [ 17 ]. The repercussions of such fluctuations wield a profound impact on their overall lives and well-being, rendering them potentially more responsive to variations in work status as they progress with age. While numerous cross-sectional studies have affirmed the association between work status and various diseases, focusing exclusively on the status at the time of the assessment [ 16 , 18 , 19 ], it proves challenging to thoroughly examine the link between momentary work status and NCDs due to the gradual and progressive nature of NCDs over time. In essence, to explore the association between changes in work status and subsequent health outcomes, longitudinal research presents a more suitable approach for yielding reliable and comprehensive results [ 20 ]. Hence, the primary aims of the present study are twofold: (1) to explore the association between changes in work status and PA among Korean middle-aged and older adults employing a longitudinal panel study design, and (2) to elucidate the intricate interplay between alterations in work status and NCDs in this population, thereby augmenting the extant body of knowledge and providing insights for healthcare professionals, policymakers, and individuals. Methods Study design The Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) constitutes an ongoing longitudinal panel survey administered by the Korea Labor Institute since its Initiation in 2006. Conducted biennially, the survey targets individuals aged 45 years and older in Korea, with the primary objective of generating essential data to offer valuable insights for the formulation of targeted and effective socioeconomic policies tailored to the demands of an aging society [ 21 ]. Utilizing a multistage stratified cluster sampling method based on 15 geographic areas and diverse dwelling types nationwide [ 22 ], trained surveyors obtained participants’ informed consent and conducted face-to-face interviews using a personal interviewing program, adhering to KLoSA’s established research protocol [ 22 ]. The survey has maintained a biennial recurrence, and the latest iteration, the 8th survey, concluded in 2020. Ethical approval for the present study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Statistics Korea (No. 336002). Given the public accessibility of KLoSA data and the prior acquisition of informed consent from all participants, further ethical approval was deemed unnecessary. Also, this study followed the STROBE checklist [ 23 ]. Study participants Following the KLoSA protocol, participants’ cognitive status was initially assessed using the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE). Across the first to eighth survey waves, respondents consistently maintained an average cognitive function score above 23 out of 30 points, indicating that most participants were capable of providing informed and accurate responses during interviews [ 24 ]. The present study utilized longitudinal data encompassing the initial to the eighth waves of the KLoSA spanning from 2006 to 2020. The baseline survey, initiated in 2006, involved the participation of 10,254 individuals aged 45 years and older. Probability proportional to size sampling methodology was implemented to ensure the attainment of a nationally representative sample from the entirety of the Korean population [ 22 ]. Exclusion criteria were applied to individuals who did not partake in all eight surveys ( n = 4,537) and those who failed to provide responses pertaining to diseases and PA ( n = 616). Consequently, data from 5,101 participants, spanning across all eight waves, were collected and underwent analysis for the purpose of this study (Figure 1 ). Variables Working status All 5,101 participants in the study responded to an inquiry regarding their prevailing occupational status, as exemplified by the question “Are you currently engaged in income-generating work?” The response options provided were dichotomous, allowing participants to select either “yes” or “no.” The classification of working status encompassed activities such as formal employment, entrepreneurship, and familial assistance. Changes in working status across successive survey waves were stratified into four distinct categories: 1) yes – yes (e.g., indicative of sustained employment), 2) yes – no (e.g., denoting a transition from employment to non-employment), 3) no – yes (e.g., representing a shift from non-employment to employment), and 4) no – no (reflecting a continual non-employment status). For participants who changed their occupational status multiple times during the study period, their occupational status was categorized based on their current working status at each survey wave. For instance, a participant who was not working during the initial period (2006–2008), then began working in subsequent periods and was not working again by the 2020 period, would be categorized accordingly at each survey wave. Physical activity (PA) The KLoSA assessed PA by asking whether participants engaged in any form of exercise at least once a week. If they answered yes, the frequency and duration of the exercise were recorded [ 25 ]. Following to the WHO guidelines, which recommend at least 150 min of moderate-intensity PA per week [ 26 ]; thus, PA levels were categorized into two groups: less than 150 min per week (&lt; 150 min/week) and 150 min or more per week (≥ 150 min/week). Specifically, participants who reported no exercise or exercised for 1–149 min per week were classified as “&lt;150 minutes per week” and those who exercised for 150 min or more per week were classified as “≥150 minutes per week.” Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) All study participants were asked regarding their health status, specifically inquiring whether they had received a physician’s diagnosis of a specific ailment since the preceding survey. The inquiry was framed as follows: “Have you been diagnosed with a specific disease by your doctor since the last survey?” and participants were provided with the response options of “yes” or “no.” This study concentrated on the four most prevalent NCDs as delineated by the WHO [ 27 ]. These encompassed cardiovascular diseases (e.g., angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, and/or heart attack), diabetes (e.g., hyperglycemia), cancer (e.g., malignant tumor, excluding minor cutaneous cancer), and chronic respiratory diseases (e.g., bronchitis and/or heaves). The data from each survey wave were used to assess the presence of NCDs, and no participants were excluded based on NCDs status. All participants, regardless of their NCD status during the waves used to define working status, were included in the analysis. Covariates The covariates considered in this study encompassed a spectrum of demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related variables across each survey wave. Demographic characteristics involved stratifications based on age, with categories including 45–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years, as well as the binary classification of gender assigned at birth (e.g., male or female). Socioeconomic factors incorporated marital status, encompassing categories such as married, being separated, divorced, bereaved, or separated family, and unmarried, along with educational attainment classified into four levels: elementary school or below, middle school, high school, and college and above. Body mass index (BMI) was computed based on self-reported height and weight information collected in the initial survey wave and subsequently categorized into three groups: underweight (&lt; 18.5 kg/m 2 ), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m 2 ), and overweight (≥ 23 kg/m 2 ) [ 28 ]. Additionally, activities of daily living (ADL) were categorized as either normal or requiring assistance [ 29 ], and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) were similarly classified as normal or requiring assistance [ 30 ]. For both ADL and IADL, participants were categorized as requiring assistance if they scored below the following cut-off points: a score of less than 5 out of 6 on the ADL scale indicated a need for assistance with basic activities, while a score of less than 7 out of 8 on the IADL scale suggested a need for assistance with instrumental activities. Data analysis The chi-square test was employed to systematically explore and compare the general characteristics (e.g., demographic and socioeconomic information), health-related factors (e.g., PA, ADL, IADL, and BMI), and NCDs (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases) within the study population. The current study utilized the exchangeable variance-covariance matrix, which assumes that all pairs of observations have the same correlation [ 31 ]. This assumption is well-suited to our longitudinal data, as it effectively accounts for the within-subject correlation over time and simplifies the model while adequately addressing the correlation among repeated measures [ 31 ]. Also, to scrutinize the association between working status, NCDs risk, and PA, a random-effect multinomial logistic regression was implemented in this study. The random-effects model was chosen because it accounts for unobserved individual-specific effects that could influence the outcomes and allows for the inclusion of time-invariant covariates, such as baseline demographic characteristics [ 32 ], Additionally, this model provides more efficient estimates under the assumption that individual-specific effects are uncorrelated with the explanatory variables, making it well-suited to our study’s design and objectives. Adjustments for confounding factors, including age, sex, education status, and BMI, were incorporated, and effect estimates were presented as relative risk ratios (RRR) along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using the statistical software STATA 17 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA), and the significance level was set at p &lt; 0.05. Results Table 1 presents an overview of the general characteristics and diagnostic details pertaining to NCDs within the population during the baseline years (2006–2008), specifically coinciding with the initial manifestation of alterations in work status. Significantly divergent patterns were identified across working status categories for variables including PA, all NCDs (encompassing cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases), and various covariates (age, sex, marital status, education status, PA, ADL, IADL, and BMI), with all p -values &lt; 0.05. Table 2 presents the results of the random-effect multinomial logit analysis, investigating the association between variations in working status and both PA and NCDs, while adjusting for pertinent covariates, including gender, age, education status, and BMI. Those currently working or transitioning to a working status exhibited an elevated risk of participation in PA less than 150 min per week compared to those not currently working (yes – yes: adjusted RRR = 0.688, 95% CI = 0.656–0.722, p &lt; 0.001; no – yes: adjusted RRR = 0.755, 95% CI = 0.707–0.807, p &lt; 0.001). Individuals engaged in work during the survey exhibited an elevated risk of cardiovascular diseases (yes – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.653, 95% CI = 1.547–1.766, p &lt; 0.001; no – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.168, 95% CI = 1.100–1.240, p &lt; 0.001; yes – no: adjusted RRR = 1.070, 95% CI = 1.023–1.119, p &lt; 0.001) and diabetes (yes – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.535, 95% CI = 1.464–1.610, p &lt; 0.001; no – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.124, 95% CI = 1.078–1.173, p &lt; 0.001, yes – no: adjusted RRR = 1.045, 95% CI = 1.011–1.080, p = 0.009) in comparison to those who had never worked. While certain working status categories, such as denoting a transition from employment to non-employment, exhibited no significant disparity in the RRR of cancer (yes – no: adjusted RRR = 1.026, 95% CI = 0.973–1.081, p = 0.350) and chronic respiratory diseases (yes – no: adjusted RRR = 1.068, 95% CI = 0.985–1.157, p = 0.113), individuals with current working status (e.g., yes – yes and no – yes) demonstrated a heightened risk of cancer (yes – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.871, 95% CI = 1.721–2.035, p &lt; 0.001; no – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.089, 95% CI = 1.018–1.166, p = 0.014) and chronic respiratory diseases (yes – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.467, 95% CI = 1.307–1.648, p &lt; 0.001; no – yes: adjusted RRR = 1.121, 95% CI = 1.011–1.242, p = 0.029) than their counterparts who had never worked during the survey. Discussion The current longitudinal panel study aimed to investigate the intricate association between alterations in work status and NCDs, as well as their association with PA, within the demographic of individuals aged 45 years and older in South Korea. In general, individuals engaged in work or undergoing a transition to employment displayed a notable decrease in the likelihood of engaging in regular PA. Furthermore, individuals with a work history (e.g., working to working and non-working to working) demonstrated an elevated susceptibility to all NCDs, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases, when contrasted with individuals devoid of working experience. Individuals who have initiated or sustained employment over an extended temporal span manifested a proclivity towards diminished levels of PA in comparison to those who terminated employment or remained non-employed. These observations suggest a discernible association between working status and PA engagement, indicating an elevated susceptibility to reduced PA involvement in individuals with prevailing employment status. Despite the imperative for older working-age populations to maintain adequate PA for optimal professional performance [ 33 , 34 ], the current study’s revelations highlighting adverse associations between PA and this demographic underscore a pervasive deficiency in PA within this population, constituting a noteworthy concern. As delineated in a study by Puciato and colleagues older adults often exhibit a preference for physical inactivity and sedentary behavior, potentially resulting in limited PA attributable to prolonged periods of office-based sitting or extensive television watching [ 35 ]. The global trend toward an extended working lifespan observed not only in South Korea but internationally, with retirement being deferred until the age of 65–70 years [ 36 ], underscores the pressing need for public health policies advocating behaviors conducive to sustaining optimal health. These initiatives should not solely concentrate on young adults but should also encompass the older working-age populations. Furthermore, actively promoting heightened engagement in PA among individuals in this age group may serve a preventive role in mitigating age-related diseases and contribute to more efficacious management of symptoms associated with the aging process. The prevalence and distribution of NCDs exhibited a notably elevated occurrence among the demographic comprising individuals of older working age. This observation suggests an augmented susceptibility to cardiovascular, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory disease ailments among older individuals engaged in employment in contrast to their non-employed counterparts. While prior research has extensively documented the adverse impact of unemployment or precarious working conditions on NCDs in individuals under 60 years of age [ 37 – 40 ], our current study diverges by revealing a higher risk of NCDs in older working-age population compared to those who have retired or lack employment history. The discerned outcome may be attributed to diverse factors, potentially encompassing prolonged exposure to occupational stressors, lifestyle habits associated with occupational demands, and cumulative health effects manifesting over an extended working lifespan. Consequently, our study suggests the need to foster and modify positive health behaviors within the older working-age population. Proposing routine health screenings for this population emerges as a pragmatic strategy for the early detection and prevention of risk factors associated with NCDs before their symptomatic onset. While the current findings align with certain previous studies that indicated higher NCDs risk scores in older working-age populations compared to their younger working counterparts [ 41 , 42 ], further research is warranted to comprehensively explore the multifaceted determinants contributing to this distinctive pattern in the older working-age population. Moreover, it is essential to consider the potential for reverse causation in our findings. Specifically, individuals with NCDs might remain employed primarily to afford treatment expenses, which could create an apparent association between employment and higher prevalence of NCDs. Prior research has suggested that individuals with chronic conditions, such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancers, often require ongoing medical treatment and medication, leading to substantial costs [ 43 ]. As a result, these individuals may feel compelled to continue working to secure the financial resources necessary for their healthcare needs. Studies have documented that financial necessity is a major reason for employed individuals with chronic conditions to remain in the workforce [ 44 , 45 ]. The potential for reverse causation implies that the relationship between employment status and NCDs is complex and bidirectional. While employment status can affect health outcomes, the presence of NCDs may also drive individuals to stay employed, creating a cycle relationship that complicates the interpretation of our findings. Future research should incorporate longitudinal data with more detailed information on employment and health history to clarify these complex relationships and better understand the directionality of the observed associations. The current study demonstrates notable strengths and limitations. Principal strengths lie in the longitudinal design and the considerable sample size, facilitating a representative cross-sectional depiction of Korean middle-aged and older adults. Consequently, the enhanced generalizability of our findings extends to the national level. However, certain limitations necessitate being acknowledged. Firstly, the study lacked the capacity to identify specific types and durations of PA. Although the established association between working status and PA is noteworthy, additional analytical depth involving supplementary questionnaire items on exercise type and duration could yield valuable insights. Secondly, despite efforts to control covariates influencing both PA and NCDs, variables related to job type and reasons for changes in working status were not adjusted. Therefore, future research should integrate these factors for more precise interventions within the aging population. Thirdly, the reliance on self-reported questionnaire data in South Korea introduces potential biases, as individuals may exhibit a propensity to either underestimate or overestimate their responses. Fourthly, the exclusion of approximately 2,000 participants due to death or incomplete follow-up could introduce selection bias and potentially affect our findings. This high dropout rate, particularly due to death, might serve as a competing risk that could distort our results. Future studies should compare the baseline characteristics of participants who remained in the study until the end with those who did not, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between changes in working status and health outcomes. Lastly, the study exclusively focused on middle-aged and older adults (45 years and older), limited to the Korean population. Expanding the scope of future research to encompass more diverse age groups and international cohorts would enhance the generalizability and applicability of the findings. Conclusion This longitudinal panel study delineated a discernible pattern wherein individuals engaged in occupational activities or undergoing transitions into employment exhibited a noteworthy decrease in the probability of engaging in regular PA. Individuals with work history, irrespective of transitioning from non-working to employment or maintaining continuous employment, demonstrated an elevated susceptibility to various NCDs, encompassing cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases, when contrasted with their counterparts lacking work experience. These findings underscore the intricate interplay between working status, PA, and the risk of NCDs within an aging population, as well as emphasizing the need to expand health prevention efforts among active individuals in late working age. Therefore, targeted interventions can be suggested to promote sustained PA among older workers, who may be particularly vulnerable to physical inactivity due to occupational demands. Such interventions could include workplace wellness programs integrating regular exercise and flexible work schedules accommodating PA breaks.
