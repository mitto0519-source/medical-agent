Background This study examined the association of soy foods consumption with obesity in Korean adults. Methods Data on 131,477 adults (47,828 men and 83,649 women) were selected from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study-Health Examinees study. Consumption of soy foods was estimated using a food frequency questionnaire based on classes of non-fermented soy foods or fermented soy paste. General obesity was defined as a body mass index ≥25 kg/m 2 and abdominal obesity as a waist circumference ≥90 cm in men and ≥85 cm in women. The association of soy foods consumption with obesity was examined using multiple logistic regression. Results Greater consumption of non-fermented soy foods was inversely associated with abdominal obesity in both men and women (≥7 servings/week vs. non-consumers: odds ratio [OR], 0.71; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.57 to 0.87; P for trend=0.001 in men; OR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.70 to 0.94; P for trend &lt;0.001 in women). Among non-fermented soy foods, higher consumption of legumes was inversely associated with general and abdominal obesity in women. Consumption of tofu or soymilk was inversely associated with obesity in both men and women. Higher consumption of fermented soy paste was associated with a reduced OR for general obesity in men and increased OR for abdominal obesity in women. The inverse association between non-fermented soy foods consumption and obesity was more prominent in postmenopausal women. Conclusion Greater consumption of non-fermented soy foods is inversely associated with obesity in Korean adults. Further cohort studies are needed to confirm the relationship between soy foods consumption and obesity. As reported by the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022, 16% of adults (18 years and older) worldwide were obese. 1 The global prevalence of abdominal obesity was 41.5% in a meta-analysis of 13.2 million individuals. 2 In Korea, the prevalence of general and abdominal obesity among adults has steadily increased in the last 10 years (from 30.6% in 2013 to 38.4% in 2022 for general obesity and from 20.0% in 2013 to 24.5% in 2022 for abdominal obesity). 3 Both general and abdominal obesity are major risk factors for chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and cancer, emphasizing the critical need for obesity prevention and management. Consumption of healthy foods plays a pivotal role in the prevention and management of obesity. Among the foods associated with healthy diets, soy foods, including legumes, tofu, soymilk, and fermented soy paste, are notable because of their low energy density and high content of essential nutrients, such as high-quality proteins, dietary fiber, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds. 4 Soy foods are increasingly recognized for their beneficial impacts on chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, T2DM, and cancer. 5 , 6 Soy foods consumption has also shown particular benefits for postmenopausal women, providing protective effects against weight gain and reduced bone formation associated with the menopause-related decline in estrogen level. 7 Therefore, the recent WHO guideline recommends pulses as the primary source of dietary carbohydrate. 8 However, the relationships between soy foods consumption and general and abdominal obesity have not been supported by robust research findings. A meta-analysis of clinical studies on the effects of soy intake on metabolic syndrome reported significant reductions in serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and lower fasting blood glucose and insulin levels in the experimental group that received soy-based foods compared with the control group. 9 However, no significant differences were observed in body weight, waist circumference, or body mass index (BMI). Two observational studies showed inverse associations of higher legumes consumption with obesity. 10 , 11 A Chinese case-control study of middle-aged and elderly individuals reported that consuming legumes 0–1 time per week compared with 7 times a week was associated with an increased risk of obesity. 10 A US cross-sectional study showed that higher legumes consumption was associated with lower weight gain over 10 years. 11 Few studies have examined the association of other soy foods, such as tofu, soymilk, or fermented soy paste with obesity. A previous study of Korean women reported different associations of soy foods consumption with cardiovascular disease by fermentation status and types of soy foods, including an inverse relationship of cardiovascular disease with consumption of total soy foods and tofu; no significant association was observed for fermented soy paste. 12 Given the frequent consumption of soybeans, tofu, and fermented soy paste (e.g., doenjang, gochujang, and ssamjang) in traditional Korean diets, it is essential to investigate their associations with health outcomes. With the prevalence of obesity rising rapidly among Korean adults, identifying the associations between soy foods consumption and obesity (both general and abdominal obesity) may offer valuable insights into dietary factors that are beneficial for obesity prevention and inform targeted dietary guidelines emphasizing soy foods consumption. Moreover, as the nutritional profiles and health effects of soy foods differ based on fermentation status and food types, evaluating the relationships between specific types of non-fermented and fermented soy foods and obesity is crucial. Therefore, the present study explored the associations of non-fermented and fermented soy foods consumption with general and abdominal obesity in Korean adults using baseline data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study-Health Examinees (KoGES-HEXA) study. METHODS Data and study population This study used the baseline data of the KoGES-HEXA study, which is a large prospective cohort study that investigated risk factors for chronic diseases common among Korean adults. 13 The KoGES-HEXA study was conducted from 2004 to 2013 and included 173,195 participants 40 to 79 years old. Exclusion criteria were incomplete food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) (n=3,064); implausible energy intake (&lt;800 or ≥4,000 kcal in men and &lt;500 or ≥3,500 kcal in women) (n=2,667); and missing data regarding household income (n=26,754), educational level (n=479), smoking (n=208), alcohol drinking (n=249), physical activity (n=108), BMI (n=354), waist circumference (n=369), menopause status among women (n=265), female hormone medicine (n=2,897), and previous diagnosis of cancer (n=4,304). Finally, 131,477 participants (47,828 men and 83,649 women) were included in the data analysis. All study participants provided written informed consent. This study qualified for an exemption issued by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hannam University (IRB exemption approval number: 2023-E-01-09-0625). Assessment of soy foods consumption Dietary data were obtained using the validated semi-quantitative FFQ. The reliability and validity of the FFQ used in the KoGES were previously reported. 14 The study participants provided information about frequency and amount of soy foods intake during the past year by referencing a list and photographs of each related food in the FFQ, comprising legumes, tofu, soymilk, and fermented soy paste. Legumes, tofu, and soymilk were classified as non-fermented soy foods, while legumes included all types of legumes and those pickled in vinegar. Tofu in stew or soup, such as doenjang jjigae, was classified in the tofu category. Fermented soy paste included mixed dishes and sauces with variable soy content and additional ingredients, such as doenjang soup, cheonggukjang, doenjang, and ssamjang. The frequency of soy foods intake was classified as rarely, 1 time/month, 2–3 times/month, 1–2 times/week, 3–4 times/week, 5–6 times/week, 1 time/day, 2 times/day, and 3 times/day. One serving of soy foods was defined as follows: legumes (15 g), tofu (60 g), soymilk (200 mL), and fermented soy paste (9 g). Three average serving sizes per consumption (0.5, 1, and 1.5 servings) were used in the questionnaire. Soy foods consumption was categorized by frequency on a weekly basis as follows: non-fermented soy foods, legumes, tofu (none, &lt;1 serving, ≥1 to &lt;3 servings, ≥3 to &lt;7 servings, and ≥7 servings), soymilk (none, &lt;1 serving, ≥1 to &lt;2 servings, and ≥2 servings), and fermented soy paste (&lt;1 serving, ≥1 to &lt;3 servings, ≥3 to &lt;7 servings, and ≥7 servings). Energy and nutrient intake were evaluated using the dietary data obtained from the FFQ. The percentages of intake of carbohydrate, protein, and fat were calculated using the percentages of energy from each nutrient. Dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals were calculated as intake per 1,000 kcal/day. Definitions of general and abdominal obesity This study used measured height, body weight, and waist circumference from the obtained anthropometry measurements in the KoGES-HEXA study. Height and body weight were measured using a digital height-weight measuring device, and waist circumference was measured using a measuring tape with light clothing. All data were processed by experienced investigators using standardized methods. BMI was calculated as body weight divided by the square of height (kg/m 2 ). General obesity was defined as a BMI of 25 kg/m 2 or more, regardless of sex, following the guidelines of the Korean Society for the Study of obesity. 15 Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference ≥90 cm in men or ≥85 cm in women. 15 Measurement of covariates Information on sociodemographic factors (e.g., age, household income, and education level), lifestyle variables (e.g., smoking status, alcohol drinking, and physical activity), menopause status, and use of female hormone was collected by trained interviewers using computer-assisted personal interviewing. Household income was divided into &lt;2 million Korean won (KRW) per month, &lt;4 million KRW per month, or ≥4 million KRW per month. Education level was categorized as middle school or less, high school or less, and college or more. Smoking status and alcohol drinking were categorized as current, former, and none. Physical activity was categorized as active or inactive. Statistical analyses Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Participant characteristics were expressed as means±standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. To evaluate the differences in general characteristics according to non-fermented and fermented soy foods consumption, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Energy and nutrient intake were presented as means±SDs, and differences in energy and nutrient according to non-fermented and fermented soy foods consumption were compared using a generalized linear model after adjusting for covariates (age, education level, household income, smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity, BMI, and energy intake, where applicable). The association between soy foods consumption and obesity was analyzed using a multiple logistic regression analysis model to estimate odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and P for trends. In the logistic regression analysis models, the non-consumers group was the reference group for the ORs, and the median for each category of soy foods consumption was used as a continuous variable to calculate P for trends. Age, household income, education level, smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity, and energy intake were adjusted as potential confounding variables in both men and women. Menopause status and hormone use were adjusted only in women. For analysis of abdominal obesity, BMI was also adjusted. Statistical significance was set at P &lt;0.05. RESULTS Participant characteristics according to soy foods consumption The general characteristics of the participants classified by soy foods consumption are presented in Table 1 . The mean age of the study population was 52.9±8.3 years (53.7±8.7 years in men and 52.5±8.1 years in women), and the prevalence of general and abdominal obesity were 32.9% (40.2% in men and 28.8% in women) and 24.6% (29.8% in men and 21.6% in women), respectively. Regardless of sex, the group with the highest consumption of non-fermented soy foods had a higher proportion of individuals who were younger, had higher education and household income, current alcohol drinkers, and engaged in physical activity ( P &lt;0.001). Among women, the high non-fermented soy foods consumption group showed a higher proportion of premenopausal women compared with non-consumers ( P &lt;0.001). Regarding the consumption of fermented soy paste, the highest consumption group had a larger proportion of individuals who were older, those with lower education and household income, and those engaged in physical activity among both men and women ( P &lt;0.001). Individuals who frequently consumed fermented soy paste had lower proportions of current smokers and higher proportions of current alcohol drinkers compared with non-consumers in men ( P &lt;0.001). Among women, the highest consumption group of fermented soy paste showed lower proportions of current alcohol drinkers and higher proportions of premenopausal women compared with non-consumers ( P &lt;0.001). Energy and nutrient intake according to soy foods consumption Energy and nutrient intake according to soy foods consumption is shown in Table 2 . In both men and women, total energy intake showed an increasing trend with higher consumption of soy foods irrespective of fermentation status ( P for trends &lt;0.001). The proportion of energy from carbohydrate decreased with higher soy foods consumption while that of protein and fat increased. Intakes of dietary fiber, vitamin A, vitamin E, thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, vitamin B6, vitamin C, folate, calcium, phosphorus, iron, potassium, and sodium tended to increase with increasing soy foods consumption in men and women ( P for trends &lt;0.001). Association of soy foods consumption with general and abdominal obesity by sex Table 3 presents the ORs for general and abdominal obesity according to soy foods consumption by sex. Greater consumption of non-fermented soy foods was inversely associated with abdominal obesity in both men (≥7 servings/week vs. non-consumers: OR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.57 to 0.87; P for trend=0.001) and women (OR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.70 to 0.94; P for trend &lt;0.001) after adjusting for potential confounding variables. Among non-fermented soy foods, higher consumption of legumes was inversely associated with general and abdominal obesity in women but not in men. Tofu consumption was associated with a reduced OR for abdominal obesity in both men and women. Soymilk consumption was associated with a reduced OR for general obesity both in men and women and inversely associated with abdominal obesity only in men. In women, a greater consumption of fermented soy paste was associated with an increased OR for abdominal obesity (≥7 servings/week vs. &lt;1 serving/week: OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.03 to 1.22; P for trend=0.017) and a positive trend for general obesity ( P for trend=0.015). In men, the group with the highest fermented soy paste consumption showed a lower OR for general obesity (≥7 servings/week vs. &lt;1 serving/week: OR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.80 to 0.93; P for trend=0.013) compared with the lowest consumption group. Association of soy foods consumption with general and abdominal obesity by menopausal status Table 4 shows the ORs for general and abdominal obesity according to soy foods consumption by menopausal status. Higher consumption of non-fermented soy foods was inversely associated with abdominal obesity in postmenopausal women (≥7 servings/week vs. non-consumers: OR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.68 to 0.95; P for trend &lt;0.001). The highest consumption group of legumes showed a negative relationship with general obesity both in premenopausal and postmenopausal women and with abdominal obesity only in postmenopausal women. The highest consumption group of tofu showed an inverse association with abdominal obesity only in postmenopausal women (≥7 servings/week vs. non-consumers: OR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.67 to 0.93; P for trend=0.013). Soymilk consumption showed lower ORs for general obesity in postmenopausal women. Regarding fermented soy paste consumption, there was a positive association between the highest consumption and abdominal obesity in premenopausal women (≥7 servings/week vs. &lt;1 serving/week: OR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.12 to 1.55; P for trend=0.001). DISCUSSION The current study investigated the associations of non-fermented and fermented soy foods consumption with general and abdominal obesity in Korean adults. Our findings revealed that the consumption of non-fermented soy foods (e.g., legumes, tofu, and soymilk) was inversely associated with general and abdominal obesity in both men and women, whereas fermented soy paste consumption was positively associated with abdominal obesity in women. Postmenopausal women exhibited a stronger inverse association between non-fermented soy foods consumption and obesity compared with premenopausal women. The findings of this study highlight a discrepancy in the associations between non-fermented and fermented soy foods consumption and obesity. Consuming seven or more servings of non-fermented soy foods was associated with a reduced risk of having obesity but fermented soy paste consumption was positively associated with abdominal obesity. Notably, a higher consumption of legumes was inversely associated with general and abdominal obesity in Korean women. In addition, tofu and soymilk consumption were inversely associated with obesity in both men and women. Consistent with our findings, previous observational studies have shown an inverse association between legumes intake and obesity. A Chinese case-control study of middle-aged and elderly individuals reported that, compared with consuming legumes 7 times a week, consuming legumes 0–1 time per week was associated with a 2.4-fold increased risk of overweight/obesity, and that individuals consuming more than 15% of their weekly food intake from legumes had a 70% lower risk of overweight/obesity compared with those consuming less than 5%. 10 The United States cross-sectional study reported that consuming more than 47 g per 1,000 kcal legumes was associated with lower weight gain over 10 years, presenting legumes intake as a reliable predictor of 10-year weight change. 11 Legumes are rich in nutrients that are beneficial for obesity prevention. For example, soy protein can reduce the expression of certain lipogenic enzymes to lower blood levels of LDL-cholesterol and triglycerides, which contribute to its anti-obesity effects. 16 Additionally, the abundant dietary fiber in legumes increases intestinal bulk, enhances satiety, and reduces serum lipid levels while also promoting bowel health by increasing stool bulk. 17 Soy is an excellent source of polyunsaturated fatty acids. These fatty acids positively influence lipid metabolism and cardiovascular disease risk factors. 18 Moreover, legumes are rich in bioactive substances and micronutrients that may have additional favorable effects on obesity. 19 Further studies on the underlying mechanisms regarding the anti-obesity effects of legumes are needed. The present study showed inconsistent associations of fermented soy paste with obesity in men and women. Few studies have been conducted on the association between fermented soy paste and obesity. Fermented soy paste is a traditional food widely consumed in East Asia, including douchi in China, miso in Japan, and doenjang in Korea. Doenjang contains bioactive compounds like isoflavones and saponins, which have proven anti-cancer effects; 20 however, it is also has a high sodium content. A 12-week randomized controlled trial (RCT) in Korea found that overweight adults who consumed doenjang supplements, receiving 9.9 g/day of freeze-dried doenjang, equivalent to 40 g of fresh doenjang, experienced significant reductions in body weight, body fat, and visceral fat compared with the placebo group. 21 However, in the present study, unlike other soy foods, fermented soy paste consumption was positively associated with abdominal obesity in women. Meta-analyses identified higher sodium intake as a significant factor contributing to obesity risk. 22 According to Korea’s national nutrition statistics, the top five sources of sodium intake in 2023 were salt, kimchi, soy sauce, doenjang, and instant ramen seasoning, with fermented soy paste accounting for the fourth main food source of sodium. 23 However, in this study, the differences in sodium intake across fermented soy paste consumption groups were relatively modest. This might be attributed to the limitations of estimating sodium intake based on FFQ data, challenges of capturing sodium intake from mixed dishes, and the greater contribution of other major sodium sources—such as salt, kimchi, and instant noodle seasoning—compared with that of fermented soy paste. Tofu, a soybean-based food, is frequently consumed among Koreans, and soymilk is commonly consumed as a plant-based alternative to cow’s milk. 24 Evidence on the association of tofu or soymilk consumption with obesity are insufficient. A meta-analysis of RCTs investigating the effects of soymilk consumption on cardiovascular disease risk factors reported that consuming soymilk for less than 4 weeks significantly reduced systolic blood pressure; however, no notable changes were observed in body weight or BMI. 25 In terms of other cardiometabolic diseases, a study using data from three United States cohorts examined the association of soy foods and isoflavone intake with the risk of T2DM and found no significant association between consuming tofu (≥1 servings/week vs. non-consumer: hazard ratio [HR], 0.93; 95% CI, 0.84 to 1.04; P for trend=0.19) or soymilk (consumer vs. non-consumer: HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.83 to 1.02; P for trend=0.11) and a risk of T2DM. 26 Additionally, a meta-analysis revealed that the highest tofu and natto consumption significantly reduced the risk of cardiovascular disease compared with the lowest group (tofu: total relative risk, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.80 to 0.99; natto: total relative risk, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.75 to 0.90). 27 While these studies suggest that tofu and soymilk consumption may help reduce the risk of certain cardiometabolic diseases, the findings remain inconsistent, and research specifically exploring their relationship with obesity is limited. The current study demonstrated the positive effects of tofu and soymilk consumption on general and abdominal obesity, emphasizing the need for further research to clarify this association. In this study, the inverse association of non-fermented soy foods with obesity was more pronounced in postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal women. Nevertheless, premenopausal women also exhibited a reduced OR for general obesity with increased consumption of legumes. A previous meta-analysis of RCTs for postmenopausal women also found that genistein, a type of isoflavone, significantly improved glucose metabolism, reducing both insulin levels and insulin resistance. 28 However, another meta-analysis of RCTs involving overweight or obese individuals reported significant reductions in body weight, BMI, and waist circumference only in premenopausal women by consumption of soy products. 29 Previous findings can be explained by greater metabolic benefits of soy isoflavones in postmenopausal women. 30 The sharp decline in estrogen levels in postmenopausal women changes fat metabolism and enzyme activity and increases the risk of weight gain and fat accumulation. 31 Structurally similar to 17-beta-estradiol, soy isoflavones bind to estradiol receptors, exhibiting both estrogenic and anti-estrogenic activity. 18 Soy isoflavones activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors, which positively impact both glucose and lipid metabolism and body weight. 32 Notably, isoflavones in soybeans have been shown to exert beneficial biological effects in preventing T2DM, osteoporosis, and cardiovascular disease. 33 These characteristics of isoflavones might contribute to the anti-obesity effects of non-fermented soy foods in postmenopausal women. This study has several strengths. First, data from a large-scale study of adults aged between 40 and 79 years living in urban areas of Korea were used to examine the associations of soy foods consumption with obesity. Second, this study categorized soy foods commonly consumed by Koreans into four main types (legumes, tofu, soymilk, and fermented soy paste) and analyzed the specific associations with general and abdominal obesity in Korean adults. Third, the analyses controlled for potential confounding variables of age, household income, education, smoking, alcohol drinking, physical activity, energy intake, menopausal status, and female hormone use to evaluate the independent effects of soy foods consumption on obesity. However, this study also has several limitations. First, as it relied on cross-sectional data, causal relationships between soy foods consumption and obesity could not be established. Second, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, there might be potential confounding factors between soy foods intake and obesity. Third, the study participants were recruited from urban areas of Korea, and might not be representative of the entire Korean adult population. Despite these limitations, this study is significant as it identified the associations of various types of soy-based foods (non-fermented vs. fermented) with general and abdominal obesity. In conclusion, this study found that non-fermented soy foods consumption was significantly inversely associated with obesity in both men and women, but fermented soy paste consumption was positively associated with abdominal obesity in women. Among the analyzed soy food categories, legumes consumption showed protective effects against both general and abdominal obesity in women, while tofu and soymilk consumption were also inversely associated with obesity in Korean adults. This inverse association was more pronounced in postmenopausal women compared with premenopausal women. These findings provide foundational data supporting the positive role of non-fermented soy foods consumption in obesity prevention and management in Koreans. Our findings may help guide the development of dietary guidelines tailored to healthy weight management for Koreans. Given the limited research on soy foods consumption and obesity, this study underscores the need for longitudinal and clinical studies to evaluate the nutritional value of soy foods and elucidate their mechanisms of action in obesity prevention.
