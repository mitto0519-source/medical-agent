Objectives A rapidly changing sociocultural environment has led to shifts in the lifestyles of adolescents, which have affected their mental health. This study investigated the relationship between adolescent health behaviors and experience of depression. Methods We analyzed data from the Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey, conducted nationwide in South Korea during 2010–2013 and 2020–2023. The sample included 536,450 middle and high school students aged 13–18 years. Complex samples multiple logistic regression was conducted to examine the association between health behaviors and experience of depression. Results Compared to data from 10 years earlier, among middle school students, the odds ratio (OR) for experiencing depression associated with smoking increased from the past (OR = 1.68, 95 % confidence interval [CI] = 1.59–1.78) to more recent years (OR = 1.95, 95 % CI = 1.75–2.17), and for drinking, it increased from the past (OR = 1.75, 95 % CI = 1.68–1.84) to more recent years (OR = 1.90, 95 % CI = 1.79–2.02). Similarly, among high school students, the OR for experiencing depression associated with smoking increased from the past (OR = 1.46, 95 % CI = 1.40–1.51) to more recent years (OR = 1.70, 95 % CI = 1.60–1.81), and for drinking, it increased from the past (OR = 1.48, 95 % CI = 1.43–1.52) to more recent years (OR = 1.52, 95 % CI = 1.46–1.59). Conclusions The associations between smoking and drinking and experience of depression appear to have strengthened recently. These findings suggest that continued attention to these health behaviors may be important for adolescent mental health. Highlights • Associations between smoking, drinking, and experience of depression were stronger. • Middle school smokers and drinkers are at higher risk of depressive symptoms. • Early intervention is needed for adolescents exposed to high-risk health behaviors. These changes can lead to anxiety and emotional tension, placing a strain on mental health ( Park and Yu Nan, 2016 ). According to the World Health Organization's report ‘Mental Health of Adolescents,’ 14 % of adolescents worldwide experience mental health difficulties, and in most cases, a lack of appropriate treatment exacerbates the burden of disease ( World Health Organization, 2021 ). Adolescent mental health is likely to affect adulthood and can lower overall life satisfaction, making management of mental health and prevention of poor mental health essential ( World Health Organization, 2021 ). In particular, adolescent depression is a common mental disorder, with global incidence increasing by approximately 50 % from 1990 to 2017, such that it has emerged as a serious social issue ( Liu et al., 2020 ). Adolescent depression causes symptoms such as sadness, insomnia, and loss of appetite, and it increases the risk of suicide among adolescents by up to 30 times ( Cui, 2015 ; Noh et al., 2015 ; Stringaris, 2017 ). Therefore, it is crucial to identify the factors associated with adolescent depression and develop intervention strategies to address them. Previous studies have emphasized the relationship between adolescent lifestyle health behaviors and mental health, analyzing the associations between smoking, drinking, eating habits, sleep quality, and physical activity on depression ( Pedrelli et al., 2016 ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2023 ; Yoo and Kim, 2020 ). For example, adolescents who smoke and drink are at higher risk for depression, and insufficient sleep and irregular eating habits significantly increase the likelihood of poor mental health ( Altwicker-Hámori et al., 2024 ; Gariepy et al., 2020 ; Maimaris and McCambridge, 2014 ; Sinai et al., 2021 ; Wang et al., 2024 ). These findings suggest that promoting healthy lifestyles among adolescents plays a key role in the prevention and management of poor adolescent mental health ( Pedrelli et al., 2016 ; Wang et al., 2023 ; Yoo and Kim, 2020 ). However, sociocultural changes, including the Coronavirus Disease (COVID-19) pandemic and advancements in digital technology, have significantly altered adolescent lifestyles ( Dresp-Langley and Hutt, 2022 ; Lee and Kwon, 2021 ; Martín-Rodríguez et al., 2022 ). For example, the implementation of online learning due to social distancing measures has intensified restrictions on outdoor activities, leading to an increase in smartphone and social media usage ( Kim, 2022 ). These changes have led to negative lifestyle adjustments, such as decreased physical activity, impaired sleep patterns, and less healthy eating habits ( Han et al., 2022 ). Additionally, there has been an increase in smoking and alcohol consumption following the pandemic ( Stanton et al., 2020 ). Nevertheless, previous research has primarily analyzed the impact of sociocultural changes on adolescent health behaviors, with a limited focus on how these changes may have altered the association between health behaviors and experience of depression. Most previous studies have grouped middle and high school students into a single category. However, lifestyle factors such as smoking, drinking, eating habits, and sleep patterns vary significantly depending on an adolescent's age, and their impact on mental health also differs by age ( Altwicker-Hámori et al., 2024 ; Gariepy et al., 2020 ; Maimaris and McCambridge, 2014 ; Sinai et al., 2021 ). In particular, the risks associated with smoking and drinking increase with age and are more likely to persist into adulthood ( Her and Oh, 2022 ; Prieto-Ursúa et al., 2020 ). Therefore, it is important to consider school level differences when evaluating the association between adolescent health behaviors and mental health. Considering these factors, we examined how the association between adolescent health behaviors and experience of depression changed between 2010–2013 and 2020–2023. Specifically, we hypothesized that sociocultural changes would have strengthened the association between certain health behaviors and experience of depression. By analyzing middle and high school students separately, we sought to identify differences according to school level. Our results could help to inform effective mental health intervention strategies for adolescents. 2 Methods 2.1 Research data and participants This study used data from the Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey, a nationally representative survey conducted annually by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency in South Korea. Data from 2010 to 2013 (sixth to ninth surveys) and 2020–2023 (sixteenth to nineteenth surveys) were grouped into two periods to compare differences in the associations between health behaviors and experience of depression. The Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey targets middle and highschool students in Korea to assess trends in and the current status of health behaviors, such as dietary habits, sleep, smoking, drinking, and physical activity, through an anonymous, self administered online survey. The sample is stratified by region and grade level, with 400 middle schools and 400 high schools selected through stratified cluster sampling. Our final analysis included 536,450 participants, consisting of 306,311 participants from the 2010–2013 surveys and 230,139 participants from the 2020–2023 surveys. This study was exempted from review by the Institutional Review Board of Kongju National University (approval number: KNU_IRB_2024–042). 2.2 Measured variables 2.2.1 Dependent variables Experience of depression was the dependent variable, which was measured by the question, “In the past twelve months, have you ever felt so sad or hopeless for two weeks or more in a row that you stopped doing your usual activities?” This question has been widely utilized in large scale studies, including the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, as a standardized measure for adolescent depression ( Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023 ). Respondents were classified according to whether they answered “No” (indicating no such experience in the past twelve months) or “Yes” (indicating such an experience in the past twelve months). 2.2.2 Independent variables The independent variables were the demographic characteristics of the adolescents, including gender, residential area, self-reported academic achievement and perceived family socioeconomic position, and living arrangement. Self-reported academic achievement and perceived family socioeconomic position were assessed using the questions “How do you rate your academic achievement over the past twelve months?” and “How do you perceive your family's economic status?” The response options for both questions were high, middle-high, middle, middle-low, and low. Following the classification criteria used in previous studies based on the Korean Youth Risk Behavior Survey, responses were reclassified into three categories. Responses of high and middle-high were categorized as high, middle was categorized as middle, and middle-low and low were categorized as low ( Kim et al., 2017 ). Living arrangement was assessed using the question “What is your current living arrangement?” The original response options were: living with family, living with relatives, boarding or living alone, dormitory, and foster care. These responses were classified into two groups: living with family and not living with family. Health behaviors included breakfast frequency, fast food intake, lifetime smoking experience, current smoking rate, lifetime drinking experience, current drinking rate, weekday sleep duration, and physical activity for at least 60 min per day. Breakfast intake was categorized according to the format of the Sixth (2010) Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey questionnaire into “zero times”, “one to two times per week”, “three to five times per week”, and “six to seven times per week”. Fast food intake was reclassified as “fewer than three times per week” or “three or more times per week” based on the raw data guidelines of the Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey. Respondents who answered “No” to the question “Have you ever smoked even one or two puffs of a regular cigarette?” and “No” to the question “How many days have you smoked at least one cigarette in the past 30 days?” were categorized as “No”, while all other responses were categorized as “Yes”. Responses of “No” to the question “Have you ever had at least one drink of alcohol?” and “No” to the question “How many days have you had at least one drink of alcohol in the past 30 days?” were categorized as “No”, while all other responses were categorized as “Yes”. Sleep duration was calculated by measuring the difference between students' bedtime and wake up time on school days, and was classified into “fewer than seven hours”, “seven to nine hours”, and “more than nine hours”. Physical activity for at least 60 min per day was categorized as “No” for those who answered “None in the past seven days” and as “Yes” for all other responses, in accordance with previous studies ( Huang et al., 2025 ; Royer and Wharton, 2022 ). 2.2.3 Statistical analysis A complex sampling design, incorporating stratification, clustering, weights, and finite population correction factors, was used for complex sample analysis. Complex sample frequency analysis was conducted to compare the frequency of demographic characteristics and health behaviors of adolescents between the periods 2010–2013 and 2020–2023. Additionally, complex samples multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors affecting the experience of depression in adolescents. The analysis was conducted separately for middle and high school students to understand the factors influencing each group. A p -value of &lt;0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). 3 Results Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics, health behaviors, and experience of depression among the participants. Compared to 2010–2013, notable changes were observed in 2020–2023. Among both middle and high school students, the proportion of male students decreased, whereas the proportion of those living in urban areas increased. The proportion of students reporting a low perceived family socioeconomic position decreased, while those reporting a high perceived family socioeconomic position increased. Regarding health behavior variables, smoking and drinking rates decreased, whereas sleep deprivation and fast food consumption increased. The proportion of students who ate breakfast six to seven times per week declined, while the proportion of those who did not eat breakfast increased. The proportion of students engaging in physical activity at least once per week increased. The proportion of students who had experienced depression declined across both school levels. Table 2 and Fig. 1 illustrates the odds ratios (OR) for experiencing depression based on adolescent health behaviors. In the case of middle school students, smoking was associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing depression with the OR increasing from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.68, 95 % confidence interval [CI] = 1.59–1.78) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.95, 95 % CI = 1.75–2.17). Drinking was also associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing depression, with the OR increasing from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.75, 95 % CI = 1.68–1.84) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.90, 95 % CI = 1.79–2.02). Sleep deprivation was linked to a higher likelihood of experiencing depression, with students sleeping for fewer than seven hours having a higher OR than those sleeping for seven to nine hours, although this OR decreased from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.54, 95 % CI = 1.50–1.59) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.50, 95 % CI = 1.46–1.55). The OR for students consuming fast food three or more times per week also decreased significantly from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.34, 95 % CI = 1.29–1.40) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.23, 95 % CI = 1.19–1.27). Students who did not eat breakfast had a higher likelihood of experiencing depression than those eating breakfast six to seven times per week, and the OR increased from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.21, 95 % CI = 1.16–1.27) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.28, 95 % CI = 1.22–1.33). In the case of high school students, smoking was also associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing depression, with the OR increasing from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.46, 95 % CI = 1.40–1.51) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.70, 95 % CI = 1.60–1.81). Drinking was associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing depression, with the OR increasing from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.48, 95 % CI = 1.43–1.52) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.52, 95 % CI = 1.46–1.59). Sleep deprivation was linked to a higher likelihood of experiencing depression, with students sleeping for fewer than seven hours having a higher OR than those sleeping for seven to nine hours, although this OR decreased from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.38, 95 % CI = 1.33–1.43) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.32, 95 % CI = 1.26–1.37). The OR for students consuming fast food three or more times per week also decreased from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.31, 95 % CI = 1.26–1.36) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.27, 95 % CI = 1.22–1.31). Students who did not eat breakfast had a higher likelihood of experiencing depression than those eating breakfast six to seven times per week, with the OR increasing from 2010 to 2013 (OR = 1.21, 95 % CI = 1.16–1.26) to 2020–2023 (OR = 1.25, 95 % CI = 1.20–1.30). 4 Discussion The findings revealed that the associations between the health behavior variables and experience of depression were observed in both the past and current data. The odds ratios for smoking, drinking, and skipping breakfast were higher in the more recent data compared to the past, while the odds ratios for fast food intake and sleep deprivation were lower. Smoking and drinking rates among adolescents have decreased compared to the past, which is consistent with most previous studies ( Caluzzi et al., 2022 ; Lee et al., 2023 ; Mejia et al., 2023 ). This is likely due to the implementation of proactive public health policies, such as strengthening of legal regulations, making it more difficult for adolescents to access cigarettes and alcohol ( Caluzzi et al., 2022 ; Hawkins et al., 2016 ; Shin et al., 2023 ). It has been reported that adolescents are more likely to engage in smoking and drinking behaviors when their peers, particularly close friends, also engage in such behaviors ( Kim et al., 2019 ). Therefore, social distancing and online learning implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic may have further contributed to the decline by limiting opportunities for direct peer interaction. However, the risks of experiencing depression associated with smoking and drinking have increased. This observation supports previous studies that indicated that current adolescents who smoke and drink are more likely to experience mental health problems than previous generations ( Gage and Patalay, 2021 ; Jokela et al., 2020 ; Lawrence and Williams Jill, 2016 ). According to previous studies, adolescent smokers and drinkers reported higher levels of depressive symptoms than their non-using peers during the COVID-19 pandemic ( Gohari et al., 2024 ; Taş and Üneri, 2023 ). These previous findings support the results of the present study, suggesting that the association between smoking and drinking and the experience of depression among adolescents has become stronger compared to the past. Although current smoking and drinking rates are lower among middle school students than among high school students, the risk of experiencing depression was higher among those who smoked and drank, implying that middle school students exposed to smoking and drinking are at higher risk than are mature high school students. Thus, middle school students who currently smoke and drink should be prioritized as the primary target group for mental health intervention strategies, and strengthened early intervention efforts ( Park et al., 2023 ). An important point is that the associations between everyday lifestyle habits, such as fast food intake, sleep deprivation, and skipping breakfast and experience of depression have changed over time. Despite a continuous increase in fast food intake and sleep deprivation among adolescents, the odds ratios for experience of depression decreased compared to the past. This may be related to changes in adolescent lifestyles as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. With the implementation of social distancing measures and online learning, adolescents spent more time at home. In this context, as dining out and school-provided meals became unavailable and structured routines such as morning school attendance disappeared, adolescents increasingly relied on food delivery and developed more irregular sleep patterns ( Chen et al., 2021 ; Kharel et al., 2022 ; Rocha and Fuligni, 2023 ). It is possible that sleep deprivation and fast food consumption have gradually come to be perceived as routine behaviors among adolescents, which may have contributed to a weakening of their associations with experience of depression. In contrast, the risk of experiencing depression has increased among adolescents who skip breakfast compared to the past. After the COVID-19 pandemic, more adolescents skipped breakfast as their wake-up times became later and daily routines collapsed ( Sugimoto, 2023 ). Previous studies have reported that this trend had a negative effect on mental health ( Zhang et al., 2024 ). Skipping breakfast can lead to insufficient nutrient intake and impaired mood regulation, both of which are closely related to depressive symptoms ( Lang et al., 2015 ). Furthermore, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be excluded, as adolescent depression has been linked to reduced appetite ( Noh et al., 2015 ). In other words, despite the sociocultural changes in adolescents' lifestyle habits compared to the past, adolescent's lifestyles continue to negatively impact mental health, necessitating ongoing monitoring and management ( Ra, 2022 ; Twenge Jean et al., 2017 ). These differences suggest that the associations between adolescent health behaviors and mental health are not fixed, but may vary depending on changes in their daily lifestyles and environments. These findings provide important implications for designing targeted mental health interventions. However, as the changes in odds ratios observed for each variable were generally minimal, these associations should be interpreted with caution. This study had several limitations. First, as a cross sectional study using data from the Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey, although we identified the associations between adolescent health behaviors and experience of depression, it was difficult to precisely determine the temporal sequence and causality. Second, the dependent variable of experience of depression was measured using a subjective single item question rather than a multi-item survey based on a professional scale, which may not have fully reflected the psychological state of the participants and may have limited interpretation of the results. Third, the health behavior items were limited to the past seven days, while the mental health items were limited to the past twelve months, which could have led to differences in responses depending on individuals' recent circumstances, such as exam periods, conflicts with friends, or temporary health issues. To overcome these limitations, future research using panel data is necessary, and follow up studies using multi item surveys or professional scales would provide more reliable measurements. Despite these limitations, this study is significant as it compared data from the present with data from ten years ago to understand changes in the relationships between adolescent health behaviors and experiences of depression. It also emphasizes the importance of a tailored approach, reflecting recent changes. 5 Conclusion This study confirmed that adolescent health behaviors were significantly associated with experience of depression in both time periods. The odds ratios for experience of depression associated with smoking, drinking, and skipping breakfast were higher in the more recent period.In particular, middle school students who engaged in smoking and drinking exhibited relatively higher odds ratios than high school students, suggesting that this group may warrant particular attention in mental health strategies. In contrast, the odds ratios related to fast food intake and sleep deprivation decreased compared to the past, although these behaviors remained associated with experience of depression. However, since no statistical tests were conducted to compare odds ratios between time periods, these differences should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, mental health intervention strategies that reflect changes in the associations between adolescent lifestyles and experience of depression may help to more effectively support adolescent mental health and prevent depression.
