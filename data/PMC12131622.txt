Background Cyberbullying victimization (CV) among college students has become an increasing serious issue in recent years, raising widespread concern. CV can result in a series of negative consequences in college students, at worst, leading to suicide. Therefore, it is crucial to better understand the mechanisms underlying the link between CV and suicidal ideation (SI). The aim of this study was to explore the mediating role of psychological pain (PP) between CV and SI, as well as the moderating role of school bonding (SB) in this mediation. Methods A cross-sectional survey was conducted among a sample of 1,247 Chinese college students aged 17 to 25 years (M = 19.98, SD = 1.57; 41.38% male, 58.62% female) in Guizhou Province. The survey utilized validated questionnaires, including the European Cyberbullying Intervention Project Questionnaire, the Positive and Negative Suicidal Ideation Scale, the Psychache Scale, and the School Connectedness Scale. SPSS 25.0 was used for the descriptive statistics and correlation analysis, and the mediated model was tested. Results CV was directly related to SI, with PP playing a partial mediating role in this relationship. SB was found to moderate both first and second halves of the mediating pathway. Conclusion PP plays a mediating role between CV and SI. SB moderates the indirect effects of CV on SI. These findings shed light on a potential mechanism of the relationship between CV and SI. Furthermore, they suggest that laws and regulations should be developed or improved by the relevant government departments to provide legal means for preventing college student CV. Additionally, colleges and families should pay attention to the psychological needs of college students and be proactive about alleviating their PP. Finally, colleges and universities should provide support and programming to help college students establish a solid SB. Online social networking has significantly influenced personal interactions, communication styles, and daily life. While it provides numerous conveniences, studies have also highlighted its potential negative effects, including impacts on mental health and social relationships [ 1 ]. Being a victim of cyberbullying is one of such negative consequences. Cyberbullying victimization (CV) refers to one’s experience of being cyberbullied [ 2 ]. Cyberbullying refers to the repeated use of electronic or digital media by individuals or groups to disseminate hostile or offensive messages, causing harm or discomfort to others [ 2 ]. Compared to traditional bullying, cyberbullying is characterized by anonymity, openness, rapid dissemination, and persistence, which may lead to more severe psychological harm [ 3 ]. Moreover, recent global events such as the COVID-19 pandemic have potentially exacerbated the situation, with increased online interactions and feelings of isolation potentially increasing vulnerability to cyberbullying and its adverse effects [ 4 ]. CV has been referred to as “invisible fists”, in that it has great incidence and can cause widespread harm. A systematic review of 63 studies from 11 countries between 2015 and 2019 showed that CV rates among adolescents ranged from 13.99 to 57.5%, with this rate being as high as 44.5% in China [ 5 ]. Teenagers, especially college students, are particularly vulnerable as a group to cyberbullying. During university, traditional bullying behaviors gradually decrease while cyberbullying increases [ 6 ]. Cyberbullying may result in learning difficulties, anxiety, and depression in college students [ 7 , 8 , 9 ]. In the most severe cases, it can lead to suicidal ideation (SI) and even suicidal behavior [ 10 ]. SI is when an individual thinks, considers, or plans to commit suicide, but does not yet act on these thoughts [ 11 ]. Longitudinal studies have shown that victims of cyberbullying have a higher risk of suicide, and that CV can predict adolescent SI [ 12 , 13 ]. A meta-analysis has suggested that CV is a risk factor for SI [ 10 ]. Given the serious consequences of CV on college students’ SI, understanding the mechanisms at play in this relationship is crucial for developing preventive interventions. In this regard, Previous research has explored the mediating roles of stress, belief in a just world, depressive symptoms, perceived burdensomeness, low self-esteem, hopelessness, etc., between CV and SI [ 13 – 15 ]. However, few studies have considered psychological pain (PP) as a mediating variable between CV and SI. PP (or “psychache”) is a mentally painful experience of guilt, anger, despair, and other negative emotions caused by the obstruction of one’s psychological needs [ 16 ]. The three-step theory of suicide (3ST) provides a useful framework for understanding the development of SI and behavior. According to 3ST, suicide development occurs in three stages: the emergence of SI, the role of connectedness, and the transition from ideation to action [ 17 ]. In the first stage, SI arises when an individual experiences unbearable and persistent PP [ 17 ]. 3ST posits that this PP is a critical precursor to suicidal thoughts. Although the theory also acknowledges the role of hopelessness in advancing from ideation to action, our study specifically focuses on the mediating role of PP between CV and SI. This focus is due to PP being identified as the initial emotional state that contributes to SI [ 18 ], particularly in the context of traumatic events such as cyberbullying. Empirical research supports this perspective, suggesting that PP can double the risk of suicide in adolescents and predict suicidal tendencies over time [ 19 ]. Given that SI often begins with PP, traumatic events like bullying are key sources of this pain among adolescents [ 20 ]. Frequent experiences of cyberbullying have been shown to increase the level of negative emotions among college students, thus increasing their risk of suicidal behavior [ 21 ]. Certain forms of cyberbullying, such as social exclusion, can elicit “social pain” by activating brain regions associated with physical pain [ 22 ]. This neural overlap underscores the profound impact that social rejection and isolation can have on psychological well-being, potentially leading to SI [ 23 ]. Furthermore, PP has also been found to play a mediating role in the relationship between being a victim of bullying and SI (note that this study did not separately explore the mediating role of PP between CV and SI) [ 24 ]. We therefore have reason to believe that PP also plays a mediating role between cyberbullying and SI. In addition to mediation, moderated mediation models can help in understanding the conditions under which certain effects occur. In the context of cyberbullying, moderated mediation can elucidate how certain variables might enhance or buffer the impact of cyberbullying on SI. According to the social development model [ 25 ], school bonding (SB) is such a moderating variable. Attachment, commitment, and participation are all a part of SB, and can reduce students’ problem behaviors and promote their mental health development [ 26 ]. SB refers to the multifaceted emotional connection an individual develops with their educational institution. This encompasses their sense of belonging, identification with the school, and perception of care, recognition, and support from the entire school community, including peers, teachers, and staff [ 27 ]. Previous research has shown that both the specific dimensions of school bonding and SB as an overall construct are closely associated with mental health. High levels of school belonging, teacher-student relationships, and a perceived inclusive school climate have been associated with higher levels of mental health [ 28 ]. A strong sense of SB can promote greater psychological adjustment, increase positive affect and life satisfaction, enhance self-esteem and self-concept, reduce internalizing and externalizing symptoms, and effectively buffer the impact of CV on SI [ 29 – 31 ]. Conversely, individuals with low SB are more likely to exhibit lower academic performance and engage in behaviors such as smoking, alcoholism, antisocial behavior, and drug use [ 32 – 34 ]. Despite the clear positive impacts of SB, few studies have considered its moderating effect on CV and SI in mediating models. Therefore, we believe it is necessary to explore the moderating role of SB in the mediation model of the effect of CV on SI, that is, SB plays a moderating role between CV and SI. In the second phase of the 3ST, connectedness plays an important role in the effect of PP on SI [ 17 ]. Connectedness refers to one’s attachment to their job, projects, roles, interests, or any perceived purpose or meaning that keeps them engaged in living. In the context of school, this connectedness is reflected in one’s attachment to the school environment, personnel, academic commitments, and involvement in school activities [ 30 , 35 ], and it constitutes an important aspect of SB. When connectedness outweighs psychological pain, the likelihood of SI tends to decrease [ 17 ]. Thus, it can be inferred that SB may alleviate the effect of PP on SI. Previous studies have shown that social support plays a moderating role in the impact of hopelessness and SI [ 36 ], and hopelessness, like PP, is a core emotional state linked to suicidality [ 17 ]. Given that SB serves as an important source of campus-based social support and encompasses both connectedness and support functions, we believe that SB plays a moderating role in the second half of the indirect pathway of the above mediation model, that is, SB plays a moderating role in the relationship between PP and SI. SB also encompasses one’s perception of care and support of their school and teachers, which are important sources of social support. According to the stress-buffering model of social support, social support can reduce the psychological impact of stressful events on college students [ 37 ]. Individuals with low SB have a higher risk of developing anxiety and depression symptoms [ 38 , 39 ], while school belonging has been shown to alleviate long-term problems associated with CV by reducing depression [ 40 ]. Student connectedness, as an important component of SB, also plays a moderating role between cyberbullying and adjustment problems such as depression [ 31 ], and strong negative emotions such as anxiety and depression lead to PP [ 41 ]. Therefore, we suggested that SB plays a moderating role in the first half of the indirect pathway of the above mediation model, that is, SB plays a moderating role in the relationship between CV and PP. The current study To summarize, although Current studies have explored the mediating mechanisms between CV and SI, two critical gaps hinder both theoretical advancement and intervention design: a) Limitations in Mediating Mechanisms: Although some research has supported the mediating role of PP in the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation (SI) [ 24 ], few studies have specifically examined PP as a mediator in the context of CV—particularly among college student populations. b) Limitations in Moderating Mechanisms: Current studies primarily examine single-point moderation effects (e.g., the direct moderation of CV on SI [ 31 ]), failing to consider that protective factors such as SB may exert their influence dynamically across multiple mediation stages. This gap limits our understanding of how school-based protective mechanisms operate across different stages to disrupt the cyberbullying-suicidality pathway. To address these gaps, the present study proposes an integrated moderated mediation model that combines the 3ST with the theory of SB [ 25 – 27 ](Fig. 1 ). Our framework uniquely conceptualizes SB as a multilevel protective system capable of mitigating the CV-SI pathway at multiple stages. The hypotheses are as follows: H1 PP plays a mediating role between CV and SI. Specifically, we expect that CV will increase PP, which in turn will lead to higher levels of SI. H2 SB moderates the direct pathway in the moderated mediation model (i.e., between CV and SI), such that stronger SB weakens the direct relationship between CV and SI. H3 SB moderates the first half of the indirect path in the moderated mediation model (i.e., between CV and PP), such that stronger SB reduces the PP caused by cyberbullying. H4 SB moderates the second half of the indirect path in the moderated mediation model (i.e., the relationship between PP and SI in the context of CV), such that stronger SB reduces the impact of PP on SI. Methods Participants and procedure This study employed self-report questionnaires as the primary data collection method. The choice of self-report measures was primarily due to their feasibility and cost-effectiveness in large-scale surveys. Self-reporting allowed us to collect a large amount of data within limited time and resources. While we acknowledge potential limitations such as social desirability bias, the benefits of this method in terms of practicality and efficiency were deemed to outweigh these concerns for the purposes of this study. In this study, 1,350 students from three universities in Guizhou Province, China, were invited to participate in a questionnaire survey using a convenience sampling method. The universities chosen included students from various disciplines, ensuring some diversity in academic backgrounds. The sample size analysis we conducted was primarily aimed at detecting medium to large effect sizes. While the sample size in this study is sufficient to detect medium to large effects, it may not be reliable for detecting very small effects. Future studies may require larger sample sizes to detect smaller effects. To ensure the accuracy of the data, we conducted strict screening of the collected questionnaires. Participants with more than three missing answers or those who provided patterned responses were excluded from the analysis to maintain data integrity and accuracy. For cases with fewer missing responses (three or fewer), we used the Multiple Imputation method to handle the missing data, which helped retain as much information as possible and reduce bias. After this process, a total of 1,247 valid questionnaires were recovered, with an effective rate of 92.37%. As shown in Table 1 , participants’ age ranged from 17 to 25 years, with M ± SD = 19.98 ± 1.57 years. There were 516 male students (41.38%) and 731 female students (58.62%). Among them, 681 students (54.61%) were from urban areas, and 566 students (45.39%) were from rural areas. There were 438 freshmen (35.12%), 387 sophomores (31.03%), 246 juniors (19.73%), and 176 seniors (14.11%). Before distributing the questionnaire survey to collect data, this study received strict ethical approval from the universities where the researchers were based, as well as consent from the universities being surveyed. The questionnaires were completed in students’ classrooms and administered by two trained personnel using a uniform guide and questionnaire. They emphasized the anonymity of respondents and explained the importance of response authenticity. To ensure participation was entirely voluntary and not influenced by external rewards, no incentives were offered for participation in the study. All participants signed informed consent. The consent process included the following steps: (a) Participants were provided with a detailed information sheet explaining the purpose of the study, the nature of their involvement, and their rights as participants. (b) Participants were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences. (c) The potential risks and benefits of participation were clearly explained. (d) Participants were given the opportunity to ask questions and seek clarification. (e) Only after participants fully understood and agreed to these terms were they asked to provide their consent. Given the sensitive nature of some questions (particularly those related to cyberbullying and SI), we provided all participants with contact information for counseling services and mental health resources, should they feel distressed during or after the survey. Measures Cyberbullying victimization (CV) The European Cyberbullying Intervention Project Questionnaire (ECIPQ) was developed by Del Ray et al. (2015) [ 42 ], and the Chinese version of the ECIPQ was revised by Zhu et al. (2022) [ 43 ]. The questionnaire measures exposure to and perpetration of cyberbullying during the previous three months, and assesses two dimensions, cyberbullying and CV, using seven items to measure each dimension (e.g., “Someone spread rumors about me online.”). Each item is scored on a five-point Likert scale with the following response options: 0 = never, 1 = once or twice, 2 = once or twice a month, 3 = once a week, and 4 = more times a week. This study adopted the cyberbullying victimization subscale of the Chinese ECIPQ. The higher the total score of the subscale, the more serious one’s experiences of CV. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the cyberbullying victimization subscale was 0.789. The confirmatory factor analysis found that: χ²/df = 2.91, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.03, indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. Suicidal ideation (SI) The Positive and Negative Suicide Ideation Inventory (PANSI), developed by Osman et al. (1998) [ 44 ] and revised by Wang et al. (2011) [ 45 ], was used to assess respondents’ SI. The Chinese version of PANSI includes two dimensions measuring positive and negative SI, using a total of 14 items (e.g., “I feel that there is no hope for the future, and I have thoughts of ending my life.”). Each item is scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The higher the total score, the higher the respondent’s level of SI. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the Chinese version of PANSI was 0.877. The confirmatory factor analysis found that: χ²/df = 3.61, CFI = 0.91, TLI = 0.90, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.04, indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. Psychological pain (PP) The Psychache Scale was used to evaluate the PP of college students in this study. The original scale was developed by Holden et al. (2001) [ 46 ], and the Chinese version was translated by Qin (2008) [ 47 ]. The scale contains 13 items (e.g., “My psychological pain affects everything I do.”). Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always). The higher the total score, the greater the respondent’s PP. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the Chinese version of the Psychache Scale was 0.945. The confirmatory factor analysis found that: χ²/df = 4.31, CFI = 0.89, TLI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.04, indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. School bonding (SB) The School Connectedness Scale as developed by Yu et al. (2011) [ 48 ] was used to assess respondents’ SB. The scale measures the three dimensions of school belonging, classmate support, and teacher support, using a total of 10 items (e.g., “I am proud to be a part of this school.”). Each item is rated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). The higher the total score, the higher the respondent’s level of SB. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the School Connectedness Scale was 0.750. The confirmatory factor analysis found that: χ²/df = 4.51, CFI = 0.90, TLI = 0.88, RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.04, indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. Statistical analyses We employed a moderated mediation model to elucidate the potential mechanisms by which CV influences SI. This comprehensive model allows us to simultaneously examine direct effects, indirect effects (mediation), and how these effects are influenced by moderating variables. It should be noted, however, that the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal conclusions about the temporal sequence between CV and SI. While our moderated mediation model tests theoretically-driven pathways, longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to establish causality. Through this model, we can explain the complex process of how cyberbullying affects SI, including direct impacts, indirect impacts (through mediating variables), and how these impacts are modified by moderating variables. SPSS 25.0 was used for statistical analyses. First, the Harman single factor test was used to test for common method deviation. The results showed that there were eight factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first factor explained only 30.05% of the variance, less than the critical standard of 40%, indicating no common methodological bias. Next, Pearson correlations were used for descriptive statistics. Finally, all continuous variables were standardized. Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed that all Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.211 to 1.642, well below the conservative threshold of 5, and all tolerance values exceeded 0.752, surpassing the acceptable threshold of 0.20 [ 49 ]. These results indicate no serious multicollinearity concerns. Hayes’ PROCESS (2013) macro (Model 4) was used to assess the mediating role of PP, and Hayes’ PROCESS (2013) macro (Model 59) was used to assess the moderated mediation [ 50 ]. The bootstrapping method was used to test the statistical significance; when the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not contain zero, the effect was considered to be significant. Results Descriptive statistics The results of the correlation analysis are shown in Table 2 . There were significant positive correlations between CV, PP, and SI. There were significant negative correlations between SB and the other variables. The independent sample T-test showed significant gender differences in SI ( t = 4.71, p &lt; 0.001), while no significant differences were found based on place of residence ( t = 0.34, p &gt; 0.05). Additionally, ANOVA revealed no significant differences in SI across grade levels ( F = 1.90, p &gt; 0.05) and age groups ( F = 1.04, p &gt; 0.05). Therefore, gender was used as the control variable in subsequent analyses. The results of mediation analysis After controlling gender, Model 4 of SPSS PROCESS was used to test the mediating role of PP between CV and SI in college students. The results are shown in Table 3 . CV significantly was significantly associated with PP ( β = 0.40, p &lt; 0.001), PP was positively associated with SI ( β = 0.57, p &lt; 0.001), and CV had a positive effect on SI ( β = 0.05, p &lt; 0.05). The R 2 values indicate that the model explains 16% of the variance in PP and 37% of the variance in SI. Additionally, the significant F-statistics ( F = 119.62 for PP, F = 242.38 for SI, all p &lt; 0.001) further support the model’s adequacy. The bias-corrected bootstrap method was used to test the mediating effect, with 5,000 bootstrap samples run using PROCESS. The 95% CIs for both the indirect effect ( β = 0.23, 95% CI [0.182, 0.287]) and the direct effect ( β = 0.05, 95% CI [0.005, 0.102]) did not include zero, suggesting that PP partially mediated the relationship between CV and SI. The indirect and direct effects accounted for 81.21% and 18.79% of the total effect, respectively. The completely standardized indirect effect ( β = 0.23) reached a medium-to-large magnitude according to mediation-specific criteria [ 51 ], while the high proportion mediated further suggests strong indirect pathways consistent with epidemiological thresholds [ 52 ]. The results of the moderated mediation analysis Model 59 of SPSS PROCESS was used to examine the moderating role of SB in the mediation model. The R 2 values indicate that the model explains 21% of the variance in PP and 41% of the variance in SI. Combined with the robust F-statistics ( F = 81.38 for PP, F = 141.11 for SI, all p &lt; 0.001), these results demonstrate strong explanatory power. The 95% CI of the interaction term did not include zero, meaning that the moderating effect was significant. As Table 4 shows, SB had a moderating effect on CV’s effect on PP (95% CI [-0.132, -0.039]), and it also moderated the relationship between PP and SI (95% CI [-0.088, -0.008]). However, SB did not moderate the direct effect of cyberbullying on SI (95% CI [-0.058, 0.037]). Furthermore, the results of the conditional indirect effect analysis indicate that the indirect effect of CV on SI through PP was moderated by SB. As shown in Table 5 , the conditional indirect effects were significant at different levels of SB. Specifically, when SB was low (one standard deviation below the mean), the indirect effect of CV on SI through PP was 0.24 (95% CI [0.180, 0.298]). When SB was high (one standard deviation above the mean), the indirect effect decreased to 0.11 (95% CI [0.069, 0.160]). These results indicate that stronger SB weakens the indirect relationship between CV and SI via PP. To better understand the moderated mediation model, SB was divided into high and low groups according to plus or minus one standard deviation. Based on this grouping, simple slope analyses were conducted to further investigate how CV related to PP, and how PP related to SI, at these different levels of SB. The results showed that the relationship between CV and PP was weaker for individuals with high SB ( β simple = 0.24, 95% CI [0.155, 0.321]) compared to those with low SB ( β simple = 0.41, 95% CI [0.352, 0.466]. This indicates that high SB attenuated the relationship between CV and PP (see Fig. 2 ). Similarly, the association between PP and SI was weaker for individuals with high SB ( β simple = 0.48, 95% CI [0.423,0.541]) compared to those with low SB ( β simple = 0.59, 95% CI [0.532, 0.639]). This suggests that high SB also attenuated the association between PP and SI (see Fig. 3 ). Discussion Based on the 3ST as well as the SB theory, this study constructed a moderated mediation model to explore the relationship between CV and SI, and its internal mechanism. Our findings illustrate that CV affects SI in college students through the mediation of PP. The question of when the effect is more significant has also been clarified, with our analysis results demonstrating that the mediated pathway is moderated by SB. The results of this study have both important theoretical significance and practical value for the prevention and intervention of SI due to CV. The results of this study show that CV is positively associated with SI in college students, which is consistent with previous research findings [ 6 , 12 , 13 ]. A systematic review showed that the rate of cyberbullying victims ranges from 13.99 to 57.5% [ 5 ]. Compared to traditional bullying, cyberbullies can conceal their true identity, while simultaneously making the cyberattack more public, allowing it to spread more quickly, and allowing the duration of the bullying to be more persistent [ 3 ]. As such, cyberbullying may have a greater impact on victims’ SI than traditional bullying. In our study, the correlation between cyberbullying and SI ( r = 0.28) was found to be stronger than in some studies [ 6 ], which reported an effect size of r = 0.20, but lower than others [ 13 ], where the correlation was r = 0.34. These differences may reflect variations in sample characteristics, cultural context, or the specific instruments used to assess cyberbullying and SI. Therefore, caution should be exercised in making direct comparisons. Nevertheless, our findings align with the broader literature suggesting that cyberbullying is meaningfully associated with increased suicide risk, underscoring the need for continued attention and research in this area. Our findings also show that PP plays a partial mediating role between CV and SI, supporting H1. This is consistent with the first stage of the 3ST of suicide, which identifies PP as the initial driver of SI [ 17 ]. By focusing on early psychological processes, our study specifically examines how CV may undermine basic psychological needs and elicit negative emotions that increase levels of PP. PP is the pain one experienced due to psychological causes and emotional trauma which differs from physical trauma. It is closely related to the experience of trauma and other negative events, such as experiencing bullying, which denies them certain basic psychological needs. This increases the psychological pressure an individual experiences, arousing negative emotions such as guilt, anger, and depression, and alongside this, one’s level of PP will also increase [ 20 , 53 , 54 ]. Depression and other negative emotions aggravate PP, and if it reaches an unbearable level, one may begin to feel a sense of hopelessness, which can lead to them considering suicide as a means to escape their suffering [ 16 , 17 , 55 ]. Therefore, CV, as a negative experience, can diminish college students’ satisfaction of basic psychological needs such as love and belonging, safety, and respect. This frustration of needs can elicit negative emotions such as depression, which in turn increases levels of PP. When PP reaches an unbearable level, it can lead to hopelessness, ultimately leading to SI. According to the 3ST, although PP is a key contributor to SI, the theory emphasizes that PP alone is not sufficient to trigger SI unless it is accompanied by hopelessness [ 16 , 17 ]. In other words, hopelessness typically arises when accumulated PP becomes unbearable, and together they lead to SI. Nonetheless, 3ST also identifies PP as a necessary precursor and the initial driver in the development of suicidal desire [ 17 ]. In this study, we intentionally focused on the early phase of this process—prior to the emergence of hopelessness—by examining how cyberbullying acts as a proximal factor that triggers PP, which may already be associated with an increased risk of SI, particularly among college students. Our aim is to elucidate the upstream mechanisms by which cyberbullying generates PP and contributes to early-stage suicidal ideation. Future research should build upon this framework by incorporating hopelessness to more fully capture the later stages of SI development. The results of this study also show that the SB does not moderate the direct path of the mediation model, however it does play a moderating role in the first and second halves of the indirect path of the mediation model. First, SB was found to not moderate the direct path of the mediating model between CV and SI ( β = 0.05). This finding may be attributed to the fact that the impact of CV on SI is primarily realized through the mediating pathway of PP ( a*b = 0.23). SB exerts its protective role by moderating the indirect pathway of CV on SI. Specifically: (a) it buffers the negative impact of CV on PP, which aligns with Davis et al.‘s findings that school belongingness can mitigate the long-term consequences of cyberbullying by reducing negative emotions such as depression [ 40 ]; and (b) it reduces the risk of PP translating into SI, consistent with Mento et al.‘s observation that intense negative emotions (e.g., anxiety, depression) exacerbate PP, thereby increasing suicide risk [ 41 ]. Second, we found that SB can moderate the first half of the mediated pathway, that is, SB played a mediating role between cyberbullying and PP, supporting our H3. The short-, medium-, and long-term negative effects of cyberbullying have been widely recognized, and students who are victims of cyberbullying have been found to be at greater risk of psychosocial maladjustment and psychopathological disorders and symptoms [ 56 , 57 ]. As a stressor, CV hinders the basic psychological needs of individuals, leading to them experiencing negative emotions such as anxiety or depression, and increasing their level of PP [ 20 , 53 , 54 ]. Social support, an important coping mechanism for college students, plays a buffer role in stressful situations [ 37 ], and can come in a variety of different forms. The support one feels from their school and teachers are included in SB, and are important components of social support, helping reduce students’ PP in several ways. Teachers can provide students with emotional support to help relieve the anxiety, depression, or other negative emotions and PP caused by cyberbullying [ 58 , 59 ]. Schools and teachers can also attempt to stop cyberbullying to some degree, and provide cyberbullying victims with psychological interventions as soon as possible so as to reduce the PP and other adverse consequences it causes [ 60 ]. Third, the results of our study show that SB plays a moderating role in the second half of the indirect pathway of the mediation model between CV and SI, which is to say that it moderates the effect of PP on SI, supporting H4. The 3ST suggests that the protective effect of connectedness on SI should be considered once PP has begun to produce SI. Attachment to school, attachment to personnel, school commitment, and school involvement are all key components of SB [ 30 , 35 ] and these are also manifestations of connectedness. According to 3ST, SB as an aspect of connectedness can prevent the escalation of SI among at-risk students, due to their experiencing of pain and hopelessness [ 11 ]. Attachment to school can provide students with a sense of belonging, security, and comfort, helping to alleviate SI caused by pain [ 61 , 62 ]. Attachment to personnel (i.e., teachers, colleagues) can provide students with a sense of security when they are under emotional distress and feeling threatened or suffering from PP, thereby acting as a protective factor for students [ 63 , 64 ]. When the degree of attachment between teachers and students is higher, students are more likely to feel supported and protected by their teachers, thus reducing their likelihood of SI [ 65 ]. Meanwhile, students with a low level of school commitment and participation tend to have low self-control ability and are prone to impulsivity [ 66 ], which may make them more likely to experience extreme thoughts such as SI, or to consider harmful behaviors such as suicide, when experiencing PP. In conclusion, the findings of this study provide significant insights into the mechanisms by which cyberbullying affects SI among college students. Our moderated mediation model reveals that PP plays a crucial mediating role between CV and SI, suggesting that cyberbullying may indirectly increase suicide risk by exacerbating psychological distress. Furthermore, we found that SB significantly moderates this relationship, highlighting the importance of SB in prevention and intervention efforts. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying the effects of cyberbullying but also provide an empirical basis for developing targeted intervention strategies. Implications In summary, this study reveals the mediating role of PP between CV and SI, and reveals how SB moderates the mediating effect of PP. It is highly valuable to deepen our understanding of the effects and mechanisms by which CV influences SI among college students, as well as to expand the existing theoretical research on this topic. The results of this study have important implications for the prevention of suicide of college students due to cyberbullying experiences. First, addressing cyberbullying is essential. The correlation coefficient of 0.28 between CV and SI highlights the need to strengthen prevention and intervention efforts against cyberbullying. Specific measures include: Technological Prevention: Establish a big data monitoring system to detect and intervene in cyberbullying incidents [ 67 ]. For example, deploy AI-driven keyword detection systems on campus social platforms (e.g., WeChat, QQ groups, or Rednote) to flag bullying content and enforce a mandatory 24-hour response protocol. Legal Measures: Enact an Anti-Campus Bullying Law that requires schools to report cyberbullying incidents to public security authorities and clarifies the severe psychological harm caused by cyberbullying. Additionally, establish corresponding legal responsibilities and punishment mechanisms for perpetrators, such as fines, mandatory psychological counseling, or community service, to enhance the deterrent effect and enforceability of the law. Psychological Interventions: For example, interventions based on Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) can help address negative thinking patterns, empathy training can enhance affective and cognitive empathy to reduce cyberbullying, social skills training can help build resilience, peer support can be facilitated through group therapy sessions, and psychoeducation programs can raise awareness about cyberbullying and healthy coping strategies [ 68 , 69 ]. Second, screening and intervention for PP should be prioritized. Given that PP mediates 81.21% of the total effect of CV on SI, attention should be paid to students’ PP levels. Specific measures include: Screening and Identification: Incorporate PP scales into college students’ mental health screenings. This aligns with the first step of the 3ST and helps identify at-risk students before hopelessness develops. Psychological Interventions: For students identified with PP, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) can help them accept their pain while focusing on personal values and goals, thereby reducing the impact of pain on their lives [ 70 ]. Additionally, mindfulness-based interventions (e.g., Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, MBSR) can help students alleviate PP and enhance emotional regulation through mindfulness practices [ 71 ]. Third, strengthening SB is crucial. SB significantly buffers the pathways from CV to PP ( β simple reduction: 0.41→0.24) and from PP to SI (0.59→0.48), demonstrating that SB is an important protective factor. Therefore, colleges and universities can take the following measures to actively enhance SB: Creating an Inclusive Campus Environment: For example, organize a series of activities at the beginning of each semester (e.g., campus tours, team-building games) to help new students quickly integrate into campus life and build a sense of belonging. Establish peer support networks, such as a “Senior-Junior Mentorship Program,” where senior students provide academic and life guidance to junior students, fostering cross-grade interaction and support. Enhancing Teacher-Student Interaction and Support: For instance, implement “Open Office Hours,” encouraging teachers to set aside fixed weekly times for informal discussions with students about academic, life, or psychological concerns, thereby bridging the teacher-student gap. Organize “Teacher-Student Lunches” monthly to create a relaxed atmosphere and strengthen emotional connections between teachers and students. Increasing Student Engagement: For example, introduce a “Student Suggestion Box” system to encourage anonymous feedback on campus issues, with regular updates on improvement measures to enhance students’ trust in the school. Establish student committees to involve students in school decision-making (e.g., curriculum design, event planning), boosting their sense of participation and responsibility. Limitations While the findings of this study have great significance and contribute to the literature, there are also some limitations which should be noted. First, the cross-sectional nature of our study limits our ability to infer causality and temporal mediation effects, as highlighted by Maxwell et al. [ 72 ]. Future research should adopt a longitudinal design to better capture the dynamic development and internal mechanisms of CV, PP, and SI among college students. Second, while our convenience sampling from three universities in Guizhou Province captured key demographic variations (54.61% urban vs. 45.39% rural residents; representation across all undergraduate years) and institutional diversity (including comprehensive, technical, and vocational orientations), the geographical restriction limits the generalizability of findings to other Chinese cultural contexts. Future studies should adopt stratified random sampling across multiple provinces to assess regional cultural variability, while maintaining balanced grade-level representation as demonstrated in our sample. Additionally, exploring nuanced classification methods (e.g., differentiating rural subtypes) could reveal subtle intra-cultural differences. Third, while we implemented procedural safeguards (e.g., anonymous administration) and statistical controls (Harman’s single-factor test &lt; 40%) to mitigate common method bias, the exclusive use of self-reports may still introduce measurement error, including social desirability or recall bias. The classroom setting for data collection, while ensuring a high response rate, may have increased the likelihood of social desirability bias, particularly when addressing sensitive topics like CV and SI. Future research should explore alternative data collection methods, such as online surveys, to potentially reduce this bias, and incorporate reports from parents, teachers, and peers for more comprehensive and objective data. Fourth, while we controlled for gender differences, pre-existing mental health conditions (e.g., chronic depression or anxiety disorders) may confound the observed relationships. For instance, individuals with baseline mental health vulnerabilities may exhibit heightened sensitivity to cyberbullying’s psychological impacts and greater susceptibility to PP escalation. Future studies should incorporate standardized mental health screenings to disentangle these effects and clarify the unique contribution of CV to SI. Fifth, while our study focused primarily on PP, the Three-Step Theory (3ST) suggests that both PP and hopelessness are crucial in the development of SI. Future research should integrate both factors into a more comprehensive framework. Sixth, future studies should consider additional variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the context in which cyberbullying and SI occur. Future research should consider collecting a broader range of demographic variables, including socioeconomic status, family background, and ethnicity. Other important factors to consider include: The impact of online belongingness on SI, given the increasing importance of online social interactions among young people; Additional demographic variables such as socioeconomic status and mental health history; Baseline mental health states including depression, anxiety, and PTSD; Internet and social media use patterns; Biological factors alongside psychosocial factors in examining the effects of CV on SI. Finally, our findings are based on data from a single cultural context. China’s collectivist norms and unique educational dynamics—such as the Confucian emphasis on teacher authority and institutionalized family-school partnerships in bullying resolution—may amplify the protective role of SB. Future cross-cultural studies should test this model in individualistic societies (e.g., the U.S.) and other collectivist contexts (e.g., Japan) to assess its cultural generalizability. In conclusion, while these limitations affect the interpretation and generalizability of our results, they also provide clear directions for future research. Addressing these limitations in subsequent studies will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the relationships between CV, PP, and SI among college students. Conclusion This study proposes a moderated mediation model to explain the mechanism of the effect of CV on SI in college students. The results of our analyses suggest that PP plays a mediating role in this relationship, and that SB moderates this mediating effect. Specifically, in this mediation model, SB moderates the relationship between cyberbullying and PP, as well as between PP and SI.
