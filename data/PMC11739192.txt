To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Abstract Purpose of Review Nutition has long been of importance in the care of Huntington’s disease (HD). The purpose of this review is to summarize recent research relevant to HD nutrition, and to describe some emerging theoretical approaches to research in this area. Recent Findings Clinical studies have identified swallowing problems and fear of choking as major impediments to maintaining nutritional status with HD. Tube feeding is associated with co-morbidities, and provides limited benefits. Non-human models of HD have been utilized to study diets and supplements. Application of findings from these models to humans has not been shown to be of comparable benefit. Summary While studies of nutritional factors in non-human models of HD have shown some promising results, trials in humans have found little efficacy for diets or supplements. The complexity of human metabolic pathways may require a more sophisticated omics approach to identify and study more beneficial interventions. Introduction Huntington’s disease (HD) is a hereditary neurological disorder resulting from an expanded CAG sequence on chromosome 4. This genetic segment codes for a protein called ‘huntingtin.’ This genetic modification results in physical and mental health issues typically beginning past 40 years of age, although there is also a juvenile onset form of HD. Symptoms include dementia, dysphagia, gastrointestinal dysfunction, weight loss, and the defining characteristic dance-like movement disorder which gave this disease the name Huntington’s chorea [ 1 ]. To date there have been no effective treatments or cures identified for HD [ 2 ]. However it is believed that energy metabolism may in involved in the etiology and progression of this disease [ 3 ]. Therapeutic approaches have included antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents targeting relevant pathways [ 4 ]. Many studies have examined dietary interventions, and work in this area has been ongoing from at least the 1980s to the present. This review will summarize studies from 2018 to 2024 on potential benefits for HD from modified diets and nutritional supplements, as well as studies looking at nutrition assessment and care. Through the twin areas of ongoing research and development of better understanding in patient care, progress can be made in improving the lives of individuals with HD through optimizing nutritional support. Methods A literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct, Web of Science and Google Scholar using the search terms: “Huntington diet”, “Huntington nutrition”, Huntington supplement” and “Huntington assessment”. Studies published in English between January 1, 2018, and June 1, 2024, were examined for relevance specifically to diet and nutrition in HD. After removing duplicates, these searches yielded 47 relevant publications. 26 of these were review articles, leaving 21 original research articles. The latter are shown in Table 1 . Results Nutrition Assessment and Patient Care Dysphagia, difficulty swallowing and choking on foods, is a significant problem in HD. Schindler et al. conducted fiberoptic evaluation of swallowing function in 61 patients across progression stages of HD [ 5 ]. They found a strong correlation between dysphagia severity, with 35% of early-stage up to 100% of advanced-stage patients demonstrating dysphagia when tested. Silent aspiration was also identified at all stages, including nearly 8% of early-stage patients and 27% of advanced-stage patients. The presence of dysphagia and aspiration can be distressing to patients and their caregivers, as was noted in a study by Kalkers et al. [ 6 ] Their survey of patients and caregivers found that higher scores on the Huntington’s Disease Dysphagia Scale were a predictive factor for fear of choking. A multiple interventions were reported in managing dysphagia in this survey, including modification of food and beverage consistencies, supervision during meals, and Speech-language therapy measures. Dysphagia has consequences in terms of nutritional status, as was noted by Pizzorni et al. [ 7 ] They performed fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing in patients with HD ( n = 54), Parkinson’s disease (PD) ( n = 33) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) ( n = 61). They also evaluated all of these for nutritional status via the Mini Nutritional Assessment and Mealtime Assessment Scale. Impaired swallowing was found to be an independent predictor of malnutrition across these varied neurodegenerative diseases. Poor nutritional status has long been identified concurrently with HD progression, with weight or BMI typically being monitored to assess this. BMI as a measure of obesity has been brought into question since it does not evaluate fat distribution or body composition [ 8 ]. This may also be true in evaluating nutritional status in HD. Costa de Miranda et al. performed dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) evaluations of body composition on 21 patients with HD and 29 matched controls to look for differences in bone mineral density (BMD), lean body mass (LBM), fat mass, and fat distribution [ 9 ]. They found lower BMD in all HD patients compared to controls. Differences in body composition and fat distribution were also identified, which varied by gender. They conclude that body composition testing can be a valuable tool in evaluating nutritional status, especially in earlier stage HD when other measures may not yet note changes. Declining nutritional status and increasing dysphagia often lead to opting for enteral nutrition support and placement of a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube. Two studies were identified that examined results of PEG feeding for HD patients. Hamedani et al. utilized a retrospective cross-section of HD and ALS patients from the National Inpatient Sample to compare characteristics of patients with these disorders who underwent PEG placement, as well as the comorbidities encountered [ 10 ]. As compared to patients with ALS, HD patients were more likely to have had aspiration pneumonia, sepsis, and/or a prolonged length of stay. Looking at the impact of feeding tube placement specifically with HD, Farag et al. compared 52 HD patients who had PEGs with 52 comparison HD patients without PEGs, matched for sex, stage, age, CAG length, and disease burden score [ 11 ]. The most common complications following PEG placement were aspiration, infections and tube dislodgement. There were no statistically significant differences in trajectory of weight change or mortality, suggesting that PEG feeding was of limited value in this population. They note that quality of life was not assessed in this study, which may be the deciding factor for PEG placement in HD. Diet Interventions Given the very limited treatment alternatives, diet and nutrition have been studied in hopes of identifying a means to delay age of onset or to minimize early to mid-stage symptoms. Alganzo et al. investigated the effects of a year-long individualized diet intervention for 11 HD patients [ 12 ]. Prior to study enrollment, Shoulson-Fahn scores rated three patients at stage 1, four at stage 2, two were at stage 3, and one each at stages 4 and 5. Their diet intervention included calorie levels set at 25–35 kcals/kg/day, with an additional 400 kcals from an oral supplement. Consistency modifications were prescribed as needed. Certain foods were included or excluded due to possible biochemical impacts on HD progression. Dairy products were limited due to their relationship to lower plasma urate concentration. Foods rich in uric acid were included to potentially increase levels of gamma-amino butyric acid (GABA). Increased dietary monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) may also contribute to higher levels of GABA, so food sources of these were encouraged. Foods high in branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) such as cereals, legumes, nuts, meats, eggs, and fish were included because they are the sources of a variety of neurotransmitters. Lastly a variety of fresh produce was included as a source of antioxidants. Study subjects were provided with nutrition counseling, predefined menus, recipes and shopping lists. They report that compliance with the recommended diet was high, but additional oral supplementation was needed for some patients to meet their calorie needs. Over the one-year intervention, eight of the eleven subjects gained weight, while the others showed only small decreases. Evaluations on other dimensions of HD were more mixed, possibly due to the different starting points of disease stage in these patients. A similar view of synergistic food components potentially being of benefit with HD supports studies looking at the mediterranean diet. This diet, based on the traditional diet pattern of people lining in the mediterranean region, includes whole grains, legumes, nuts and seeds, fish and seafood, generous amounts of fresh produce, and sources of mono-unsaturated fats, primarily olive oil. An early study by Marder et al. looked at the relationship between the mediterranean diet pattern and onset of HD symptoms in 1001 people the HD gene enrolled in the Prospective Huntington at Risk Observational Study (PHAROS) [ 13 ]. This study found no significant relationship between a composite MeDi score and HD symptom onset. Interestingly, they did find that a higher consumption of dairy products was associated with a more rapid progression of disease. A systematic review by Christodoulou et al. in 2020 reported 18 studies examining the mediterranean diet and its potential impact on HD progression [ 14 ]. They found mixed results among these studies, with some showing benefit in cognitive and motor scores, while others did not. Their overall conclusion was the a higher MeDi score was associated with a better quality of life, fewer comorbidities, and lower abdominal obesity. These same authors themselves investigated mediterranean diet and HD in a case-control study of 36 HD cases and 37 age and gender matched controls, utilizing the Cyprus Food Frequency Questionnaire (CyFFQ) to collect intake data, and the MedDiet and MEDAS scores to assess adherence [ 15 – 17 ]. They found a pattern of increasing daily calorie intake from controls to asymptomatic HD gene positive, with symptomatic HD patients having the highest intakes. Comparisons between these groups for macro and micronutrients showed significant differences, but not in a pattern that could be predictive of disease progression. These authors interpreted this as being potentially related to food preferences and challenges related to HD. Another diet that has received attention regarding potential benefits for neurodegenerative disorders in the ketogenic diet. Ketogenic diets are substantially different from the mediterranean diet discussed above. They limit the amount of carbohydrates, with a higher proportion of fat and protein, with fat to carbohydrate ratios typically being in the 3:1 to 4:1 range [ 18 ]. Ketogenic diets have been used to treat intractable epilepsy since the 1920s [ 19 ]. Given the known effects of elevated ketones on seizures, and the possibilities that this is mediated through mitochondrial energy metabolism, synaptic stabilization and levels of neurotransmitters, or an anti-inflammatory role, this diet has been considered for application with other neurological disorders. Potential neurometabolic mechanisms for how a ketogenic diet could be of benefit in a range of neurodegenerative diseases were described and discussed in a review by Jaing et al. [ 20 ] They note that this diet could have a protective effect in Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, ALS, and Huntington’s disease. The hypothesis that a time-regulated ketogenic diet (KD) could be of benefit in HD was tested in a study by Whittaker et al. using the BACHD mouse model of HD [ 21 ]. After feeding HD mice a ketogenic diet for three months, they observed that compared to mutant controls the KD fed mice had a dramatically altered gut microbiome. The KD also caused delay in some motor symptoms, as measured by rotarod and challenge beam tests, but did not improve grip strength. Importantly, the KD also resulted in improvements to the sleep patterns of these mice, since sleep alterations are a common problem experienced by people even in the early stages of HD [ 22 ]. While no large-scale studies of ketogenic diet and HD were found during this review, a single case study of a 41-year-old man with HD was reported by Philips et al. [ 23 ] After following a time-restricted ketogenic diet for 48 weeks, improvements were seen in his motor symptoms, activities of daily living, and HD-related mood and behavioral problems. However his cognition did not improve. His weight remained stable. While broader generalizations cannot be made from a single case study, these results seem consistent with the results of the mouse study noted above. Dietary Supplements Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress have been theorized to be important mechanisms in the progression of HD [ 3 ]. A variety of nutritional factors may have epigenetic effects and/or impact neuroinflammation or other mechanisms in neurodegenerative diseases [ 24 ]. Several recent review articles described potential benefits and mechanisms of action of dietary supplements for people with neurodegenerative disorders. These include antioxidants, vitamins, and a variety of micronutrients [ 25 – 28 ]. However randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of supplements have historically not shown benefits for Huntington’s disease. These have included vitamin E (d-alpha-tocopherol), Omega-3 fatty acids (ethyl-eicosapentaenoic acid), Coenzyme Q10 and Creatine [ 29 – 34 ]. Other reviews have examined the potential roles of phytonutrients acting as antioxidants or anti-inflammatory agents [ 35 ]. Additional phytonutrients considered in recent reviews include polyphenols and phenolic, flavonoids, indoles and sulforaphane [ 36 – 40 ]. Some of these substances have been evaluated in experimental models of HD. Supplementing modified C. elegans worms with the flavonoid rutin found that rutin maintained the function of ASH neurons, and decreased degeneration compared to un-supplemented controls [ 41 ]. Green tea supplementation, as a source of polyphenols, reduced mutant huntingtin induced neurodegeneration in a Drosophila (fly) model of HD, but did not extend their lifespan [ 42 ]. Curcumin, a component of the herb turmeric, has been extensively studied for both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties and potential favorable impact on the gut microbiome [ 43 ]. A study of life-long curcumin supplementation in the R6/2 mouse model of HD showed significant benefits, including maintenance of normal motor and GI functions [ 44 ]. As a potential source of antioxidants, extracts of three different types of lettuce ( lactuca satvia ) were given to rats with HD-like symptoms induced by 3-nitropropionic acid (3-NP) [ 45 ]. When compared to un-supplemented controls, HD-like symptoms were attenuated. The variety of lettuce having the highest concentration of antioxidant nutrients showed the highest degree of efficacy in this study. Aloe emodin, an anthraquinone found in aloe and rhubarb, was found to decrease development of HD symptoms when fed as a supplement to R6/1 mice [ 46 ]. Elderberries, a source of both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory substances, were given as a supplement to rats injected with 3-NP as an experimental model of HD [ 47 ]. Rats consuming elderberry powder over a two-month intervention significantly recovered from motor failure and muscle incoordination compared to the control group. Based on the average weights and dietary consumption of elderberry reported in this study, a 60 kg human would need to eat about 2 kg of elderberries per day, or about 100 g of elderberry powder per day for an equivalent dose. Looking once again at the C. elegans model of HD, supplementation with guarana (paullinia cupana) extract showed protective effects [ 48 ]. Guarana provides antioxidant activity, but it is also a stimulant, commonly used in energy drinks. Caution may be warranted with application of a stimulant product. A study of caffeine consumption in HD patients found that caffeine consumption greater than 190 mg/day (about 16 ounces of brewed coffee, or 2 cups) was significantly associated with an earlier age of onset of HD symptoms [ 49 ]. A mendelian randomization study examined the potential for a causal relationship between coffee consumption and age of HD symptom onset [ 50 ]. They found that in a range from zero to six cups of coffee per day, a 50% increase in coffee consumption was associated with an age of HD onset two years earlier than baseline. In addition to the investigation of systemic effects described above, there has also been a specific focus on the gastrointestinal system and microbiome. Reviews by Wronka et al. and Ekwudo et al. discuss the potential relationships and mechanisms by which the gut microbiome may impact the emergence and progression of HD [ 51 , 52 ]. Gut microbiome disruption was shown in preclinical and clinical HD in a study by Wasser et al. [ 53 ] Since microbiota are key in communication between the gut and brain, these authors noted that these changes could represent a potential target for clinical intervention to slow the onset and progression of HD symptoms. Dietary fiber is known to modify the composition of the gut microbiome in a variety of ways that may be beneficial for health [ 54 , 55 ]. The potential role of dietary fiber in HD was studied in R6/1 mice by Gubert et al. [ 56 ] They found that the high fiber diet (10%) improved gastrointestinal function and cognitive and affective behaviors in these mice. For an average human eating about 2,000 kcals per day, or about 500 g of food, a 10% fiber diet would include about 50 g of fiber per day. That is almost double the recommendation in the US Dietary Guidelines, which is 28 g per day in a 2,000-kcal diet. Omics and Future Research The studies discussed above recognized the potential of overall diet patterns, as well as supplementation of individual vitamins and bioactive substances in HD. However further research will need to develop greater understanding of how entire metabolic systems work synergistically in health and illness. In addition to following measures of symptomology, identifying one or more biomarkers of disease progression in presymptomatic individuals would be crucial in understanding what impacts disease onset and progression before it is manifest [ 57 ]. The completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003 provided new models to the study of biological systems [ 58 ]. Using the methods pioneered in analysis of the genome, a cascade of other “omics” sciences such as epigenomics, lipidomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and transcriptomics began. In the search for relationships in complex metabolic systems, Christodoulou et al. utilized data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), a public functional genomics data repository, to identify differently expressed genes (DEGs) and metabolites in pre-symptomatic and symptomatic HD [ 59 ]. Applying systems bioinformatics methods, they were able to identify different gene-expression and metabolites for each HD stage. Identifying the proteins and metabolic pathways associated with these may help to identify target for intervention, by addition of nutrients or other means. Akyol et al. reviewed studies of metabolomics relevant to HD onset and progression [ 60 ]. They define metabolomics as “the study of global metabolite profiles in a system (cell, tissue, or organism) under certain conditions” and note that metabolomics methods can be used to identify biomarkers in various states of health or illness that can be tracked to monitor the efficacy of prospective treatments. These authors note that further development of multi-omics approaches will be very valuable to HD and other research. One study utilizing this multi-omics methodology was an examination of vitamins B6, B12, and folate in a yeast model ( saccharomyces cerevisiae ) of HD by Pradhan et al. [ 61 ] Data for differential gene expression comparisons were obtained from the GEO. They applied integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis and found that vitamin B6, B12 and folate mediated modulation of pathways important for preventing protein aggregation as is seen in HD. Further studies utilizing these sophisticated models and methods hold potential for identifying optimal nutritional interventions for HD. Conclusion Interest in food and nutrition as means of ameliorating HD symptoms has been ongoing for many years, and continues apace with emerging research. As with the general public, enthusiasm for the use of modified diets and food and nutrient supplements often outpaces evidence-based recommendations. In particular, research results from non-human models must be viewed with an appropriate degree of caution. Furthermore, in estimates by this author noted above, the amounts of some products that had favorable effects in HD rodents might be challenging for a person with HD to consume on a daily ongoing basis. To support patients and their families in making decisions in this area, nutrition education is essential, however a scoping review by Russell et al. found only thirteen published protocols for any neurological disorders, and none specifically for HD [ 62 ]. As research findings develop, nutrition education will need to be developed and disseminated for people with HD to reap the benefits of new discoveries.
