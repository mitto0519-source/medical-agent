Abstract BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVES The Korean population traditionally consumes more iodine-rich seaweeds than Western populations due to dietary culture and geographical location. However, the association between excessive iodine intake and thyroid disease remains controversial. This study aimed to evaluate the association between iodine intake and thyroid disease using the largest-scale Korean cohort data. SUBJECTS/METHODS This study utilized data from 190,524 participants in the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study. Iodine intakes were assessed using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire. Iodine intake was classified into quintiles, and the association between iodine intake and the prevalence of thyroid disease was evaluated using multivariate logistic regression analysis. RESULTS Of the 190,524 participants, 8,387 (4.40%) were diagnosed with thyroid diseases by a physician. In the total population, higher iodine intake was not associated with an increased risk of thyroid disease after adjusting for confounding variables. Additionally, the odds ratio for the highest iodine intake group compared to the lowest iodine intake group was 0.89 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.71–1.11; P for trend = 0.7434) in men and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.85–1.00; P for trend = 0.2286) in women. CONCLUSION Excessive iodine consumption was not associated with thyroid disease in Korean adults. Further longitudinal studies are required to assess the long-term effects of iodine intake on thyroid disease. INTRODUCTION The thyroid gland is a human endocrine organ responsible for the secretion of thyroid hormones, and an excess or deficiency of these hormones can lead to thyroid disease [ 1 , 2 ]. The 2022 Health Insurance Statistical Yearbook indicates that the number of hospitalized patients due to thyroid disorders increased from 1,378,000 in 2018 to 1,583,000 in 2022, accompanied by a 36.1% increase in medical expenses in Korea during the same period [ 3 ]. Korea is surrounded by the sea on three sides, leading to high consumption of iodine-rich seaweeds, such as sea mustard and kelp, which are the primary sources of dietary iodine for the Korean population [ 4 , 5 ]. The daily iodine intake of 56,818 Korean adults aged 19 yrs or older is reported to be approximately 326.2–817.0 μg for men and 257.0–802.4 μg for women [ 6 ]. Another Korean study reported an even higher average daily iodine intake of 1,083.9 μg/day among the total population, with men consuming approximately 1,264.72 μg/day and women consuming 907.49 μg/day [ 7 ]. In Japan, where the dietary reliance on iodine-rich seaweed is similar to that of Korea, the mean habitual iodine intake was 1,572 μg/day for men and 1,414 μg/day for women. The main sources of iodine in the Japanese diet are kelp (60%) and soup stock (30%) [ 8 ]. According to a review paper, the average iodine intake in Japan is estimated to be between 1,000 and 3,000 μg/day [ 9 ], considerably higher than that of South Korea. Iodine is an essential component for the synthesis of thyroid hormones, which play a pivotal role in growth and development [ 10 ]. In adults, the body stores approximately 15–20 mg of iodine and 70–80% is in the thyroid [ 11 ]. Of the iodine that is not retained in the body, approximately 90% is excreted through urine, with the remainder being expelled via feces or sweat [ 12 ]. While excessive iodine intake could pose health problems, particularly for individuals with thyroid abnormalities, most healthy people can efficiently manage excess iodine using mechanisms such as the Wolff-Chaikoff effect and the sodium iodide symporter (NIS) [ 13 ]. Studies examining the association between iodine intake and thyroid disease in Asia have reported inconsistent findings [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ]. These discrepancies could be due to ethnicity, dietary sources of iodine, or traditional differences in iodine consumption levels. Previous Korean studies examining the relationship between excessive iodine intake and thyroid disease have predominantly focused on vulnerable populations, such as pregnant or lactating women, as well as children and adolescents [ 14 , 19 , 20 ]. Studies of the association between high iodine intake and thyroid disease using the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), which is representative of the Korean general population, have been inconsistent [ 21 , 22 ], and more research is needed to determine the association between iodine intake and thyroid disease using large-scale Korean data with nutrition intake information. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between iodine intake and risk of thyroid disease in Korean adults using large cohort data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES). SUBJECTS AND METHODS Participants This study included men and women aged 40 yrs or older who participated in KoGES, the largest and longest-running cohort in Korea. A total of 211,569 participants were initially included in the study. After excluding those with no history of thyroid disease diagnosis (n = 13,813), those diagnosed with thyroid cancer (n = 960), those without dietary survey data (n = 3,022), and those with implausible energy intake values (&lt; 800 kcal/day or &gt; 4,000 kcal/day for men; &lt; 500 kcal/day or &gt; 3,500 kcal/day for women) (n = 3,250) [ 23 ], the final analysis included 190,524 participants. All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Gachon University (IRB No. 1044396-202110-HR-220-01). The study followed the ethical principles specified in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. Definition of thyroid disease status Thyroid disease status was determined based on response to the following question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with thyroid disease by a physician?”. Those who answered “yes” were classified into the “thyroid disease group,” whereas those who answered “no” were classified into the “normal group.” Assessment of covariates General characteristics and lifestyle data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained researchers. Covariates for our study included education level (≤ 6, 7–12, &gt; 12 yrs), marital status (single, married, other), smoking status (non-smoker, ex-smoker, current smoker), alcohol consumption (non-drinker, ex-drinker, current drinker), exercise (yes or no), self-perceived health status (good, average, poor), menopausal status (yes or no), and presence of chronic disease (yes or no). The diagnosis of chronic disease was determined by a physician based on the response to the following question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, or cancer (excluding thyroid cancer)?”. Responses were classified as either “yes” or “no.” Height, weight, waist circumference, and hip circumference were measured by trained researcher. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the participant’s weight (kg) by the square of height (m 2 ). Additionally, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were measured. Nutrient intakes and cruciferous vegetable intake Nutrient intake data were collected using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (SQ-FFQ) developed for the KoGES [ 24 ]. The daily average intake of 23 nutrients (energy, carbohydrates, protein, fat, dietary fiber, ash, calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, zinc, iron, vitamin A, retinol, carotene, vitamin E, vitamin C, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, vitamin B 6 , folic acid, and cholesterol) was calculated using the food composition tables seventh edition provided by the Korean Nutrition Society [ 25 ]. Nutrient intake was expressed as nutrient density per 1,000 kcal to standardize intake levels. To facilitate comparisons with iodine intakes reported in other studies, we included both unadjusted and energy-adjusted data (per 1,000 kcal). An iodine database was constructed using food composition databases from reliable national and international organizations to assess iodine intake. The food composition database was utilized in the following order: Korean Food Composition Table edition 10.2 [ 26 ], Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan 2015 [ 27 ], and the updated food composition database of the KoGES [ 28 ]. An iodine database was developed for 396 foods, representing 93.6% of the 423 foods listed in the FFQ recipe. This database included 294 foods (69.5%) with directly analyzed values and 102 foods (24.1%) with imputed values. Goitrogens are compounds found mostly in cruciferous vegetables, including kale, turnips, radishes, cabbage, broccoli, and Brussels sprouts, which interfere with iodine uptake and inhibit the synthesis of thyroid hormones, including triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4) [ 29 ]. The daily intake of cruciferous vegetables was calculated using the consumption data from FFQ items, including Chinese cabbage and radish. Statistical analysis Continuous variables were presented as means and SEs, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences between the normal and thyroid disease groups were assessed using a Student’s t -test for continuous variables and χ 2 test for categorical variables. To determine the risk of thyroid disease according to iodine intake, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted. Variables were included or excluded based on predefined entry and stay criteria, with a significance level of 0.15 for entry (SLENTRY) and 0.10 for retention (SLSTAY). Consumption of cruciferous vegetables is known to be potentially associated with thyroid diseases [ 29 ], therefore, we additionally adjusted for cruciferous vegetable intake in the multivariable models. Iodine intake was categorized into quintiles, with the lowest quintile group considered as the reference group. Three models were analyzed: model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for sex and age; and model 3 was adjusted for sex, age, BMI, cohort, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, menopausal status (female), exercise, self-perceived health status, presence of chronic disease, total energy intake (kcal/day), and cruciferous vegetable intake (g/day). Statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). P &lt; 0.05 was recognized statistical significance. RESULTS General characteristics of participants Table 1 compares the general characteristics of the normal and thyroid disease groups. Among the total of 190,524 participants, 8,387 (4.40%) were diagnosed with thyroid disease by a physician. Among men, 860 (1.28%) were affected, while among women, 7,527 (6.11%) were affected. The thyroid disease group was slightly older than the normal group (normal group 53.3 ± 0.02 yrs [range, 40–87] vs. thyroid disease group 53.7 ± 0.09 yrs [range, 40–78], P &lt; 0.0001). Among the cohorts, the city cohort had the highest proportion of individuals with thyroid disease. The thyroid disease group had poorer self-perceived health status and demonstrated higher rates of chronic disease compared to the normal group. Comparison of anthropometric and biochemical variables according to thyroid disease status A comparison of the anthropometric measurements and biochemical values between the two groups indicated a significant difference in all variables within the total population ( Table 2 ). Significant differences in waist circumference, hip circumference, SBP, DBP, TG, and TC levels were observed between the two groups in men. In women, significant differences were observed in height, BMI, waist circumference, SBP, FBG, and TC levels. Comparison of the nutrient and cruciferous vegetable intake according to thyroid disease status The differences in nutrient intake according to thyroid disease status are presented in Table 3 . Among the total population, energy intake was 1,746.1 ± 1.19 kcal in the normal group, and 1,696.7 ± 5.44 kcal in the thyroid disease group, showing a statistically significant difference ( P &lt; 0.0001). Iodine intake was 349.0 ± 0.96 µg in the normal group and 367.0 ± 4.65 µg in the thyroid disease group, also showing a significant difference ( P &lt; 0.0001). However, no significant difference was observed in iodine intake between the normal and thyroid disease groups in either men or women. Similarly, cruciferous vegetable intake was 170.5 ± 0.30 g in the normal group and 159.7 ± 1.32 g in the thyroid disease group, showing a significant difference ( P &lt; 0.0001). There was no difference in cruciferous vegetable intake between the 2 groups in men, whereas a significant difference was observed in women. Association between iodine intake and thyroid disease Table 4 shows the association between iodine intake and thyroid disease. In the total population, the median iodine intake for the lowest and highest quintile groups was 78.2 and 726.0 µg, respectively. In model 1, increased iodine intake appeared to be associated with a higher prevalence of thyroid disease ( P &lt; 0.0001) in the total population. However, after adjusting for all confounding variables, this association was no longer statistically significant. In men, after adjusting for all confounding variables, the odds ratio (OR) for the highest iodine intake group compared to the lowest was 0.89 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.71–1.11; P for trend = 0.7434). In women, the OR for the highest iodine intake group compared to the lowest was 0.92 (95% CI, 0.85–1.00; P for trend = 0.2286). These findings indicate no significant association between excessive iodine intake and thyroid disease in middle-aged Korean adults. Additionally, we analyzed the association between iodine intake and the prevalence of thyroid disease for each quintile after making cruciferous vegetable intake into quintiles. However, no significant association was observed between iodine intake and thyroid disease in the lowest and highest cruciferous vegetable intake group ( Supplementary Table 1 ). DISCUSSION This study was conducted to investigate the association between iodine intake and prevalence of thyroid disease in an iodine-replete region using large cohort baseline data. A total of 190,524 middle-aged Korean participants were categorized into quintiles based on daily iodine intake. No significant association between thyroid disease and dietary iodine intake was found in the total population, nor in men or women. The association between iodine intake level and thyroid disease differs among populations [ 30 ]. A Korean study using data from 2013–2015 KNHANES reported that the high iodine intake group (&gt; 2,400 μg) exhibited a 2.42-fold higher risk of thyroid disease (95% CI, 1.01–1.79; P for trend = 0.038) in females, but not in males, when compared to the low iodine intake group (&lt; 95 μg) [ 21 ]. However, the number of subjects with thyroid disease in the group with iodine intake exceeding 2,400 μg was very small (2 for men and 19 for women) in this study, making it difficult to ensure the reliability of the statistical model. Furthermore, this study estimated iodine intake by calculating urinary iodine concentration (UIC) from one spot urine, whereas our study computed iodine intake using FFQ. Thus, the results between studies could be different. Another Korean study [ 22 ] using the 2007–2009 KNHANES (n = 9,998) reported that increased dietary iodine intake was associated with a higher prevalence of thyroid diseases among individuals with iodine intake below 2,400 μg. However, similar to our study, the inclusion of individuals with an iodine intake of 2,400 μg or more revealed no significant association between iodine intake and thyroid disease. A study examining the association between iodine intake and thyroid disease among coastal region populations in Japan found that the prevalence of hyperthyroidism was consistent across regions, whereas the prevalence of hypothyroidism varied by region. In particular, the prevalence of hypothyroidism was higher in the thyroid autoantibody-negative group with high urinary iodine (UI) levels compared to the group with normal UI levels [ 31 ]. Another study from Japan found that excessive iodine intake from seaweed was not associated with an increased risk of thyroid cancer in Japanese women, even after adjusting for all covariables [ 32 ]. In China, regions with adequate (median UIC, 243 μg/L) or excessive (median UIC, 651 μg/L) iodine intake had a higher cumulative incidence of subclinical hypothyroidism and autoimmune thyroiditis than regions with mildly deficient iodine intake (median UIC, 84 μg/L) [ 17 ]. A prospective study of hypothyroidism incidence over 7 yrs post-implementation of the iodine fortification program in Denmark, a historically iodine-deficient area, demonstrated an overall increase in the incidence of overt hypothyroidism. Nevertheless, this increase was noted solely in Aalborg, a region that had experienced moderate iodine deficiency, while Copenhagen, which had previously exhibited mild iodine deficiency, showed no statistically significant increase in hypothyroidism incidence [ 33 ]. The varying outcomes regarding the association between iodine intake and thyroid disease seem to be influenced by factors such as ethnicity, gender, and differences in the iodine-rich food sources. In addition, the physiological absorption and efficacy of iodine in seaweed may vary from that of iodine as a single compound. The observed null association between iodine intake and thyroid disease in our population may be elucidated by the potential underlying mechanism: First, in the healthy population, approximately 92% of ingested iodine is excreted through urine [ 34 ]. Secondly, the Wolff-Chaikoff effect indicates that excessive iodine intake suppresses the overproduction of thyroid hormones [ 35 ]. Third, high consumption of goitrogen-containing cruciferous vegetables may inhibit iodine uptake and thyroid hormone synthesis in Koreans [ 36 ]. Fourth, vegetables commonly consumed in Korea are rich in polyphenols and other compounds that may inhibit thyroid hormone production [ 37 ]. To elaborate further, the system that promotes more excretion may be more active with higher iodine intake, notably with an increased likelihood of NIS expression [ 38 ]. The Wolff-Chaikoff effect is a protective mechanism that reduces thyroid hormone production when the body contains too much iodine [ 35 ]. Moreover, Koreans consistently consume cruciferous vegetables, such as those found in kimchi and radish, with nearly every meal, and their overall vegetable intake is relatively high. The average daily vegetable consumption is 249 g for men and 205 g for women in South Korea [ 39 ], compared with 127.9 g in the Netherlands, 97.86 g in the United Kingdom, and 255.0 g in the United States [ 40 ]. Cruciferous vegetables contain goitrogens, which interfere with the uptake of iodine by the thyroid gland and inhibit the synthesis of thyroid hormones. Excessive consumption of goitrogenic foods by individuals who are iodine deficient can compromise normal thyroid function, leading to goiter or hypothyroidism [ 36 , 41 ]. However, consumption of cruciferous vegetables did not affect the association between iodine intake and thyroid disease in our study. Polyphenols found in vegetables have also been reported to be associated with the inhibition of thyroid hormone synthesis [ 37 ]. Certain polyphenols, particularly flavonoids, can impede the function of thyroperoxidase, an enzyme crucial for the synthesis of thyroid hormone [ 42 ]. Briefly, although Koreans usually consume a high amount of iodine, excess iodine is also excreted in significant amounts. Additionally, a high intake of cruciferous vegetables and other vegetables may help diminish excessive thyroid hormone production, thus, elucidating why the risk of thyroid disease does not appear to be elevated. However, further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying this process. In general, thyroid disease caused by excessive iodine intake may be more prevalent in specific populations, particularly among those with pre-existing autoimmune thyroid conditions or individuals at risk for iodine deficiency [ 2 ]. Additionally, consuming the same amount of iodine from salt versus seaweed may result in different absorption rates due to the food matrix. Therefore, more refined research is needed to investigate the differences in the effects of iodine from iodized salt versus iodine obtained through dietary sources. This suggests that a certain level of iodine intake may not increase the risk of thyroid disease in Koreans, as they are exposed to iodine from childhood and may have developed an adaptation to it due to the traditional consumption of iodine-rich foods in their diet. In this study, when comparing iodine intake per day between the control group (349.0 ± 0.96 µg) and the thyroid disease group (367.0 ± 4.65 μg) in total, a significant difference was observed ( P &lt; 0.0001). However, no significant differences were found between the two groups when analyzed separately by sex. The mean iodine intake levels in both groups were higher than the recommended nutrient intake (150 μg) of the Korean Dietary Reference Intakes but significantly lower than the tolerable upper intake level (2,400 μg) [ 43 ]. Another Korean study reported that the dietary iodine intake in the thyroid disease group was 533.2 µg/day, compared to 592.9 µg/day in the normal group in men. In women, the iodine intake was 510.9 µg/day in the normal group and 445.8 µg/day in the thyroid disease group, showing no significant difference between the 2 groups [ 21 ]. The Total Diet Study [ 5 ], which used dietary data from the 2013–2017 KNHANES, analyzed the average iodine intake of Korean adults aged 19–64 yrs was 417.1 μg/day and the median intake was 132.9 μg/day. These results were obtained after the authors excluded 124 subjects who consumed more than 10 g of dried seaweed on the dietary survey date as outliers. Han et al . [ 22 ] reported that the average iodine intake of Korean adults was 837.5 μg/day (953.1 μg/day for men; 763.5 μg/day for women) based on data from the 2007–2009 KNHANES. The variations in iodine intake amount observed across studies may be attributed to differences in dietary assessment tools and iodine database. The KNHANES used the one-day 24-h recall method, whereas this study utilized the FFQ. Furthermore, this difference in iodine intake is likely due to variations in the iodine content of the food databases used [ 6 ], as primary sources of iodine in the Korean diet are sea mustard (42.1%), kelp (21.8%), and laver (13.1%) [ 5 ]. A recent study using data from the 2016–2021 KNHANES estimated iodine intake from brown seaweeds, revealing that individuals consumed 559.16 μg of iodine from brown seaweeds, with over 60% of total intake derived from kelp broth [ 44 ]. Current iodine intake levels in Korea, particularly from sources such as kelp broth, have not been well-studied, despite the widespread acknowledgement of high iodine consumption. Future studies should prioritize a thorough evaluation of iodine intake, including contributions from dietary sources like kelp broth. The strengths of this study include the use of large-scale cohort data from over 190,000 Korean participants, providing substantial statistical power and generalizability. Additionally, this study used a validated FFQ to calculate iodine intake over a long period and utilized a reliable iodine database. However, this study has several limitations. Due to its cross-sectional design, this study cannot rule out reverse causation, where individuals with thyroid disease might modify their iodine intake. The accurate estimation of iodine intake requires measurements such as UIC from 24-h urine samples, which were not measured in the KoGES study. In addition, the FFQ used in this study did not include a food item assessing iodized salt intake because it is extremely uncommon for Koreans to utilize iodized salt. Another limitation could be that the diagnosis of thyroid disease was based on participants' self-reported responses to a physician’s diagnosis, although this method is widely used in large-scale epidemiological studies. In conclusion, an analysis of large-scale cohort data from Korean adults did not identify a significant association between higher iodine intake and the prevalence of thyroid disease. Further longitudinal prospective cohort studies and intervention studies are needed to validate these findings.
