Abstract Background: About one-third of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) cases show poor response to first-line treatments. Ketamine’s role in modulating glutamate, implicated in OCD pathogenesis, warrants investigation. Cases: Four adolescents with treatment-resistant OCD received ketamine infusion (0.5 mg/kg) every other day for six sessions with monitoring using the Children’s Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (C-YBCOS) and Clinician-Administered Dissociative State Scale (CADSS). Three patients showed progressive improvement after multiple infusions with maintained benefits at 2-month follow-up. One patient developed ketamine-induced psychosis. Conclusion: Ketamine shows promise as adjuvant treatment in adolescent OCD, though psychomimetic risks require careful consideration. INTRODUCTION Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) affects approximately 2% of the population, with bimodal onset peaks in childhood/early adolescence and early adulthood.[ 1 ] Treatment typically involves serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with exposure and response prevention (ERP). However, around one-third of patients remain significantly symptomatic despite conventional treatments.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Glutamatergic dysfunction is increasingly recognized in OCD pathophysiology.[ 2 ] Neuroimaging studies demonstrate altered glutamate/glutamine levels in the caudate nucleus and anterior cingulate cortex in treatment-naïve OCD patients, which normalize with successful treatment.[ 4 ] As an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, ketamine modulates glutamatergic neurotransmission and has shown promise in adult treatment-resistant OCD.[ 2 , 4 , 5 ] Previous investigations in adults have produced mixed results.[ 5 , 6 ] Rodriguez et al .[ 3 ] reported rapid obsessional relief after single ketamine infusion, while Bloch et al. [ 4 ] found variable responses. Although several studies have examined ketamine for adolescent mood disorders, its efficacy and safety profile for adolescent OCD remains underexplored.[ 7 , 8 , 9 ] This case series examines ketamine’s effects, response patterns, and safety profile in adolescents with treatment-resistant OCD. This case series examines ketamine’s effects in adolescents with treatment-resistant OCD is defined as failure to achieve significant symptom reduction after at least two adequate SSRI trials and structured CBT. We selected adolescents aged 13–19 with severe, persistent symptoms (CY-BOCS ≥ 24) despite conventional treatments. All treatments followed standard clinical practice with informed consent obtained from parents/guardians and age-appropriate assent from patients after comprehensive discussion of treatment rationale, risks, benefits, and alternatives. CASE SERIES This case series presents four adolescents with treatment-resistant OCD, who received ketamine infusion therapy. Table 1 summarizes the key clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes across all cases, while Table 2 provides detailed treatment history for each patient prior to ketamine trial. Ketamine hydrochloride (0.5 mg/kg) was administered intravenously over 45 min on alternate days for five doses with continuous vital sign monitoring and CADSS assessment. Case 1 A 13-year-old female with 8-month history of sexual obsessions and compulsive searching presented with severe OCD (CY-BOCS = 40). Previous unsuccessful treatments included fluoxetine (80 mg/day, 16 weeks), sertraline (250 mg/day, 14 weeks), and eight sessions of CBT with poor engagement. Current medications were fluvoxamine (300 mg/day) and clomipramine (75 mg/day) for 18 months with minimal improvement. Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) score was six, suggesting no clinical depression. Following first ketamine dose, no symptom improvement occurred. After second dose, CY-BOCS decreased to 24, but improvement lasted only 16 hours. Post-third infusion, she developed tactile hallucinations (insects crawling sensation) with CADSS score of 98, persisting &gt;24 hours, consistent with ketamine-induced psychosis.[ 10 ] Treatment was discontinued, and risperidone initiated. Psychotic symptoms resolved by day six. At 2-month follow-up, CY-BOCS was 32 on original medications. Case 2 A 17-year-old male with 2-year history of contamination obsessions and washing/checking compulsions (CY-BOCS = 32) had failed fluvoxamine (300 mg/day, 16 weeks) and venlafaxine (225 mg/day, 12 weeks). He had completed 12 CBT sessions with moderate engagement but limited improvement. Physical examination revealed asymmetrical rigidity and tremor, absence of KF ring and S. Ceruloplasmin 32 mg/dl, referred for Neurology evaluation and diagnosed with young-onset parkinsonism. No improvement occurred after first ketamine infusion (CADSS = 10). From second infusion, CY-BOCS declined progressively, plateauing at 22 after the fifth session. No parkinsonian feature changes were observed. At 6-month follow-up, CY-BOCS remained stable at 20. Case 3 A 19-year-old male with 4-year history of religious obsessions (CY-BOCS = 39) had failed fluoxetine (80 mg/day, 20 weeks) and venlafaxine (225 mg/day, 16 weeks). He had completed 15 CBT sessions with good initial engagement but limited progress with religious-themed exposures. His baseline HAM-D score was 10, suggesting mild depression. No improvement followed first ketamine infusion (CADSS = 7). After second infusion, CY-BOCS decreased to 25. Progressive improvement continued through fifth session (CY-BOCS = 20), with stability after sixth session. At 2-month follow-up, CY-BOCS improved to 18. Case 4 A 17-year-old male with 3-year history of harm obsessions and mental compulsions (CY-BOCS = 34) had failed fluoxetine (80 mg/day, 14 weeks) and sertraline (200 mg/day, 12 weeks). He had received 10 CBT sessions with fair engagement but difficulty with mental ritual prevention. His HAM-D score was nine, suggesting mild depression. Following pattern of previous cases, meaningful improvement began after second ketamine infusion, with CY-BOCS reaching 20 after fifth session. At 2-month follow-up, CY-BOCS remained stable at 19. DISCUSSION Our case series reveals several important patterns in ketamine response for adolescent treatment-resistant OCD, as illustrated in Table 1 and Figure 1 . Age-dependent response pattern: The youngest patient (13 years) developed psychosis while older adolescents (17–19 years) showed favorable responses without significant adverse effects. This suggests age-related differences in ketamine sensitivity, potentially related to developmental glutamatergic system changes during adolescence. Andersen and Teicher[ 9 ] have demonstrated significant age-dependent changes in N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor density and subunit composition during adolescent neurodevelopment. Temporal response pattern: Minimal improvement occurred after first infusion across all cases, with clinical benefits emerging only after second administration and progressing through fifth session [as shown in Table 1 ]. This contrasts with ketamine’s rapid antidepressant effects but aligns with Bloch et al .’s[ 4 ] observations in adult OCD. This temporal pattern suggests ketamine’s anti-obsessional properties may require repeated administration for optimal therapeutic benefit. Adverse effects: Ketamine-induced psychosis in our youngest patient highlights the need for careful monitoring, particularly in early adolescence when glutamatergic systems undergo critical developmental changes. Ketamine’s psychotomimetic effects result from disinhibition of glutamatergic transmission and subsequent downstream signaling cascades[ 4 , 6 ] that may be particularly sensitive during early adolescent neurodevelopment.[ 10 ] Durability of response: Maintained improvement at 2-month follow-up suggests ketamine may induce neuroplastic changes persisting beyond immediate pharmacological effects, similar to mechanisms proposed for its enduring antidepressant action.[ 2 , 6 ] The molecular basis likely involves ketamine’s effects on α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazole propionic acid (AMPA) receptor trafficking, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, and downstream mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling.[ 2 , 6 ] Several limitations merit consideration: small sample size, concurrent medications [detailed in Table 2 ], variable CBT engagement, lack of standardized assessment for family accommodation, and variable follow-up periods. Future research should include controlled trials with larger age-stratified samples, standardized protocols, neuroimaging correlates, and longer follow-up periods. CONCLUSION Ketamine shows promise as an adjunctive treatment for adolescent treatment-resistant OCD, with effectiveness potentially influenced by age-related neurodevelopmental factors. While our findings suggest therapeutic benefits in older adolescents, the psychotomimetic risk observed in our youngest patient underscores the need for careful patient selection and monitoring. Larger controlled studies are needed to establish efficacy, safety, and optimal treatment protocols for this challenging clinical population. Conflicts of interest There are no conflicts of interest.
