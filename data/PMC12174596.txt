Highlights • Precise neuronavigated rTMS (MNI:40,39,11) targeted right DLPFC improves insomnia. • Significant insomnia &amp; mood symptom reduction vs control. • DLPFC functional connectivity reorganization mediates rTMS clinical benefit. Background Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) offers a promising approach for the treatment of insomnia; however, the precise targets and underlying neural mechanisms remain unclear. This randomized, wait-controlled trial aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy of neuronavigated rTMS targeting the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) in chronic insomnia disorder (CID) and to identify potential neural mechanisms associated with therapeutic outcomes. Methods Fifty patients with CID were randomized to receive 20 sessions of 1 Hz rTMS targeting the right DLPFC or to a waitlist control group. Stimulation coordinates were selected (MNI: 40,39,11) based on our previous neuroimaging meta -analysis, and were precisely localized using MRI-guided neuronavigation. Clinical assessments and resting-state fMRI were conducted before and after the intervention, respectively. Target-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis was used to map rTMS-associated network changes, while causal mediation analysis was used to examine the relationships between neural changes and clinical improvements. Results Compared to waitlist controls, the rTMS group showed greater improvements in insomnia and mood symptoms (all p &lt; 0.001), with higher response rates (54.55 % vs. 9.09 %) and remission rates (68.18 % vs. 13.64 %). FC analysis showed significant group × time effects on the bilateral DLPFC, middle cingulate cortex, and right anterior cerebellar vermis. Mediation analysis indicated that FC changes in the right DLPFC mediated 24 % of the improvement in insomnia severity (Insomnia Severity Index, p = 0.048). Conclusion These preliminary findings suggest that precision neuronavigated rTMS targeting the right DLPFC may alleviate insomnia symptoms, with the observed clinical improvements potentially related to the reorganization of the DLPFC network. While these results are encouraging, further research based on placebo-controlled study designs is required to confirm these effects and better understand the underlying mechanisms. This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the integration of precision targeting with neuroimaging to explore the mechanisms underlying the effects of rTMS in insomnia treatment. Insomnia affects approximately 10 %–20 % of the general population, with approximately half of these cases progressing to a chronic course ( Buysse, 2013 ), known as chronic insomnia disorder (CID). Individuals with CID are at increased risk of emotional and cognitive disturbances, including anxiety, depression, and impaired memory and concentration ( Hertenstein et al., 2018 ). Current treatment options for CID mainly consist of pharmacological approaches, such as benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepine hypnotics, and behavioral interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia ( Riemann et al., 2017 , van der Zweerde et al., 2019 ). However, pharmacological treatments are linked to side effects, dependence, and limited long-term efficacy, whereas cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia, although effective, is time-consuming and may not be accessible to all ( Morin et al., 2009 ). Consequently, there is a need for more effective and accessible treatment options for CID. Noninvasive brain stimulation, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), has recently emerged as a promising treatment for insomnia, demonstrating both safety and efficacy ( Lanza et al., 2023 , Zhou et al., 2024 ). The most commonly used rTMS protocol for insomnia consists of 1 Hz stimulation applied over the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), which reduces insomnia severity ( Krone et al., 2023 ). Traditional studies have often employed cap-based targeting to stimulate the DLPFC; however, this method is associated with inherent variability in target localization and is more susceptible to operator-dependent differences than neuronavigation-based targeting ( Caulfield et al., 2022 ). Conversely, neuronavigated rTMS has shown more consistent results, particularly in the treatment of resistant depression ( Morriss et al., 2024 , Zhao et al., 2024 ). Despite these advancements, the precise mechanisms by which rTMS exerts its therapeutic effects on insomnia remain poorly understood. This study aimed to validate the therapeutic efficacy of a neuronavigated rTMS protocol targeting the right DLPFC for the treatment of CID in a randomized wait-controlled trial. The stimulation coordinates were determined based on previous neuroimaging meta -analytical findings to enhance precise and clinically relevant targeting ( Gong et al., 2020 ). Furthermore, this study sought to elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying rTMS-induced sleep improvement using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data. Finally, mediation analysis was employed to test the hypothesis that rTMS treatment improves sleep quality by altering DLPFC function. 2 Method 2.1 Participants The sample size calculation was based on expected differences in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores, selected a priori as the primary outcome, using a medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.5) derived from previous rTMS studies on insomnia. Response (&gt;50 % PSQI reduction) and remission (PSQI ≤ 7) criteria were predefined based on established clinical benchmarks for insomnia interventions. The sample size required for this study was calculated using G*Power software ( Faul et al., 2009 ). This calculation indicated that a total sample size of 44 participants would provide adequate power to identify group differences in the matched pairs. Finally, 55 patients were recruited. Of these, 50 met the inclusion criteria and were formally enrolled in the study. All candidates underwent comprehensive screening, including a structured clinical interview based on ICSD-3 criteria, medical history review, physical examination, routine blood tests, and MRI screening. The following psychiatric conditions were excluded: current major depressive episode, generalized anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders, and substance use disorders (assessed using MINI International Neuropsychiatric Interview). The inclusion criteria for CID were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for CID set out in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition ( Sateia, 2014 ); (2) a PSQI score greater than 7, (3) age between 18 and 55 years, and (4) no use of sleeping pills or other psychotropic medications within two weeks before enrollment. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of other neuropsychiatric diseases or severe chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, heart disease, or cancer); (2) history of substance addiction (including drugs, nicotine, or alcohol); (3) epilepsy; (4) pregnancy or lactation; (5) contraindications to MRI; and (6) brain lesions or white matter hyperintensities identified using conventional T2-weighted MRI; (7) age &gt;55 years based on the fact that older adults often present with more complex sleep pathologies and comorbidities that could confound the interpretation of the effects of rTMS in primary insomnia. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Chengdu Second People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2022022) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. The study was registered in China Clinical Trials (registration number: ChiCTR2200064508; https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=172816). The study was conducted at Chengdu Second People’s Hospital, with active enrollment starting from July 2022 to October 2022. Following the initial baseline assessments, the participants were randomly assigned to either the rTMS treatment group ( n = 25) or the waitlist group ( n = 25). Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence by an independent researcher who was not involved in the clinical assessments or treatment delivery. Due to the nature of the intervention, the participants were not blinded to their group assignments (active rTMS vs. waitlist). However, the outcome assessors were blinded to the group allocation during all clinical evaluations. Participants in the waitlist control group received no intervention during the 4-week study period, but were offered rTMS treatment after completing the follow-up assessments. The study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1 . 2.2 Clinical assessment The PSQI was used to evaluate subjective sleep quality, the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) was used to assess insomnia ( Backhaus et al., 2002 , Buysse et al., 1989 , Morin et al., 2011 ), and the Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS) was used to measure daytime sleepiness ( Johns, 1991 ). Depression and anxiety levels were evaluated using the Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) ( Zung, 1971 , Zung et al., 1965 ). For all patients with CID, assessments were conducted within three days prior to and following the intervention. 2.3 rTMS procedure Low-frequency rTMS (1 Hz) was delivered to the right DLPFC using a MagPro X100 device (MagVenture, Farum, Denmark) equipped with an active-cooling butterfly shaped coil (Cool-B70 A/P). The stimulation was delivered at 80 % of the resting motor threshold intensity, which was defined as the minimum stimulation level that produced 50 mV motor-evoked potentials in five out of ten consecutive trials ( Groppa et al., 2012 ). Each stimulation time was 3 s, with an interstimulus interval of 1 s, and 1500 pulses were delivered. The selection of stimulation parameters (1 Hz, 80 % resting motor threshold, 1500 pulses/session) was based on prior evidence demonstrating efficacy of low-frequency right DLPFC stimulation in reducing hyperarousal and improving sleep continuity in insomnia ( Lanza et al., 2023 , Shi et al., 2021 ). The participants received 20 treatments on weekdays over 4 consecutive weeks. Treatment integrity was ensured through the daily verification of stimulation parameters by an independent technician and continuous monitoring of coil placement via neuronavigation. All participants underwent the full 20-session protocol as intended, with no reported protocol deviations. The right DLPFC was identified as a potentially effective target for insomnia treatment, based on the findings of previous studies and our neuroimaging meta -analysis ( Gong et al., 2020 , Lanza et al., 2023 , Shi et al., 2021 ). Precision neuronavigation targeting the right DLPFC was achieved using a multi-step protocol. First, high-resolution T1-weighted images were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using DARTEL in SPM12 to generate participant-specific transformations. Second, the stimulation site was restricted to the right DLPFC, defined as a spherical region (6-mm radius) centered at the MNI coordinates (40, 39, 11) based on our meta -analysis ( Gong et al., 2020 ). Third, the Localite TMS Navigator TS system continuously tracked coil position relative to the precision target, maintaining ≤ 3 mm displacement tolerance via visual/auditory feedback. Finally, treatment integrity was ensured through daily verification of stimulation parameters by an independent technician and continuous monitoring to minimize coil drift throughout the 20-session protocol. 2.4 Image acquisition and preprocessing MRI was performed using a GE Healthcare Discovery MR750 3.0-Tesla MRI scanner (Milwaukee, WI, USA). High-resolution structural images were captured using a spoiled gradient-recalled echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) of 7.06/3.04 ms, a flip angle (FA) of 12°, an acquisition matrix at 256 × 256, a field-of-view of 240 × 240 mm, and a slice thickness of 1.0 mm with no gap. The imaging sequence consisted of 192 slices with a single excitation. Functional images were obtained using an eight-minute gradient-recalled echo-planar imaging pulse sequence. The parameters for the functional images were as follows: TR/TE, 2000/30 ms; FA, 90°; acquisition matrix, 64 × 64; slice thickness, 3.5 mm, 33 slices; and 240 time points. During the MRI scanning, the participants were instructed to relax, close their eyes, and avoid falling asleep. The use of soft earplugs and foam pads minimizes scanner noise and head movement. Post-scanning, all participants confirmed that they remained awake throughout the scanning process. The rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using SPM12 and DPABI 8.2, implemented in MATLAB 8.0 (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) ( Yan et al., 2016 ). The first five initial volumes were removed to account for magnetization equilibrium and adaptation to the experimental environment. The remaining 235 images were slice-time-corrected, reoriented, realigned, and co-registered to T1-weighted structural images, which were segmented using DARTEL ( Ashburner, 2007 ). Participants with excessive head motion above 1.5° or 1.5° of angular motion relative to the first volume were excluded from subsequent analyses. There was no significant difference in the mean framewise displacement between the groups. Images from all participants were normalized to the standard stereotactic MNI space and smoothed using a Gaussian kernel (full width at half maximum = 6 mm). Voxel time series were further detrended and temporally filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz). We normalized the variance of each time series to control for fluctuations in the signal intensity. Noise associated with white matter/cerebrospinal fluid signals and 24 head motion-related covariates were regressed. The global signal was not regressed given the controversy regarding its application to rs-fMRI data ( Chai et al., 2012 , Murphy et al., 2009a ). None of the participants were excluded from the fMRI analysis. 2.5 rTMS-target (right DLPFC) based functional connectivity network Stimulated right DLPFC (rTMS target)-based voxel-wise functional connectivity was assessed using DPABI 8.0. The right DLPFC (MNI coordinates: 40, 39, 11) was selected as the seed region to construct the DLPFC functional connectivity (FC) network. The average time course within the right DLPFC, defined as the seed time course, was correlated with the time course in all brain voxels using Pearson’s cross-correlation. A Fisher’s Z-transformation was applied to normalize the correlation coefficients (CC) and improve their distribution to approach a normal distribution [Z = 0.5ln(1 + CC)/(1 − CC)] ( Liu et al., 2017 , Lowe et al., 1998 ). This process generated a map representing the individual patterns within the rTMS target FC network. 2.6 Statistical analysis Statistical analyses of demographic and behavioral data were performed using R statistical software (version 4.4.3). Demographic and baseline behavioral data were compared between the two groups using independent t -tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables (sex). To evaluate the treatment effects, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess group-by-time interactions for clinical outcomes. The primary outcome measure was the change in the patients' sleep quality, which was assessed using the PSQI at the end of treatment. Secondary outcomes included changes in ISI, ESS, SDS, and SAS scores following rTMS treatment. Response rates (≥50 % PSQI reduction from baseline) and remission rates (PSQI ≤ 7) ( He et al., 2024 , Zhou et al., 2024 ) were compared between the two groups using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as p &lt; 0.05. A voxel-wise repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to identify group-by-time interactions for brain function alterations using the DPABI toolbox. Permutation testing with threshold-free cluster enhancement was applied to the threshold results at p &lt; 0.05. Threshold-free cluster enhancement was executed within the permutation analysis of the linear model package in DPABI, utilizing 5000 permutations and a cluster-forming threshold of z &gt; 2.3. Mediation analysis was performed to investigate whether significant changes in the DLPFC FC network resulting from rTMS treatment mediated improvements in insomnia symptoms (n = 22). Changes in the ISI and PSQI scores served as dependent variables, whereas FC changes in significantly altered regions within the DLPFC network before and after rTMS treatment were treated as mediators. Mediation analysis was performed using the “mediation” package in R, ensuring robust modeling of variable relationships ( Yimer et al., 2023 ). We calculated the average direct effects (ADE) and average causal mediation effects (ACME) to quantify the direct and indirect effects of rTMS treatment on insomnia symptoms. The significance of our mediation models was assessed using bootstrapping methods, generating 95 % confidence intervals for the indirect effects with 5000 bootstrap samples ( Luo et al., 2024 ). To evaluate the robustness of the mediation results, sensitivity analysis was conducted using the “medsens” function in the “mediation” package, allowing for the assessment of potential biases or confounding factors affecting the mediation results. 3 Results 3.1 Participant characteristics A total of 50 patients with CID were deemed eligible for the study, including 38 women (76 %) with a mean age of 33.7 years (SD = 10.14). The participants were randomly assigned to either the neuronavigated rTMS group (N = 25) or the waitlist group (N = 25). Of the initial cohort, 22 patients in the rTMS group completed the 4-week treatment and 22 patients completed the follow-up neuropsychological assessments. No significant differences were observed in baseline demographic and clinical variables between the rTMS and waitlist groups ( Table 1 ). The flow of participants throughout the study is shown in Fig. 1 . 3.2 Primary outcome Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant group × time interaction effects across sleep and mood scales ( Table 2 ). For the primary outcome (PSQI change), the rTMS group demonstrated substantially greater improvement compared to the waitlist group (7.46 ± 2.44 vs 1.72 ± 3.57), with significant Group × Time interaction effect ( p &lt; 0.001, η 2 = 0.48), group ( p = 0.01, η 2 = 0.14), and time effects ( p &lt; 0.001, η 2 = 0.70). The PSQI response rate was 54.55 % (n = 12) in the rTMS group and 9.09 % (n = 2) in the waitlist group, with a significant group difference ( p = 0.0028 , odds ratio [OR] = 11.3 (95 % confidence interval [CI]: 2.0–123.1)). The remission rate was 68.18 % (n = 15) in the rTMS group and 13.64 % (n = 3) in the waitlist group, with a significant group difference ( p &lt; 0.0001 , OR = 12.6 (95 % CI: 2.5–89.1)) ( Fig. 2 ). 3.3 Secondary outcomes For the secondary outcomes (ISI, SDS, SAS and ESS change), the rTMS group demonstrated substantially greater improvement compared to the waitlist group (ISI, 8.77 ± 4.72 vs 1.54 ± 4.81; ESS, 3.05 ± 4.68 vs 0.59 ± 3.47; SDS, 8.06 ± 8.08 vs 0.56 ± 7.57; SAS, 6.93 ± 6.47 vs 0.51 ± 6.72). The Group × Time interaction effects of the four scales were significant (ISI, p &lt; 0.001, η 2 = 0.38; ESS, p = 0.006, η 2 = 0.17; SDS, p &lt; 0.001, η 2 = 0.24; SAS, p &lt; 0.001, η 2 = 0.25). The time effects were significant on the SDS and SAS scales (SDS, p = 0.003, η 2 = 0.24; SAS, p = 0.002, η 2 = 0.25). No significant group effects were observed on any of the four scales. ( Table 2 ). 3.4 Effects of rTMS on target-based FC network As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 3 , the voxel-wise-based repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant group × time interaction effects on target-based FC networks in the bilateral DLPFC, bilateral middle cingulate cortex (MCC), and right anterior cerebellar vermis (aCbv). Specifically, in the bilateral DLPFC and MCC, FC increased after rTMS treatment, while FCs decreased in the waitlist group. For the right aCbv, the negative FC between the DLFPC and right aCbv increased after rTMS treatment, while it was reversed in the waitlist group. 3.5 Mediation analysis results In the rTMS group, a negative association was observed between the change in the ISI score and changes in the right DLPFC FC value ( r = −0.67, p = 0.001). Consequently, we explored the mediating role of right DLPFC FC (mediator) in the effect of rTMS treatment (independent variable) on the ISI score (dependent variable) in the rTMS group. The results of the mediation analysis are shown in Fig. 4 and Table 4 . Notably, the indirect effect of rTMS treatment effect on ISI score, mediated by the change in the right DLPFC FC value, was estimated to be β = −1.78 ( p = 0.048), with this pathway accounting for approximately 23 % of the total effect (Proportion Mediated = −0.23). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis revealed that the ACME becomes zero when the ρ value reaches −0.35, suggesting a critical threshold for the relationship between the right DLPFC FC value and the ISI scores, beyond which the mediation effect diminishes. 3.6 Side effects and safety The most common side effects during treatment were transient symptoms, such as drowsiness (3/22) and mild headache (5/22). All side effects were tolerated and gradually resolved after treatment. No serious adverse events were observed. 4 Discussion This study offers new insights into the clinical efficacy and underlying brain mechanisms of a neuronavigated rTMS protocol targeting the right DLPFC for CID. Participants who underwent rTMS exhibited significant improvements in sleep quality, along with reductions in depression and anxiety. Importantly, mediation analysis revealed that rTMS treatment influenced insomnia severity partly through alterations in the functional connectivity of the DLPFC. While our findings demonstrated significant improvements in insomnia symptoms following neuronavigated rTMS compared to waitlist controls, we acknowledge that without a sham control condition, we cannot completely discount the potential influence of placebo effects or participant expectations. This finding suggests that neuronavigated low-frequency rTMS may enhance the function of the right DLPFC, thereby contributing to clinical improvement in insomnia. By leveraging neuroimaging-guided stimulation targets, this study integrated the clinical application of neuronavigated rTMS with its potential brain mechanisms, offering a foundation for more personalized and targeted brain stimulation therapies for insomnia. The neuronavigated rTMS group achieved significantly improved clinical features of insomnia and mood symptoms compared to the control group. These clinical improvements demonstrate the effectiveness of low-frequency rTMS targeting the right DLPFC in reducing the severity of insomnia and mood symptoms in patients with CID, which is consistent with previous reports ( Jiang et al., 2013 , Wu et al., 2021 ). In the present study, the PSQI remission rate was 68.18 %. The high efficiency may be partly ascribed to the precise stimulation of the target in each treatment by using MRI-based navigation, allowing for more consistent and reproducible therapeutic effects ( Sack et al., 2009 , Shi et al., 2021 ). Unlike standardized protocols, the approach used in this study leverages meta -analysis-derived coordinates for precise targeting ( Gong et al., 2020 ), leading to enhanced therapeutic efficacy. These results highlight the specific therapeutic effects of neuronavigated rTMS targeting the right DLPFC in patients with CID. The right DLPFC was selected as the therapeutic target because of its central role in cognitive control and emotional regulation ( Miller and Cohen, 2001 ), both of which are commonly disrupted in insomnia and mood disorders. Neuroimaging studies have consistently shown altered activity and reduced functional connectivity in the DLPFC of patients with CID ( Fasiello et al., 2022 , Li et al., 2018 , O'Byrne et al., 2014 ), implicating its involvement in heightened arousal and impaired top-down regulation of emotional responses ( Fasiello et al., 2022 , Gong et al., 2020 ). Our previous study also found decreased FC between the cognitive control network and the dorsal attention network, along with a decreased functional gradient in the DLPFC in patients with CID ( Chen et al., 2025 , Wu et al., 2024 ). In the present study, neuronavigated rTMS was applied to the right DLPFC, increasing the DLPFC FC network between the bilateral DLPFC, MCC, and aCbv. The MCC serves as a critical neural substrate for affective modulation and motivational monitoring, constituting a principal node within the salience network. Our previous neuroimaging findings revealed increased cortical thickness in the MCC of patients with comorbid CID and CID with depressive symptoms ( Yu et al., 2020 , Yu et al., 2018 ). The enhanced functional connectivity between the DLPFC and MCC after rTMS suggests that neuromodulatory stimulation may potentiate the internetwork synergy between the executive control and salience networks. The aCbv plays a pivotal role in motor coordination, postural control, and balance maintenance ( Cullen, 2023 ). Recent studies revealed its functional involvement in aversive emotional processing and affective regulation ( Flace et al., 2021 , Stanley et al., 2023 ). Furthermore, neuromodulatory stimulation targeting the aCbv has demonstrated potential therapeutic efficacy for the management of chronic psychiatric disorders ( Schutter and van Honk, 2009 ). The increased negative FC between the DLPFC and aCbv may reflect enhanced top-down emotional regulation. These findings suggest that rTMS targeting the right DLPFC not only enhances inhibitory control within the DLPFC network, but also promotes functional integration with other networks, such as the salience and cerebellar networks, thereby improving sleep quality. Mediation analysis provided additional insights into the mechanisms underlying the efficacy of rTMS treatment. Specifically, changes in FC within the right DLPFC network partially mediated the relationship between rTMS treatment and improvements in insomnia symptoms, accounting for approximately 23 % of the total treatment effect on ISI scores. Mediation analysis is particularly important in this context as it quantifies indirect effects and offers causal insights into the neural mechanisms responsible for therapeutic outcomes ( Apostol et al., 2023 , Murphy et al., 2009b ). By identifying changes in FC as mediators, this study established a direct link between rTMS-induced neural reorganization and clinical improvement. Mediation analysis, which is increasingly used in neuropsychiatric research, helps uncover the pathways through which interventions exert their effects, offering a deeper understanding of causal mechanisms ( He et al., 2024 , Zhou et al., 2025 ). The 23 % mediation effect of DLPFC FC changes suggests additional pathways (e.g., default mode network, limbic system, or neurotransmitter systems such as GABA/glutamate pathways) for rTMS treatment ( Apostol et al., 2023 , Lanza et al., 2023 ). Our findings suggest that reorganization of the DLPFC-driven network is central to the therapeutic benefits of rTMS. Collectively, these results highlight the potential of precise neuronavigated rTMS to address the neurobiological underpinnings of CID and improve patient outcomes. Despite these promising findings, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the limited sample size, though sufficient for detecting significant effects, restricts the generalizability of the results to a broader population–particularly in relation to secondary outcomes such as ESS, SDS, and SAS. This raises the possibility of Type II errors. Larger cohorts are needed to confirm effects on mood symptoms. Second, the follow-up duration was relatively short, precluding conclusions regarding the long-term efficacy and durability of treatment effects. Third, this study did not employ objective sleep monitoring methods, such as polysomnography or actigraphy, and future research should validate therapeutic efficacy by combining subjective and objective indicators. Fourth, this study employed a structural MRI-guided TMS approach, and the efficacy differences between various neuronavigation techniques (e.g., fMRI vs. sMRI) require further exploration. Finally, the most notable limitation of this study was the absence of a placebo-controlled condition. While a waitlist control design was used to compare the active rTMS treatment, the lack of a sham (bogus rTMS) condition meant that the potential placebo effects or participant expectancies could not be ruled out as contributors to the observed improvements. It is recommended that a triple-arm (active rTMS vs. sham vs. waitlist) double-blind design be used for future validation. 5 Conclusion These findings establish neuronavigated rTMS in the right DLPFC as a promising therapeutic intervention for CID. The dual benefits of improving sleep quality and mitigating comorbid mood symptoms–likely mediated by functional connectivity reorganization–warrant further validation in sham-controlled trials. These findings provide a foundation for future research on precision rTMS protocols for CID and other neuropsychiatric disorders, thereby advancing the field of precision brain stimulation therapies. 6 Disclosure statement The authors declare that there is no conflict of interest.
