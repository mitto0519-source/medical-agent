Abstract Background Viral hepatitis is the major contributor to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Studies indicated that the co-infection of hepatitis C and hepatitis B virus also prompts liver damage progression. Therefore, in the present study, the prevalence of HCV-HBV co-infection and the impact of HCV-HBV co-infection on the progression of liver damage was evaluated amongst the HCV-infected patients in Pakistan. Methods In this study 2500 HCV-positive patients were recruited from Pakistan. The presence and prevalence of HCV and HBV was confirmed through ELISA and nested PCR. To determine the liver damage due to viral infection levels of ALT, ALP, and total bilirubin were also determined. Diagnostic history of patients was thoroughly documented through serological tests and liver biopsy reports. Viral genotypes and viral loads were determined through multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and time PCR, respectively. Results The study outcomes showed that 12.5% of the HCV-infected patients were co-infected with HBV. Co-infection development was more common in females than in males, and females were at a higher risk of developing the infection ( p-value = &lt; 0.0001, OR = 2.437). Despite the variation among different age groups, there was no significant difference in co-infection prevalence. HCV genotype 3a was found to be most prevalent while in HBV genotype D was found to be prevalent among the patients. The HCV patients frequently developed co-infection with HBV genotype D. It was also determined that viral load for HBV genotype D was higher compared to non-D genotypes while for HCV viral load was higher in non-3a genotypes. Conclusions This study evaluated the prevalence of HCV and HBV co-infection among HCV-positive patients, revealing that 12.5% patients were co-infected with HBV. Co-infection was more common in females, who had a higher risk of developing it. The study also revealed that HBV genotype D was the most prevalent in co-infected patients, with no significant age-related differences in co-infection rates. Background Hepatitis, defined as the inflammation of the liver, contributes to approximately 1.4 million fatalities annually worldwide, predominantly due to its role in promoting liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [ 1 ]. Hepatitis is usually asymptomatic, yet may exhibit symptoms such as loss of appetite, diarrhea, vomiting, nausea, and jaundice in certain instances. Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) is characterized as an enveloped positive-stranded RNA virus, whereas Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) is a DNA virus belonging to the Hepadnaviridae family [ 2 ]. Annually, millions globally contract HCV infection, with a fraction of 15–45% able to clear the infection, while a significant 60–80% fraction progresses towards HCC [ 3 ]. HCV is noted for its significant genetic diversity and is stratified into seven genotypes, with genotype 3 being prevalent in Pakistan [ 4 , 5 ]. Several therapeutic strategies have emerged since HCV’s discovery. The most promising results have emerged from interferon (INF) based therapy, where direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) effectively inhibit viral replication [ 6 ]. HCV treatment is contingent upon the duration of infection and the specific HCV genotype present, typically necessitating a combinatory therapeutic approach, such as pegylated (Peg) interferon and ribavirin [ 6 , 7 ]. Co-infection of HCV with other viruses, such as HIV and HBV, has been reported, however, effective management strategies for these co-infections are largely unestablished. Relapse of HCV infection has been noted to be common in patients co-infected with HBV [ 8 ]. Up till now, several genotypes of HBV have been identified. HBV genotype D have been shown to be associated with severe liver damage and HCC. Studies have shown that genotype D of HBV is most prevalent amongst the Pakistani population with a prevalence rate of 63.7% [ 9 ]. Patients co-infected with HBV/HCV display an increased rate of progression towards liver cirrhosis, fibrosis, and a heightened risk of HCC in contrast to patients with HCV mono-infection [ 10 , 11 ]. Evidence suggests that HCV-HBV co-infection often culminates in a more severe and aggressive liver injury relative to mono-infections [ 10 , 11 ]. In-vitro studies have revealed that HCV and HBV can co-replicate independently within hepatocytes [ 12 , 13 ], and that the magnitude of viral replication in co-infection mirrors that observed in HCV/HBV mono-infections [ 8 , 12 , 13 ]. This absence of viral interaction suggests that the interplay between the viruses is closely tied to host immune responses [ 14 ]. There have been reports regarding the interaction of HCV core protein with HBV replication [ 15 , 16 ]. Furthermore, various studies have also shown that in certain cases, HCV tends to inhibit the replication of HBV via the activation of host RIG-1-Like helicases [ 17 ]. Several reports have revealed that during coinfection, one virus dominates over the other. This happens when there is a reciprocal inhibition of one viral genome by the other virus when both HBV and HCV are simultaneously present in the same cells. The virus that tends to dominate undergoes active replication while suppressing the replication of the non-dominant virus. In certain cases, both viruses tend to replicate equally without masking the replication of each other [ 18 ]. In cases where HBV is initially controlled or suppressed, HCV treatment can lead to the reactivation of HBV. This phenomenon is often observed when HCV treatment (especially interferon-based therapies) can result in the resurgence of HBV replication, leading to HBV dominance. This is more likely in individuals who are HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) positive with low viral load or in immune-suppressed patients [ 19 ]. However, in patients who are immunosuppressed, such as those with HIV co-infection, HBV may take precedence over HCV due to immune modulation. This is particularly the case when HBV genotype D, known for its higher replication efficiency, is present [ 18 ]. To tackle co-infection, it is of utmost importance that the prevalence and impact of co-infection on hepatic health is understood. Therefore, the present study aimed to study the prevalence of HCV/HBV co-infection in the Pakistani population. The outcomes of the study were a significant step forward in enhancing disease knowledge and devising a strategy for its detection. Methods Study approval and sample collection After approval from the Institutional Review Board of Atta-ur-Rahman School of Applied Biosciences (IRB-ASAB), all protocols were carried out by following the guidelines provided by ASAB-IRB. The IRB approval number is IRB No. 01-2019-03/06. A total of 2500 HCV-infected Pakistani patients were recruited in the study. The patients were recruited from various hospitals situated in Rawalpindi and Islamabad and sampling was done consecutively. Blood samples were collected from patients who have HCV infection. Only patients with confirmed HCV and HBV status were recruited in the study. Study power was calculated through Clin calc sample size calculator ( https://clincalc.com/stats/samplesize.aspx ) and study power was determined at 320 samples with power of study considered to be 0.8. Patients diagnosis was confirmed through serological testing done through ELISA. The percentage of DAA naïve patients was 59.9% while that of treated patients was 40.1%, respectively. 33% of patients with HBV/HCV co-infection had cirrhosis. During patient recruitment, we ensured that diagnostic history was thoroughly documented through serological tests and liver biopsy reports. The biopsy data provided detailed information on key indicators of liver disease progression, including parenchymal changes, fibrosis stage, inflammation grade, and steatosis. This information is essential for a comprehensive assessment of liver damage. We collected comprehensive patient history, which included data on lipid profiles, diabetes mellitus (DM) and dyslipidemia, to provide a thorough understanding of the patients’ metabolic health. MASLD and DM were utilized as exclusion criteria in this study to ensure that our findings accurately reflected the impact of HCV-HBV co-infection on liver function. Liver cirrhosis was diagnosed based on clinical criteria, morphological basis using CT-scans or ultrasounds according to standard definitions. Patients who were tested negative for HBV/HCV were excluded from the study. In the study, BMI and waist circumference were measured for all patients as part of the assessment of metabolic factors that may influence liver health. However, patients with BMI ≥ 30 (obesity) or significant abdominal fat (waist circumference &gt; 102 cm for men, &gt; 88 cm for women) were excluded to ensure that metabolic conditions such as obesity did not confound the results of liver damage related specifically to HCV and HBV co-infection. Furthermore, patients with co-infection or triple infections with either HIV, HEV, or HDV were also excluded. Patients with co-morbidities such as non-viral liver cancer, primary cancers from other organs, alcoholic fatty liver, non-alcoholic fatty liver, AIDS, immune deficiency were also excluded. The oral and written consent of the patients was taken before commencing the sampling. Serological tests The levels of ALT, ALP, bilirubin levels were determined to analyze the effect of viral coinfection on the liver. ALT Colorimetric kit method (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was used for quantitative assessment of ALT levels. EDTA (Ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid)-vacationers’ tubes (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA) were used for the collection of blood samples. The optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm. The calculations were performed according to the manual instructions. Total bilirubin was determined by colorimetric assay kit (Bilirubin Assay Kit, Sigma-Aldrich) and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm. Lastly, ALP activity was also measured using colorimetric alkaline phosphatase assay kit and absorbance was measured at 405 nm. ELISA The status of HCV-positive cases was confirmed through an HCV micro ELISA detection kit (J. N = Mitra, New Delhi, India) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These 2500 HCV-positive cases were screened for HBV through the detection of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) using a HEPALISA kit (J. Mitra, New Delhi, India) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nucleic acid isolation HCV RNA was isolated following manufacturer instructions provided by the Qiagen RNA isolation kit (Hilden, Germany). The HBV DNA was isolated following manufacturer instructions provided by the Qiagen DNA isolation kit (Hilden, Germany). The nucleic acid quantification was done using NanoDrop 2000 from Thermofisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Design of primers Primer 1 was used for designing all primers. The sequences of primers are given in Table 1 . HCV and HBV detection Viral RNA was used to reverse-transcribe the 5’ NCR of HCV using Moloney murine leukaemia virus reverse transcriptase (MMLV RTase, Fermentas) in a 20 𝜇L reaction mixture. The resulting cDNA was synthesized by using an ABI Veriti 96-well thermocycler. First round PCR was performed with 5 µL of the synthesized cDNA using C1 AND C2 primers., followed by nested PCR using inner primers C3 and C4. Viral DNA was used to detect HBV DNA. The first round of PCR utilized HBVR 5 and HBVF 6, followed by nested PCR using inner primers HBVF7 and HBVF8. The resulting PCR products were visualized on agarose gel and stored at -20∘ C. HCV and HBV genotyping HCV and HBV were genotyped using multiplex PCR. Briefly, 10 µL of viral RNA was utilized to synthesize cDNA via reverse transcription using 200 U of M-MLV RT at 42 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, the first round PCR employed 5 µL of the synthesized cDNA with P9 as the reverse primer and P10 as the forward primer. The resulting first-round product underwent amplification in the second round as Mix1 and Mix2. Mix 1 contained primers for genotypes 2a, 2b, 3b, 1b and genotypes 1a, 3a, 4, 5a, and 6a in Mix 2. Afterwards, 5 µL of the second round PCR products were subjected to electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel with a 100 bp DNA marker and analyzed under UV light for genotype confirmation based on specific PCR bands. HBV genotyping was done by adding 12µlof Dream Taq Green Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) to the mixture of 2 µl of DNA, 1 µl of each primer, HP1 reverse primer and HP2 forward primer. The resulting first-round product underwent amplification in the second round as Mix1 and Mix2. Mix 1 contained primers for genotypes A, B and C and genotypes D, E and F in Mix 2. Afterwards, 5 µL of the second round PCR products were subjected to electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel with a 100 bp DNA marker and analyzed under UV light for genotype confirmation based on specific PCR bands. HCV and HBV quantification HCV viral load in the plasma of patients was evaluated through absolute real-time PCR quantification. HCV was quantified BY following manufacture instructions provided by HCV Quantification kit (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany) in the iQ5 real-time PCR detection system. HBV viral load in the plasma of patients was evaluated through absolute real-time PCR quantification. HCV was quantified by following the manufacturer instructions provided by using the HBV Quantification kit (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany) in the iQ5 real-time PCR detection system. Statistical analysis The statistical analysis was performed on the data using GraphPad Prism for Windows version 5, SPSS, and Microsoft Excel. Risk factors associated with co-infections were determined by multivariate analysis performed through GraphPad Prism and OR, 95% CI, and P-values were attained for respective risk factors. Logistic regression was performed to determine the association between viral load and viral genotypes. Results Demographics of the study population The study was conducted on 2500 HCV patients out of which 312 were co-infected with HBV infection. The demographics of the study population are summarized in Table 2 . It was revealed that 312 patients out of 2500 developed coinfection with HBV. The study revealed that HCV-HBV co-infection was more common in females than in males and females were at a higher risk of developing the infection, while males are at lower risk of co-infection development as present in the Table 2 . The OR value for females is 2.437 with a significant p-value of &lt; 0.0001. Furthermore, the relation between age and risk of co-infection was also determined and it was found that age was not statistically associated with the risk of the development of HBV-HCV coinfection. Liver function parameters in HCV-HBV co-infected patients The ALT, ALP, and bilirubin levels were taken as indicators of liver impairment. HCV and HBV-infected individuals had higher levels of these serological markers. The ALT levels were significantly higher in co-infection between genotypes HCV 1a and HBV genotype D (Fig. 1 A). Viral co-infection leads to rapid liver damage. The results indicated higher chances of increased liver damage in co-infected individuals. While serum levels of ALP, and bilirubin were also found to be elevated in the co-infected patients with HCV genotype 4 with HBV genotype C, D and HCV 3a and untypable genotype of HBV, respectively (Fig. 1 B and C). Comparison of HCV genotypes and HBV genotype co-infection The study revealed that HCV genotype 3a was the most prevalent genotype in comparison to other genotypes. The frequency distribution was found to be 68.6% for genotype 3a. The frequency of 2a was around 11%, whereas the rest of the genotypes’ frequency was below 10% (Fig. 2 ; Table 3 ). Genotype D was the most prevalent genotype among co-infected patients with a frequency of 88.1% (Fig. 3 ; Table 3 ). The co-infection was highly prevalent between HCV 3a and HBV D genotype and HCV 2a and HBV D genotype. There were very few cases of co-infection with other genotypes. However, HCV 2a and 3a dominated the other genotype cases. The HCV patients mostly developed co-infection with the HBV D genotype. Viral load comparison in HCV and HBV co-infected patients The viral loads of HCV and HBV were also studied, and genotype-specific viral load was calculated. Patients who were co-infected with HCV 2a and 4b had the highest mean viral load. Similarly, patients co-infected with HBV genotypes D and E had the highest mean viral load. The association of HCV and HBV viral load varies according to the genotype. The association of viral genotypes with corresponding viral loads was determined through logistic regression analysis. The most prevalent genotype in the in the co-infected samples were genotype 3a of HCV and genotype D of HBV. The risk of co-infected patients to have high viral load (&gt; 800,000 IU/ml for HCV and &gt; 20,000 IU/ml for HBV) was determined for these genotypes, indicating that genotype D of HBV was associated with high viral load, while genotype 3a of HCV (Table 4 ). Discussion Hepatitis is the inflammation of the liver and around 1.4 million people around the globe die of viral hepatitis which is the leading cause of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [ 20 ]. The co-infection of HCV patients with HBV worsens the situation [ 21 ]. In the present study, the prevalence of co-infection of HCV and HBV was studied in 2500 patients. The present study revealed that the HCV-HBV co-infection was moderately prevalent among the Pakistani population. The study reported that the prevalence of HBV-HCV coinfection among these patients was about 12.5%. This high prevalence rate of co-infection in the studied samples. Previous reports have shown that the rates of HBV-HCV coinfections range from 5 to 20% in various regions of the world. It has been estimated that the prevalence of HBV-HCV coinfection is between 3 and 15% in certain populations in the USA. The prevalence of HBV-HCV coinfection in the Latin American countries have been estimated to range from 2 to 30% [ 21 ]. The data from these studies shows that the prevalence of HBV-HCV coinfection is higher in certain high-risk groups such as IV drug users, sex workers, patients receiving blood transfusion or organ transplantation [ 22 ]. This high rate of coinfection might be due to higher levels of viral transmission adaptation into the population. However, it was revealed that HCV-HBV co-infection was more common in females than in males and females were at a higher risk of developing the infection (OR = 2.437; P-value = &lt; 0.0001). This was probably due to the lack of awareness and illiteracy among the female population of the country. Females are biologically more susceptible to viral infections due to hormonal influences and differences in immune response. Socio-cultural factors such as limited access to healthcare and lower health literacy among women in Pakistan may exacerbate this vulnerability. However, previous studies have shown that females are at a higher risk of developing a viral infection. That is probably due to the gender-based immunity difference among males and females [ 23 ]. Despite females being vulnerable to disease development, the rate of viral clearance specifically of HCV and HBV is higher among females [ 24 , 25 ]. Sex hormone estrogen is known to play an important role in the spontaneous clearance of HCV in females due to its immune stimulating effect, while the androgens are of immune-suppressing nature. The higher prevalence of HCV in females can be attributed to the fact that females are more exposed to blood, blood based products, and syringes during ear piercing, pregnancy, and labor thus having higher risk of infection [ 26 , 27 ]. The impact of age on the prevalence of HCV-HBV co-infection was also studied. However, no significant difference in the prevalence of HCV-HBV co-infection was found but, the patients belonging to the age group 20–40 years had higher chances of developing co-infection. Whereas the older age group 50–80 years had a lower risk for infection. Younger and older populations might have a lower prevalence of co-infection due to varying levels of exposure and immune response efficiency. The middle-aged group (20–40 years) is often more active socially and sexually, leading to higher exposure risks. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of viral infection among different age groups is similar to our results [ 28 ]. The study revealed that HCV serotype 3a and 2a infection were prevalent in comparison to other genotypes. In the case of HBV infection, the genotype D was prevalent. In Pakistan HBV genotype D is most prevalent among other HBV genotypes with a percentage of 63.71% [ 9 ]. Previous studies have reported that the risk of development of HCC is more frequent in individuals who are infected with HBV D genotype [ 29 , 30 ]. In our study, HCV genotype 3a and HBV genotype D were the most prevalent in co-infection. This finding is important as it can have various implications regarding the epidemiology, patient treatment response and prognosis. Various reports have established the link between HBV genotype D and liver cancer. Co-infection of HCV with HBV genotype-D can lead to increased rates of liver cirrhosis and HCC. However, the current data regarding the specific molecular mechanisms involved in HCV and HBV genotype D coinfection is poorly understood and further studies are required to attain a clear insight into these complex biological processes. Previous studies have revealed that HCV-HBV co-infected patients show increased progression of liver cirrhosis, fibrosis, and increased risk of HCC in comparison to HCV mono-infected patients [ 31 ]. The comparison of ALT levels among different genotypes was also investigated which showed higher ALT levels in the patients co-infected with HCV genotype 1a indicating its fatal effects. Elevated ALT levels in co-infected patients, particularly those with HCV genotype 4, suggest that specific genotypes have more aggressive pathogenicity, leading to severe liver damage. ALT is a key enzyme released during liver inflammation and damage, serving as a marker for liver toxicity. Patients infected with HCV genotype 1a have been shown to have elevated levels of ALT compared to other HCV genotypes [ 32 ]. Various other studies have also highlighted an increase in the levels of liver enzymes in patients with HCV-HBV co-infection compared to mono-infection with HCV [ 33 , 34 ]. This study also showed that ALP levels were higher in patients infected with HCV 4a and C, D genotypes of HBV. This finding is consistent with another study indicating that ALP levels in the patients infected with genotype 4a were elevated compared to other genotypes [ 35 ]. Elevated level of ALP is marker of liver damage. Studies have shown that genotype 4a is associated with high viral load and ultimate elevated levels of serum ALP depicting liver damage [ 36 ]. Furthermore, the levels of bilirubin were found to be elevated in HCV 3a genotype. Previous study has shown that genotype 3 of HCV was associated with high levels of bilirubin and PT-INR compared to genotype 1 and 2. HCV genotype 3 is associated with severe hepatic disease and elevated levels of bilirubin indicate severe liver disease or cirrhosis [ 37 ]. This study has effectively demonstrated the impact of different viral genotypes on liver damage which can lead to the effective management of disease for specific genotype infection. The investigation of HCV and HBV viral load revealed the dominance of HBV genotype D over the replication of HCV genotypes in patients co-infected with HBV-HCV. The viral load differences observed in the study suggest that certain genotypes, such as HBV genotype D, possess a higher replicative capacity, which can overshadow HCV replication. This dominance might result from interactions between viral proteins and host cellular mechanisms that favour one virus over the other. The high prevalence of HCV genotype 3a and HBV genotype D could be due to their higher transmissibility and adaptation within the host population. HBV genotype D’s dominance over HCV genotypes might be driven by its higher replication efficiency and competitive advantage in co-infected cells. HBV genotype B predominance is also reported to be masked by HDV genotype 1 in duel infection of HBV-HDV in Vietnamese patients [ 38 ]. The replicative dominance of one virus genotype over another is presumed to be greatly influenced by ethnic factors [ 39 , 40 ]. However, the exact underlying mechanism behind the dominance of one virus genotype over the other is not clearly understood so far but the higher viral load of one virus in duel infections might have an impact on disease severity as well as treatment outcomes. Conclusions Viral co-infection has a grave impact on liver health. Hence, it is essential to determine the prevalence of co-infection in order to tackle it. In this study the co-infection prevalence of HCV and HBV was investigated. The findings revealed that HCV-HBV co-infection was significantly more prevalent in females than males highlighting a higher susceptibility among females. Patients with co-infection had significantly higher HCV and HBV viral loads compared to those with HCV alone, suggesting increased disease severity. Age was not statistically associated with the risk of co-infection. HCV and HBV genotypes 3a and D, respectively were the most prevalent in Pakistani population. The outcomes of the present study provided a broader picture of the occurrence of HCV-HBV co-infection. Based on this study, future investigations must be directed towards studying the mechanism of co-infection and their impact on hepatitis prognosis. Further research can also facilitate in identifying a diagnostic strategy for HCV-HBV infections. Electronic supplementary material Below is the link to the electronic supplementary material.
