ABSTRACT This study investigated the perception of spiciness in kimchi among individuals classified as either the spicy food‐tolerant group (ST) or the spicy food‐sensitive group (SS). Four types of commercially available kimchi with varying levels of capsaicin content were analyzed. Participants were segmented into the ST and SS groups using hierarchical cluster analysis based on their spiciness perception of ramen samples with different capsaicin intensities. Sensory evaluations of kimchi included assessments of appearance, color, crispness, spiciness, saltiness, and umami using a nine‐point hedonic scale and a five‐point just‐about‐right (JAR) scale. Notably, spiciness exhibited significant differences in both liking and JAR ratings ( p &lt; 0.001). A direct comparison of spiciness liking for K4 showed that SS participants had significantly lower preference compared to ST participants. Open‐ended responses revealed that 34.1% of ST participants found K4's spiciness “just about right,” whereas 83% of SS participants found it too strong. The ST group exhibited higher spiciness tolerance and preference, particularly for strongly spiced kimchi, whereas the SS group preferred milder kimchi and rated the spiciest samples poorly. These findings underscore the importance of individual and cultural factors in sensory evaluation and provide insights for developing kimchi products tailored to diverse consumer preferences. Practical Applications The findings of this study reveal that individual differences in spiciness perception and tolerance significantly influence kimchi preferences. This information can guide the development of customized kimchi products catering to varying consumer needs and global markets, thereby enhancing product acceptance and marketability. The results indicated no significant differences in age, gender, education, or kimchi consumption frequency (p &gt; 0.05). However, significant group differences were found in spicy food preference and the frequency of spicy food consumption in delivery meals (p &lt; 0.05) (Table 2 ). This result suggests that the responses in the consumer acceptance testing were not influenced by social demographics. A clear difference between the two groups was observed regarding the proportion of spicy food in delivered meals (Table 2 ). In the response stating that the proportion of spicy food is less than 5%, the SS showed a significantly higher percentage (59.3%) compared to the ST (21.2%) (p &lt; 0.05). Similarly, for the response indicating that the proportion of spicy food exceeds 30%, the ST had a proportion of 28.8%, while the SS reported 0% (p &lt; 0.05). Regarding the preference for spicy food (nine‐point scale), the ST had a score of 6.8, while the SS scored 5.4. This indicates that the group with a higher tolerance for spicy food showed a significantly greater preference for spicy food (p &lt; 0.05). Notably, the number of participants in the ST group (N = 52) was higher than in the SS group (N = 27), reflecting the dietary culture of Koreans, who often seek spicy food. Human perception of spiciness varies significantly depending on ethnicity and dietary habits (Törnwall et al. 2012 ). Previous studies have highlighted how cultural factors influence taste perception and food preferences (Prescott 1998 ; Trachootham et al. 2018 ; Kim et al. 2020 ). Korean food culture traditionally incorporates various spicy seasonings, such as green onions, garlic, ginger, mustard, and red pepper powder (Cho 2011 ), making strong spiciness a commonly accepted characteristic in everyday meals. Additionally, a prior study reported that consumer preferences for strong spiciness increase with frequent use of hot sauces and chili‐based seasonings (Lee et al. 2012 ). 3.3 Consumer Acceptability of Four kimchi Samples With Different Spiciness Intensities Consumer acceptance testing results are presented in Tables 3a and 3b . Two‐way ANOVA was conducted, and F ‐values and p ‐values for the effects of sample, eating status, and the interaction between sample and eating status on consumer acceptability are shown in Table 4 . Significant interactions were observed between sample and eating status for spiciness liking, indicating that spiciness liking for kimchi samples differed according to eating status (p = 0.001). Significant differences were found for color, overall liking, spiciness, and saltiness across samples, while color, spiciness, and saltiness also showed significant differences in JAR ratings. Notably, spiciness exhibited significant differences in both liking and JAR ratings ( p &lt; 0.001). Eating status also significantly influenced JAR ratings of spiciness and umami, suggesting that spiciness tolerance affected perceived spiciness. According to Table 3a , there was no significant difference in appearance liking for ST participants across samples. However, for color liking, K4 had the highest score (6.8 on a nine‐point scale), while K2 had the lowest score (5.6, p &lt; 0.001). Previous research by Chon et al. ( 2021b ) suggested that the appearance characteristics of cabbage kimchi, including color, influence perceived spiciness and color preference. In contrast, SS participants showed no significant differences in appearance or color liking across samples. A direct comparison of color liking between ST and SS (Figure 2A ) revealed no significant difference, although SS's color liking was lower for K4. For overall liking, both groups rated K4 significantly lower than other samples (Table 3a ). A direct comparison of overall liking for K4 (Figure 2B ) showed no significant difference between groups; however, ST participants gave a higher score (4.3) compared to SS participants (3.4). Open‐ended responses indicated that 58.5% of ST and 83.0% of SS participants disliked K4's spiciness, describing it as too strong. Overall liking for K4, the spiciest sample, was lowest among SS participants, reflecting their lower tolerance for spicy foods. For saltiness liking, ST participants rated K1 highest (5.7) and K2 lowest (4.9). Similarly, SS participants rated K1 highest (6.1) and K4 lowest (4.8). Regarding umami liking, no significant differences were observed among samples for ST participants, while SS participants rated K4's umami liking significantly lower (4.8, p &lt;0.05). In terms of spiciness liking, ST participants rated K1 and K3 significantly higher (5.5 and 5.6, respectively, p &lt;0.01), while K2 and K4 received lower ratings (4.3 and 4.5). In the SS group, K4 received the lowest spiciness liking score (3.2, p &lt;0.001), significantly lower than K1 and K3 (both 6.1). A direct comparison of spiciness liking for K4 (Figure 2C ) showed that SS participants had significantly lower preference compared to ST participants. Open‐ended responses revealed that 34.1% of ST participants found K4's spiciness “just about right,” whereas 83% of SS participants found it too strong. Additionally, 39% of ST participants considered K2's spiciness too weak, corresponding to its lower spiciness liking score (4.3). These findings align with previous studies suggesting that regular chili eaters experience less intense chili burn compared to non‐eaters, and oral burn is more distracting for non‐eaters (Cliff and Heymann 1993 ; Prescott and Stevenson 1995 ; Reinbach et al. 2007 ). Similarly, Thai individuals with a high preference for spicy foods demonstrated lower taste sensitivity and perception compared to Japanese individuals with milder preferences (Trachootham et al. 2018 ). Human perception of oral burn is influenced by genetic differences and prior exposure to spicy foods (Karrer and Bartoshuk 1995 ), supporting the importance of spicy food experience in shaping perceived spiciness. 3.4 JAR Analysis Results of Four Kimchi Samples With Different Spiciness Intensities JAR analysis results for the ST and SS groups are summarized in Table 5 , which compares the groups' perceptions for each kimchi sample. For color, none of the SS participants perceived the color of K1 as strong, whereas 7.7% of ST participants perceived it as strong. Conversely, for K3, 3.7% of SS participants perceived its color as strong compared to 0% in the ST group, indicating a significantly higher perception of color intensity among SS participants (p&lt;0.05). Although the two groups had similar perceptions of K4's color intensity (Table 5 ), ST participants exhibited a higher liking score, suggesting that those who tolerate spiciness well have a greater preference for strong red colors (Figure 2A ). Regarding K3's spiciness, 51.9% of ST participants perceived it as weak, compared to 25.9% of SS participants, indicating that ST participants significantly perceived spiciness as weaker. Additionally, 25.9% of SS participants considered K2's spiciness as “just‐about‐right,” compared to only 9.6% of ST participants. This aligns with a prior study indicating that individuals accustomed to eating spicy foods perceive the same level of spiciness as less intense (Trachootham et al. 2018 ). For K4's spiciness, all SS participants found it to be strong. For umami JAR ratings, 7.7% of ST participants and 29.6% of SS participants found K2's umami intensity to be strong, indicating that SS participants perceived umami intensity as significantly stronger (p &lt; 0.05). Overall, the JAR analysis revealed significant effects of kimchi sample type on color, spiciness, and saltiness (p &lt; 0.001; see Table 5 ). Eating status significantly affected spiciness and umami perceptions, though the interaction between kimchi sample type and eating status did not significantly influence JAR evaluations. 4 Discussion This study revealed clear differences in spiciness perception and preference between participants with different levels of tolerance for spicy foods. The spicy‐tolerant group (ST) showed significantly higher liking for the spiciest kimchi (K4), while the sensitive group (SS) rated it lower (p &lt; 0.05), frequently describing it as “too strong.” This suggests that familiarity with spicy foods influences both sensory perception and hedonic response. One possible explanation is sensory adaptation to capsaicinoids. The ST group, which consumed spicy foods more frequently, may have developed desensitization to capsaicin. This aligns with previous findings that regular capsaicin exposure reduces oral burn and increases acceptance (Prescott and Stevenson 1995 ; Reinbach et al. 2007 ; Nolden and Hayes, 2017 ; Lyu et al. 2021 ). These findings are further supported by studies showing that chili eaters experience milder oral burn than non‐eaters and that spiciness is more distracting to those with less exposure (Cliff and Heymann 1993 ; Trachootham et al. 2018 ; Karrer and Bartoshuk 1995 ). Additionally, the strong red color of K4 may have enhanced its appeal among ST participants through a learned association between color and flavor intensity. Such sensory expectations can influence both visual and taste perception. Although demographic variables were similar between groups, their eating habits differed greatly. Most ST participants reported consuming spicy foods in over 30% of their meals, while SS participants reported less than 5%. This supports the idea that habitual exposure plays a key role in shaping spiciness perception and preference (Byrnes and Hayes 2013 ). Cultural factors may also reinforce these differences. ST participants’ preferences reflect typical Korean dietary patterns, which frequently include spicy foods, as noted in cross‐cultural studies (Lee et al. 2012 ). Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of individual and cultural eating habits in shaping consumer responses to spicy foods. Understanding these factors is essential for guiding product development, especially for products like kimchi, where spiciness is a defining sensory attribute. 5 Conclusions This study was conducted to investigate the differences in perceived spiciness of kimchi according to the degree of eating spicy food. Participants were divided into a group that ate spicy food well (ST) and a group that did not (SS) and evaluated the spiciness intensity and overall preference of each kimchi sample. The results of the study showed that the ST group tended to evaluate the spiciness of kimchi higher and preferred spicier kimchi, while the SS group preferred less spicy kimchi. Individual eating habits, particularly the frequency of spicy food consumption, appeared to influence participants’ spiciness preference and perception. Participants who regularly consumed spicy foods tended to exhibit greater liking for spicy kimchi and reported lower sensitivity to its spiciness. It was observed that a majority of the ST regularly consumed spicy foods more frequently. The limitations of this study include the focus on cabbage kimchi and a limited number of samples representing various degrees of spiciness. Future studies should expand the scope to include different types of kimchi, such as radish kimchi and cucumber kimchi, and a wider range of capsaicinoid contents to better understand consumer spiciness preferences across a broader spectrum of kimchi varieties.
