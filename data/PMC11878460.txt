INTRODUCTION Schizophrenia is a heterogeneous disorder that usually has an onset in the second decade of life and often runs a chronic course. Traditionally, schizophrenia is characterized by positive and negative symptoms. However, over the years, the understanding of the range of symptoms of schizophrenia has improved. It is now understood that a person with schizophrenia may present with positive symptoms, negative symptoms, disorganized speech, disorganized behavior, catatonic symptoms, depressive symptoms, manic symptoms, and cognitive symptoms. Among all these symptoms, cognitive impairment, although it has been reported to be associated with marked disability, has not been formally included in the current nosology despite initial consideration (ICD-11; DSM-5). As cognitive impairments are nonspecific and overlap with bipolar disorder, these were not included in the nosology.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] In the contemporary literature, cognitive disturbances are considered to be the central feature of schizophrenia, which is seen in more than 80% of the patients and is considered to be the main contributor to functional disability, indirect cost, unemployment, and inability to live independently.[ 4 , 5 ] There is a consensus that cognitive impairments in schizophrenia are often present before the emergence of psychotic symptoms, are present during the acute psychotic episode, and also persist even after remission of the psychotic.[ 4 ] Considering the importance of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia, it is important to have a clear understanding about its assessment and management. Accordingly, this guideline attempts to discuss the extent of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia, associated factors, and management of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia. This guideline provides a broad framework for assessment and management of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia. These guidelines can be modified for implementation as per the need and feasibility. Process of development of these guidelines For development of these guidelines, a thorough search of different search engines (PUBMED, Google scholar, Science Direct and Scopus) was done to search for the relevant peer-reviewed literature by using the combination of key words Schizophr*, Cognit*, Social Cognition (SC), Cognitive remediation (CR), course of cognition, antipsychotic medications, and pharmacological management. A search for available guidelines, recommendations, and consensus papers was also done. Separate searches were also done to identify assessment tools and interventions developed and validated/tested in India. While considering the literature, we focused primarily on systematic reviews and meta-analysis. Whenever meta-analysis and systematic reviews were available, these were given more weightage and were included. If no systematic review and meta-analysis were available, we considered randomized controlled trials or cross-sectional studies. However, for Indian data, open-label studies were also considered. Information published only in the form of abstract was not included. Cognitive domains affected by schizophrenia In terms of cognitive impairment, the existing literature suggests that schizophrenia patients show impairment in general intelligence along with deficits in other cognitive domains. The variation in the level of impairment is 1.5 to 2 standard deviations below in patients with schizophrenia as compared to healthy controls.[ 4 , 5 ] Initial studies varied in the assessment instruments for the assessment of cognitive domains, due to which it was not easy to reach a consensus. At the beginning of the 21 st century, a review of factor analyses studies evaluating the cognitive impairment in patients with schizophrenia identified seven cognitive domains, namely, attention/vigilance, processing speed, working memory, verbal learning and memory, visual learning and memory, reasoning, and verbal comprehension. However, verbal comprehension was considered resistant to change and hence was dropped. During this time, there was increasing interest in SC, and it was also considered to be significantly impaired in schizophrenia patients (Nuechterlein et al ., 2004) [ Figure 1 and Table 1 ].[ 6 ] Based on this review, the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) designed the Measurement and Treatment to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS) test battery for assessment of cognitive functions in patients with schizophrenia.[ 6 ] Later, a confirmatory factor analysis study suggested that these seven domains could be further reduced to three domains: attention/working memory, processing speed, and learning. The most robust predictors of these three factors were symbol coding, spatial span, and visual learning, with symbol coding emerging as the single best predictor of overall cognitive performance.[ 7 ] The most affected cognitive domain in patients with schizophrenia that correlates with general cognitive performance is processing speed. SC is not a single domain but comprises five domains, that is, theory of mind, emotional processing, social perception, social knowledge, and attribution style [ Table 1 ]. A recent meta-analysis suggests that compared to the healthy controls, patients with schizophrenia perform poorly in all the SC domains.[ 8 ] The course of cognitive functioning in schizophrenia patients The course of schizophrenia can be divided into premorbid functioning, prodrome, first episode, and chronic course. In general, there is some consensus that the majority of the persons who ultimately develop schizophrenia have neurocognitive (NC) and SC impairment starting from childhood, the severity of which, especially nonverbal deficits, increases during adolescence. This increase in deficits during adolescence is considered to be a more reliable predictor of the subsequent development of schizophrenia.[ 4 , 5 ] From a clinical perspective, evidence suggests that when assessed at the age of 16 years, compared to those who do not manifest schizophrenia, those who go on to manifest features of schizophrenia have significantly poorer academic performance and poorer performance in mathematics several years before the onset of schizophrenia.[ 10 ] Data also suggest that persons with schizophrenia are less likely to enter higher education.[ 10 ] There is some evidence to suggest that possibly there is some further worsening in the cognitive functioning in the prodromal phase among those at high risk of developing psychosis. A meta-analysis showed that among the clinically high-risk groups, compared to those who do not go on to develop psychosis, those who go on to develop psychosis have a worsening of processing speed from their baseline performance.[ 11 ] Furthermore, available evidence suggests that among the clinically high-risk group, those who finally go on to develop psychosis have much more severe cognitive impairment.[ 12 ] Compared to healthy controls, the clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR-P) group shows poorer performance in the domains of executive function, attention, processing speed, verbal fluency and visual, verbal and working memory, and general intelligence.[ 12 ] Compared to healthy controls, drug naïve patients with first episode schizophrenia (FES) also have cognitive deficits in the domains of verbal memory, processing speed, and working memory.[ 13 ] There is a lack of consensus on the course of cognitive symptoms after the psychotic breakdown due to methodological issues related to the available data. Accordingly, available evidence supports further decline and also improvement in cognitive functioning.[ 4 , 5 ] Studies that have evaluated the course of SC in patients with schizophrenia, especially during the first 5 years of illness, suggest that these are relatively stable.[ 14 ] To summarize, compared to healthy controls, patients with schizophrenia perform poorly on various NC domains since childhood, which becomes much more prominent during the adolescence, with further decline during the prodromal phase and peaks at the first psychotic breakdown. After recovery from the first episode, the cognitive impairment improves slightly and the patient then continues to have cognitive deficits that either are stable or show further decline over the years. Factors influencing neurocognitive impairment Schizophrenia as a disorder has multifactorial etiology, and the same is true for cognitive impairment as a symptom. Genetic factors, antenatal (obstetric complications, influenza infection, preterm birth), natal, and postnatal factors are considered to increase the risk of cognitive impairment in childhood in patients with schizophrenia. In terms of genetic factors, the cognitive impairment is considered to be polygenic. Early childhood environmental factors such as childhood trauma and socioeconomic deprivation have also been linked to cognitive impairment in schizophrenia patients.[ 5 ] The clinical factors that are reported to influence cognitive impairment in different meta-analyses include age of onset,[ 15 ] chronic smoking,[ 16 ] metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.[ 17 , 18 ] The evidence of association of cognitive impairment and cannabis use in schizophrenia is contradictory and inconclusive[ 5 ] [ Table 2 ]. Factor analysis studies of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) suggest that among the different symptom domains of schizophrenia, only the disorganization domain is significantly associated with cognitive impairment.[ 19 ] Some of the studies have also linked level of cognitive impairment with level of physical activity. One study showed that compared to patients who were less physically active, patients who indulged in light physical activity had better cognitive performance in the domains of attention/concentration and processing speed.[ 20 ] A recent meta-analysis concluded that duration of untreated psychosis does not have significant impact on the NC deficits.[ 22 ] There is lack of meta-analysis evaluating the factors influencing SC. Consequences of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia NC impairment in schizophrenia is associated with multiple negative consequences[ 5 ] [ Figure 2 ]. SC impairment has also been linked to the level of community functioning, with SC having a larger impact than the NC impairment.[ 23 ] Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that SC mediates the relationship of NC deficits and functional outcomes.[ 24 ] Impact of antipsychotic medications on cognition As per the available evidence, there is a differential effect of various antipsychotic medications on cognition. Different meta-analyses and network meta-analyses have attempted to synthesize the available evidence in the past 1 decade or so [ Table 3 ]. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials that evaluated the impact of second-generation antipsychotic medications (SGAMs) on cognition that included 37 studies involving 3576 participants reported that sertindole was better than clozapine, quetiapine, and first-generation antipsychotic medications (FGAMs) on the composite cognitive score. On verbal working memory, ziprasidone performed better than clozapine, olanzapine, and quetiapine. The FGAMs performed poorly than risperidone on the verbal working memory. In terms of executive functioning, clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, ziprasidone, and FGAMs performed worse than sertindole. When the effect was seen on the processing speed, FGAMs performed worse than sertindole and quetiapine. In terms of long-term verbal working memory, olanzapine performed better than clozapine, whereas on verbal fluency, FGAMs performed worse than olanzapine and clozapine. On visuospatial abilities, olanzapine performed better than the FGAMs and clozapine.[ 25 ] Another meta-analysis compared the effect of two classes of SGAMs (i.e. -pines and -dones) on cognition in schizophrenia and reported that the -pines (olanzapine, clozapine, quetiapine) improve attention/working memory, processing speed, executive functioning, fluency, nonverbal memory, and verbal memory. In terms of -dones (risperidone and ziprasidone), improvement was noted in attention/working memory, executive functioning, motor function, nonverbal memory, processing speed, and verbal memory. However, the effect sizes for both the groups of medications were small (effect sizes for dones = 0.254; pines = 0.202). Both the groups of medications did not have any significant effect on language, motor functioning, or visuospatial skill. In the studies that compared the -pines and -dones, the majority (four studies) did not find any difference between the two groups of antipsychotic medications; two studies suggested pines to be better than -dones, and one study found -dones led to more cognitive impairment than -pines.[ 26 ] A meta-analysis that compared the effect of oral and long-acting injectables on various outcomes in patients with schizophrenia and evaluated the impact on cognitive functions as a secondary outcome reported that there is no difference between the two groups, with 16 out of the 19 studies reporting lack of difference between the two groups, two reporting long-acting formulations to be better than oral agents in terms of cognitive outcome, and only one study considering oral agents to be better than the long-acting injectables.[ 27 ] A network meta-analysis of 54 trials involving 5866 patients evaluated the effect of various antipsychotic medications on cognition. The meta-analysis included FGAMs (chlorpromazine, haloperidol, fluphenazine, perphenazine) and SGAMs (amisulpride, clozapine, lurasidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, remoxipride, risperidone, sertindole, and ziprasidone) and showed differential effects of various antipsychotics on various domains [ Table 3 ]. Overall, amisulpride, lurasidone, and sertindole were shown to have a positive impact on cognitive function and occupied the first-ranking positions in multiple cognitive domains. Olanzapine, on the other hand, improves the greatest number of cognitive domains, including the composite score. On the other hand, placebo was found to be better than remoxipride, clozapine, and haloperidol on most of the cognitive domains and the composite score.[ 28 ] Another meta-analysis suggests that compared to patients on SGAMs, patients on haloperidol perform poorly in the domains of cognitive composite (mean difference: -0.13; 95% CI: -0.33 to -0.03), processing speed (mean difference: -0.17; 95% CI: -0.28 to -0.07), attention (mean difference: -0.14; 95% CI: -0.26 to -0.02), motor performance (mean difference: -0.17; 95% CI: -0.31 to -0.03), memory and verbal learning (mean difference: -0.21; 95% CI: -0.35 to -0.08), and executive function (mean difference: -0.27; 95% CI: -0.43 to -0.11). However, no significant difference was noted in the domains of working memory, visual learning, visuoconstruction, and SC.[ 29 ] Emerging preliminary evidence from different studies has reported beneficial effects on third-generation antipsychotic medications (i.e., Cariprazine and Lumateperone) on some of the domains of cognition.[ 30 , 31 ] However, it is crucial to remember that the data are not robust enough to make any conclusions. Recently, the European Psychiatric Association evaluated the available evidence for treatment for cognitive impairment in schizophrenia and made three important recommendations with respect to antipsychotic medications. First, compared to the FGAMs, the SGAMs have a favorable cognitive profile. Second, patients with cognitive impairment currently receiving FGAMs should be switched to SGAMs. Third, no one SGAM is superior to others in terms of beneficial effects on cognition.[ 32 ] In the Indian context, atypical antipsychotic medications are the most commonly prescribed antipsychotic medications in schizophrenia patients and FGAMs form very small proportion (8–15%) of all the prescriptions, as either monotherapy or part of polypharmacy.[ 33 , 34 ] Accordingly, the need to change from FGAMs to SGAMs may be applicable to a very small proportion of patients in the Indian context. Effect of other agents on cognition in patients with schizophrenia Many other medications have been evaluated for their impact on the cognition in patients with schizophrenia [ Table 4 ]. The evidence for antidementia drugs plus antipsychotic medications in patients with schizophrenia was evaluated by a meta-analysis that included data from seven double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trials with a sample size of 225 participants. The results showed that the overall effect of adding antidementia was superior to the combination of antipsychotic medications and the placebo in terms of cognition as measured by using minimental status examination scores (standardized mean difference = −0.79, 95% CI = −1.23 to − 0.34, P = .0006).[ 35 ] A systematic review that evaluated the impact of psychotropics on SC showed that benzodiazepines (i.e., diazepam) have a negative impact on the emotional recognition among the healthy volunteers.[ 36 ] Available data from large-sample-size cross-sectional studies suggest that anticholinergic agents and long-term use benzodiazepines have been demonstrated to adversely affect cognitive functioning in individuals with schizophrenia.[ 32 , 37 , 38 , 39 ] With respect to antidepressants, again available data suggest lack of procognitive effects or minimal procognitive effects.[ 40 , 41 , 42 ] One meta-analysis that included 17 studies and data of 466 patients evaluated the effect of intranasal oxytocin on SC. The meta-analysis found a small significant effect on theory of mind (ToM) but no significant effect of intranasal oxytocin on emotion recognition and empathy.[ 43 ] A meta-analysis that evaluated the effect of anti-inflammatory agents (i.e. aspirin, davunetide, estrogen, erythropoietin, selective estrogen receptor modulators, omega-3, minocycline, or pregnenolone) reported that augmentation with minocycline and pregnenolone is associated with significant beneficial effects of some of the domains of cognition.[ 44 ] There is preliminary evidence from a review that included five randomized controlled trials, and one open-label study to suggest the beneficial effect of modafinil on cognition (short-term memory, attention, and the ability to shift mental sets).[ 46 ] Available evidence does not support the role of cannabidiol,[ 45 ] glutamate modulators,[ 47 ] and varenicline.[ 48 ] Psychosocial interventions for management of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia Different interventions have been evaluated to address cognitive impairment in schizophrenia patients. These include CR, exercise, and lifestyle interventions. Among these, CR is the most commonly used intervention to improve the cognitive functioning in schizophrenia patients. Cognitive remediation: The Cognitive Remediation Expert Working group[ 49 ] defines CR as “a behavioral training intervention targeting cognitive deficit (attention, memory, executive function, SC, or metacognition), using scientific principles of learning, with the ultimate goal of improving functional outcomes. Its effectiveness is enhanced when provided in a context (formal or informal) that provides support and opportunity for extending to everyday functioning”. [ 49 ] Different interventions are reported to fulfil the definition of CR, and these are categorized as individual- or group-based, pencil-and-paper or computerized, bottom-up or top-down, and different combinations of these elements. The studies that have evaluated the beneficial effect of CR have varied in terms of frequency and structure of the sessions and the duration of the intervention. Numerous meta-analyses have assessed the effectiveness of CR in treating schizophrenia over the years. A recent large meta-analysis included information from 130 studies involving 8851 participants. Of these, 57 studies were performed in Europe, 38 in the United States, 22 in Asia, 4 in Canada, 4 in the Middle East countries, 3 in Australia, and 2 in Brazil. The majority (n = 89) of the studies were single-center studies. The studies included participants from inpatient (n = 35), outpatient (n = 79), and both settings (n = 16). About one-sixth of trials (17.7%) were blinded trials. Comparison groups included treatment as usual in one-third of the studies (34.3%), active treatment as usual group (15.1%) studies, nonspecific control (30.8%), and active intervention (19.9%) group. The meta-analysis showed that CR was useful for improving cognition (global cognition and various cognitive domains) and global functioning. An engaged and skilled therapist, the systematic development of cognitive strategies, integration with psychosocial rehabilitation, and the length of therapy were considered to be critical components of cognitive rehabilitation that were essential to its effectiveness. The format and delivery of CR did not significantly influence the efficacy. In terms of patient-related factors, lower number of years of education, lower premorbid intelligence quotient, and higher severity of baseline symptoms were associated with higher improvement in cognition.[ 50 ] Another metanalysis have also reported beneficial effects of CR in improving cognition in schizophrenia. In this meta-analysis, the variables that have been shown to impact the effect of CR were strategy-coaching (episodic memory) and a discussion group (also called a “bridging”) to assist in applying learnt cognitive abilities to daily life (on verbal memory and global cognition).[ 51 ] The severity level of psychopathology was not found to influence the impact of CR on cognition.[ 50 , 51 ] Different meta-analyses have also evaluated the impact of social-cognitive training on SC. A meta-analysis that included 42 controlled trials involving 1868 patients showed that the intervention was associated with improvement in emotion recognition, mental state attribution, and social perception. In terms of patient-related variables, the lower number of years of education of the participants was related with larger effects of social cognitive training on mental state attribution. Studies with smaller samples showed larger effects of social cognitive training.[ 52 ] Another meta-analysis included 46 randomized studies involving 1979 participants. The social-cognitive training was categorized as focused (targeted/broad-based) and inclusion of cognitive remediation therapy (CRT). Compared to treatment as usual, it was seen that targeted (i.e. interventions targeting 1 or 2 domains of SC) social-cognitive training without CR led to improvement in emotion perception. Furthermore, individual treatment performed better for emotion perception. All treatments (i.e., social cognitive training with or without CR) significantly improved social perception. Only broad-based social cognitive training improved the ToM. Overall, combining social CR with CRT did not influence the impact of social CR.[ 53 ] Based on the available evidence, the European Psychiatric Association recommended use of CR training and social cognitive training for management of cognitive deficits and social cognitive deficits in schizophrenia patients.[ 50 ] Additionally, it is suggested that a qualified therapist carry out CR and incorporate it into a psychosocial rehabilitation program.[ 50 ] Physical Exercise and Lifestyle Interventions: Physical exercise and lifestyle interventions have also been evaluated for their impact on the cognitive deficits in schizophrenia patients. Many meta-analyses have evaluated the role of physical exercise on cognition in schizophrenia patients. A meta-analysis that included data from ten controlled trials involving 385 participants showed a beneficial effect of aerobic exercises on global cognitive performance, working memory, attention/vigilance, and SC. However, heterogeneity was noted for the effect of aerobic exercises on working memory, attention/vigilance, and SC.[ 54 ] The duration of aerobic exercise/physical activity in different studies has varied from 20 to 60 minutes sessions, with 2 to 4 sessions per week for 4 to 12 weeks. Other meta-analyses have evaluated the role of improvement in diet alone or in combination with physical activity and did not find any significant beneficial effect of the intervention on the cognitive functioning.[ 55 , 56 ] Another meta-analysis that evaluated the role of mindfulness-based exercises (such as Yoga, tai-chi, and qicong) showed that there is a small benefit of these interventions on the working memory.[ 57 ] However, it is essential to note that these interventions have been evaluated in a limited number of studies that have a limited sample size. Role of Brain Stimulation Treatments in Management of Cognitive Impairment in Schizophrenia At present, three brain stimulation techniques, that is, electroconvulsive treatment (ECT), repetitive transcranial brain stimulation (rTMS), and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), are used in the management of schizophrenia. Since the beginning, ECT has been reported to have a negative impact on cognition. A systematic review that evaluated the role of ECT in management of schizophrenia and included studies reporting the cognitive outcomes showed lack of negative impact of ECT on the cognition, with some of the studies actually reporting beneficial effects of ECT on cognition.[ 58 ] Another review evaluated the role of electrode placement on the cognitive outcomes and showed that right unilateral or bifrontal ECT has more favorable cognitive outcomes when compared to bitemporal ECT.[ 59 ] A recent study attempted to evaluate the predictors of cognitive changes in schizophrenia patients receiving ECT and reported that lower pre-ECT Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores and female gender predicted improvement in MoCA post-ECT. On the other hand, age and voluntary admission predicted deterioration in MoCA score.[ 60 ] Systematic reviews also suggest lack of adverse impact of ECT on cognition in treatment-resistant schizophrenia patients.[ 61 ] Only a small number of Indian researches have assessed the cognitive outcomes of ECT-treated schizophrenia patients. A study showed deterioration in cognitive function with ECT, with threshold bitemporal ECT being associated with more cognitive impairment when compared to right unilateral and bifrontal ECT.[ 62 ] Another study from India showed bifrontal ECT was associated with lower cognitive impairment when compared to bitemporal ECT at the end of the ECT course.[ 63 ] In a follow-up study, which compared the cognitive performance of patients who received bifrontal and bitemporal ECT, it was evident that after a mean duration of about 98 days, the acute cognitive deficits induced by ECT actually improve by 3 months with electrode placement having no significant impact on the cognitive outcomes.[ 64 ] Different studies have attempted to evaluate whether treatment with rTMS or tDCS is associated with improvement in cognitive functioning. A meta-analysis evaluated the cognitive benefits with rTMS and tDCS across different neuropsychiatric conditions, including schizophrenia. This meta-analysis included 24 (14 studies on rTMS and 10 studies on tDCS) studies involving patients with schizophrenia and reported lack of improvement in cognitive functions with these treatments in schizophrenia patients.[ 65 ] Similar outcomes have been reported in other systematic reviews and meta-analyses.[ 66 , 67 ] However, another meta-analysis that included nine studies involving 351 patients with schizophrenia, receiving high-frequency rTMS, positioned on the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex with a total pulse &lt;30,000, was associated with improvement in working memory, compared to sham rTMS.[ 68 ] Similarly, another meta-analysis showed beneficial effects of multisession tDCS on working memory.[ 69 ] On the other hand, another meta-analysis that evaluated the role of tDCS on multiple cognitive domains reported some beneficial role in working memory, attention, and SC. However, it is crucial to remember that there was a great deal of variability in the included research.[ 70 ] Other meta-analyses have also confirmed the beneficial role of transcranial electrical stimulation on the working memory.[ 71 ] Emerging evidence is inconclusive about combining CR measures and tDCS or rTMS for incremental benefit.[ 72 , 73 ] Overall, it can be concluded that the current level of evidence suggests that ECT does not lead to significant cognitive impairment in the long run and there is some beneficial effect of other brain stimulation treatments on working memory. However, the evidence is not robust to recommend use of rTMS and tDCS for cognitive impairment in schizophrenia patients. Tests for assessment of cognition in schizophrenia Numerous test batteries are recommended for evaluating SC and NC deficiencies in individuals with schizophrenia. These can be understood as test batteries or screening instrument [ Table 5 ]. In the following section, we will discuss some of the important and commonly validated instruments in schizophrenia patients. The Measurement and Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS™) was designed by the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) to improve research in the area of cognition in schizophrenia while evaluating the psychopharmacological agents and CR. The MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) is now considered to be the gold standard battery for assessing cognitive impairment in schizophrenia patients. It is also available in Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, and Telegu. It includes ten tests covering the seven recommended domains of cognition to be assessed in schizophrenia patients [ Table 5 ]. It is validated for persons aged 20–59 years and takes 60–90 minutes to complete.[ 74 , 75 ] The battery has good test–retest reliability.[ 76 ] However, one of the limitations for using this test battery in routine clinical practice is the long duration required for its administration. The Hindi version of the MATRICS has been used in a multicentric study funded by the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH).[ 77 ] Tests equivalent to MATRICS battery have been validated in India.[ 78 , 79 ] Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS) is a brief test battery that can be completed in 30 minutes. It includes tests for 6 NC domains [ Table 6 ]. The results of these tests can be summed up to create a composite score. The composite score has been shown to have high test–retest reliability.[ 80 ] It has been demonstrated to outperform the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) in terms of test–retest reliability and internal consistency.[ 81 ] The Schizophrenia Cognition Rating Scale (SCoRS) is an 18 items scale that evaluates six cognitive domains (attention, reasoning and problem solving, memory, working memory, language production, and motor skills). Each item is rated on a 4-point scale (none-1; mild-2; moderate-3; and severe-4) pertaining to cognitive functioning in the past 2 weeks. The rating is done by interviewers based on the information provided by the patient and an informant (usually a family member).[ 82 ] Brief Cognitive Assessment Tool for Schizophrenia (B-CATS) is a brief battery that can be administered in less than 12 minutes, and it includes three tests, that is, trail making test, category fluency (animals, fruits, vegetables), and digit symbol.[ 83 ] It has good internal consistency and test–retest reliability.[ 84 ] The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) includes an assessment of executive functions, visuospatial abilities, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, recall, and orientation. It is also available in Hindi. The maximum attainable score on MoCA for any participant is 30 points. One-point correction is made if the person is educated for less than 12 years.[ 85 ] It requires 10–15 minutes to be administered, and this has been validated in schizophrenia patients against MCCB. Significant associations were noted between five out of the seven subtests of MoCA (visuospatial-executive, attention, language, abstraction, and delayed recall) and MCCB subtests. No correlation was noted for the MoCA subtests of the naming and orientation. A cutoff of &lt;25 is considered to be an indicator of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia, rather than the original &lt;26.[ 86 ] The Hindi version of MoCA has been adapted and validated in India in elderly subjects.[ 87 ] Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP) is a paper-pencil screening tool for assessing cognitive impairment in patients with psychotic or affective disorders. It consists of five subscales. The total score is calculated by summing up the scores for each of the subscale, with higher scores indicating better cognitive functioning. It can be completed in 15 minutes. It is available in three different forms so that the test can be repeated and the learning effect can be avoided. The test has good psychometric properties.[ 88 ] A cutoff score of &lt;70 indicates cognitive impairment, with a sensitivity of 87.9 and a specificity of 80.6.[ 89 ] Cognitive Screening Scale for Schizophrenia (CSIS) is a short screening instrument of 11 subscales, which has good internal consistency. The initial scale included subscales for assessment of a larger number of cognitive functions. However, the final scale includes six subscales. A cutoff score of 16 was found to have a sensitivity of 86% and a specificity of 70%.[ 90 ] The Self-Assessment Scale of Cognitive Complaints in Schizophrenia (SASCCS) is a 21-item self-rated questionnaire that assesses five cognitive domains – memory, attention, executive functions, language, and praxia. It has good psychometric properties (internal consistency and reliability).[ 91 ] Social Cognition Rating Tools in Indian Setting (SOCRATIS) includes a series of tests designed to evaluate three areas of SC ToM, attribute styles, and social perception. It was created in India. Two first-order activities (the Sally-Anne and Smarties task), two second-order tasks (the Ice Cream Van and the Missing Cookies narrative), two metaphor-irony tasks, and the faux pas detection test are among the adaptations of the ToM tests. A modification of the Social Cue Recognition examination is the social perception test that is part of the battery. The Internal, Personal, and Situational Attributions Questionnaire (IPSAQ) served as the model for the attributional bias test. Every SOCRATIS test has recognized group validity and strong content validity (&gt;4 score by &gt;75% of experts). All tests of the SOCRATIS have good content validity (&gt;4 score by &gt;75% of experts) and known group validity.[ 92 ] Tool for Recognition of Emotions in Neuropsychiatric Disorders (TRENDS), created in India, assesses a person’s capacity to identify the whole spectrum and a variety of emotional emotions. The instrument consists of 52 still photos and 28 films, each lasting 4 seconds, that show six fundamental emotions with neutral face expressions and two variable intensities—high and low. The images are arranged in a random order.[ 93 ] Facial Emotion Recognition Deficits (FERD) is a modification of TRENDS for used in mothers with severe mental illness that uses images of the children. It includes neutral facial expressions and six fundamental emotions that are displayed at two distinct intensities. The scale has good inter-rater agreement and internal consistency.[ 94 ] AIIMS Facial Toolbox for Emotion Recognition (AFTER) includes static facial images covering the eight emotions – neutral, happiness, anger, sadness, disgust, contempt, fear, and surprise. Each emotion is expressed as high and low. It has good inter-rater reliability.[ 95 ] Facial Emotion recognition task by Mandal includes five photographs each for six basic emotions. It has been evaluated in a group of patients with schizophrenia.[ 96 ] The Indian Spontaneous Expression Database for Emotion Recognition consists of spontaneous facial expressions presented as 428 segmented video clips, validated using machine learning algorithms. The Kappa coefficient between the four raters was 0.85.[ 97 ] Observable Social Cognition A Rating Scale (OSCARS) is a semistructured interview that consists of eight items. The interview can be conducted with the patient or the informant. It can also be used as an informant-based questionnaire. Both methods take approximately 15–20 minutes. Each item is rated on 7-point scale based on the anchor points given in the scale.[ 98 ] The scale has been developed in English and has been recently translated to Hindi. Revised reading the mind in the eye test for the Theory of Mind has been validated in Bengali and has been shown to to be a valid test for assessment of the ToM in the Indian context.[ 99 ] However, it is important to note that all the above-described assessment methods have only been used in research settings and normative data are not available for these scales. Hence, their utility in clinical practice is doubtful. Data from India Many Indian studies have compared the NC functioning of schizophrenia patients and healthy controls and suggest that patients perform poorly when compared to the healthy controls.[ 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 , 105 , 106 ] Studies from India that have compared SC of schizophrenia patients and healthy controls suggest that patients perform poorly than the healthy controls.[ 99 , 107 , 108 ] A study from Chandigarh documented higher SC deficits in patients with first episode psychosis, when compared to healthy controls.[ 109 ] Studies also suggest that among schizophrenia patients, NC deficits are associated with functioning/dysfunction,[ 110 , 111 ] insight,[ 112 ] and employment.[ 113 ] Data also suggest that SC in schizophrenia influence functioning/dysfunction[ 111 , 114 ] and disability.[ 114 ] One study showed that cognitive functions of motor speed, attention, and executive functions have significant correlation with negative symptoms.[ 110 ] Other studies suggest association of higher cognitive impairment with longer duration of illness[ 104 , 115 ] and positive[ 104 , 115 ] and negative symptoms.[ 104 ] One study did not find association of cognitive functions with current employment status and performance at work.[ 104 ] The information that is now available indicates that childhood/adolescent-onset schizophrenia is linked to greater deficiencies in NC in the areas of verbal fluency, processing speed, and cognitive flexibility than does schizophrenia that manifests in adults.[ 116 ] Many NC tests and SC batteries have been validated in India (refer to the above section for the same). In terms of effect of pharmacological intervention, one study suggests that 3-month clozapine therapy leads to improvement in SC but not NC deficits.[ 117 ] Many studies have evaluated the role of CRT in schizophrenia. A study from NIMHANS evaluated the role of home-based cognitive retraining for FES patients, which showed significant benefits in the domains of divided attention, concept formation and set-shifting ability, and planning. However, the overall sample size for the study was small.[ 118 ] Another multinational study involving patients from India and United States showed that CRT leads to significant gains in the domains of verbal working memory and a trend toward improvement in attention/vigilance.[ 119 ] Another study from NIMHANS compared the efficacy of caregiver-assisted home-based CR programs with clinic-based CR programs and reported that it is feasible to provide CR to the schizophrenia patients with the help of caregivers in home settings and there is no significant difference in the efficacy of both the intervention programs.[ 120 ] Another study from Chandigarh evaluated the role of computerized CRT and showed significant beneficial effects of the same on sustained attention and the processing speed, when compared with treatment as usual.[ 121 ] One study from Lucknow evaluated the role of add-on family-assisted cognitive remediation therapy (FACT) in schizophrenia patients and compared it with treatment as usual (pharmacotherapy and brief counselling) and reported that there was improvement with FACT on attention/vigilance, working memory, processing speed, household responsibility, and SC.[ 122 ] A second study from the same center evaluated the role of home-based CR and showed that compared to treatment as usual, CRT is associated with significant improvement in cognitive functioning, reduction in disability, and improvement in quality of life.[ 123 ] Other studies have also shown beneficial effects of yoga on cognitive functions.[ 124 ] Studies have also evaluated the add-on role of yoga on cognitive functions, and these suggest that add-on yoga to the ongoing treatment leads to significant improvement in the facial emotion recognition deficits, plasma oxytocin levels, and socio-occupational functioning.[ 125 , 126 ] Assessment of schizophrenia patients from the perspective of managing cognitive impairment Indian Psychiatric Society (IPS) has already published the clinical practice guidelines for management of schizophrenia in adults, children, and the elderly.[ 127 , 128 , 129 ] Accordingly, the general principle of assessment and management of schizophrenia as laid down in the abovementioned guidelines must be followed. Here, we would discuss the specific issues pertaining to assessment and management of cognitive impairment [ Table 7 and Figure 3 ]. While history taking, whenever possible, the evaluation of cognitive performance should be a focus. Although the initial assessment and management of schizophrenia focus on the reduction in psychopathology, the available literature suggests that psychopathology does not have much impact on cognitive functioning. Hence, cognitive functions should be assessed at the earliest opportunity when the patient is cooperative for the same and then these should be monitored on a regular basis. In high-risk groups, such as those with a family history of schizophrenia and children of patients with schizophrenia, cognitive assessment should be done at the earliest, and if they are found to have a cognitive impairment, the cognitive remedial measures should be instituted at the earliest. Assessment instruments may be chosen based on the feasibility. However, wherever feasible and whenever feasible, patients should undergo a comprehensive assessment for cognitive impairment. Additional assessment should also focus on the impairment in various domains of life (function, education, work, quality of life, social functioning, etc), which are being affected by the NC and SC deficits. If required, these could be assessed by using structured instruments to understand the exact level of the deficits in various domains of life. Considering the side-effect profile of the atypical antipsychotic medications and the negative impact of adverse metabolic parameters on cognitive functions, the importance of assessment and monitoring of body weight, body mass index, and metabolic parameters should be understood, and this needs to be incorporated in the day-to-day management of schizophrenia. Assessment of level of physical activity should also be incorporated in the routine clinical assessment. A detailed history of substance use should be obtained, and all the efforts must be made to keep the patient in an abstinent state. Management of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia The management should include multiprong strategies to prevent development or progression of cognitive impairment and to reduce the existing NC and SC deficits. The strategies can be broadly understood as psychoeducation, pharmacological measures (including selection of antipsychotic medications and use of anticholinergic agents) and nonpharmacological measures. Cognitive impairments should also be considered while using somatic therapies. Among the people at risk of schizophrenia, efforts should be made to evaluate, monitor, and if required manage the cognitive impairment, even before the emergence of other symptoms of schizophrenia [ Figure 4 ]. Psychoeducation The psychoeducation for patients with schizophrenia should cover all the important aspects of the illness such as understanding the knowledge of the patient and the caregivers about the illness and providing information about the diagnosis, symptoms, etiology, treatment options, course and outcome, pharmacological management, nonpharmacological management, the importance of medication and treatment adherence, need for abstinence from substances, role of communication patterns including the expressed emotions, issues related to marriage, pregnancy and child birth, relapse and identifying the early signs of relapse, role of stress in relapse and continuation of symptoms, and improving the adaptive coping mechanisms to deal with the residual psychopathology.[ 127 , 128 , 129 , 130 ] However, from the perspective of NC, patients and caregivers should be informed that cognitive symptoms are part and parcel of the illness and do not improve much with the available antipsychotic medications. They should be informed that nonpharmacological measures such as CR and aerobic exercises can lead to improvement in the cognitive functioning. Similarly, the patients and caregivers must be informed about the adverse impact of weight, high body-mass index, and abnormal metabolic parameters on the NC functions. The patients should also be asked to take a healthy diet, do regular physical activity, and remain abstinent from substances to avoid further weight gain and the development of abnormal metabolic parameters. Those with pre-existing obesity, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension should also be encouraged to take their medications for these illnesses regularly and consult their physician from time to time to minimize the impact of these on cognitive functions. Studies in healthy controls and persons with different illnesses suggest that a lack of sleep can also have adverse impacts on the cognitive functioning.[ 131 ] Hence, the patients must be encouraged to have adequate sleep. Furthermore, the higher risk of dementia in individuals with schizophrenia should be communicated to patients and their caregivers.[ 132 ] Selection of antipsychotic medications Besides the other consideration,[ 124 ] the selection of antipsychotic medications in schizophrenia patients should take into consideration the anticholinergic properties of the medications and whereever feasible, medications with lower anticholinergic properties may be considered. However, it is important to maintain a balance between achieving adequate symptom controls versus the lower anticholinergic properties. Considering the negative impact of FGAMs on NC functions, whenever feasible, in patients with cognitive impairment, the FGAMs should be changed to a SGAMs. However, the current level of evidence suggests that there is lack of difference among various SGAMs on the cognitive functioning.[ 32 ] In patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia, clozapine will remain as a medication of choice. However, while using clozapine, all other listed measures need to be considered for a comprehensive management of the patient. Whenever feasible, anticholinergic agents should be used sparingly and when used should be used in the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration. While prescribing medications for other illnesses too, the clinicians should be aware about their anticholinergic potential and choose medications accordingly. Consideration while using ECT There is lack of conclusive evidence for the adverse effect of ECT on cognitive functions in schizophrenia patients on the long term. Hence, ECT should not be withheld when clinically indicated, just because of fear of worsening of the cognitive functioning. However, while using ECT, measures such as use of right unilateral or bifrontal ECT may be considered initially. However, if the patient does not respond to these electrode placements, bitemporal ECT should be considered. rTMS and tDCS There is lack of conclusive evidence at present to suggest that rTMS and tDCS lead to improvement in cognitive functioning in patients with schizophrenia. Accordingly, there is a need for further good quality evidence to prove the beneficial effect of these interventions on the cognitive functioning. Cognitive remediation As per the Cognitive Remediation Experts Group, CR for schizophrenia is “ a behavioral training-based intervention that aims to improve cognitive processes (attention, memory, executive function, SC, or metacognition) with the goal of durability and generalization ”. CRT involves use of various cognitive exercises and techniques to improve the cognition. It basically adopts a strength-based approach and utilizes the concept of neuroplasticity of the brain and aims to improve the cognitive skills of the persons to improve the overall functioning and the quality of life. CR should be started as early as possible, provided the patient is cooperative for the same. The duration of CR can vary from patient to patient depending on their progress and level of improvement. In terms of approaches for CRT, two approaches have been documented, that is, the compensatory approach and the restorative approach. The compensatory approach involves use of aids such as smart phone and other aids to compensate for the cognitive difficulties. This is also known as cognitive adaptation training.[ 133 ] In real sense, the compensatory approach is not CRT. The restorative approach involves improving the cognitive functioning in a particular domain by using the behavioral training involving a combination of ‘drill and practice’ and methods to generalize these skills. The drill and practice can be done by a pen-and-pencil method or computerized method.[ 133 ] The different techniques used in the majority of the CT programs include errorless learning, self-monitoring, and scaffolding. The ‘errorless learning’ involves teaching the participants to carry out various cognitive tasks by minimizing the opportunity for making mistakes. This means that the participants are presented the task in which the task or the therapist supports all the steps required to complete the task. In order to further promote error-free learning, tasks are occasionally presented in a graded fashion, meaning that they are sometimes presented slowly at first and then accelerated, or the participant is asked to begin with easier tasks before the difficulty level is raised. Other times, the tasks are built up backward from the end result to the beginning. With continued training sessions, the support provided to the patient is withdrawn so that they become independent of the support provided by the therapist or the task.[ 134 ] The second technique known as “self-monitoring” involves allowing the person to develop a rehearsal technique with respect to the instructions of the task and the task completion. This can involve verbalization of the instructions for the given task, initially overtly and later covertly.[ 134 ] The third technique is known as “scaffolding”, which is understood as linking the complexity of the task to the level of ability of the participants. However, it is important to note that this is not the same as errorless learning, which involves increasing the complexity of the task with learning.[ 134 ] Different packages are available for the CRT. One such approach is the Neuropsychological Educational Approach to Cognitive Remediation (NEAR) Model.[ 135 ] In routine clinical practice, some of the simple measures as listed in Table 8 can be used. Wherever feasible, patients with significant cognitive impairment should be evaluated by a clinical psychologist trained in neuropsychology or a psychiatrist with adequate training to use different cognitive tests. They should conduct assessment based on locally validated tools to identify cognitive deficits and plan individualized cognitive retraining programs. The CR measures should be carried out by mental health professionals trained to administer these interventions. However, in Indian settings, very few patients can access the services of such specialists. Hence, in the absence of trained mental health professionals, mental health professionals with various levels of training may encourage patients to go through the cognitive exercises as listed in Table 8 . The patients can also be encouraged to use cognition enhancing apps (e.g. lumocity, mindlamp, etc). Patients may also be encouraged to engage in cognitively stimulating activities, for example, grain sorting, rangoli making, solving crosswords, sudoku, puzzles, and playing chess or cards with increasing cognitive demand. Aerobic Exercises/Physical Activity: Evidence from meta-analysis suggests that aerobic exercises improve global cognition, working memory, attention/vigilance, and SC. It was also seen that the higher level of exercise is associated with more positive impact on the cognition. Furthermore, when exercise is supervised by physical activity professionals, it is associated with better outcome.[ 54 ] Accordingly, clinicians should encourage the patients to do regular aerobic exercises, and if feasible, the patients should be encouraged to do so under the supervision of a physical activity professional. In the inpatient setting, physical activity professionals can be included as part of the team to train patients for various aerobic exercises. The duration of aerobic exercise/physical activity in different studies has varied from 20 to 60 minutes sessions, and 2 to 4 sessions per week can be recommended. Based on the interest of the patients, mindfulness-based exercises can also be considered. Use of yoga The preliminary evidence from a randomized controlled trial suggests that use of yoga (loosening exercises, breathing practices, Suryanamaskāra, sitting and supine and prone posture Āsanas along with Prāṇāyāma and relaxation techniques) for a period of 1 month can lead to improvement in SC.[ 126 ] Hence, whenever feasible and if the patient is interested in doing so, the same must be encouraged to be done on a regular basis. Meaningful occupation There is some evidence from a randomized controlled trial to suggest that providing employment helps improve patients’ cognitive functions.[ 136 ] Accordingly, all the patients should be assisted to follow any occupation, particularly income-generating ones in addition to other cognitive enhancing measures.[ 137 ] CONCLUSIONS NC impairment and social cognitive deficits are part and parcel of clinical presentation of schizophrenia. These deficits often emerge before the actual development of psychotic symptoms. These deficits have an adverse impact on the overall functioning, social functioning, educational attainment, work performance, and overall quality of life of the patients. Accordingly, assessment and management of cognitive impairment should be an integral part of holistic care. The assessment should be an ongoing process, and the management should involve multiprong strategies to improve the overall outcome of patients with schizophrenia.
