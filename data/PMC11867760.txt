Abstract This case report describes a patient who received hormone replacement therapy for secondary panhypopituitarism and subsequently developed diabetes. His physician decided to discontinue growth hormone (GH) replacement, which was previously deemed contraindicated. Following the diagnosis of fatty liver, the patient began to exhibit liver damage that progressed over the ensuing years, ultimately leading to cirrhosis. Common factors linked to cirrhosis were excluded, leading to the belief that GH deficiency over several years was the primary contributor to cirrhosis. Therefore, when treating patients with GH insufficiency and diabetes, clinicians should carefully consider the potential implications of GH replacement therapy. Introduction Non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) is a form of chronic hepatitis that can progress to cirrhosis and liver cancer, with obesity being the main risk factor. Recent research has shown that age, genetic factors, metabolic abnormalities such as diabetes and hyperlipidemia, and endocrine abnormalities are all involved in the development and pathogenesis ( 1 - 5 ). We recently encountered a middle-aged man with secondary panhypopituitarism who likely experienced rapid progression to NASH cirrhosis due to an interruption in growth hormone (GH) replacement therapy. He was effectively treated with hormone replacement therapy for secondary hypopituitarism following surgery for craniopharyngioma. However, GH replacement therapy was unavoidably interrupted when he developed type 2 diabetes during the course of the illness. Considering the rarity of cases linking NASH cirrhosis with GH deficiency, we present this case to raise awareness among clinicians. Case Report A 49-year-old man was referred to our hospital for treatment of esophageal varices. These varices were discovered during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed at his local doctor's private clinic after he complained of persistent gastric discomfort. At 22 years old, he was diagnosed with craniopharyngioma triggered by vision loss and underwent tumor resection. Following surgery, he developed secondary panhypopituitarism and was started on various hormone replacement therapies (hydrocortisone, levothyroxine sodium hydrate, and gonadotropin), excluding GH. At 34 years old, he was started on a maintenance dose of GH preparation, which was the first GH preparation approved by insurance at the time for severe adult GH deficiency. Over the next eight years, he experienced weight gain, elevated transaminase levels, and glucose intolerance in an unfavorable family environment despite attempts at improvement through diet and exercise therapy ( Fig. 1 ). At 42 years old, he was diagnosed with diabetes mellitus, and the maintenance dose of GH preparations was discontinued owing to regulations in place at the time. At that point, no signs of cirrhosis were observed. During his visit to our hospital at 49 years old, a physical examination revealed obesity, with a height of 170 cm, weight of 112 kg, and body mass index of 38.8. Blood tests showed a low platelet count at 97,000 /μL, and serum liver fibrosis indices, such as collagen type IV (6.0 ng/mL), hyaluronic acid (183.4 ng/mL), fibrosis-4 index (2.26), and Mac-2-binding protein glycosylation isomer (1.38 C.O.I.), were all abnormally high, indicating the development of liver fibrosis. The findings of hepatic fibrosis development were consistent with an abnormally high liver stiffness measurement of 22.7 kPa by FibroScan Ⓡ , and the findings of fatty liver on abdominal ultrasonography were supported by an elevated controlled attenuation parameter value of 267 dB/m ( Fig. 2A, B ). He had no history of alcohol consumption, and serologic studies ruled out hepatitis B and C virus infections, as well as autoimmunity ( Table ). Abdominal computed tomography revealed an atrophic liver with irregular margins and signs of cirrhosis; however, no mass lesions were found. Splenomegaly and varices around the esophagus were evident, although no ascites was present ( Fig. 2C, D ). Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed four Form 3 varices with red color signs in the lower esophagus and portal hypertensive gastropathy in the gastric mucosa, but no varices were noted in the stomach or duodenum ( Fig. 3A-C ). After four sessions of endoscopic injection sclerotherapy, the varices disappeared, reducing the risk of rupture of esophageal varices. GH preparations, previously considered “contraindicated” in diabetic patients under Japanese drug administration regulations, were moved to the “cautious administration” list under the revised regulations in 2021. Consequently, GH administration was resumed after the completion of endoscopic injection sclerotherapy in this case, and the patient experienced an improvement in systemic general fatigue with a decrease in body weight over 10 kg. Furthermore, the indices of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis calculated using FibroScan Ⓡ were significantly improved ( Fig. 4 ). Discussion The disease characterized by fatty liver on histological and imaging findings in the absence of a history of heavy alcohol consumption is known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Recently, a new term, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, has been proposed to describe a disease concept in which fatty liver coexists with obesity, type 2 diabetes, or two or more metabolic disorders (such as hypertension, visceral fat accumulation, glucose intolerance, and dyslipidemia) ( 6 ). This term reflects the idea that the influence of various metabolic abnormalities on the development of liver pathology should not be overlooked. Conventional NAFLD is classified into simple NAFL, which progresses slowly, and NASH, which is progressive and carries a high risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer. NASH is a progressive liver disease with a poor prognosis, characterized by inflammation, cellular damage, and fibrosis, in addition to a simple fatty liver. The multiple hit theory suggests that NASH is caused by the accumulation of various factors, such as insulin resistance linked to visceral obesity, abnormal lipid metabolism, abnormal adipokines, increased oxidative stress, a decreased mitochondrial function, and endotoxin influx from the small intestine ( 7 ). In recent years, endocrine abnormalities have emerged as potential causes of NASH. NASH is believed to develop as a result of the accumulation of visceral fat, increased insulin resistance, or abnormal lipid metabolism triggered by hormone secretion irregularities ( 3 - 5 ). Specifically, persistent GH deficiency can lead to obesity, abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism, and a condition resembling metabolic syndrome ( 8 - 10 ). Progression to NASH and NASH-related cirrhosis has been reported in some cases of NAFLD ( 11 ). The association between GH and NASH was investigated in a clinical study by Nishizawa et al.. They compared the frequency of NAFLD complications between two groups: 66 adult patients with GH deficiency and 83 healthy controls matched for the age, sex, and body mass index ( 12 ). They concluded that adult GH deficiency is an independent risk factor for NAFLD development, apart from obesity, as evidenced by a significant difference in the NAFLD complication rate: 77% in the adult GH deficiency group versus 12% in the control group, representing a 6.4-fold higher frequency of complications in the adult GH deficiency group. They found that GH replacement therapy improved the liver function in 19 adult patients with GH deficiency and significantly reduced the serum concentration of type IV collagen, a marker of liver fibrosis. Furthermore, a quantitative analysis of histological changes in five patients who had undergone a liver biopsy before and after treatment, based on the NAFLD activity score, showed a significant improvement in the degree of fat deposition and the liver fibrosis assessment index ( 12 ). Based on these findings, GH replacement therapy for adult GH deficiency is expected to improve NAFLD pathogenesis. Adams et al. also reported that 10 of 21 (48%) patients with panhypopituitarism, a condition often linked to GH dyssecretion, showed histological evidence of NASH, burnout, NASH, or cirrhosis ( 13 ). They observed that, in a 66-month prospective study of these 21 patients, 6 patients died, with 2 of these deaths during the observation period being liver-related, while 2 others required liver transplants due to liver failure. This suggests that GH plays an important role in the adult human body, particularly in the liver, and its impaired secretion may contribute to the development of NAFLD and NASH with potentially unfavorable long-term outcomes. In our case, the blood test results ruled out hepatitis virus involvement, and there was no history of alcohol consumption. However, the patient showed signs of fatty liver on abdominal ultrasonography and panhypopituitarism, suggesting cirrhosis likely due to the progression of NASH pathology. During the seven years when GH replacement therapy had to be temporarily suspended due to glucose intolerance, GH deficiency developed in the body. This led to further weight gain and increased fatigue. Reviewing the blood test results for the past 13 years that were available for confirmation, we found that the platelet count began to decline after 43 years old, and the AST/ALT ratio clearly increased after 45 years old, suggesting that the liver may have been accelerated to become cirrhotic during this period ( Fig. 4 ). Previous contraindications of GH analogs for diabetics have recently been lifted. To our knowledge, there is no clear explanation of why GH preparations were contraindicated for administration to diabetic patients. The only acceptable hypothesis was that GH may have been deemed undesirable for administration to diabetic patients based on its mechanism of inhibiting insulin action by increasing free fatty acids via lipolysis. As a result, the present patient had been receiving continuous GH replacement therapy since 50 years old, and his general fatigue had significantly improved. In the future, there is hope that the patient's NASH condition, including the histological findings, will steadily improve. Clinicians should be aware that GH deficiency is one of the key factors involved in the pathogenesis of NASH. When treating patients with GH insufficiency who also have diabetes, it is crucial to remember that GH replacement therapy must be approached comprehensively from a long-term perspective, while carefully considering the advantages and disadvantages of GH replacement therapy.
