Vanvitelli” Caserta, Italy 2 Department of Sciences and Technologies, University of Sannio, Benevento, Italy 358 358 The global epidemic of obesity is accompanied by a rising burden of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). NAFLD is characterized by hepatic accumulation of lipids, causing cellular stress and hepatic injury, which ultimately leads to chronic liver disease. Abnormal lipid accumulation is associated with perturbed endoplasmic reticulum (ER) proteo-stasis in hepatocytes. Alteration in ER function, known as “ER stress,” is emerging as a key feature of metabolic disorders. Excess lipid exposure leads to ER stress and, conversely, ER stressors disrupt lipid metabolism. During the last years, studies on the relationship between ER stress and microRNAs (miRNAs) has burst on the scene. Based on these considerations, the aim of this study was to investigate the involvement of miR-18a-5p/SREBP1/PERK pathway in ER stress and how this pathway can affect autophagy and apoptosis in early stages of NAFLD induced by high fat diet. To this purpose, male wistar rats fed on a high-fat diet for 5 weeks were been used. In addition, in order to verify the central role of the miR-18a-5p/SREBP1 axis, an in-vitro model of Hep-G2 cells transfected with mimic miR-18a-5p and subsequently treated with an oleate/palmitate mixture for 24 hours were been used. The results indicated that in both the experimental models the expression of miR-18a-5p was downregulated by excess fat. This downregulation was associated with increase of SREBP1c and PERK levels, highlighting a state of ER stress. Furthermore, the activation of PERK signaling induced an increase in autophagic process, and an increase in pro-apoptotic markers and a decrease in anti-apoptotic markers level, suggesting also an induction of apoptosis. Taken together, our study reveals the involvement of miR-18a-5p/SREBP1/PERK axis in the ER stress induced by a fat diet, thus providing promising drug targets for tailored therapy in the advanced fat liver setting. We identified microRNA-22 (miR-22) as an essential rheostat involved in the control of lipid and energy homeostasis as well as the onset and maintenance of obesity. We demonstrated through transgenic animal models that miR-22 overexpression promotes obesity and hepatic steatosis even when mice are fed a regular chow diet. Mechanistically, we show that miR-22 orchestrates multiple pro-lipogenic programs and adipogenesis through both direct and indirect targets. Our findings thus identify a critical non-coding RNA target and an effective therapy for treatment of obesity and other metabolic disorders. Methods In this randomized controlled trial, adult females diagnosed with obesity and lipedema were randomized (1:1, stratified for BMI categories) to either a ketogenic low-energy diet (keto) with 75 g/day of carbohydrates or a non-ketogenic low-energy diet (control) with 180 g/day of carbohydrates for 8 weeks, both with 1200 kcal/day. Body composition was assessed using dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and circumference of hip, waist, calf, and thigh was measured with a tape using standardized procedures before and after the intervention. Results A total of 57 females were included (BMI 37.3±5 kg/m2, age 48±11 years), and 51 participants completed the intervention. The keto group had a significant larger weight loss, FM loss in the whole body and % fat loss in both legs and gynoid area compared to controls (10.5±2.0 kg vs. 7.4±2.5 kg, P&lt;0.001; 6.9±3.3 kg vs. 5.1±1.6 kg, P=0.023;1.8±1.6 % vs. 0.7±1.2 %, P=0.017; 2.4±2.3 % vs. 1.2±1.2%, P=0.031, respectively). A greater reduction in calf, thigh and hip circumference was also found in the keto group (2.2±1.2 vs. 0.7±4.0 cm, P=0.043; 5.7±4.1 vs. 3.1±2.4 cm, P=0.009 and 8.2±4.6 vs. 4.2±3.6 cm, P=0.008, respectively). The keto group had a larger loss of % fat in the whole body, trunk, and android area compared to controls (2.6±1.5 % vs. 1.5±0.9 %, P=0.004; 3.6±2.2 % vs. 2.1±1.3 %, P=0.006; 3.9±3 % vs. 2.2±1.9 %, P=0.022, respectively). Conclusion A ketogenic low-energy diet seems to be superior to a control diet in reducing weight and FM, as well as circumference of the lower extremities, the areas most affected by lipedema. However, further research with a larger sample size and long-term follow-up is needed. LBP4 − Management and Intervention LBP4.42 Predictive factors of successful weight loss after liraglutide treatment for obesity Pairon C Thissen J Loumaye A Department of Endocrinology and Nutrition, Cliniques universitaires Saint-Luc, Brussels, Belgium 412 412 Introduction Altough liraglutide, a glucagon like peptide-1 analogue, is effective in the treatment of obesity, a poor response in term of weight loss may still be seen in some patients. In our study, we aim to analyze liraglutide adherence and effectiveness in real-life practice and identify patient clinical and/or biological factors that are associated or predictive of successful response to liraglutide. Methods Retrospective and longitudinal study of patients treated with once daily subcutaneous injection of liraglutide for an obesity resistant to lifestyle modifications, between 2010 and 2022, at Cliniques Universitaires Saint-Luc, Brussels, Belgium. The clinical and biological parameters were classically recorded in medical consultation. Results 108 patients received a prescription for liraglutide at the usual dose. 75 (69,4%) patients came to the first follow-up visit, and 57 (52,8%) patients, with complete follow-up data for at least 6 months, were included in the study. The main reason for the discontinuation of treatment was adverse events (12/75 [16,0%] patients who started treatment). Included patients were more frequently women (71,9%), had an average age of 44,6 (± 12,0) years and a BMI of 37,2 (± 4,7) kg/m 2 . The mean weight loss (WL) was 9,0 (± 5,9) % of the initial body weight with an average maximum dose of liraglutide of 2,2 (± 0,8) mg/day. The time to reach the maximum WL was 6,0 (± 3,1) months. Of the 57 patients, 77,2% had a WL ≥ 5%, 40,4% had a WL ≥ 10% and 12,3% had a WL ≥ 15%. Patients with a successful WL ≥ 5%, in comparison to those with a WL &lt;5% had a younger age (42,9 ± 11,9 vs 50,8 ± 10,4 years; p=0,041), a lower BMI (36,6 ± 4,5 vs 39,6 ± 5,1 kg/m2; p=0,049) and less cardiometabolic comorbidities (53,4% vs 30,8%; p=0,035 had &lt; 2 comorbidities). In particular, patients with successful weight loss, had less frequently high blood pressure (45,5% vs 84,6%; p=0,013). Eating behaviors such as high-volume food intake (54,5% vs 53,8%; p=0,965) or snacking (63,6% vs 84,6%; p=0,153) were not different between the two groups. The fact of having a dietary follow-up (43,2% vs 53,8%; p=0,498) or restarting sport (31,8% vs 30,8%; p=0,943) did not seem to have an impact on weight loss either. Uni and multivariate binary logistic regression revealed that a lower BMI category was predictive of a successful weight loss with liraglutide (HR 0,379 [0,148-0,966]; p=0,042). Conclusion Patients who lost 5% or more of their body weight using liraglutide were younger, with a lower baseline BMI and fewer cardiometabolic comorbidities. If these criteria are confirmed in further studies, this may provide an opportunity to better identify patients who are good responders to liraglutide and to refer other patients to other obesity treatments.
