Background Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory skin disease. Recent studies have indicated its impacts on systemic health beyond dermatological symptoms. The gut-skin axis theory, which explores the interaction between gastrointestinal and skin health, may explain the influence of rosacea on gastrointestinal disorders. This study investigates the potential link between rosacea and the risk of developing irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) using the Korean National Health Insurance Service-National Sample Cohort (NHIS-NSC). Methods Data for this retrospective cohort study were sourced from 746,060 individuals in the NHIS-NSC database, classified according to the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases (KCD) version 6, based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision. The drugs were also identified by their GNL code in Korea. We focused on individuals diagnosed with rosacea and their subsequent development of IBS. This study was conducted with 1,087 patients diagnosed with Rosacea between 2002 and 2013 in NHIS-NSC. A total of 4,348 controls were matched at 1:4 based on sex, age, and index data. Adjustments were made for potential confounders such as age, sex, and comorbidities through multivariate Cox proportional hazard models, to assess the risk of IBS among patients with rosacea compared to those without it. Results Our findings show that individuals with rosacea have a significantly higher incidence of IBS than those without rosacea. After adjusting for multiple potential confounders, the association remained significant, with rosacea patients showing a 21% increased risk of developing IBS compared to controls. The study also noted that the incidence of IBS increased as rosacea became chronic. Conclusion The findings support the hypothesis that rosacea may be associated with an increased risk of IBS, potentially mediated by systemic inflammatory pathways and neurological interactions as suggested by the gut-skin axis. This indicates that gastrointestinal symptoms and diseases should be considered when managing rosacea patients. Further studies are needed to explore the underlying mechanisms of this association and to confirm these findings in other populations. These include cardiovascular disorders such as hypertension and coronary artery disease, 6 and autoimmune diseases such as type 1 diabetes. 7 Notably, the link between rosacea and neurological ailments, such as migraines, and psychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety, also highlights its potential systemic impact. 8 9 These relationships suggest that the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying rosacea are complex and multifactorial, involving not only inflammatory processes but also neurovascular and immune system dysfunction. 10 Thus, its effective management requires a holistic approach that addresses these diverse but potentially interconnected health issues. Rosacea and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) share common characteristics of chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation, suggesting potential comorbidity. IBS is characterized by symptoms such as abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits, which may be indicative of a broader systemic inflammatory process. This study aimed to investigate the link between rosacea and IBS through the gut-skin axis, elucidating conditions manifesting in patients with rosacea and supporting the development of therapeutic strategies that address both dermatological and gastrointestinal symptoms in a Korean population. METHODS Data collection This study utilized data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service-National Sample Cohort (NHIS-NSC), which includes longitudinal health insurance and health examination data covering approximately 2% (1 million) of the Korean population. The NHIS-NSC database includes anonymized records to maintain the privacy of the individuals. Study design and patients This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Korea National Health Insurance Service-National Sample Cohort (NHIS-NSC) spanning 2002–2013. Initially, we selected individuals aged 20 years or older, totaling 746,816 patients. Within this group, rosacea was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes L71.8 (Other rosacea) and L71.9 (Rosacea, unspecified), and cases were included if diagnosed at least once. IBS was identified using the ICD-10 code K58 (IBS), and cases were included if diagnosed at least once. From this group, we excluded 666 individuals who had been diagnosed with IBS before their rosacea index date and an additional 90 patients who had been prescribed treatment for rosacea in 2002. This process refined our final study population to 746,060 individuals ( Fig. 1 ). The case group included 235 IBS patients and 852 non-IBS patients, out of 1,087 individuals with rosacea. To control for confounding factors, we matched each rosacea patient with 4 control patients without a diagnosis of rosacea based on sex, age, and index date. The control group, comprising 683 IBS patients and 3,665 non-IBS patients, totaling 4,348 individuals, was confirmed to have no prior diagnosis of rosacea. The final analyzed groups consisted of 1,087 patients with rosacea and 4,348 without, allowing for focused analysis on the potential relationship between rosacea and the subsequent development of IBS ( Fig. 1 ). Covariates The covariates included age (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years), sex (male and female), income (high, medium, and low), region of residence (metropolitan and rural area), and comorbidities. Comorbidities were assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), on which the scores for all categories were combined to calculate a total score. 10 11 The higher that score, the higher the comorbidity burden. Statistical analysis The demographic characteristics and comparison of incidence of psychiatric disorders between the rosacea patients and the control group were measured by the χ 2 test. The Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank test were used to assess the cumulative incidence of psychiatric disorders in the rosacea patients and the control group. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were evaluated by multivariable Cox proportional hazard models. Dataset creation was performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software (ver. 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Results were considered statistically significant if P ≤ 0.05. Ethics statement This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kangwon National University Hospital (approval No. KNUH-2024-09-008). The requirement for informed consent was waived. RESULTS Characteristics of rosacea and control patients This study involved 1,087 rosacea patients and 4,348 controls. There were no significant differences in age or sex between groups. Rosacea patients had significantly ( P &lt; 0.001 unless stated otherwise) higher income levels (42.3% vs. 34.4%) and exhibited a greater overall comorbidity burden, as indicated by the CCI scores. Significant differences in underlying conditions between the groups included higher prevalence rates in the rosacea group for hypertension (5.9% vs. 3.9%), autoimmune diseases (1.1% vs. 0.3%), thyroid disorders (8.5% vs. 4.8%), alcohol dependence (3.3% vs. 2.1%), and depression (5.4% vs. 3.7%) ( Table 1 ). Kaplan-Meier curve analysis for assessing IBS risk Kaplan-Meier curve analysis shows that patients with rosacea had a higher cumulative incidence of IBS compared to the control group, as indicated by the Kaplan-Meier curve. Patients with rosacea consistently exhibited a higher cumulative incidence of IBS than the control group, with the difference between the 2 groups becoming more pronounced over time. A log-rank test was performed to compare the cumulative incidence rates between the 2 groups ( P &lt; 0.001). This statistically significant result indicates that patients with rosacea are at a substantially higher risk of developing IBS compared to the control group. These findings suggest a strong association between rosacea and an increased risk of IBS ( Fig. 2 ). Association between rosacea and IBS in different analytical models An initial univariate analysis revealed a significant risk of IBS among rosacea patients, with a HR of 1.46 (95% CI, 1.26–1.70). Adjusting for income slightly reduced the HR to 1.44, demonstrating that economic factors do not fully account for the increased risk. Further adjustments for both income and region resulted in an HR of 1.39 (95% CI, 1.19–1.61), indicating a minor influence of geographic factors. Adjusting for income, region, CCI score and comorbidity diseases resulted in an HR of 1.37 (95% CI, 1.18–1.59). The most comprehensively adjusted model, which included adjustments for income, region, CCI score, comorbid diseases and drugs, yielded an HR of 1.21 (95% CI, 1.03–1.41), confirming that rosacea is an independent risk factor for IBS ( Table 2 ). DISCUSSION Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the centrofacial region, including the cheeks, chin, nose, and eyes. 11 12 13 14 It is characterized by recurrent episodes of flushing, persistent erythema, inflammatory papules and pustules, and telangiectasia. A recent systemic review estimated the global prevalence of rosacea at 5.5% of the adult population. 15 This condition can lead to embarrassment, low self-esteem, anxiety, depression, and stigmatization, adversely impacting quality of life, social, and psychological well-being. 11 16 17 Recent studies have reported possible associations of rosacea with increased risks of cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, neurological, autoimmune, and psychiatric disorders, along with an increased risk of cancer. 18 19 20 Among these comorbidities, gastrointestinal conditions were the most frequently reported, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which consists of ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, celiac disease, IBS, gastroesophageal reflux disease, Helicobacter pylori infection, and small intestine bacterial overgrowth. 18 20 21 22 These associations suggest shared inflammatory pathways and perhaps a common genetic or microbial predisposition that underlies these conditions. 23 The emerging concept of the gut-skin axis proposes that gastrointestinal health influences skin homeostasis and allostasis through complex interactions between the immune, metabolic, and nervous systems. 24 25 The gut microbiome is regarded as a major regulator of the gut–skin axis, with a bidirectional modulation between the gut microbiome and host immunity. 24 25 26 Additionally, intestinal bacteria produce cytotoxins and nitric oxide that can cause inflammation of the gastric mucosa and affect physiological processes such as vasodilation and immune regulation. Notably, H. pylori has the ability to synthesize cytotoxins and gastrin, contributing to skin inflammation and flushing. 27 Our research identifies rosacea as an independent risk factor for IBS, revealing that the most comprehensive model adjusted for income, the CCI, disease score and drug, shows an adjusted HR of 1.21 (95% CI, 1.03–1.41). Our study consistently demonstrates an increasing risk of IBS among rosacea patients over an 8-year follow-up period, highlighting the growing disparity in IBS incidence compared to those without rosacea. This persistent risk, particularly significant with prolonged rosacea, suggests that the gastrointestinal effects of rosacea may evolve or intensify over time. Previous studies have reported an increased prevalence of gastrointestinal diseases among rosacea patients but did not quantify the temporal progression of this risk. Our research addresses this gap by demonstrating that the risk of developing IBS not only exists but also increases with the chronicity of rosacea. The increasing risk of developing IBS with prolonged rosacea may be influenced by several factors. First, continuous exposure to inflammatory triggers such as certain foods, alcohol, and stress, which can exacerbate rosacea, might lead to chronic inflammation. 28 This ongoing inflammation may activate similar inflammatory pathways that are also involved in IBS. For example, peripheral blood mononuclear cells in patients with IBS have been found to produce high levels of tumor necrosis factor, a cytokine implicated in rosacea. 29 Second, changes in the skin and gut microbiome are evident in rosacea patients. 30 Dysbiosis, whether due to chronic inflammation or the effects of antibiotics, can impair gut barrier function, potentially inducing or exacerbating IBS. 31 Lastly, changes in vascular neuromodulation might also play a crucial role. Neurovascular changes observed in rosacea patients could affect gut motility and sensitivity, contributing to the onset of IBS symptoms. 32 33 34 35 36 There are multiple limitations of this study that warrant further discussion. Although we investigated the incidence of IBD among patients with rosacea, we did not explore the occurrence of rosacea in patients diagnosed with IBD. To understand fully the bidirectional relationship between these conditions, future studies should examine statistically the incidence of rosacea among individuals with IBD. Additionally, as this was a retrospective study using NHIS-NSC data, several potential influential factors were not addressed. Subtypes, severity, and specific laboratory parameters of rosacea were outside the scope of this study due to data limitations. External factors such as diet, stress, occupational factors, and environmental influences, which could affect the incidence of rosacea, were also not included in the analysis. Furthermore, reliance on KCD codes for disease classification may introduce inaccuracies, as misclassification or incorrect coding could skew the results. In conclusion, our study highlights the interrelationship between rosacea and IBS, identifying rosacea as a risk factor for developing IBS. Through an 8-year analysis, we demonstrated that the risk of IBS continues to increase with the duration of rosacea. This finding suggests that the approach to treating rosacea may need to evolve. We recognize the systemic and broad health impacts of rosacea and present these findings with the view that comprehensive patient care, including attention to gastrointestinal health as well as skin symptoms, is necessary.
