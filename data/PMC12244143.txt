Studies in adults have identified potential biomarkers for therapeutic response in depression; however, less is known in the context of depression in young people. Understanding predictive biomarkers could improve treatment selection, considering the low treatment response rates in adolescents and young adults with depression. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between cortisol, immune, and neural markers and treatment outcomes of psychotherapy or antidepressant treatment in young people with depression. We searched OVID Medline, EMBASE, Global Health, and PsycINFO for peer-reviewed studies investigating cortisol, immune and neuroimaging markers in adolescents diagnosed with MDD, ages 10–24, undergoing psychotherapy or antidepressant treatment. Of 25 included studies, 15 assessed neural markers, 8 assessed immune markers, 1 assessed cortisol markers, and 1 assessed neural and cortisol markers. Elevated cortisol response to stress was associated with greater symptom improvement following psychotherapy or antidepressant treatment. Findings regarding the impact of SSRIs on Interleukin (IL)- 1 beta, IL-4, IL-6 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are inconsistent, suggesting a potential inflammatory subtype but also highlighting the need for further research. Increased activation and connectivity in brain regions involved in emotion regulation and decreased connectivity of the default mode network were associated with better treatment outcomes after psychotherapy and antidepressant treatment. Our results suggest that neural and immune markers might be involved in the treatment mechanisms through which depression improves. We identified several potential biomarkers associated with treatment outcomes; however, the heterogeneity among study designs and biomarker measurements was high. Future research should investigate biomarkers of differential treatment response, replicate existing studies and focus on predictive analyses of treatment outcomes. Meyer-Lindenberg 1 Introduction Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a chronic mental health disorder affecting around 7–11% of adolescents worldwide before the Covid-19 pandemic ( Avenevoli et al., 2015 ; Balazs et al., 2012 ), and a global estimate of 25.2% of adolescents during the Covid-19 pandemic ( Racine et al., 2021 ). MDD negatively impacts psychosocial functioning, and pre-adult onset depression is associated with an increased risk of comorbid disorders, interpersonal difficulties, and adolescent suicide ( Tuisku et al., 2014 ; Zisook et al., 2007 ), which is the second leading cause of death in 15–19-year-olds (L. Liu et al., 2022 ). Intervening at an early stage during adolescence and young adulthood has the potential to change the trajectory away from chronic mental health disorders and towards better chances in life. However, the clinical heterogeneity of adolescent depression and the wide spectrum of biological mechanisms underlying its onset are responsible for a large variation in response to mental health interventions ( Manfro et al., 2022 ). Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is the first choice of treatment for young people with depression, and fluoxetine, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), is usuallyrecommended for young people up to 18 years with more severe depression or when psychotherapy is unsuccessful or not available ( NICE, 2019 ). Young people older than 18 might also be recommended other psychotropic treatments, such as serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) ( NICE, 2022 ), but these are not recommended for under 18-year-olds. Other empirically supported psychotherapies for young people include Interpersonal Therapy (IPT), family therapy, psychodynamic psychotherapy, non-directive supportive therapy (NDST), and brief psychosocial intervention ( NICE, 2019 ). However, many young people with depression do not respond to treatment, with response rates to psychotherapy or SSRI treatment estimated at 41%–67% ( Cuijpers et al., 2021 ; Hetrick et al., 2007 ). Modest response rates to treatment in individuals with depression have been attributed to the heterogeneity of the disorder, with each individual benefitting from a different type of treatment ( Goldberg, 2011 ). Biomarkers that could predict which type of treatment an individual would be most likely to respond to have the potential to improve the current "trial and error" paradigm of treatment selection and advance precision psychiatry ( Arns et al., 2022 ). Research in adults with MDD has already identified several potential biomarkers predictive of treatment response, such as cortisol, immune, and neuroimaging markers ( Chamberlain et al., 2019 ; Fischer et al., 2017 ; Nikkheslat et al., 2020 ; Perlman et al., 2019 ). Neuroimaging studies have reported higher anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) activity and decreased connectivity within the default mode network (DMN) to be associated with better antidepressant response in adults with depression ( Perlman et al., 2019 ; Pizzagalli, 2011 ). Elevated levels of c-reactive protein (CRP) and elevated whole blood mRNA expression of interleukin (IL)-1-beta, macrophage inhibiting factor (MIF) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) have been found to distinguish treatment-resistant from responsive adults with depression ( Cattaneo et al., 2020 ; Chamberlain et al., 2019 ). Furthermore, responders to antidepressant augmentation with minocycline had higher baseline IL-6 and CRP concentrations than non-responders ( Nettis et al., 2021 ). Similarly, higher pre-treatment cortisol levels in depressed adults have been associated with less improvement after psychotherapy ( Fischer et al., 2017 ). Only a few studies have investigated biological mechanisms of recovery in adolescents and young adults; however, similar to research in adults, existing studies mainly reported on cortisol, immune and neuroimaging markers ( Zajkowska et al., 2021 ), Despite the growing body of research on biomarkers in adults, few studies have investigated biomarkers in adolescents and young adults with MDD. Since the immune system, the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and brain structure and function have been reported to change and develop until the age of 25, biomarkers associated with treatment response in adolescents and young adults might differ from adults and should be investigated separately from them ( Goddings et al., 2019 ; Roberts and Lopez-Duran, 2019 ; Stumper et al., 2020 ). However, no systematic review of biomarkers associated with treatment outcomes in young people with depression has been conducted so far. 1.1 Aims and objectives This systematic review aimed to investigate: a) biomarkers including cortisol, immune and brain abnormalities associated with treatment outcomes and b) changes in these biomarkers following treatment in adolescents and young adults with MDD. Since psychotherapy and SSRI treatment are the recommended treatment choices for young people with depression, this review focused on outcomes following empirically supported psychotherapy and SSRI treatment. 2 Methods 2.1 Literature search strategy The protocol for this systematic review was not pre-registered; however, the review was conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) ( Page et al., 2021 ). We used broad search terms across four databases from inception until March 2023: OVID Medline, EMBASE, Global Health, and PsycINFO. Only peer-reviewed studies measuring cortisol, immune and neuroimaging markers in adolescents with MDD undergoing psychological or SSRI treatment were eligible for inclusion. The search terms were devised based on the existing literature in the field, empirically supported psychological treatments, NICE (2019) recommended depression treatments for children and adolescents within the UK and discussions between the authors. Considering the already wide scope of the review, we decided to focus only on talking therapies and medication. We included search terms for NICE (2019) recommended and empirically supported psychological therapies: CBT, acceptance and commitment therapy, interpersonal psychotherapy, family therapy, non-directive supportive therapy and mindfulness-based interventions. For a complete list of search terms, see Appendix. Each search included search terms for Adolescents AND Depression and either search terms for SSRI treatment or psychotherapy, with an AND search term for each biomarker separately (e.g., "Adolescents AND Depression AND Psychotherapy AND Cortisol"). Since we had three types of biomarkers (cortisol, immune, and neuroimaging) and two types of treatment (psychological and SSRI treatment), we ran six separate searches to identify studies that examined a biomarker in relation to either psychological or SSRI treatment. 2.2 Eligibility of studies After deduplication, studies were assessed for eligibility according to the PICO model ( Richardson et al., 1995 ). We included studies if the following inclusion criteria were met: 1) a MDD diagnosis determined through a categorical diagnostic interview or a validated clinical measure of depressive symptoms; 2) adolescents and young adults 10–24 years old; 3) administration of either psychological therapy or SSRI treatment 4) depressive symptoms as an outcome assessed by a validated clinical measure 5) cross-sectional or longitudinal measurement of a cortisol, immune or neuroimaging marker; 6) publication in a peer-reviewed journal in the English language. Given the high comorbidity of adolescent depression with anxiety disorders and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and the small number of studies investigating biomarkers in young people with depression, we decided to include studies with these comorbidities in this review ( Bron et al., 2016 ; Garber and Weersing, 2010 ). Studies were excluded if any of the following exclusion criteria were met: 1) absence of MDD; 2) a comorbid diagnosis other than an anxiety disorder or ADHD 3) participants were exclusively part of a specific medical subpopulation (e.g., adolescents with diabetes) 4) participants had an inflammatory or neurological condition other than ADHD. 2.3 Analysis Full-text screenings and quality assessments were carried out by one reviewer (AZ). Any uncertainties regarding study inclusion or quality assessment were resolved through discussion (with ZZ, VM). The data extraction was carried out by one reviewer (AZ) under the supervision of the co-authors (ZZ, VM). The Systematic Assessment of Quality in Observational Research (SAQOR) and the SAQOR recommendations for the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) rankings were used to assess the quality of the included studies (see Table 5 ) ( Ross et al., 2011 ). The GRADE has four categories for the quality of evidence: very low, low, moderate, and high. Based on the study's design and the number of SAQOR categories that were rated as "adequate", we determined the final modified GRADE rating presented in this review. Studies that used a randomized controlled design start at high quality, while studies with an observational design start at low quality due to the risk of residual confounding variables ( Guyatt et al., 2011 ). A study was given a "very low" grade under the modified GRADE ranking if fewer than four SAQOR categories were rated as "adequate". 3 Results 3.1 Systematic search Out of 3599 articles (2871 after removing duplicates), 25 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review (see Fig. 1 ). Two studies, including 28 participants, investigated cortisol markers (see Table 1 ), 8 studies, including 290 distinct participants, investigated immune markers (see Table 2 ), and 16 studies, including 483 distinct participants, investigated neuroimaging markers (see Table 3 ). One study investigated both cortisol and neuroimaging markers (see Table 1 , Table 3 ) and is therefore counted twice. 3.2 Cortisol stress response Two studies found that higher baseline levels of cortisol in response to a stress test predicted greater depression improvement after both SSRI and interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) treatment (see Table 1 ). Adolescents who improved in depressive symptoms after eight weeks of SSRIs had significantly higher levels of cortisol after the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST) compared with those who did not improve after SSRI treatment ( Klimes-Dougan et al., 2018 ). Similarly, higher cortisol levels 30 min after a conflict task predicted greater improvement in depressive symptoms after 16 weeks of IPT ( Gunlicks-Stoessel et al., 2013 ). 3.3 Immune markers Eight studies examining immune markers met our inclusion criteria; however, due to multiple publications on one sample, a total of 6 distinct samples were included in this review (see Table 3 ). The most frequently studied immune markers were IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, and TNF-α, each cited in three or more studies (see Table 4 ). Other immune markers, including MCP-1, IL-2, IL-5, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-13, IL-1Ra, IL-17A, Maresin-1, and IFN-γ, were reported on in only one or two studies. 3.3.1 Immune markers associated with treatment outcome One study reported that higher levels of plasma TNF-α at baseline were associated with non-response to fluoxetine ( Zohar et al., 2018 ). Similarly, higher serum monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) levels after 8 months of SSRI treatment were associated with more severe depressive symptoms ( Martins et al., 2021 ). Higher levels of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and IL-10 were associated with less severe depressive symptomology at pre-and post-treatment (H. Lee et al., 2020 ) Puangsri et al. (2023) reported increased lymphocyte percentages, a decreased monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) and decreased neutrophil and monocyte percentages after 12 weeks of SSRI treatment in treatment responders compared with non-responders. Furthermore, non-responders had a higher platelet count at baseline compared with responders. 3.3.2 Immune marker changes following SSRI treatment One study reported decreases in TNF-α levels after SSRI treatment ( Martins et al., 2021 ). In contrast, H. Lee et al. (2020) , Pérez-Sánchez et al. (2018) and Zohar et al. (2018) reported no significant changes in plasma and serum levels of TNF-α, respectively, after SSRI treatment. One study reported decreased serum levels of IL-8 and MCP-1 after SSRI treatment ( Martins et al., 2021 ). However, de la Peña et al. (2020) reported no significant decrease in IL-8 and MCP-1 after SSRI treatment. H. Lee et al. (2020) reported increased plasma levels of IFN-γ, IL-2 and IL-10 after 12 weeks of SSRI treatment. Pérez-Sánchez et al. (2018) also reported increased serum levels of IL-2 and IL-5 but no significant changes in IFN-γ and IL-10 levels after 8 weeks of SSRI treatment. Qiu et al. (2023) and Pérez-Sánchez et al. (2018) both found increased serum levels of IL-4 after SSRI treatment; however, this was not replicated by H. Lee et al. (2020) . Qiu et al. (2023) reported decreased serum levels of IL-6 and IL-1β after treatment; however, Martins et al. (2021) , H. Lee et al. (2020) , and Pérez-Sánchez et al. (2018) did not find any changes after treatment. Becerril-Villanueva et al. (2019) found no significant changes in IL-7, IL-9, or IL-17A levels after eight weeks of SSRI treatment. Similarly, de la Peña et al. (2020) did not report a significant change in MCP-1, macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-1α, MIP-1β, IL-8, interferon gamma-induced protein (IP)-10, and eotaxin levels after eight weeks of SSRI treatment. Puangsri et al. (2023) did not find any change in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) after treatment. 3.4 Neuroimaging markers Sixteen neuroimaging studies met our inclusion criteria; however, due to multiple publications on three bigger studies (IMPACT, Cullen, Straub), a total of 9 distinct samples were included in this review (see Table 3 ). Some studies used more than one neuroimaging modality and are therefore discussed in more than one section. 3.4.1 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) One study found increased cortical thickness in the left medial orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) after SSRI treatment ( Koenig et al., 2018 ). However, SSRI-related changes in cortical thickness were not correlated with clinical improvement after treatment. Klimes-Dougan et al. (2022) reported an association between less left anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) cortical thickness at baseline and depressive symptom improvement at mid-treatment of IPT-A. No additional associations between cortical thickness and symptom improvement were found after treatment. 3.4.2 Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) J. Lee et al. (2021) reported decreased fractional anisotropy (FA) values in the left anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC), the left posterior limb of the internal capsule (PLIC), the left superior corona radiata (SCR), the left anterior thalamic radiation (ATR), the left superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), and the left corticospinal tract (CT) after 12 weeks of SSRI treatment. The change in FA values was not associated with depressive symptom change, but the severity of depressive symptoms was positively correlated with the mean FA values at baseline. A second study found that increased white matter connectivity of the right thalamus and the left middle frontal gyrus with other brain areas at baseline predicted better improvement after CBT treatment ( Tymofiyeva et al., 2019 ). 3.4.3 Reward-related neural activity Straub et al. (2015) reported decreased activation in the bilateral amygdala, the bilateral subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC), and the bilateral hippocampus during reward processing in adolescents with MDD after CBT. Greater activation of the right sgACC during reward processing at pre-treatment was associated with greater symptom improvement. Furthermore, greater CBT-related decreases in right and left sgACC activity during reward processing were associated with greater symptom reduction ( Straub et al., 2015 ). An electroencephalography (EEG) study found that theta response to losses, which was elevated in adolescents with MDD relative to healthy controls, decreased after CBT ( Webb et al., 2021 ). They also reported that a larger late positive potential (LPP) response to rewards and a larger delta response to losses at pre-CBT positively predicted depressive symptom improvement. However, pre-to post-CBT reductions in theta response to losses were not associated with depressive symptom improvement ( Webb et al., 2021 ). Forbes et al. (2010) , who measured striatal and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) reactivity during reward anticipation and outcome pre-CBT, did not find any association with symptom improvement post-CBT. 3.4.4 Emotion-task-related neural activity Chuang et al. (2016) reported that adolescents with MDD displayed significantly elevated activation in the left OFC during emotion processing of happy stimuli compared with healthy controls before CBT. After CBT treatment, OFC activation in adolescents with MDD decreased to the point that group differences in OFC activation between healthy controls and adolescents with MDD disappeared. However, neither baseline OFC activation nor pre-to post-CBT signal change during emotion processing predicted symptom improvement ( Chuang et al., 2016 ). Klimes-Dougan et al. (2022) found an association between greater right ACC activation during negative emotion processing at baseline and greater depressive symptom improvement at mid-treatment of IPT-A. Greater right ACC activation was also associated with greater symptom improvement after treatment. Similarly, Klimes-Dougan et al. (2018) reported that greater activation of the bilateral ACC and the left medial frontal gyrus during negative emotion processing at baseline was associated with greater symptom decline after SSRI treatment. Furthermore, in another study, greater decreases in bilateral rostral and subgenual ACC and inferior frontal cortex activity during negative emotion processing after SSRI treatment were associated with greater symptom reduction. Increased emotion-task-related activity in the right parahippocampus, the bilateral insula, the bilateral middle frontal cortices, and the left cerebellum after SSRI treatment were also associated with greater clinical improvement ( Cullen et al., 2016 ). 3.4.5 Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) Three studies reported that resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) between the amygdala/sgACC and regions of the affective, cognitive, and salience network increased after CBT ( Chattopadhyay et al., 2017 ; Straub et al., 2017 ; Villa et al., 2020 ). One study also reported decreased rsFC between the right amygdala and precuneus/calcarine fissure after CBT ( Straub et al., 2017 ). Symptom severity change was associated with rsFC change between the sgACC and brain regions in the cognitive control and affective network and between the amygdala and brain regions in the cognitive control, affective, and salience networks ( Straub et al., 2017 ; Villa et al., 2020 ). Furthermore, regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) increased from pre-to post-CBT; however, this increase was not associated with clinical improvement ( Sosic-Vasic et al., 2017 ). Higher pre-treatment rsFC between the amygdala and the left anterior insula/left dlPFC and between the right sgACC and the right dlPFC predicted better treatment outcomes after CBT ( Straub et al., 2017 ). Similarly, greater rsFC between the left amygdala and the ACC was associated with greater depressive symptom improvement after IPT-A ( Klimes-Dougan et al., 2022 ). Compared with CBT, SSRI treatment was associated with decreases in rsFC from pre-to post-treatment. SSRI treatment was associated with decreased rsFC between the right dorsal ACC and the left precentral gyrus ( Chi et al., 2021 ). However, Chi et al. (2021) did not find a correlation between rsFC change and clinical outcomes. Chu et al. (2021) reported decreased rsFC between brain areas involved in emotional regulation and processing (hippocampus, pars opercularis, fusiform, thalamus), regions in the DMN, and the cognitive control network after SSRI treatment. Reduction in depression severity was associated with pre-to-post-treatment increases in rsFC between the amygdala and the right medial and middle frontal gyri and decreases between the amygdala and the precuneus/calcarine fissure ( Cullen et al., 2016 ). Increased pre-treatment rsFC between the amygdala and the right central parietal opercular cortex/the Heschl's gyrus ( Klimes-Dougan et al., 2018 ) and between the right medial orbitofrontal cortex and the right supramarginal gyrus was associated with better treatment outcome ( Chu et al., 2021 ). On the other hand, increased rsFC between the amygdala and the right precentral gyrus/the left supplementary motor area (SMA) predicted a worse treatment outcome after SSRI treatment ( Klimes-Dougan et al., 2018 ). 3.5 Quality assessment Regarding the quality assessments, 2 studies were rated as "moderate quality", 18 were rated as "low quality", and 5 were rated as "very low quality".(see Table 5 ) ( Guyatt et al., 2011 ). The modified grade ranking "low quality" was given frequently, as most studies did not include a comparison group and reported limited information about controlling for distorting influences. 4 Discussion This systematic review identified possible cortisol, immune and neuroimaging markers that might be associated with treatment outcomes and changes in these biomarkers following treatment in young people with MDD. In particular, elevated cortisol and immune system dysregulation as well as changes in the neural connectivity between, and activity in areas of emotion processing, the salience network, and the default mode network (DMN) seem to be associated with better treatment outcomes. Contrary to research in adults, which reported that higher cortisol levels were associated with worse treatment outcomes after psychotherapy or SSRI treatment ( Fischer et al., 2017 ), this review suggests that higher cortisol levels after a stress task may correlate with greater improvement in depressive symptoms after IPT or SSRI treatment ( Gunlicks-Stoessel et al., 2013 ; Klimes-Dougan et al., 2018 ). However, neither study compared the cortisol stress response of adolescents with MDD with healthy controls. It is, therefore, unclear whether the cortisol levels in adolescents with greater depression improvement were elevated or whether the cortisol levels in adolescents with less depression improvement were reduced compared with healthy controls. Adolescents with a higher cortisol stress response might be more reactive to psychosocial stressors and their environment, which could explain why they benefit more from CBT than adolescents who are less reactive to their environment ( Boyce and Ellis, 2005 ). A lower cortisol stress response, in turn, might indicate blunted HPA axis functioning, which has been associated in some cases with early childhood trauma ( Mayer et al., 2020 ; Roberts and Lopez-Duran, 2019 ). Childhood trauma was found to be associated with worse treatment responses to CBT and antidepressant treatment in adolescents with MDD ( Lewis et al., 2010 ). Childhood trauma might therefore be a confounding factor leading to both blunted HPA stress reactivity and worse treatment outcomes in the studies discussed above. However, the limited number of studies investigating cortisol levels and treatment outcomes in young people with depression and their exploratory nature severely limits the interpretability of these findings. Future research should include a healthy control group and measures of early trauma to clarify the role of the HPA axis in the treatment response of young people with depression. Our review identified studies investigating immune markers in relation to SSRI treatment and not psychotherapy outcomes. Only four immune markers (IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, and TNF-α - see Table 4 ) were examined by at least three studies, with reporting on other immune markers such as IL-8, MCP-1, IL-2, IL-10, and IFN-γ limited to one or two studies, indicating the need for further research in this area. Findings were heterogeneous, and due to the small number of exploratory studies investigating the same immune markers, it is unclear whether the mixed findings reflect developmental differences, the duration of illness or the heterogeneity and small sample sizes of the reported studies. Furthermore, only two studies reported whether they examined fasting blood samples ( Martins et al., 2021 ; Qiu et al., 2023 ), adding to the heterogeneity of included studies. While previous research consistently reported fluoxetine to modulate IL-1β and IL-6 levels in adults with depression ( García-García et al., 2022 ), our review found mixed results. Specifically, only one study in our review reported decreased IL-1β and IL-6 levels after treatment with fluoxetine ( Qiu et al., 2023 ), while three studies did not find any changes (H. Lee et al., 2020 ; Martins et al., 2021 ; Pérez-Sánchez et al., 2018 ). Pérez-Sánchez et al. (2018) reported mid-treatment decreases in IL-6 levels, but they returned to baseline levels after treatment. Martins et al. (2021) and Zohar et al. (2018) reported no significant difference in baseline IL-1β levels compared to healthy control, which aligns with earlier studies in adolescents with MDD ( Calarge et al., 2019 ; Öztürk et al., 2021 ). Interestingly, the studies that found a reduction in IL-6 levels were the ones that reported elevated levels at baseline ( Pérez-Sánchez et al., 2018 ; Qiu et al., 2023 ), while the studies that did not find any changes did not report elevated baseline levels of IL-6 (H. Lee et al., 2020 ; Martins et al., 2021 ). This suggests that SSRIs might be more effective in individuals with an inflammatory subtype of depression. This is in line with the recent findings from a clinical trial in adults, looking at minocycline as an add-on treatment to existing antidepressant medication, which showed that responders to minocycline had higher baseline IL-6 levels compared with non-responders ( Nettis et al., 2021 ). Our review found mixed evidence regarding the effect of SSRIs on TNF-α in young people with depression, with reports of both decreased ( Martins et al., 2021 ) and unchanged TNF-α after SSRI treatment (H. Lee et al., 2020 ; Pérez-Sánchez et al., 2018 ; Zohar et al., 2018 ). Interestingly, Pérez-Sánchez et al. (2018) found decreased TNF-α levels mid-treatment; however, baseline levels were restored after eight weeks of SSRI treatment. The heterogeneity between findings could be due to the differences in baseline cytokine levels between the studies or possibly the heterogeneity of depression itself, highlighting the need for further investigation in young people with depression. A meta-analysis of studies investigating adults with depression showed reduced TNF-α after antidepressant treatment (J. J. Liu et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2019 ), which is in line with findings by Martins et al. (2021) and Zohar et al. (2018) . TNF-α is synthesized by dendritic cells and macrophages and plays a crucial role in activating downstream inflammatory pathways ( Tracey, 2002 ). TNF-α is also implicated in kynurenine pathway activation and can indirectly lead to increased kynurenine and decreased serotonin expression, which was previously associated with more severe depressive symptoms ( Haroon et al., 2020 ). TNF-α has been found to be significantly elevated in adults with depression ( Osimo et al., 2020 ) and elevated at trend level in adolescents with depression compared to healthy controls ( D'Acunto et al., 2019 ). Our review found an association between baseline elevated TNF-α levels and non-SSRI response ( Zohar et al., 2018 ). This finding is consistent with previous research in adults ( Cattaneo et al., 2013 , 2020 ; Choi et al., 2021 ) and suggests the possibility of an inflammatory subtype of depression that may require a different treatment approach. Our review results showed findings of both increased IL-4 ( Pérez-Sánchez et al., 2018 ; Qiu et al., 2023 ) and unchanged IL-4 levels (H. Lee et al., 2020 ) after SSRI treatment. A previous meta-analysis in adults with MDD reported unchanged IL-4 levels after SSRI treatment; however, similar to our findings, heterogeneity between included studies was high ( Wang et al., 2019 ). IL-4 is theorized to improve stress-induced depression by indirectly triggering brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) production and downregulating inflammatory cytokines ( Harsanyi et al., 2023 ). Increased levels of IL-4 after treatment might reflect an anti-inflammatory effect of SSRIs on the immune system. Only one study investigated the effect of SSRI treatment on inflammatory ratios in adolescents with MDD ( Puangsri et al., 2023 ). Interestingly, the results were consistent with a possible anti-inflammatory effect of SSRIs; however, since there was only one study reporting on this outcome, the findings should be interpreted with caution. SSRI treatment seemed to induce more changes in lymphocyte and monocyte percentages compared to neutrophil or platelet counts, which may indicate that improvement in depressive symptoms is linked to changes in lymphocyte and monocyte populations. These findings are consistent with an earlier study by Amitai et al. (2022) , who investigated the effect of SSRIs on adolescents with MDD or anxiety disorder. In contrast, adult studies have reported a decreased neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) after SSRI treatment ( Demircan et al., 2016 ; Öztürk et al., 2019 ). Inflammatory ratios, including the NLR, have been reported to differ across age groups ( Moosmann et al., 2022 ); therefore, SSRIs may have different immune-modulating effects in young people compared to adults. Of note is that the participants in Puangsri et al. (2023) were between 18 and 24 years old, representing a slightly older cohort than most of the other studies included here. Interestingly, Puangsri et al. (2023) observed a higher platelet count at baseline in SSRI non-responders compared with responders. A higher platelet count might be associated with SSRI non-response by elevating inflammatory cytokines related to antidepressant treatment resistance ( Carvalho et al., 2013 ; Cattaneo et al., 2020 ). The results from studies in our review that investigated the OFC suggest a role of treatment (SSRI and CBT) in affecting cortical thickness and function of the OFC. However, the OFC thickness and function changes appear not specifically related to treatment response and are potentially more associated with disease progression ( Chuang et al., 2016 ; Koenig et al., 2018 ; Villa et al., 2020 ). Our review found that higher neural reward-responsiveness at pre-treatment might be associated with greater symptom improvement after CBT ( Straub et al., 2015 ; Webb et al., 2021 ), which is in contrast to findings in adults with depression ( Burkhouse et al., 2018 ; Kujawa et al., 2019 ). The reward circuitry undergoes reorganization during adolescence, with changes in the mesolimbic dopamine pathway and the PFC rendering adolescents hypersensitive to rewards ( Walker et al., 2017 ). Previous research found reduced reward-related activity in the lateral prefrontal cortex in adolescents with depression and at high risk of depression compared to healthy adolescents ( Yoon et al., 2022 ). One possible interpretation of our findings could be that higher neural reward-responsiveness in adolescents with depression may reflect a more responsive reward system that is more amenable to change with CBT. Developmental differences in the reward circuitry between adolescents and adults, such as greater neural plasticity that is present in the developing adolescent brain ( Walker et al., 2017 ), may contribute to differences in their response to CBT. Furthermore, Webb et al. (2021) found that increased LPP, which reflects attention to emotionally or motivationally salient content, was associated with better treatment outcomes, which might suggest that adolescents who can sustain engagement to rewards are more likely to be successful with CBT. Greater ACC activation at baseline was associated with greater depressive symptom improvement after IPT-A and SSRI treatment ( Klimes-Dougan et al., 2018 , 2022 ), replicating findings in adults with MDD ( Fu et al., 2013 ; Pizzagalli, 2011 ). While ACC response does not seem to differentially predict treatment response to pharmacological or psychotherapeutic interventions, lower ACC activation at baseline seems to identify individuals with poor prognoses who might benefit from more intensive treatment from the start. Both SSRI treatment and CBT led to decreased rsFC between the sgACC and the precuneus/calcarine fissure ( Cullen et al., 2016 ; Straub et al., 2017 ), possibly reflecting a decrease in DMN activity, which is reported to be hyperconnected in depression ( Kaiser et al., 2015 ). DMN activity is associated with rumination and self-referential processing ( Kim, 2012 ), and the normalization of DMN connectivity after CBT and SSRI treatment possibly reflects a shared treatment mechanism. Overall, pre-treatment connectivity between the amygdala, the ACC and the dlPFC was associated with CBT and IPT-A response ( Klimes-Dougan et al., 2022 ; Straub et al., 2017 ). The dlPFC, the ACC and the amygdala are thought to be part of the cognitive control network (CNN), which is responsible for regulating thoughts and actions ( Li et al., 2018 ). On the other hand, pre-treatment connectivity between the amygdala and frontal brain regions was associated with SSRI response ( Cullen et al., 2016 ; Klimes-Dougan et al., 2018 ). These findings might reflect the different treatment mechanisms of antidepressant treatment and psychotherapy, with antidepressant treatment potentially acting on the fronto-limbic system to improve the negative emotional processing bias indicative of MDD and psychotherapy potentially improving cognitive control ( Disner et al., 2011 ; Li et al., 2018 ). However, it is difficult to draw clear conclusions about treatment mechanisms based on the current literature, as existing studies focus on different neuroimaging techniques, brain regions and therapies. Although this review compares the impact of SSRIs and psychological treatments on brain structures and functions, it is important to note that these represent different treatment modalities and further research is needed to determine whether there are shared or distinct treatment mechanisms. The limited number of studies in this area and their exploratory nature do not really allow an in-depth analysis of the treatment targets and potential mechanisms that underly both psychotherapeutic and psychopharmacological treatment of depression.One limitation of this systematic review is the small number of exploratory studies looking at the same biomarker and the same treatment, which becomes apparent when grouping them by measurement modality and by the low quality of the studies according to the GRADE framework. Furthermore, while the GRADE framework assesses the quality of the evidence presented, we did not measure the risk of bias of the included studies. This and the differences in biomarker measurement and analysis make it difficult to draw strong conclusions about treatment-specific or overarching biomarker change and their association with treatment outcomes. We also acknowledge that reported changes in biomarkers after treatment might reflect maturational changes, as adolescence and young adulthood are periods of change for neural connectivity, the HPA axis, and the immune system ( Goddings et al., 2019 ; Roberts and Lopez-Duran, 2019 ; Stumper et al., 2020 ). While most of the included studies were within a similar age range; it should be noted that we included a very heterogenous age group in this review. As a result, it is important to consider the different maturational states within and between the included studies, as well as differing therapy recommendations for depression treatment among children, adolescents, and young adults. For example, SNRI treatment and other psychotropic medicines, while not recommended for under 18-year-olds, are used for individuals over 18 ( NICE, 2022 ). However, as this review predominantly includes studies with participants aged 11–19, SSRI treatment is the most relevant choice for this age group. Moreover, the inclusion of ADHD as a comorbid condition with depression might influence our findings, given that 5 out of 17 distinct samples in this review included participants with comorbid ADHD. Lastly, it is important to note that this review focuses on empirically supported psychological therapies and SSRIs, excluding other relevant treatments, such as exercise-based interventions for this age group, which are part of brief psychosocial intervention treatment for children and adolescents and present an interesting area for further research. Therefore, this review highlights the need for further good-quality research and replication studies investigating biomarkers associated with treatment outcomes in young people with MDD. This is of particular importance, considering that adolescence and young adulthood represent a critical window for the development of neural, immune, and stress systems and for early intervention, which might prevent long-term detrimental changes in these systems. 5 Conclusions In conclusion, our review found some evidence for the involvement of stress, immune and neural systems in the treatment process and identified potential predictors of treatment outcomes in young people. Biomarkers associated with treatment outcomes in young people with MDD seem to partially replicate findings in adults, particularly when focusing on immune and neural markers. In contrast, some differences in findings in adults were reported in the context of cortisol levels and HPA axis response to stress. However, as the number of studies is still limited and exploratory in nature, further research is needed to identify and replicate biomarkers which may be more relevant for depression in adolescence and young adulthood.
