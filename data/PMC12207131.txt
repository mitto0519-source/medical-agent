Abstract Background Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated that a considerable proportion of patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) frequently exhibit pronounced dyslipidaemia. However, the causal dynamics between MDD and dyslipidaemia remain elusive. Aims To comprehensively disentangle the genetic causality between MDD and various phenotypes of blood lipids, thereby facilitating the advancement of management strategies for these conditions. Methods We conducted a two-sample univariable Mendelian randomisation (MR) analysis using different models, including the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method and causal analysis using the summary effect (CAUSE) estimates, as well as a multivariable MR analysis. This analysis used summary statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of MDD and five lipid traits: low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol and triglycerides (TG), encompassing 5 237 893 individuals of European and East Asian ancestries. For MDD, a total of 598 701 individuals were included, with 500 199 individuals of European ancestry (N case =170 756, N control =329 443) and 98 502 of East Asian ancestry (N case =12 588, N control =85 914). Lipid data were collected from 4 639 192 individuals through the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (European, N=4 096 085; East Asian, N=543 107). Next, we used the two-step MR to explore the mediating factors between MDD and TG, and the risk factors affecting TG through MDD. Finally, we conducted a GWAS meta-analysis and enrichment analysis. Results In univariable MR, we observed a negative causal effect of low-density lipoprotein on MDD in both European populations (IVW: odds ratio (OR): 0.972, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.947 to 0.998, p=0.037) and East Asian populations (IVW: OR: 0.928, 95% CI 0.864 to 0.997, p=0.042). Additionally, we identified a bidirectional causal relationship between TG and MDD, with TG having a causal effect on MDD (IVW: OR: 1.052, 95% CI 1.020 to 1.085, p=0.001) and MDD having a causal effect on TG (IVW: OR: 1.075, 95% CI 1.047 to 1.104, p&lt;0.001). Multivariable MR analysis further supported the role of TG in MDD (OR: 1.205, 95% CI 1.034 to 1.405, p=0.017). CAUSE estimates indicated that the causal model of MDD on TG provided a better fit than the sharing model (p=0.003), while the association of TG on MDD was more likely due to horizontal correlated pleiotropy than causality. Mediation analyses revealed that waist-hip ratio (WHR) mediated 69% of the total causal effect of MDD on TG, while other identified risk factors exhibited lower mediating proportions either mediated through MDD (≤17%) or originating from MDD (≤29%). The GWAS meta-analysis highlighted potential pathways related to lipid processes and nucleosome assembling, with significant cell types identified in brain regions and liver tissues. Conclusions The findings indicate that genetic proxies of MDD are associated with elevated levels of TG, with WHR serving as a clinical indicator of the association. This suggests that interventions targeting WHR may be effective in reducing TG levels in patients with MDD. lipid metabolism abnormalities, but the exact causal relationship remains unclear, and the range of lipid types examined is not comprehensive. Furthermore, the cross-population effects, mediating factors and underlying biological pathways and cell types are yet to be thoroughly investigated. WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS This study, using the largest genome-wide association studies dataset and bidirectional two-sample two-step Mendelian randomisation (MR) methods, confirmed a bidirectional causal relationship between MDD and triglycerides (TG) levels. Waist-hip ratio (WHR) was identified as a key mediator, explaining 69% of the total effect of MDD on TG levels. Additionally, meta-analysis and enrichment analysis uncovered key lipid-related and nucleosome assembly pathways, implicating brain and liver tissues in MDD-related metabolic dysregulation. HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY Identifying WHR as a key mediator may influence clinical guidelines for managing metabolic health in patients with MDD. This study’s robust MR methods and cross-population analyses support personalised interventions targeting lipid metabolism, offering potential improvements in outcomes for patients with MDD and comorbid metabolic disorders. Introduction Depression, particularly major depressive disorder (MDD), is a significant global health issue, affecting approximately 280 million people worldwide. 1 The 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study highlights that MDD accounts for a substantial proportion (37.3%) of disability-adjusted life-years related to mental disorders, indicating its profound impact on global health. 2 MDD is frequently associated with metabolic syndrome (MetS), with a meta-analysis reporting a prevalence of 32.6% in severe mental illness, including MDD, which is higher than in the general population. 3 This comorbidity increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases, significantly affecting patient outcomes. Dyslipidaemia, a key feature of MetS, is also closely linked to depression, with studies indicating that patients with MDD often exhibit elevated triglyceride (TG) levels and reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol. 4 Dyslipidaemia contributes not only to systemic inflammation but also to disruptions in brain lipid signalling, which can interfere with neurotransmitter pathways and impair neuroplasticity. 5 The link between dysregulated lipid metabolism and MDD is further reinforced by evidence of chronic inflammation, wherein peripheral metabolic changes are mirrored in central nervous system dysfunction. For instance, increased TG levels have been shown to correlate with higher concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-8 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, which exacerbate neuroinflammation and contribute to cerebrovascular endothelial dysfunction, thereby worsening depressive symptoms. 5 In addition, the antidepressants frequently prescribed for MDD can significantly impact metabolic homeostasis, thereby increasing the likelihood of obesity, dyslipidaemia and MetS, 6 underlining the necessity of monitoring lipid profiles in patients undergoing long-term pharmacological treatment. The relationship between MDD and lipid metabolism is not merely unidirectional but reflects a complex bidirectional dynamic. Dysregulated lipid metabolism in individuals with MDD can amplify oxidative and nitrosative stress, leading to endothelial dysfunction and heightened cardiovascular risk. 3 Conversely, the metabolic burden imposed by dyslipidaemia can exacerbate hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction and amplify systemic inflammation, perpetuating a cycle of depressive symptoms and metabolic dysregulation. 5 This intricate connection underscores the overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms shared by MDD and MetS. Exploring the genetic and mechanistic underpinnings of these associations is crucial. By uncovering the causal pathways linking lipid abnormalities to MDD, targeted interventions could be developed to address both metabolic and mental health concerns simultaneously. Such an approach holds promise for improving overall health and reducing the burden of comorbidities in affected populations. However, the relationship between MDD and blood lipids has long remained controversial. Extensive analyses through cross-sectional and meta-analyses have identified significant variations in the relationship between MDD and blood lipids across different study designs and lipid phenotypes. In studies of the effect of blood lipids on MDD, TG levels exhibit a notable positive correlation with MDD. 7 However, findings for the correlation between HDL and MDD are inconsistent across studies, with some studies reporting a positive association, 7 while a recent longitudinal study reported a negative correlation. 8 Conversely, in studies of the effect of MDD on blood lipids, MDD shows a positive correlation only with TG, 9 while displaying negative correlations with low-density lipoprotein (LDL), HDL and total cholesterol (TC) levels. 10 This conflicting evidence points to a complex, bidirectional relationship between MDD and blood lipids. Recent genetic studies have also failed to consistently define the genetic causal relationships between MDD and various blood lipid phenotypes. For instance, a Mendelian randomisation (MR) study with large sample sizes for blood lipids (n=188 577) and different depression phenotypes (n=480 359) found that 10 TG was positively correlated with MDD, and a similar pattern was noted with HDL. 11 Additionally, reverse causation was examined in another study which focused on depression (n=2 113 907) and MetS (n=291 107), including its components TG (n=441 016) and HDL (n=403 943), revealing that MDD was positively correlated with TG and inversely correlated with HDL. 12 Despite numerous investigations, a definitive conclusion regarding the relationship between MDD and blood lipid phenotypes has not yet been established. Our study leverages the largest genome-wide association study (GWAS) dataset available, drawing from European and East Asian populations (N=5 237 893). We aimed to elucidate the bidirectional causal relationships and underlying mediating mechanisms between MDD and five lipid phenotypes. This effort is intended to provide clearer insights into the intricate genetic interconnections affecting these critical health issues, potentially paving the way for targeted therapies and improved patient outcomes. Methods Study design This study employed a comprehensive approach to investigate the relationships between MDD and blood lipid phenotypes, as illustrated in figure 1 . Initially, we undertook a two-sample MR analysis, incorporating both univariate and multivariate MR methods, to dissect the causal effects between MDD and five blood lipid phenotypes. Subsequently, a systematic mediation analysis was conducted to delineate the roles of potential risk factors or mediators in this relationship. Furthermore, we characterised the MR loci through a detailed GWAS meta-analysis and enrichment process. This entire study adhered to the MR Enhanced Epidemiological Observation Study (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines ( online supplemental file 1 ). Data of GWAS summary statistics For each trait, we sought data from studies with the largest available European and East Asian ancestry samples. The GWAS summary of MDD for European samples was for 170 756 cases and 329 443 controls (dataset), 13 and for East Asians, it incorporated data on 12 588 cases and 85 914 controls (dataset). 14 For dyslipidaemia, we used GWAS results from 4 639 192 individuals collected from the extensive Global Federation of Lipid Genetics (dataset), 15 comprising Europeans (N=4 096 085) and East Asians (N=543 107) ( online supplemental table 1 ). Additionally, we used a substantial amount of GWAS summary data from the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Open GWAS database for subsequent MR analyses (dataset). 16 Mendelian randomisation Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that reached genome-wide significance (p&lt;5×10 −8 ) were selected as instrumental variables. We performed linkage disequilibrium clumping with parameters set to an r 2 &lt;0.001 within the 10 mb window to identify independent SNPs associated with the exposure. The strength of each chosen SNP was quantified by calculating the F statistics: F=(2×EAF×(1−EAF)×β 2 )/[(2×EAF×(1−EAF) ×β 2 )+(2×EAF×(1−EAF)×N×SE(β) 2 )]. 17 In this formula, EAF denotes the effect allele frequency, and SE(β) represents the standard error of the SNP–exposure effect estimate. An F-statistic greater than 10 was considered a prerequisite for the inclusion of instrumental variables. In the univariable MR analysis, we employed the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, 18 which generally shows greater potency, and used five additional methods as supplementary analyses: weighted mode, 19 simple mode, MR-Egger regression, 20 weighted median 21 and MR-pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (PRESSO). 22 These methods were engaged in a two-sample MR framework to ensure the robustness of our results. Sensitivity analyses were performed using a variety of methods including Cochran’s Q statistic (for the IVW method estimation), I 2 and the intercept of MR-Egger regression, as well as the MR-PRESSO outlier test ( online supplemental table 3 ). These tests aimed to identify instrument failure and horizontal pleiotropy. A Cochran’s Q statistic and I 2 greater than 30%, or p values less than 0.05 suggested heterogeneity. Additionally, a non-zero intercept in MR-Egger regression (p&lt;0.05) indicated the presence of pleiotropic SNPs, while the MR-PRESSO test evaluated the outliers ( online supplemental table 2 ). To assess the direct effects of multiple blood lipids on MDD, we employed multivariate MR, aiming to unravel any potential confounding factors. This method determines the causal effects of specific blood lipids on MDD, independent of other blood lipids. Additionally, we used the Causal Analysis Using the Summary Effect Estimates (CAUSE) method to confirm significant causal relationships. This method calculates posterior probabilities of causal and shared (non-causal) effects by estimating the difference in the expected log pointwise posterior density, comparing the fit of the sharing model with the causal model. 23 All the MR analyses, along with the sensitivity tests, were conducted using R statistical software (V.4.2.0), employing several packages including TwoSampleMR and multivariable MR (MVMR), and CAUSE 23 packages. Mediation analysis To determine if an intermediate trait mediated the relationship between exposure and outcome, a two-step MR approach was employed. In the first step, univariable MR was used to estimate the effects of risk factors from the IEU Open GWAS database on liability to MDD and TG (outcome of interest, see Results section below), as the forward direction analysis. We also conducted a backward analysis to eliminate traits with bidirectional associations, as these do not allow for a clear determination of the causal direction between the phenotype and the mediator. We progressed to the next step based on the following findings: (1) strong evidence supporting the effect of a trait on the liability to the mediator, yet limited evidence in the reverse direction and (2) significant evidence of an effect on liability to TG. A false discovery rate of 5% was used to confirm the robustness of these associations. In the second step, using MVMR, we quantified the ‘indirect’ or ‘mediated’ effect of the exposure through the mediator on the liability to TG. For our analysis, nine traits were identified as potential mediators for the impact of MDD on TG, and MDD was considered as a potential mediator for 25 traits affecting TG. Confidence intervals (CIs) were derived using the sum of squares method to ensure the precision of our estimates. GWAS Meta-analysis and Enrichment For MDD–TG, TG–waist–hip ratio (WHR) and WHR–MDD, we performed an Association analysis based on SubSETs identifying concordant and discordant directional associations and plotted these subsets coordinates in Manhattan. The analysis of genomic annotations in functional mapping and annotations (FUMA) was used to identify loci in each subset of nearby genes, 20 kb window. We also tested genes in concordant and discordant subsets for enrichment across the various gene ontology sets accessed through the R package of ‘clusterProfiler’. Subsequently, we used FUMA’s cell type enrichment analysis and tissue enrichment analyses to evaluate each subset to obtain enrichment evidence from all available human single-cell RNA-seq reference data in the brain and 54 different tissue types from the Genotype-Tissue Expression Project Version 8. Results Causal relationship between MDD and higher TG levels In the univariable MR analysis aimed at untangling the effects of blood lipids on MDD risk, we observed a significant positive association between TG levels and MDD in the European population (IVW: odds ratio (OR): 1.052, 95% CI 1.020 to 1.085, p=0.001; table 1 and online supplemental table 3 ). In the East Asian population, the association was directionally consistent and marginally significant (OR: 1.085, 95% CI 0.986 to 1.193, p=0.094; table 1 and online supplemental table 3 ). A meta-analysis further corroborated the positive association between TG levels and MDD (OR: 1.205, 95% CI 1.034 to 1.405, p=0.017; online supplemental figure 1 and online supplemental table 4 ). Contrastingly, a negative association between LDL and MDD emerged in both European and East Asian samples, but the significance of this association was not confirmed in the meta-analysis (European: OR: 0.972, 95% CI 0.947 to 0.998, p=0.037; East Asian: OR: 0.928, 95% CI 0.864 to 0.997, p=0.042; meta-analysis: OR: 1.231, 95% CI 0.903 to 1.677, p=0.188; table 1 and online supplemental table 4 ). Other lipids showed no significant direct correlations with MDD ( table 1 and online supplemental table 3 ). Additionally, a significant negative association was found for non-HDL cholesterol levels in the European population (OR: 0.705, 95% CI 0.542 to 0.918, p=0.009; online supplemental figure 1 and online supplemental table 4 ), which was also confirmed by the meta-analysis (OR: 0.718, 95% CI 0.554 to 0.931, p=0.012; online supplemental figure 1 and online supplemental table 4 ). The reverse direction analysis for the European dataset showed that there was a significant positive correlation between MDD and TG in the IVW MR model, with an OR value of 1.075 (95% CI 1.047 to 1.104; p&lt;0.001; table 1 and online supplemental table 3 ). However, we found no evidence of a significant causal effect of MDD with LDL, HDL, non-HDL and TC, IVW (OR: 1.005, 95% CI 0.975 to 1.036, p=0.752; OR: 0.958, 95% CI 0.916 to 1.003, p=0.065; OR: 1.022, 95% CI 0.981 to 1.066, p=0.297 and OR: 1.024, 95% CI 0.989 to 1.061, p=0.185 ( table 1 and online supplemental table 3 ). For the East Asian dataset, the number of independent, genome-wide significant loci for MDD was not enough for the analysis. Additionally, we used the CAUSE method to perform a causal analysis of exposed traits using genome-wide summary results. After accounting for pleiotropy, we found a significant causal relationship between MDD risk and higher TG (beta: 0.040, 95% CI 0.020 to 0.070, p=0.003; figure 2 and online supplemental table 5 ) in the European population. Conversely, TG levels were not associated with MDD risk (p&gt;0.05; figure 2 and online supplemental table 5 ). Previous observational studies further corroborate these findings, showing significantly elevated TG levels in patients with MDD compared with healthy controls (OR: 2.710, 95% CI 1.720 to 4.250, p&lt;0.001; online supplemental table 6 ). 24 25 Taken together, this convergent evidence supports a causal implication of MDD in elevated TG levels. WHR as the main mediating factor of the effect of MDD on TG We performed a two-step MR analysis for MDD and TG which exhibited significant causal effects in both the two-sample MR and CAUSE methods ( online supplemental tables 7 and 8 ). In the nine mediation factors of the effects of MDD on TG, we found that WHR mediated 69% of the total effect of MDD on TG, while the other eight risk factors mediated at most 29% ( figure 3A ). Additionally, we identified 25 risk factors that influence TG via MDD, with none of the mediated proportions exceeding 17%. Five risk factors that mediated more than 10% of the total effect of MDD on TG were: walking for pleasure (17%); types of physical activity in the last 4 weeks: heavy do-it-yourself (eg, weeding, lawn mowing, carpentry, digging) (14%); left-hand grip strength (13%); educational qualifications: advanced level (A levels)/advanced subsidiary level (AS levels) or equivalent (10%); and cheese intake (10%) ( figure 3B ). GWAS meta-analysis and enrichment We identified distinct gene sets highlighted in concordant and discordant single nucleotide variations (SNVs) for MDD–TG, MDD–WHR and WHR–TG pairs by two-sided meta-analyses, and Manhattan plots and lists of GWAS loci and nearby genes (20 kb window) are provided ( online supplemental figure 2 , online supplemental table 9 ). To better illustrate the genes associated with MDD–TG SNVs, we further analysed the roles of these genes across different tissues and cell types. By comparing consistent and inconsistent SNVs, we identified 64 genes associated with MDD–WHR–TG consistent SNPs, with notable genes including cathepsin F ( CTSF ), secretogranin III ( SCG3 ) and family with sequence similarity 171 member A1( FAM171A1 ), all of which are significantly related to the brain. In contrast, 67 genes were identified in the inconsistent SNPs, with prominent genes such as creatine kinase B ( CKB ), which are strongly associated with the brain as well, but need to be considered with caution ( figure 4 ). Then we conducted a Gene Ontology analysis using the identified distinct gene sets ( online supplemental tables 10–15 ). For MDD–TG discordant subsets and WHR–TG discordant subsets, genes were primarily enriched in pathways associated with lipid-related processes and lipoprotein particle remodelling. No lipid-related process was significant in the MDD–WHR pair. However, genes in MDD–WHR discordant subsets, WHR–TG concordant subsets and MDD–TG concordant subsets were all highly significant in the assembling and organisation process of nucleosomes, chromatin and protein–DNA complexes ( onlinesupplemental figures 3 5 ). Cell-type enrichment was significant among the WHR–TG concordant subsets, with evidence for independent associations for inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, microglia, stem cells and neurons ( online supplemental figure 6 , ( online supplemental table 16 ). We found notable tissue-level implications for the liver in the MDD–TG and WHR–TG pairs and for multiple brain regions in the MDD–WHR pair ( online supplemental figure 7 ). Discussion Main findings In this extensive study conducted on both European and East Asian populations, we uncovered two primary findings. First, we identified a negative genetic relationship between LDL and MDD in both populations. Additionally, a significant positive correlation between TG and MDD was observed in the European cohort. However, no consistent association was found between MDD and HDL, non-HDL or TC in either population. Second, we determined that WHR serves as a key mediator in the relationship between MDD and TG. Furthermore, our GWAS meta-analysis has identified CTSF , SCG3 , FAM171A1 and CKB as significant contributors to the MDD–WHR–TG axis. Our genetic findings suggest that pathways involving nucleosome constituents, DNA–protein binding and chromatin assembly, influenced by SNVs causing aberrant histone function, may explain the associations across the MDD–WHR–TG axis, affecting both depression-related gene expression and lipid metabolism regulation. 26 Our study builds on existing research investigating the relationship between MDD and lipid metabolism while offering new insights into the mechanisms underlying these associations. Previous evidence suggests that disruptions in lipid metabolism, such as reductions in LDL or TC levels, may impair brain function by altering the fluidity of neuronal membranes. This change could influence serotonin levels and uptake within the brain. 24 Specifically, reduced LDL levels may disrupt lipid rafts, which are critical for the structural stability and functionality of serotonin receptors and transporters involved in serotonin signalling. The disintegration of these rafts due to altered lipid composition, such as lower LDL, may impair serotonin function, potentially leading to depressive symptoms. 24 In addition, there is growing evidence that lipid abnormalities may influence depression through inflammatory pathways. For example, elevated TG levels have been linked to heightened systemic inflammation, which is widely implicated in both depression and metabolic disorders. Proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α and IL-6, are known to interfere with serotonin signalling, further contributing to depressive symptoms. 10 24 Furthermore, inflammation can disrupt the normal function of the HPA axis, which is involved in the body’s stress response. Dysregulation of the HPA axis leads to increased cortisol secretion. As cortisol has been shown to have neurotoxic effects, it can contribute to the development of depressive symptoms. 5 Our findings identified a significant positive correlation between TG and MDD in the European cohort, which aligns with the conclusions of previous meta-analyses. One meta-analysis that investigated lipid profiles in patients experiencing their first episode of MDD demonstrated that TG levels were significantly elevated in these patients, while HDL levels were reduced. This analysis, incorporating data from 1304 participants (690 patients with MDD and 614 healthy controls) across 4 countries, including China, found no significant differences in LDL or TC levels, suggesting that TG and HDL play a more substantial role in lipid changes associated with MDD. 25 Another comprehensive meta-analysis, synthesising data from 29 studies with 5796 participants (2179 patients with MDD and 3617 healthy controls), also reported elevated TG levels and decreased TC and very-low-density lipoprotein levels in patients with MDD compared with healthy controls. 25 These findings are consistent with our observations, which highlight TG as a central lipid abnormality associated with MDD. Building on this association, our study identifies WHR as a key mediator linking MDD and TG, especially in the European cohort. This suggests that abdominal fat distribution may underlie the impact of MDD on lipid metabolism. By reframing the relationship as bidirectional, these findings offer a new perspective on the metabolic and emotional consequences of fat distribution. Additionally, our genetic analysis identified several key genes significantly associated with brain function, including CTSF , SCG3 , FAM171A1 and CKB . Among these, SCG3 emerged as a particularly important gene, given its central role in neuroendocrine regulation and metabolic processes. Previous studies have demonstrated that SCG3 is involved in the formation of secretory granules containing neuropeptides such as orexin, neuropeptide Y, and pro-opiomelanocortin, which are not only essential for appetite regulation but also closely associated with emotional regulation and stress responses. 27 28 In patients with MDD, altered expression of SCG3 in brain regions responsible for emotional regulation, such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, may disrupt the secretion of these neuropeptides, thereby impairing emotional processing and stress reactivity. 27 From a metabolic perspective, dysfunction in SCG3 could affect WHR and TG levels by modulating fat distribution and appetite control mechanisms. 29 In contrast, CTSF , FAM171A1 and CKB showed no significant role in mediating WHR’s influence on TG elevation in MDD, and further research is needed to clarify their involvement. These findings suggest that enriched pathways involved in nucleosome structure, DNA–protein interactions, and chromatin assembly are linked to SNVs affecting histone function. This epigenetic mechanism may account for the WHR-mediated elevation of TG levels in MDD 26 and highlights WHR as a clinically relevant marker for lipid monitoring—offering new insights into the MDD–lipid metabolism interplay and its therapeutic implications. Prior research has extensively examined how lipid regulation influences depression, focusing on therapies aimed at modulating lipid profiles. For instance, treatments that adjust lipid levels have demonstrated effectiveness in alleviating depressive symptoms. 10 Additionally, electroconvulsive therapy has been suggested to partially achieve its therapeutic effects by normalising disrupted lipid metabolism. 10 Collectively, these findings support the clinical potential of lipid-modulating therapies in alleviating depressive symptoms. Limitations Though our study presents several novel findings, it has some limitations. First, the heterogeneity of our sample owing to varying clinical classifications, diagnoses and scales of the severity of MDD may have introduced confounding factors. 1 3 Second, the divergences in population habits and genetics, along with differing sample sizes across European and East Asian populations, could have influenced our causal conclusions. Our study’s emphasis on large-scale GWAS data with MR might have overlooked potential covariates in patients with MDD, including metabolic profiles, familial and past histories, and lifestyle habits. This may have directly impacted genetic correlation outcomes. 13 Further, our MR method did not have a nonlinear causal association between exposure and outcome. Additionally, our findings, derived from European and East Asian ancestry GWAS datasets, may not apply universally. The study also produced an overlap in plotted genes by concordant and discordant SNVs from MDD, WHR and TG, with no specific pathways identified as enriched. Finally, the presence of common (though not causal) genetic or environmental risk factors in both depression and dyslipidaemia may have influenced our findings. 14 Implications This study is the first to use large-scale GWAS datasets from both European and East Asian populations to analyse MDD and lipid bidirectional genetic causality in an MR study. It is also the first to characterise the genes and biological pathways that constitute the intermediary factors in the complex relationship between lipid metabolism and MDD. Several advantages add to the strength of our conclusions. First is the reliability of the research methods. In contrast to observational studies, we used MR to use genetic variation as a ‘tool’ to represent an exposure of interest and infer a causal relationship between exposure and outcome. 11 Second, while many current studies on MDD and blood lipids focus solely on a single European population, we analysed the results of two recent large-scale meta-analyses from both East Asian and European cohorts. Our findings reveal ethnic differences in the causal relationship between lipid metabolism and MDD, consistent with previous epidemiological meta-analyses. Third is our use of two-way two-sample MR to explore the interrelationship between MDD and lipid profiles, which can improve statistical efficiency. Fourth, we studied five phenotypes, including LDL, HDL, TG, TC and non-HDL, which yields a more systematic and comprehensive understanding of the MDD–lipid relationship than previous studies. Finally, we used a two-step MR to explore the mechanism of MDD-mediated TG elevation, providing new insights into the selection of indicators for clinical intervention and treatment of blood lipids. Our results suggest that a more proactive approach might be warranted in the management of TG in those at risk for MDD, and of MDD in those at risk for vascular diseases. Clinically, WHR could serve as a practical and reliable indicator to manage TG. Additionally, therapeutic strategies targeting abnormal histone epigenetic modifications or restoring compensatory histone function may offer new treatment of the comorbidities associated with dyslipidaemia in MDD, though further work is needed to clarify the potential benefits of such strategies. 10 Conclusions Our bidirectional two-sample MR study unveiled a causal link between blood lipids and MDD, especially with TG levels. Despite some limitations, the insights generated can help clinically select simpler and more reliable WHR indicators to prevent, manage, control and treat related comorbidities caused by TG abnormalities. Future investigations are warranted to further extend our understanding of the intricate causal relationship between MDD and lipid-related phenotypes.
