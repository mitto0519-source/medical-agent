Summary Background Individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) may be at increased risk of both obesity and underweight. Objective To examine the association between ASD and weight status in children and adolescents, adjusting for individual‐ and neighbourhood‐level sociodemographic factors. Methods We conducted a cross‐sectional study of children and adolescents ≥2 and ≤18 years old using health administrative and demographic data from Ontario, Canada. Using growth measurements from a large primary care database between 2011 and 2016, we categorized weight status using World Health Organization definitions. We defined ASD based on a previously validated algorithm. Results We included 568 children and adolescents with ASD and 32 967 without ASD. Comparing those with ASD to those without ASD, prevalence of underweight was 3.5% versus 1.9%, overweight 19.0% versus 18.2%, obesity 12.9% versus 7.3%, and severe obesity 5.8% versus 2.2%. In the fully adjusted multinomial logistic regression model, ASD remained associated with underweight (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.02; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.27–3.20), obesity (aOR 1.87; 95% CI 1.44–2.43) and severe obesity (aOR 2.62; 95% CI 1.81–3.80). Conclusion Children and adolescents with ASD are at increased risk of underweight, obesity, and severe obesity, independent of sociodemographic characteristics. Strategies addressing growth and weight status are warranted in this population. INTRODUCTION Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), a neurodevelopmental condition with onset in early childhood, is associated with challenges in social communication and interaction as well as restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, and activities. 1 Prevalence of ASD is increasing; 2 , 3 , 4 current estimates are 1 in 50 children in Canada, 1 in 36 children in the United States (US), and 1 in 100 children worldwide. 2 , 5 , 6 Individuals with ASD may be at increased risk of both high and low weight, owing to unique medical, behavioural, and genetic factors (e.g., food selectivity, which can result in preferential intake of high‐calorie foods or inadequate caloric intake; 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 use of medications for behavioural symptoms and/or management of co‐morbid conditions, potentially resulting in either weight loss or weight gain). 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Weight status is widely recognized as an important element of child and adolescent health, with negative effects associated with both high and low weight. Overweight and obesity, in particular, have received considerable attention as global public health problems. 17 Sequelae in children and adolescents include short‐ and long‐term effects on physical health, as well as impacts on psychosocial health, such as increased risk of peer victimization/bullying, social isolation, and lower quality of life. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 In high‐income countries, underweight is less prevalent than overweight and obesity in children and adolescents, but is also associated with important negative health effects, including increased susceptibility to infection and delays in development. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Analyses of weight status in children and adolescents with ASD vary substantially in terms of prevalence estimates, and conclusions regarding risk of underweight, overweight, and obesity relative to individuals without ASD. 30 Methodological issues in previous studies include use of parent‐reported anthropometric measurements, 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 which have been shown to be unreliable and invalid, 37 , 38 , 39 and small sample sizes, 7 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 which may lead to effects with high levels of uncertainty. Of studies with a large clinical ASD sample, numerous have relied on an external comparison group, based on national surveys. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 Importantly, underweight has been relatively neglected, with many studies considering only high and not low weight. 31 , 49 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 A better understanding of weight status of children and adolescents with ASD, at both ends of the weight status spectrum, has clinical and population‐level implications. 4 Our objective was therefore to examine the association between ASD and weight status in children and adolescents ≥2 and ≤18 years old, adjusting for relevant individual and neighbourhood sociodemographic factors, using linked health administrative and demographic data. 2. METHODS 2.1. Setting and design We conducted a cross‐sectional study of children and adolescents in Ontario, Canada using health administrative and demographic datasets linked and analysed at ICES (formerly the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences), an independent, non‐profit research institute. Under Ontario's health information privacy law, ICES is a prescribed entity authorized to collect and analyse health care and demographic data without individual consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. This study was approved by ICES' Privacy &amp; Legal Office and the Research Ethics Board at The Hospital for Sick Children. Ontario is Canada's most populous province, with more than 15 million residents. Hospital and physician services are publicly funded through the single‐payer, universal Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP). 2.2. Data sources We used unique, encoded identifiers, derived from provincial health insurance numbers, to link individual records across multiple ICES databases. We identified the study sample, determined exposure and outcome, and obtained some covariate data from the Electronic Medical Records Primary Care (EMRPC) database, also known as Electronic Medical Records Administrative data Linked Database (EMRALD). EMRPC is a large administrative dataset derived from family physicians' routinely collected electronic medical record (EMR) data. As of 30 December 2016, when data were most recently extracted, the EMRPC database captured 385 family physicians and 43 clinics using Telus Health's PS Suite EMR, representing almost 600 000 unique patients in the province of Ontario. To contribute to EMRPC, physicians must have used their EMR for at least 2 years. Anthropometric data in EMRPC are acceptably complete (66.2%) and accurate (97.3%), 57 and have been used to study paediatric overweight and obesity. 58 , 59 , 60 Previous work has demonstrated a higher proportion of upper‐income quintiles, a higher proportion of rural residence, and a lower proportion of immigrants in children and adolescents in EMRPC versus the general Ontario population aged 18 years or less. 58 We determined outpatient physician billings from the OHIP Claims Database. We obtained area‐level sociodemographic measures by linking postal codes from the Registered Persons Database (RPDB; Ontario's population‐based registry) to Canadian census data and census‐derived measures (Rurality Index of Ontario [RIO] 61 and Ontario Marginalization Index [ONMARG database] 62 ). To identify mother–child dyads, we used ICES' MOMBABY dataset, which is derived from the Canadian Institute of Health Information Discharge Abstract Database, and links maternal and newborn health records for all in‐hospital deliveries in the province (&gt;97% of deliveries 63 ). We then obtained immigration information for mother–child dyads from the Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) Permanent Resident Database. The IRCC Permanent Resident Database contains individual‐level data on all immigrants who have landed in Ontario since 1985; overall linkage to the RPDB is &gt;86%. 64 2.3. Study sample We included children and adolescents ≥2 and ≤18 years old with a height and weight measurement in the EMRPC database between January 2011 and December 2016. We excluded those with missing age or sex, an invalid health card number, &lt;1 year of EMR data, and those not rostered to an EMRPC clinic or physician. Rostering refers to a process in which a patient registers with a physician or practice; the physician commits to providing comprehensive care and the patient agrees to only seek care from their rostered physician, except when travelling or in emergency situations. 65 We limited our analyses to those ≥2 years old to avoid inclusion of an age group in whom ASD could not have been reliably diagnosed. 66 , 67 For simplicity, hereafter we use the term ‘children’ to include both children and adolescents. 2.4. Measures and variables Exposure ( autism spectrum disorder ): We defined ASD using a previously‐validated algorithm, based on the presence of an ASD‐related term in the EMR's ‘Cumulative Patient Profile’ (CPP; a free text field that summarizes key patient information). 68 With a clinical diagnosis of ASD in a comprehensive chart review as the reference standard, this algorithm demonstrated a sensitivity of 82.1% and a specificity of 100% in EMRPC. 68 Per the algorithm, children were classified as having a diagnosis of ASD if a keyword search of the CPP identified an ASD‐related term, and as not having a diagnosis of ASD if a keyword search of the CPP did not identify an ASD‐related term. Outcome ( weight status ): We categorized weight status as underweight, normal weight, overweight, obesity, or severe obesity, based on age‐ and sex‐standardized body mass index z ‐scores (zBMI). We determined zBMI by dividing weight (in kg) by height (in metres squared) and standardizing values by age and sex using the World Health Organization (WHO) Growth Standards and reference charts. 69 , 70 To ensure consistency across age groups, we—and others—apply WHO definitions of overweight, obesity, and severe obesity for children ≥5 years old to all ages (Table 1 ). 58 , 71 , 72 We defined underweight as zBMI &lt; −2, normal weight as zBMI ≥ −2 to ≤1, overweight as zBMI &gt;1 to ≤2, obesity as zBMI &gt;2 to ≤3 and severe obesity as zBMI &gt;3. These definitions translate to zBMI &lt;3rd percentile for underweight, &gt;85th percentile for overweight, &gt;97th percentile for obesity, and &gt;99.9th percentile for severe obesity. When calculating zBMI and determining weight status, we included growth measurements associated with an OHIP‐billed visit captured in EMRPC. As older children and adolescents with ASD are less likely to attend well‐child visits than their typically developing peers, we did not limit our analyses to well‐child visits. 73 We deemed growth measurements complete if both weight and height were measured in the same visit, such that zBMI could be calculated. For identification and removal of implausible height and weight measurements, we applied a previously validated approach to cleaning longitudinal paediatric EMR growth data that addresses issues including duplicate measurements, carried forward measurements, and units error/switches. 74 As this approach excludes only very extreme values (defined as a z ‐score less than −25 or more than +25) for weight and height and not BMI, we applied an additional data cleaning step of excluding very extreme zBMI values (i.e., those less than −25 and greater than 25) as implausible. For our analyses, we selected the most recent plausible zBMI for each child between 2011 and 2016, inclusive. This time period was chosen to include the most recent data available via EMRPC and maximize capture of primary care visits (particularly for older children and adolescents, who may not access preventive health care as frequently 75 , 76 , 77 ) while minimizing risk of confounding by temporal trends; EMRPC data have previously demonstrated relative stability in paediatric overweight, obesity and severe obesity prevalence from 2011 to 2015. 58 Covariates : Given the known relationships between weight status and sociodemographic measures, we included as covariates age, 5 , 60 sex, 5 , 78 family immigration status, 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 rural residence, 85 , 86 , 87 neighbourhood‐level income 84 , 88 and neighbourhood‐level material deprivation. 84 , 89 , 90 Ethnicity was not included as it is not well captured in Canadian health administrative data. We used age and sex obtained from EMRPC. We identified children as being from immigrant families if either the child or their mother (linked via the MOMBABY database) had an IRCC Permanent Resident Database record. Where no IRCC Permanent Resident Database record was available for either member of the mother–child dyad, we considered the family Canadian‐born or long‐term residents (arrived in Canada prior to 1985 at database inception). We used the RPDB to identify each child's postal code. To obtain neighbourhood‐level sociodemographic measures, postal code was linked to Canadian Census data using Statistics Canada's Postal Code Conversion File. The RIO, which includes population, population density, and travel time to access medical care, was used to classify residence as rural (RIO ≥40) or non‐rural (RIO &lt;40). 61 Neighbourhood‐level income quintile was assigned from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest) based on census household income in the individual's dissemination area, the smallest standard geographic unit for which Canadian Census data are reported. Neighbourhood‐level material deprivation quintile was assigned from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest) based on dissemination area, using the material deprivation dimension of the Ontario Marginalization Index. 62 Material deprivation refers to the inability to access and attain basic material needs. Indicators in the material deprivation dimension include housing quality, educational attainment, and family structure. 91 We did not have data on genetic obesity syndromes, as validated algorithms to identify these rare conditions have not been developed in EMRPC. Complete medication data were not available, as only those medications prescribed by family physicians via the EMR are reliably captured in EMRPC. Of note, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 agonists (e.g., semaglutide) were not routinely used for weight management in children and adolescents during the study period. 2.5. Statistical analysis We summarized characteristics of the study sample using descriptive statistics, comparing those with and without ASD. Given the large sample size, between‐group differences were assessed using standardized differences, with a standardized difference of 0.10 or greater indicating important inter‐group differences. 92 Multinomial logistic regression was used to compare weight status of children with and without ASD. First, we obtained unadjusted multinomial odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between ASD and weight status, with normal weight as the reference outcome category. We then obtained adjusted multinomial ORs and 95% CIs by including selected covariates in multivariable multinomial logistic regression models: sex, age, family immigration status, rural residence, neighbourhood‐level income quintile, and neighbourhood‐level material deprivation quintile. Missingness was very low (&lt;1% missingness for all covariates and &lt;1% of visits with incomplete covariate data); we, therefore, opted to perform complete case analysis, as is generally considered acceptable when loss of cases due to missingness is small and missing data are unlikely to be consequential. 93 , 94 To identify any multicollinearity between independent variables in the multivariable regression model, we calculated variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerances. We considered a VIF less than 10 and a tolerance greater than 0.1 as indicating no significant multicollinearity. 95 In additional analyses, we examined the ASD‐weight status association by sex (male or female) and by age group (2–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, or 15–18 years). These additional analyses were not adjusted for sociodemographic factors, owing to small cell sizes resulting from stratification. Effect modification by sex and age group was formally tested via inclusion of an interaction term in fully adjusted multinomial logistic regression models; a p‐ value&lt;0.3 for the interaction terms was considered evidence for potential effect modification. 96 To explore the sensitivity of our findings to extreme zBMI values not initially identified as implausible, we performed analyses excluding zBMI values less than −5 and greater than +5. This aligns with the WHO definition of biologically implausible values but may result in the exclusion of very high zBMI values that are, in fact, plausible and correct. 97 , 98 , 99 All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide, V.8.3 (SAS Institute, Inc. Cary, North Carolina). 3. RESULTS 3.1. Study sample We included 33 535 children ≥2 and ≤18 years old, of whom 568 (1.7%) had ASD. We identified 68 048 OHIP‐billed visits with a plausible zBMI for these 33 535 individuals (Figure 1 ); 1245 visits (1.83%) were for children with ASD. The proportion of visits with complete growth measurements was similar between those with ASD and those without ASD (1440 of 2662 [54.1%] vs. 75 242 of 137 026 [54.9%]; standardized difference, 0.02). The proportion of visits with a plausible zBMI was also similar in those with ASD versus those without ASD (1245 of 1440 [86.5%] versus 66 803 of 75 242 [88.8%]; standardized difference, 0.07). Characteristics of the children with and without ASD, at the most recent visit with a plausible zBMI, are compared in Table 2 . The proportion of males was higher in the group with ASD than in the group without ASD (452 [79.6%] vs. 16 578 [50.3%]; standardized difference, 0.64). Children with ASD were less likely to be in the youngest age group (2–4 years of age) than those without ASD (141 [24.8%] versus 10 354 [31.4%]; standardized difference, 0.15). Proportions in the highest and lowest quintiles of material deprivation were similar in those with and without ASD. There were no important differences between the ASD and non‐ASD groups in terms of family immigration status, rural residence, and neighbourhood‐level income quintile. 3.2. ASD‐weight status association There was a higher proportion of underweight, obesity, and severe obesity in children with ASD than in those without ASD (Table 3 ). In multinomial logistic regression analysis, children with ASD had significantly higher odds of underweight (OR 2.23; 95% CI 1.41–3.53), overweight (OR 1.26; 95% CI 1.01–1.56), obesity (OR 2.12; 95% CI 1.64–2.74) and severe obesity (OR 3.22; 95% CI 2.24–4.64) than those without ASD, relative to normal weight. Findings from multivariable multinomial logistic regression are shown in Table 4 . After adjustment for individual factors (age, sex, family immigration status) and neighbourhood‐level factors (rural residence, income quintile, material deprivation quintile), there was no evidence of an association between ASD and overweight (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.22; 95% CI 0.98–1.52); children with ASD remained at higher odds of underweight (aOR 2.02; 95% CI 1.27–3.20), obesity (aOR 1.87; 95% CI 1.44–2.43) and severe obesity (aOR 2.62; 95% CI 1.81–3.80), relative to normal weight. There was no indication of significant collinearity between the independent variables in the multinomial logistic regression model, as VIFs were substantially less than 10 (maximum 2.18) and tolerances were substantially greater than 0.1 (minimum 0.46). The ASD‐weight status association, by sex, is presented in Table 5 . There was no evidence of effect modification by sex ( p = 0.45). The ASD‐weight status association, by age group, is shown in Figure 2 . In children 2–4 years old there was no evidence to conclude that ASD diagnosis was associated with weight status; however, we did not identify effect modification by age group, overall ( p = 0.51). 3.3. Sensitivity analysis In a sensitivity analysis including only zBMI values between −5 and + 5, we excluded 64 zBMI measurements (0.19%). Results were similar to our primary analyses (Supplemental Tables 1, 2 ). 4. DISCUSSION Using linked health administrative and demographic datasets in Ontario, Canada, we found evidence that a diagnosis of ASD in the EMR was associated with weight status among children and adolescents ≥2 to ≤18 years old. We demonstrated that children and adolescents with ASD are at increased risk of underweight, obesity, and severe obesity, relative to those without ASD, independent of individual‐ and neighbourhood‐level sociodemographic factors. Taken together, findings suggest that strategies addressing growth—at both ends of the weight status spectrum—are warranted for children and adolescents with ASD. Our findings, adjusted for relevant sociodemographic factors, are consistent with previous meta‐analyses suggesting a significant association between ASD and obesity 30 , 100 , 101 but not ASD and overweight. 30 Many relevant studies have been limited by parent‐reported anthropometric measurements, 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 small sample sizes, 7 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 and external comparison groups. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 In contrast, our study used objective growth measurements obtained via clinical care and a very large sample size, with an internal comparison group of children and adolescents without ASD. Our demonstration that children and adolescents with ASD are at increased risk of severe obesity, specifically, represents a novel contribution to the extant literature. There is a knowledge gap regarding severe obesity, generally, 58 and our study appears to be the first to evaluate the ASD‐severe obesity association using an internal comparison group. One previous study—from Pham et al.—evaluated prevalence of severe obesity in ASD; however, a clinical sample of children with ASD was compared with the general population using US national data. 48 This approach may be problematic, owing to known geographic variation in obesity prevalence, 102 , 103 and possibly important differences between the clinical sample and the general population. Increased risk of underweight in children and adolescents with ASD, as demonstrated in this study, also represents a relatively novel finding. When assessing the relationship between a diagnosis of ASD and weight status, numerous studies have considered only overweight and/or obesity, without considering underweight. 30 , 31 , 49 , 51 , 52 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 104 Those studies that have compared prevalence of underweight in children and adolescents with and without ASD have produced varying results. A systematic review and meta‐analysis did not find an overall association between ASD and risk of underweight (pooled relative risk 1.285, 95% CI, 0.788–2.098). 53 There are numerous mechanisms by which individuals with ASD might be predisposed to either high or low weight, potentially explaining the associations documented in this study. The restricted and repetitive patterns characteristic of ASD can manifest as food selectivity, with a preference for high‐calorie foods. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Children and adolescents with ASD are also more sedentary and less physically active than their typically‐developing peers. 46 , 105 , 106 , 107 , 108 Behaviour management in ASD may contribute to relatively high weight, either via use of food as a reward or pharmacologic treatment of behavioural symptoms; 109 , 110 antipsychotic medications used to target behaviours in ASD (e.g., irritability and aggression) are known to cause weight gain. 12 , 13 , 14 In a subset of children and adolescents with ASD, obesity may be related to an underlying genetic condition (e.g., Prader‐Willi syndrome). 111 Conversely, food selectivity and other feeding problems in ASD can result in inadequate caloric intake in some children and adolescents. 10 A high prevalence of gastrointestinal symptoms, including abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, has been described in ASD, potentially affecting growth and weight status. 112 , 113 Stimulant medications—sometimes used to target behavioural symptoms like hyperactivity in ASD and/or manage co‐morbid conditions—might slow weight gain or result in weight loss. 15 , 16 Finally, certain genetic syndromes are associated with both underweight and ASD. 114 , 115 4.1. Implications Our findings underline the need for anticipatory guidance, prevention, and management efforts relevant to weight status in children and adolescents with ASD. Approaches to underweight may have many commonalities with approaches to address obesity and severe obesity (e.g., focusing on regular, balanced meals and snacks); however, strategies will need to be tailored depending on which end of the weight status spectrum is being addressed. Furthermore, such efforts should acknowledge the unique challenges related to addressing weight status in the context of ASD. Regarding obesity, evidence suggests that initiatives may be less accessible and effective for children with ASD due to behavioural rigidity making it difficult to promote healthy active lifestyles, and challenges related to social communication skills making it difficult to promote engagement in programming. 116 , 117 , 118 , 119 Behavioural rigidity and challenges related to social communication may also pose challenges in addressing underweight in the context of ASD, although these challenges have not been as clearly elucidated in the medical literature and require further research. 4.2. Strengths and limitations Strengths of this study include use of objective anthropometric measurements, subjected to rigorous data cleaning; a large sample size of children with ASD; and an internal comparison group, comprised of children without ASD. Routinely collected measurements, as in this study, have shown good agreement with research measurements, whereas self/proxy‐reported measurements result in inaccurate estimation of BMI and misclassification of overweight/obesity. 38 , 120 , 121 , 122 , 123 Although errors and implausible values remain inherent in large datasets, we used a validated and rigorous method for cleaning of EMR paediatric growth data. 74 Our results were very similar using two different definitions of biologically implausible BMIs, strengthening our conclusions regarding the ASD‐weight status relationship. Several limitations should be recognized in interpreting the results of this study. First, the algorithm used to identify ASD was highly specific but not perfectly sensitive. We likely did not capture all cases of ASD because of the characteristics of this algorithm (not capturing cases where the diagnosis is known) or because of challenges related to ASD diagnosis in young children (not capturing cases because the diagnosis is not yet known). 87 Any misclassification would have biased towards the null, suggesting that the association between ASD and weight status may be even larger than our results suggest. Second, some of our additional analyses may have been underpowered because of small sample sizes in specific subgroups (e.g., in the association between ASD and underweight in females). Third, we had incomplete information on factors that might be important in the association between ASD and weight status but are not well captured in existing databases. We did not have individual measures of socioeconomic status and ethnicity, which may have resulted in unmeasured confounding affecting our analyses. We lacked data on genetic obesity syndromes (e.g., Prader‐Willi syndrome, Bardet‐Biedl syndrome Alström syndrome, congenital leptin deficiency) and so could not control for, nor exclude based on, these conditions. We do not anticipate that this meaningfully impacted our findings, given the rarity of genetic obesity syndromes in the paediatric population. Additionally, we did not have the data necessary to explore mechanisms underlying the ASD‐weight status association. In particular, we were unable to quantify the contribution of medications such as stimulants and atypical antipsychotics to risk of underweight, obesity, and severe obesity in children and adolescents with ASD. Better understanding these mechanisms represents an important avenue for future research. Fourth, in this cross‐sectional study, we did not specifically assess when differences in weight status emerge in children with ASD. In additional analyses, we found no evidence of an association between ASD and weight status in children 2–4 years old; this finding—which might suggest that differences in weight status emerge in later childhood—should be interpreted with caution, given no evidence of effect modification by age group, overall. Further work exploring weight status trajectories would provide important information regarding optimal timing of prevention and management efforts. 5. CONCLUSIONS Children and adolescents with ASD are at increased risk of underweight, obesity and severe obesity relative to those without ASD, independent of individual‐ and neighbourhood‐level sociodemographic factors. Anticipatory guidance, prevention and management strategies relevant to growth in the paediatric ASD population are therefore important. Future research should address (1) mechanisms underlying risk of underweight, obesity and severe obesity in ASD (including medication use) and (2) growth trajectories in children and adolescents with ASD relative to their typically developing peers. Understanding when and why differences in weight status emerge in ASD would allow for appropriate tailoring of prevention and management efforts. Data Availability Statement The datasets from this study are held securely in coded form at ICES. While legal data sharing agreements between ICES and data providers (e.g., healthcare organizations and government) prohibit ICES from making the dataset publicly available, access may be granted to those who meet pre‐specified criteria for confidential access, available at www.ices.on.ca/DAS (email: das@ices.on.ca ). The full dataset creation plan and underlying analytic code are available from the authors upon request, understanding that the computer programs may rely upon coding templates or macros that are unique to ICES and are therefore either inaccessible or may require modification.
