Purposes This study aims to examine the relationships among mindfulness, future time perspective, self-efficacy, and athlete burnout in adolescent athletes, exploring the internal mechanisms influencing athlete burnout. It provides insights for coaches and young athletes to reduce burnout and foster healthy, confident, and optimistic lifestyles. Methods The study targeted adolescent soccer players in Chongqing, China, employing stratified random sampling for participant selection and utilizing SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 23.0 for data analysis. Results 1) Mindfulness showed a significant positive correlation with future time perspective and self-efficacy, and a significant negative correlation with athlete burnout. Future time perspective was positively correlated with self-efficacy and negatively with athlete burnout, as was self-efficacy itself; 2) Mindfulness had a direct effect (ES = -0.342) on athlete burnout. Future time perspective (ES = -0.152) and self-efficacy (ES = -0.060) each played significant mediating roles between mindfulness and athlete burnout, with the chain mediating effect of future time perspective and self-efficacy also being significant (ES = -0.051). Conclusion Mindfulness can influence adolescent athlete burnout directly as well as indirectly through the mediating effect of future time perspective and self-efficacy, and the chain mediating effect of these factors. Coaches should therefore focus on developing mindfulness in young athletes and also consider the impact of future time perspective and self-efficacy on burnout. Athletes often find themselves in relatively isolated and closed-off training and living environments. Combined with the long hours, high intensity, and repetitive nature of their training routines, these conditions make athletes particularly susceptible to mental health issues. In recent years, several studies have identified athlete burnout as one of the most common psychological challenges faced by athletes, especially among adolescents [ 1 – 4 ]. When young athletes encounter challenges or failures, they are more prone to experience heightened stress and burnout. This increased sensitivity to negative stimuli can amplify emotional reactions, leading to poor performance and, in some cases, withdrawal from competition. As a result, the issue of burnout among adolescent athletes has drawn growing attention. Athlete burnout is defined as a debilitating cognitive-affective syndrome characterized by emotional and physical exhaustion, a reduced sense of accomplishment, and a negative evaluation of sport participation [ 5 ]. Some scholars suggest that burnout arises from an imbalance between individual resources and environmental demands [ 5 – 7 ]. When athletes face this imbalance, they experience immediate, short-term physical and mental stress responses, which, if not effectively managed or recovered from over time, will accumulate and gradually lead to athlete burnout, the state of mental and physical dysfunction, such as mental/physical exhaustion, reduced sense of accomplishment, and negative evaluation of sport participation. Other studies have shown that mindfulness, as a positive psychological resource, can help alleviate burnout symptoms caused by resource imbalances [ 8 – 10 ]. Individuals with higher levels of mindfulness are better able to interpret challenging situations or stimuli as opportunities for growth rather than as threats, reducing their reactivity to external stressors. This ability to stay present-focused during training can help athletes maintain emotional balance, thereby lowering the risk of burnout [ 11 – 13 ]. Mindfulness refers to the ability that focuses on the present moment while consciously maintaining an open and accepting stance toward inner experiences such as emotions, thoughts, and bodily sensations, without reaction or judgment [ 12 , 14 , 15 ]. Additionally, mindfulness serves as an internal support mechanism that enhances athletes’ self-regulation and satisfaction of basic mental needs. This, in turn, fosters greater positive emotions, reduces negative emotional responses, and ultimately mitigates athlete burnout [ 1 , 11 , 16 ]. According to the Broaden-and-Build Theory of Positive Emotions, positive emotions experience broadens an individual’s immediate thought-action scope and helps to build enduring personal resources, including intellectual, physiological, psychological, and social resources. Therefore, this process reduces emotional exhaustion and the sense of burnout among athletes [ 10 , 17 , 18 ]. Several studies have confirmed that, as athletes develop higher levels of mindfulness, they are better able to engage authentically with their surroundings, enabling them to cultivate more positive emotions, regulate negative emotional responses, and ultimately reduce burnout [ 10 , 18 , 19 ]. In addition, some scholars argue that mindfulness, as a psychological protective factor, plays a positive role in mental health. Integrating mindfulness into athletic training can reduce psychological problems among children and adolescents (such as anxiety, stress, and emotional exhaustion), helping young athletes maintain a positive training mindset and thus alleviating the occurrence of athlete burnout in youth populations [ 10 , 13 , 20 ]. Moreover, research has shown that higher levels of mindfulness are associated with a reduction in rumination, while present-moment acceptance can foster greater optimism and future-oriented planning, thereby improving an individual’s future time perspective [ 21 – 24 ]. Future time perspective (FTP) refers to an individual’s integrated capacity at the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral levels to proactively plan for future goals, delay gratification, and evaluate outcomes. It emphasizes a goal-oriented psychological tendency that coordinates present actions with long-term development [ 25 , 26 ]. Additionally, future time perspective represents a personality trait that reflects an individual’s cognition, and both emotional and behavioral tendencies toward future time [ 27 , 28 ]. Individuals with higher levels of future time perspective can better regulate their current behaviors by recognizing the effectiveness of their actions and their more valuable future outcomes, enabling them to purposefully formulate long-term plans [ 29 , 30 ]. In contrast, mindfulness emphasizes awareness and attention to the present moment and is closely related to time perspective. Mindfulness and future time perspective respectively embody a positive outlook on the present and the future, both of which are dimensions of a positive time perspective [ 31 ]. Based on the theory of optimal time balance, there is an ideal balance among an individual’s past, present, and future time perspectives. Individuals who reach this balance exhibit better mental health and lower levels of burnout [ 32 – 34 ]. Scholars have found that individuals with higher levels of mindfulness maintain a more balanced present time perspective. Such individuals accept the past while actively pursuing future goals, reducing regrets about the past or unrealistic fantasies about the future, ultimately achieving a steady equilibrium in their perspectives on the past, present, and future [ 24 , 26 ]. Therefore, a positive present time perspective can predict a positive future time perspective, and an individual’s level of mindfulness can positively predict their future time perspective. Furthermore, the protective model holds that one protective factor can enhance the influence of another protective factor on an outcome variable [ 35 ]. Specifically, both mindfulness and future time perspective can be regarded as protective factors against athlete burnout, as they reflect individuals’ attitudes and orientations toward time [ 7 , 32 , 33 , 36 ]. Mindfulness emphasizes “present-oriented self-regulation,” which involves conscious and open attention to the present, reflecting a holistic and balanced time perspective [ 37 , 38 ]. In contrast, future time perspective emphasizes “future-oriented self-regulation,” where individuals with higher levels of future time perspective focus more on their future development, purposefully formulate long-term plans, and adopt more positive coping strategies when facing challenges [ 39 ]. Thus, both mindfulness and future time perspective exert a negative influence on athlete burnout. However, no studies have yet confirmed whether mindfulness influences athlete burnout through future time perspective or whether future time perspective serves as a mediator between mindfulness and athlete burnout. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 1: Future time perspective mediates the relationship between mindfulness and burnout among adolescent athletes. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s subjective assessment of their ability to complete a specific task, as well as their judgment and perception of whether their actions can achieve a certain goal [ 40 ]. In recent years, many studies have found that enhancing an individual’s level of mindfulness can contribute to increased self-efficacy [ 41 – 43 ]. Mindfulness can help individuals properly assess their own performance without self-criticism. By fostering sustained and focused attention and improving the ability to monitor attention, individuals can better accomplish their goals, thereby strengthening their self-efficacy. The conservation of resources theory holds that resource acquisition leads to positive psychological experiences, while resource depletion or potential loss of resources results in negative psychological experiences [ 44 ]. Individuals will consume psychological resources, which, over time, can lead to a series of negative psychological symptoms, such as tension, stress responses, and burnout [ 45 – 47 ], as they experience resource loss in their work. In the context of sports, athletes often undergo prolonged and intense training and competitions in pursuit of “faster, higher, and stronger” performance. Under the pressure of various factors, they may experience physical and psychological fatigue, increasing the risk of burnout [ 10 , 48 ]. Mindfulness can bolster positive psychological resources within the context of training and competition, potentially enabling athletes to manage burnout more effectively in these settings. This enhancement may also buffer against the depletion of other resources and the manifestation of negative psychological symptoms following periods of intense training or competition. As a positive psychological resource, self-efficacy further contributes to this process. A greater reservoir of individual psychological resources is generally associated with enhanced resilience to external stressors, fostering confidence in completing specific tasks within challenging environments. Maintaining an optimistic mindset during training and competition, overcoming obstacles, and achieving a sense of accomplishment are also facilitated, thereby mitigating the levels of burnout. Conversely, compromised self-efficacy may predispose athletes to experiencing suppression, tension, anxiety, and burnout. Prolonged exposure to these states can lead to emotional and physical exhaustion, a diminished sense of accomplishment, and negative evaluations of sport participation, thereby exacerbating burnout [ 10 , 49 , 50 ]. Furthermore, studies have found [ 10 , 48 , 51 ]that mindfulness can reduce judgmental thinking, allowing for greater focus on tasks and goals during training and competition. This heightened focus may also contribute to an increase in an individual’s self-efficacy, which, in turn, can mitigate athlete burnout. This evidence suggests that mindfulness may influence athlete burnout through self-efficacy, raising the question of whether self-efficacy serves as a mediating factor between mindfulness and burnout. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 2: Self-efficacy mediates the relationship between mindfulness and burnout among adolescent athletes. Moreover, the socioemotional selectivity theory holds that an individual’s goals and their perception of time are mutually coordinated, with future time perspective serving as a foundational element in social goal selection. Future time perspective serves as a motivational bridge between the individual and their behavior. A clearer orientation toward future goals (FTP) directly stimulates the individual’s belief in their ability to achieve those goals, namely self-efficacy. Therefore, individuals with higher FTP are more future-oriented, establish clear goals, and are more likely to take action to accomplish those goals, which in turn enhances their self-efficacy [ 52 ]. Studies have also demonstrated that FTP has a significant positive predictive effect on self-efficacy [ 48 , 52 , 53 ], and it can influence athlete burnout through self-efficacy. Athletes with a higher future time perspective tend to consider the future to a greater extent, develop clear plans, and take action accordingly. They adopt proactive coping strategies that enhance their problem-solving abilities, increasing their confidence in achieving their goals. As a result, their self-efficacy is reinforced, encouraging them to persist in training, maintain an optimistic attitude toward external challenges and uncertainties, and take timely measures to achieve a balance between internal and external demands. This process ultimately enhances their sense of achievement in training and competition, thereby reducing burnout associated with striving toward predetermined goals. Combined with the conservation of resources theory, both mindfulness and self-efficacy, as positive psychological resources, can help prevent the loss of personal resources and thereby reduce athlete burnout. On this basis, we can conclude that mindfulness, future time perspective, and self-efficacy are all important factors influencing athlete burnout, and also are significant indicators that negatively predict athlete burnout. Mindfulness can effectively enhance athletes’ future time perspective and self-efficacy, thereby reducing the risk of athletic burnout. Additionally, there is a positive correlation between future time perspective and self-efficacy. This indicates that an increase in mindfulness among athletes may foster a heightened future time perspective, which in turn facilitates the development of a stable sense of self-efficacy. Is “mindfulness + future time perspective + self-efficacy” the key factor in mitigating athlete burnout? However, there are very few scholars discussing the relationships among mindfulness, future time perspective, self-efficacy, and athlete burnout, particularly concerning an in-depth investigation of the chain meditating mechanism of “future time perspective + self-efficacy”. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 3: Future time perspective and self-efficacy function as chained mediators in the relationship between mindfulness and athlete burnout. Given the highly competitive nature of soccer, characterized by technical diversity, tactical variability, and team-based integration, soccer presents unique challenges to its athletes. Compared to other sports, soccer matches involve extended durations and require players to engage in long-distance running and sprinting, resulting in substantial physical exertion and increasing the risk of sport-related injuries. Furthermore, prolonged training schedules and frequently closed training environments, coupled with potential injury concerns, can impose significant psychological burdens on soccer athletes. Under these high-intensity physiological and psychological demands, soccer players are particularly vulnerable to mental health issues, making them more prone to athlete burnout [ 5 , 13 , 48 , 54 – 57 ]. Therefore, this study takes adolescent soccer players as the subject to explore the impact of mindfulness on burnout among young athletes. It further explores the potential intermediating roles of future time perspective and self-efficacy to construct a chain mediation model. By integrating mindfulness, future time perspective, and self-efficacy as a personality system, this study aims to examine their combined influence on athlete burnout, thereby revealing the underlying mechanisms that contribute to athlete burnout among adolescent athletes. This study ultimately seeks to provide coaches and athletes with relevant coping strategies and to offer insights for enhancing the physical and mental well-being of adolescent athletes. Participants and methods Participants Participants were selected from athletes aged 12 to 18 years who participated in the junior high school and senior high school divisions of the Chongqing Youth Football Championship. Since the participants were minors, written informed consent was obtained from their parents before participation. The questionnaires were completed within one hour after the end of training and were administered in paper form in an offline setting. To ensure the representativeness and reliability of the sample, this study selected athletes from the top eight teams in the Chongqing Youth Football Championship. These teams included one team that participated in the National Games on behalf of Chongqing, one club reserve team, and six school representative teams. A stratified random sampling method was employed, first stratifying by division and gender, then randomly selecting 300 athletes per division, with a quota of 150 paper questionnaires allocated per gender for balance. Following Ethics Committee approval, athletes were anonymously surveyed with assistance from team leaders and coaches. Participation was voluntary for free. Of 600 questionnaires distributed, 506 were valid and returned (84.33% response rate). Among them, 94 questionnaires were excluded due to: (a) missing data ( n = 37), (b) response carelessness ( n = 46), or (c) failure to return in time ( n = 11). An independent samples t-test was conducted on the excluded questionnaires, and no statistically significant differences were found between valid and invalid responses, indicating that non-response bias could be ignored. The basic information of the sample is shown in Table 1 : gender (278 male, 228 female), educational level (230 junior high, 276 senior high), and team role (221 starting, 285 substitute). Mean age was 16.51 ± 2.72 years, height 169.97 ± 8.37 cm, and weight 59.69 ± 8.92 kg. Research methods Questionnaires This study is a descriptive study, using a structured questionnaire as a survey tool for required data collection. For a meticulous and effective study, a two-phase questionnaire was implemented. First, a preliminary questionnaire was developed based on the research objectives and a review of relevant literature. Second, using GPower software for effect analysis, it was determined that a minimum of 146 valid responses was needed. However, to obtain more stable statistical data, a small-scale survey involving 200 participants was conducted. Through normal distribution verification, reliability analysis, and exploratory factor analysis, items with low contribution were removed. Then the final questionnaire consisted of five sections and was settled via the test of structural model verification. Personal background information: The content includes the basic information of participants’ age, height, weight, gender, educational level, and team role. Athlete burnout questionnaire (ABQ): This scale was adapted from Raedeke and Smith (2001) [ 58 ]. Sample items include “I feel extremely exhausted from training and competition,” “I am not achieving worthwhile results,” and “I feel a sense of aversion toward sport.” The scale consists of 15 items divided into three dimensions. Specifically, the dimensions included: emotional/physical exhaustion (5 items, where higher scores indicate more severe emotional and physical fatigue), reduced sense of accomplishment (5 items, where higher scores reflect a greater decline in achievement), and negative evaluation of sport (5 items, where higher scores suggest a stronger negative perception of sport participation). The scale is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “never,” 5 = “always”). Items 1 and 14 are reverse-scored, with higher scores indicating greater burnout. Exploratory factor analysis showed that the 15 items yielded three common factors. After direct oblique rotation, the cumulative contribution rate of these three factors reached 60.663%. The verification result of the measurement model of this scale is that, χ 2 /df = 1.799, and the values of CFI (comparative fit index), GFI (goodness-of-fit index), AGFI (adjusted goodness-of-fit index), TLI (Tucker–Lewis index), IFI (incremental fit index), RMSEA (root mean square error of approximation) and SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual) are 0.997, 0.996, 0.970, 0.987, 0.997, 0.044 and 0.015, respectively, which all meet the acceptable standard, indicating that the model fit well with the data obtained from the survey, supporting the structural validity of the ABQ. Retest analysis revealed that all item factor loadings ranged from 0.50 to 0.95. Composite reliability (CR) values were all greater than 0.60, and average variance extracted (AVE) values exceeded 0.50, demonstrating excellent composite reliability and convergent validity. Moreover, the square root of the AVE for each of the three dimensions was greater than its correlations with other variables, indicating strong discriminant validity. The Cronbach’s α values for the total scale and each dimension were all above 0.70, fully demonstrating good reliability (see Table 2 and Table 4 ). Therefore, the ABQ has good reliability and validity. Mindfulness scale: This study adopted the Chinese version of the Child and Adolescent Mindfulness Measure (CAMM), translated by Liu [ 59 ] based on the original developed by Greco et al. [ 15 ]. Example items include “It is hard for me to keep my attention focused on just one thing,” and “I often think about things that happened in the past instead of focusing on what is happening right now.” The scale consists of 10 items and uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “never,” 5 = “always”). All items are reverse-scored, and higher scores indicate higher levels of mindfulness in daily life. Exploratory factor analysis showed that five items could be extracted into one common factor. After direct oblique rotation, this single factor accounted for 82.299% of the cumulative variance. Retest analysis showed that all item factor loadings ranged from 0.50 to 0.95. CR values were all above 0.6, and AVE values all exceeded 0.5, demonstrating excellent composite reliability and convergent validity. Furthermore, the square root of the AVE for the single dimension was greater than its correlations with other variables, indicating good discriminant validity. The Cronbach’s α value for the overall scale was above 0.70, confirming strong internal consistency (see Table 2 and Table 4 ). Therefore, the mindfulness scale demonstrates good reliability and validity. Model validation analysis of adolescent athletes’ mindfulness, future time perspective, self-efficacy, and athlete burnout To explore the relationships among mindfulness, future time perspective, self-efficacy, and athlete burnout, and to test the mediating roles of future time perspective and self-efficacy, this study followed the mediation effect testing procedure proposed by Wen and Ye [ 64 ]. The estimation method used was Maximum Likelihood. Structural equation modeling was conducted using AMOS to analyze the relationships among mindfulness, future time perspective, self-efficacy, and athlete burnout. The model is modified to achieve adequate fit (see Fig. 1 ). The model fit indices are: χ 2 /df = 1.886 &lt; 2.000, CFI = 0.997, GFI = 0.993, AGFI = 0.965, TLI = 0.987, IFI = 0.997 (all &gt; 0.900), RMSEA = 0.048 &lt; 0.080 and SRMR = 0.015 &lt; 0.05, indicating that the model can be established. The standardized path coefficient β value and significance level from the mixed structural model in Fig. 1 indicate that mindfulness has a significant positive predictive effect on future time perspective and self-efficacy (β = 0.487, P &lt; 0.001; β = 0.258, P &lt; 0.001), but a significant negative predictive effect on athlete burnout (β = −0.342, P &lt; 0.001). Meanwhile, future time perspective also exhibits a significant positive predictive effect on self-efficacy (β = 0.450, P &lt; 0.001), but a significant negative predictive effect on athlete burnout (β = −0.314, P &lt; 0.001). Self-efficacy also has a significant negative predictive effect on athlete burnout (β = −0.228, P &lt; 0.001). Furthermore, the explained variance (R 2 ) was calculated for each endogenous variable to assess how well the model accounted for variance in these variables. The results showed that the R 2 value for future time perspective was 0.238, for self-efficacy was 0.382, and for athlete burnout was 0.528. According to Cohen’s (1988) criteria [ 65 ], these β and R 2 values fall within the range of medium to large effects, indicating that the model has good explained variance for variable variance and strong predictive capability. In order to verify the mediating role of future time perspective and self-efficacy between mindfulness and athlete burnout, the non-parametric percentile Bootstrap program is used to test the significance of the mediating role. The original data is repeatedly sampled 5000 times, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) is calculated. If the 95% CI for the standardized path coefficient does not include zero, the mediating role is considered significant. As shown in Table 5 , the 95% CI for the chain mediating role of mindfulness from future time perspective and self-efficacy to athlete burnout is [−0.052, −0.013]; the 95% CI for the mediating role of mindfulness from future time perspective to athlete burnout is [−0.146, −0.045]; and the 95% CI for the mediating role of mindfulness from self-efficacy to athlete burnout is [−0.061, −0.015]. As none of these three intervals includes 0, each mediating role is considered significant. Further analysis of the effects of each variable on athlete burnout shows that the direct effect of mindfulness on athlete burnout is −0.342, and the total mediating effect value (−0.263) is the sum of the mediating effects of the three mediating paths. The total effect is the sum of the direct effect and the total mediating effect, that is, −0.605; the mediating effect value divided by the total effect represents the effect quantity, and the effect quantities of the three mediating paths in this study are 8.43%, 25.12%, and 9.92%, respectively. Therefore, Hypotheses 1 to 3 are all valid. Discussions Influence of mindfulness on athlete burnout This study found that mindfulness has a significant negative effect on athlete burnout, indicating higher levels of mindfulness are associated with lower levels of athlete burnout, conversely, the lower the degree of mindfulness among adolescent athletes, the higher athlete burnout will occur. The results of this study are consistent with previous research [ 7 , 13 , 66 – 68 ]. Adolescent athletes with higher degrees of mindfulness tend to focus more easily on the present situation during training, possess clearer intrinsic goals, are more able to put aside external distractions, and have lower degrees of response to negative stimuli in the training or competition environment, effectively reducing emotional exhaustion, and thus reducing athlete burnout. Adolescent athletes with lower degrees of mindfulness are more easily disturbed by external factors, unable to focus on the present, distracted by other factors during training, experience a reduced sense of accomplishment during training or competition, and are more prone to generating negative evaluations of their sport participation, thus increasing athlete burnout among adolescents. Therefore, mindfulness is an important factor for adolescent athletes in reducing burnout. Mediating effect of future time perspective This study found that mindfulness has a significant positive impact on future time perspective. This result shows that adolescent athletes with higher degrees of mindfulness tend to be accompanied by higher future time perspective, conversely, adolescent athletes with lower mindfulness possess lower future time perspective. Some studies hold that mindfulness and future time perspective serve as a bridge connecting the present and the future; individuals with the high levels of mindfulness often have higher future time perspective, and vice versa [ 32 , 69 – 73 ]. Some studies also believe that, with the improvement of an individual’s mindfulness level, not only can individual psychological well-being be maintained or promoted in the present moment, but it can also stimulate an individual’s hope for the future and improve the individual’s conception and ability to utilize and manage time, thereby enhancing the individual’s future time perspective. This study also found that future time perspective has a significant negative impact on athlete burnout; this result indicates that the higher the future time perspective of adolescent athletes, the lower the level of athlete burnout; on the contrary, the lower the future time perspective of adolescent athletes, the higher the level of athlete burnout. Because athletes with higher levels of future time perspective have a clearer cognition and experience of goals, they are more able to focus on their own future development, have concrete plans for the future, and look forward to the future with confidence, openness, and optimism, and respond to the difficulties and setbacks encountered in competition or training in a proactive manner, which can alleviate the occurrence of athlete burnout. Athletes with lower levels of future time perspective feel pessimistic and disappointed about the future, lack in-depth planning, are more prone to negative and withdrawn emotions during competition or training, and experience a reduced sense of accomplishment in sports participation, thereby leading to the occurrence of athlete burnout among adolescents [ 7 , 32 , 74 , 75 ]. In addition, this study also found that mindfulness can not only directly impact athlete burnout negatively, but also indirectly impact athlete burnout negatively through the mediating role of future time perspective. Some studies suggested that, both mindfulness and future time perspective are protective factors against athlete burnout, and that mindfulness has a positive predictive effect on future time perspective [ 18 , 33 , 36 ]. To sum up, mindfulness has a negative impact on the occurrence of athlete burnout in adolescent athletes both in a direct way and through future time perspective indirectly. Furthermore, combined with the Bootstrap mediating effect test procedure, it can be inferred that Hypothesis 1, that future time perspective plays a mediating role between mindfulness and athlete burnout, is valid. Mediating effect of self-efficacy This study found that mindfulness has a significant positive impact on self-efficacy, indicating the higher the level of mindfulness, the better the level of self-efficacy, and vice versa. Some studies have found that those with high levels of mindfulness often have stronger abilities in emotional regulation and in maintaining positive emotions, and are better able to prevent the current situation from being affected by the past and the future [ 76 ], thus enhancing their self-efficacy [ 51 , 77 ]. Other studies have also found that athletes with higher self-efficacy possess a firm belief in overcoming all difficulties, as well as confidence in their control over tasks and the task environment, and that athletes with higher levels of mindfulness are better able to maintain this belief and confidence, thereby enhancing the athlete’s self-efficacy [ 10 , 42 , 43 , 48 ]. This study also found that self-efficacy has a significant negative impact on athlete burnout, showing that higher self-efficacy reduces the likelihood of burnout, whereas, with reduction in the level of self-efficacy among adolescent athletes, they are more likely to experience athlete burnout. Some studies have pointed out that athletes with higher levels of self-efficacy have more confidence in completing tasks in training or competition, thereby reducing the risk of athlete burnout, while athletes with lower levels of self-efficacy have less confidence in completing tasks in training or competition, are more prone to generating pessimistic thoughts, and adopt avoidance strategies, thereby exacerbating the risk of athlete burnout [ 8 , 48 , 66 , 78 ]. In addition, this study also found that mindfulness can not only impact athlete burnout negatively directly, but also negatively impact athlete burnout indirectly through the mediating role of self-efficacy. Because mindfulness can positively predict self-efficacy, and mindfulness and self-efficacy are both negatively correlated with athlete burnout, enhancing the level of mindfulness and self-efficacy among athletes can effectively reduce athlete burnout [ 10 , 48 , 79 ]. This result precisely supports previous research [ 8 , 10 , 13 , 48 , 79 , 80 ] that mindfulness can both negatively impact athlete burnout in adolescents, and also reduce athlete burnout in adolescents through self-efficacy. Based on the above and combined with the Bootstrap mediating effect test procedure, it can be inferred that Hypothesis 2, that self-efficacy plays a mediating role between mindfulness and athlete burnout, is valid. Chain mediating effect of future time perspective and self-efficacy Through the results of the Bootstrap mediating test program, this study shows that mindfulness has an impact on the occurrence of adolescent athletes burnout through the partial mediating effect of self-efficacy and future time perspective. This verifies Hypothesis 3. Specifically, those with higher levels of mindfulness tend to have higher levels of future time perspective, and higher levels of future time perspective are accompanied by higher levels of self-efficacy. Moreover, both future time perspective and self-efficacy are important factors in individual psychological well-being and in regulating the emotions that produce athlete burnout, thereby reducing the occurrence of athlete burnout in adolescent athletes. Conversely, those with low levels of mindfulness have relatively low future time perspective and self-efficacy, thereby increasing the risk of athlete burnout in adolescents. The aforementioned research results have already confirmed that future time perspective and self-efficacy each serve as mediators between mindfulness and athlete burnout in adolescents, and previous research [ 53 , 81 , 82 ] has affirmed a positive correlation between future time perspective and self-efficacy, demonstrating that individuals with higher future time perspective typically possess stronger self-efficacy. This confirms that adolescent athletes with higher levels of mindfulness have higher levels of future time perspective, and have sufficient anticipation and plans for the future. They tend to select positive coping strategies to handle problems when facing difficulties and setbacks, which can improve their proactivity and intrinsic motivation during training, facilitate improvements in their self-efficacy, and lead to greater confidence in completing tasks in training or competition, thereby alleviating athlete burnout [ 10 , 48 , 83 , 84 ]. Therefore, mindfulness in adolescent athletes can negatively impact the occurrence of athlete burnout through future time perspective and self-efficacy. In short, mindfulness can both directly influence the occurrence of athlete burnout in adolescent athletes, and can also indirectly influence the occurrence of athlete burnout in adolescent athletes through the mediating effect of future time perspective and self-efficacy, as well as the chain mediating effect of future time perspective and self-efficacy. This provides a theoretical basis for reducing athlete burnout in adolescent athletes. Meanwhile, it also provides an effective practical reference to understand the impact of mindfulness, future time perspective, and self-efficacy on reducing athlete burnout in adolescents. Research value and prospect This study revealed the influence of mindfulness on adolescent athlete burnout and its underlying mechanisms, which hold both theoretical and practical implications. In terms of theoretical significance, this study systematically examined the levels of mindfulness, future time perspective, self-efficacy, and athlete burnout among adolescent athletes. Under the guidance of multiple theoretical perspectives, including the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions, the theory of optimal time balance, conservation of resources theory, and the socioemotional selectivity theory, a theoretical model of “mindfulness-future time perspective-self-efficacy-athlete burnout” was constructed. It revealed the influence paths of mindfulness on adolescent athlete burnout, including the direct effect path, indirect paths with future time perspective and self-efficacy as single mediators, and the chained mediation effect of “future time perspective-self-efficacy,” thereby providing a structured theoretical framework and mechanism for understanding how mindfulness alleviates athlete burnout. In addition, the theoretical innovation of this study lies in that it is not limited to examining the internal mechanism of the influence of mindfulness on adolescent athlete burnout through the single mediation of future time perspective or self-efficacy. Rather, it emphasizes that future time perspective facilitates self-efficacy. Therefore, this study focuses on exploring the chained mediation effect of “future time perspective-self-efficacy” on the influence of mindfulness on adolescent athlete burnout, which further enriches the relevant theory on how mindfulness affects athlete burnout among adolescents. It also further confirms the key mediating roles of future time perspective and self-efficacy in the process through which mindfulness alleviates athlete burnout, and provides a referable integrated path model for future research. In terms of practical implications, the findings of this study can offer feasible guidance for coaches in actual training and psychological intervention. First, the results indicate that mindfulness has significant direct and indirect effects on adolescent athlete burnout. Therefore, coaches should attach great importance to cultivating athletes’ mindfulness during training and competition preparation. Specifically, mindfulness interventions can help athletes enhance their awareness of their current emotional and physical states, and reduce overreactions to failure, stress, and other negative events through practices such as mindfulness meditation, breathing control, and body scanning, thus slowing the depletion of psychological resources. Second, future time perspective, as a key mediating variable in the effect of mindfulness on athlete burnout, indicates that on the basis of mindfulness, athletes’ clear cognition and planning ability for future goals will further strengthen their psychological preparedness for coping with challenges. Therefore, coaches can bolster athletes’ future-oriented awareness and long-term motivation by setting phased training goals, guiding athletes to formulate personal growth plans, and conducting career planning counseling. Third, self-efficacy, as an important psychological resource affecting athlete burnout, depends on the athletes’ affirmation of their own abilities and their sense of control. In training, coaches should focus on differentiated encouragement, assign achievable challenge tasks, and provide timely positive feedback to enhance athletes’ confidence in completing tasks. Additionally, strategies such as role modeling and group task collaboration can also effectively promote the development of self-efficacy. Finally, the chained mediation effect of “future time perspective-self-efficacy” revealed in this study further emphasizes the fundamental role of future-oriented thinking in building self-efficacy. In training design, goal setting should be integrated with the cultivation of self-efficacy. Athletes should be encouraged to envision future achievements, and to continuously accumulate successful experiences through behavioral verification, thereby forming a virtuous cycle of psychological resources. In summary, this study not only provides a multidimensional perspective for understanding the formation mechanism of athlete burnout, but also offers implementable practical solutions for psychological intervention, resource mobilization, and training optimization in sports teams. Therefore, coaches should emphasize the development of mindfulness among adolescent athletes while also paying attention to the influence of future time perspective and self-efficacy on athlete burnout. Although this study has achieved certain results in theoretical construction and empirical testing, there are still the following limitations that need to be further improved in future research. First, this study adopted a cross-sectional design, which makes it difficult to determine causal relationships among variables. Therefore, future research may adopt longitudinal tracking designs or experimental intervention methods to more clearly reveal the dynamic changes and causal paths among mindfulness, future time perspective, self-efficacy, and athlete burnout, thereby more robustly verifying the chained mediation model proposed in this study. Second, the sample of this study was selected from high-level adolescent soccer players in Chongqing, which has certain group and regional limitations. Whether the findings of this study apply to other sports, different levels of competition (such as amateur athletes), or adolescent athletes from different cultural backgrounds still needs to be further tested. Future research may increase sample diversity in terms of sport type, competitive level, and regional culture to enhance the external validity and scope of application of the findings. Third, all variables in this study were measured through self-report questionnaires, which inevitably have certain limitations. On the one hand, self-reports are susceptible to social desirability bias, memory bias, and subjective interpretation, which may affect the authenticity and accuracy of the data. On the other hand, since all data were collected from a single source at the same time point, common method variance (CMV) may occur, which could exaggerate the correlations among variables. Although this study has made efforts to reduce the impact of CMV during questionnaire design and data collection (e.g., anonymous responses, reduction of leading language), the bias cannot be completely ruled out. Future research is advised to introduce multi-source data (such as coach evaluations, and behavioral observations), or use statistical techniques such as marker variables for confirmatory testing, in order to improve the reliability and explanatory validity of the results. Finally, although this study focused on three key psychological factors-mindfulness, future time perspective, and self-efficacy-the causes of adolescent athlete burnout are extremely complex. Future research can further explore the following questions: Are there other potential mediating or moderating variables that have not been included in the model (e.g., coping strategies, perceived stress)? Do different types of mindfulness interventions (such as mindfulness meditation, body scanning, or breathing awareness) have different effects on the mediators? Does the model still hold under conditions of high versus low competitive stress? An in-depth investigation of these questions may provide more targeted and operable theoretical foundations for psychological interventions and training support for adolescent athletes. Conclusion Mindfulness is significantly positively correlated with future time perspective and self-efficacy, and significantly negatively correlated with athlete burnout. Future time perspective is significantly positively correlated with self-efficacy and negatively correlated with athlete burnout. Self-efficacy is also significantly negatively correlated with athlete burnout. Furthermore, mindfulness influences athlete burnout in adolescent athletes through the separate mediating effects of future time perspective and self-efficacy, as well as through the chain mediating role of future time perspective and self-efficacy.
