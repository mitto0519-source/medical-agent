Abstract Background This study delineates brain metabolic signatures underlying neuroendocrine-psychosocial interactions in Kallmann syndrome (KS), a rare genetic disorder characterized by congenital hypogonadism and anosmia. In this prospective case-control study, 30 KS patients and 30 matched healthy controls (HCs) underwent brain [ 18 F]FDG PET scans, sex hormone assays, and standardized neuropsychological assessments. A voxel-wise group comparison analysis was conducted to identify clusters of brain metabolic patterns between KS patients and HCs. Subsequently, correlation and mediation analyses were performed to investigate the interrelationships and mediating effect among brain metabolic patterns, sex hormone levels, and psychosocial factors. Results We identified six hypermetabolic clusters in KS patients, predominantly located in the fronto-limbic system and the default mode network (DMN) in KS patients. These clusters of hypermetabolism were significantly associated fertility anxiety scale (FAS), health information avoidance scale (HIAS) and health information overload scale (HIOS). In addition, mediation analysis indicated that bilateral basal ganglia hypermetabolism acted as a significant mediator between HIAS and FAS (β = 0.274, P = 0.031). Conclusion Patients with KS exhibited a distinct hypermetabolism pattern in the DMN-prefrontal circuit, which functionally bridges neuroendocrine dysfunction and reproductive health anxiety. Introduction Kallmann Syndrome (KS) is a rare clinical and genetically heterogeneous disorder characterized by impaired or absent sense of smell [ 1 ] and abnormal synthesis, secretion, or action of Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone(GnRH) [ 2 ]. GnRH is the primary hormone regulating the reproductive axis. KS predominantly affects males, with a reported male-to-female ratio of approximately 5:1 [ 3 ]. Consequently, patients with KS typically present with either a complete or partial absence of puberty, accompanied by reproductive abnormalities, including issues in the development of the pituitary gland, hypothalamus, and olfactory bulb [ 4 ]. Patients with olfactory deficits may feel self-conscious and insecure, particularly in social settings, due to their inability to detect body odor. The absence of pubertal development and a youthful appearance further impact self-confidence, often creating barriers to dating and forming intimate relationships [ 5 ]. These psychological burdens associated with disrupted puberty should not be overlooked. Studies have shown that while testosterone therapy can improve physical function and vitality, it fails to fully alleviate anxiety, body image shame, and low self-esteem, which can have lasting effects on psychosexual health [ 6 ]. Furthermore, the emotional, psychological, and psychosexual effects of KS are often underestimated by clinicians. Recent studies indicate that 34% of KS patients experience moderate to severe depressive symptoms [ 7 , 8 ]. Notably, the psychosocial status of KS patients may lead to potential alterations in brain structure and function, particularly in areas involved in emotional regulation, cognitive memory, and visual processing, which are not fully mitigated by testosterone therapy [ 9 , 10 ]. A brain MRI study involving 45 men with KS revealed significant brain changes specifically involving the gyri recti and the contiguous medial orbitofrontal regions [ 11 ]. 2-[ 18 F]fluoro-2-deoxy glucose positron emission tomography ([ 18 F]FDG PET) is a powerful tool for non-invasive assessment of brain metabolism. [ 18 F]FDG PET can offer more detailed insights by measuring glucose consumption in the astrocyte-synapse functional unit. It can show reduced glucose utilization in neurodegeneration and increased glucose utilization during inflammatory processes [ 12 ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that abnormal brain metabolism identified by [ 18 F]FDG PET is significantly linked to psychological states, including symptoms of depression and anxiety [ 13 ]. However, the brain metabolic patterns in patients with KS remain poorly understood. The current study aims to describe the brain [ 18 F]FDG PET findings, to identify the brain functional connectivity underlying KS, and to assess associations with psychosocial factors and sex hormone levels. Materials and methods Study population A total of 30 patients with KS were prospectively recruited between August 2023 and March 2024. The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of KS. (2) completed [ 18 F]FDG PET/CT scanning and MRI. The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with brain tumors, cerebral infarction and other brain structural abnormalities; (2) clinically diagnosed as schizophrenia, depression and other mental disorders; (3) insufficient quality of PET/CT images. Additionally, a control group of 30 age- and sex-matched healthy controls with no history of neuropsychiatric disorders and normal neuropsychological tests was enrolled. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of School of Life Sciences of Central South University (No.2017030801) and adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. All participants signed written informed consent forms. Clinical evaluation and data collection Demographic information, sex hormones, treatments and psychological assessments were collected from medical records. The measured sex hormones included estradiol (E2), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), progesterone (P), testosterone (T), and prolactin (PRL). Psychological assessments were conducted using validated scales, including the Health Information Avoidance Scale (HIAS), Health Information Overload Scale (HIOS), and Fertility Anxiety Scale (FAS). These scales were carefully chosen to evaluate participants’ psychological states relevant to health information processing and fertility concerns, and were carried out by two experienced psychiatrists (≥ 5 years) prior to the PET scan. Chinese-language versions of all instruments used in this study were validated. Prior to the PET scan, all KS patients were receiving or had already received hormone replacement therapy. [ 18 F]FDG PET imaging acquisition Brain PET was performed using a PET/CT scanner (Biograph Vision 600, Siemens Medical Solutions). A low dose CT scan was obtained simultaneously for attenuation correction, as per routine clinical practice. The [ 18 F]FDG was procured from HTA Co., Ltd., with a radiochemical purity exceeding 95%. All subjects fasted for at least 6 h before receiving an intravenous injection of [ 18 F]FDG (3.7 MBq/kg), followed by a 10-minute list-mode data acquisition 45–60 min post-injection during a sensory rest period. The attenuation- and decay-corrected frames were reconstructed using TrueX + TOF (Ultra HD-PET) OSEM algorithm (12 iterations, 5 subsets) and stored in a 440 × 440 images matrix. All PET images were reconstructed with an isotropic voxel size of 1.65 × 1.65 × 1.65 mm³. The reconstructed spatial resolution was controlled by a Gaussian post-filter applied during image reconstruction, with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 1.7 mm. MRI acquisition All brain MRI were acquired using a 3.0T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Medical Solutions). A T1-weighted Magnetization Prepared–Rapid Gradient Echo (MP-RAGE) sequence with whole-brain coverage was obtained for all participants. The Specific scanning parameters for MP-RAGE are as follows: repetition time: 2300 ms, echo time: 2.03 ms, flip angle: 9°, voxel size: 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm 3 , acquisition matrix: 256 × 256 mm 2 , and 176 slices with no gap. During the scan, the participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed and stay awake. Statistical 3D parametric mapping Image processing was conducted using the Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12) toolkit. SPM was utilized to correct the data origin, and PET images were registered to corresponding MRI images. Partial volume correction (PVC) was performed using the Müller-Gärtner (M-G) method. For this step, individual T1-weighted MRI was segmented using SPM12’s Segment toolbox to generate probability maps for gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The tissue sampling distance was set to 3 mm, while other parameters were kept at their default settings. Individual [ 18 F]FDG PET volumes were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard stereotactic space. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the Gaussian smoothing kernel was set to 6 mm. Following smoothing, the standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was calculated with the whole brain as the reference area. Notably, given that KS patients exhibited widespread metabolic alterations in regions such as the cerebellum and occipital lobe, the whole brain was chosen as the reference to avoid confounding effects associated with altered metabolism in these commonly used reference areas. A two-sample t-test model in SPM12 was employed for group comparisons. To further control for potential sex effects, an additional SPM two-sample t-test was performed comparing the 26 male KS patients with male healthy controls. The SNBPI extraction function was used to identify regions of interest (ROIs). Comparisons between different groups were performed using the two-sample t-test model of SPM12. The height threshold of metabolic changes was set at p ˂ 0.001 ( p &lt; 0.05 false discovery rate [FDR] corrected at cluster). Significant clusters were visualized, reported using the Xjview ( http://www.alivelearn.net/xjview)an d MRIcroGL ( https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl/ ) toolbox. Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 25.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) or mean ± standard deviation (SD). All data were tested for normality. T test was used for normally distributed data, Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data, and Chi-square test was used for comparison of rates. Multiple linear regression was used to analyze the relationship between SUVR and hormone levels and psychology parameters. Mediation analysis was conducted to investigate whether there is a mediating effect of brain abnormal metabolism in social psychology. So as to identify specific brain regions as therapeutic targets. Results Demographic and clinical features Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. A total of 30 patients were enrolled in the KS group and 30 in the HC group, with both groups being well matched in terms of demographics. The mean age at the time of PET scan was 32 ± 7 years, and 86.7% of the participants were male. None of the subjects reported any past or present neurological or psychiatric disorders, or any history of illegal drug use or nicotine/alcohol use disorders. Additionally, the FAS scores were significantly higher in the KS group compared to the HC group ( P &lt; 0.001). Brain glucose metabolic alterations in patients with KS Our PET imaging analysis (see Fig. 1 ) identified two extensive hypometabolic clusters, found in KS patients but not in healthy controls, predominantly located within the right gyrus rectus, bilateral occipital lobes, and posterior cingulate. Moreover, six hypermetabolic clusters were identified, including bilateral gyrus rectus, paraHippocampal, amygdala, olfactory and anterior cingulate, brainstem and limbic lobe, bilateral basal ganglia, left medial orbitofrontal cortex and left posterior orbitofrontal cortex, right anterior orbitofrontal cortex and right insula, and these regions were mainly located within the default mode network (DMN) and the fronto-limbic system, as well as in the olfactory regions. Detailed anatomical locations of these clusters are provided in Table 2 , and the distribution of individual SUVR values between the groups is depicted in Fig. 2 . The distribution of metabolic clusters in the male-only KS subgroup was highly consistent with that observed in the full cohort including female participants, supporting the robustness and generalizability of the findings. The results of this supplementary analysis are provided in Table S1 and Figure S1 . Correlation analysis Fig. 3 and Table S2 present the exploratory relationships between brain PET metabolism and both psychological and hormonal parameters in KS patients. Given that Cluster3 and Cluster5 represent the largest clusters, our analysis primarily focused on the correlations between these clusters and the psychological scale scores, as well as sex hormone levels. Notably, increased metabolic values in the basal ganglia cluster (C5) were significantly positively correlated with HIAS ( r = 0.485, p &lt; 0.05) and FAS ( r = 0.420, p &lt; 0.05). Similarly, higher metabolic values in the limbic system and olfactory cluster (C3) were significantly associated with HIOS ( r = 0.412, p &lt; 0.05) and FAS ( r = 0.435, p &lt; 0.05). No significant correlations were found between the PET metabolic values and sex hormone levels (see Table S3 ). Mediation analysis As depicted in Fig. 4 , the mediation analysis revealed a statistically significant indirect effect of HIAS on FAS through the basal ganglia cluster (Indirect effect (a×b) = 0.274, 95%CI [0.07–0.65]). Notably, the direct effect of HIAS on FAS was not significant after controlling for the mediator (β = 0.152, 95%CI [-0.23-0.70]), indicating the health information avoidance effect on FAS was significantly mediated by hypermetabolism of basal ganglia. Discussion This study provides novel insights into the neurofunctional underpinnings of psychological distress in patients with KS through the identification of distinct brain hypermetabolic patterns and their mediation effects on infertility-related anxiety. The key findings reveal that KS patients exhibit pronounced hypermetabolism in regions integral to emotional regulation (fronto-limbic system) and self-referential processing (DMN), which are mechanistically linked to information avoidance behaviors and fertility anxiety. These results advance our understanding of the brain-behavior interplay in KS, highlighting potential neural substrates for targeted interventions. The observed hypermetabolism in the DMN and fronto-limbic regions aligns with prior structural MRI findings of volume reductions in medial orbitofrontal and gyri recti in KS patients [ 11 ]. However, our FDG-PET findings reveal a compensatory functional overdrive in these regions, suggesting adaptive neuroplasticity to counteract structural deficits. The DMN, a set of brain regions active during rest and disengagement from the external environment, encompasses key areas such as the posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, and medial prefrontal cortex, which are critically involved in self-referential thought and social cognition [ 14 , 15 ]. Recent functional neuroimaging studies have consistently demonstrated that the DMN exhibits high metabolic activity during resting states, particularly in these regions [ 14 ]. A recent meta-analysis further corroborated that the DMN serves as the primary neural substrate for rumination, a maladaptive form of involuntary emotion regulation [ 16 ]. In line with these findings, we hypothesize that the observed hyperactivation of the DMN in KS patients may reflect persistent rumination about infertility and body image concerns, consistent with their elevated FAS scores. Similarly, hypermetabolism in the anterior cingulate and insular cortices—key salience network nodes—likely intensifies interoceptive awareness of infertility-related stressors, perpetuating anxiety cycles [ 17 ]. Paradoxically, despite the congenital anosmia typically associated with KS, we observed hypermetabolism in the olfactory cortex. This unexpected finding may indicate maladaptive cross-modal plasticity or aberrant neurodevelopmental wiring of olfactory-limbic pathways, potentially compensating for the lack of olfactory input. This paradox underscores the complex interplay between structural deficits and functional compensatory mechanisms in KS. The strong correlations between fronto-limbic hypermetabolism and HIAS/HIOS scores resonate with established models of health anxiety [ 18 ]. Prefrontal overactivation may represent excessive cognitive control efforts to suppress infertility-related intrusive thoughts, while limbic hyperactivity (amygdala/hippocampus) could drive emotional amplification of perceived reproductive threats. Intriguingly, the mediation role of basal ganglia metabolism between avoidance of health information and fertility anxiety suggests a striatal-prefrontal mechanism: avoidance of health information may dysregulate dorsolateral prefrontal-striatal circuits governing decision-making [ 19 ], thereby heightening anxiety about fertility. This aligns with neuroeconomic theories positing that basal ganglia mediate cost-benefit analyses during uncertainty [ 20 ], which may be pathologically skewed in KS patients facing irreversible infertility. By identifying the basal ganglia as a critical mediator between health information avoidance and fertility anxiety, we propose the potential of targeted neuromodulation as a promising therapeutic strategy to alleviate the persistent psychosocial burden in KS patients. The lack of correlation between metabolic patterns and sex hormone levels challenges the conventional view that androgen deficiency alone underlies KS-related psychological sequelae. Notably, even with hormone replacement therapy, persistent neural dysregulation suggests that prenatal GnRH deficiency may induce irreversible neurodevelopmental alterations in emotion-processing networks. Evidence from animal models indicates that embryonic GnRH neurons play a crucial role in guiding the migration of olfactory and limbic circuits [ 21 ], and their absence could result in permanent miswiring between the DMN and prefrontal regions. Clinically, this suggests that testosterone/gonadotropin supplementation alone is insufficient to fully rectify these neural abnormalities, highlighting the need for adjunctive psychotherapeutic interventions. Such as cognitive-behavioral therapy for health anxiety, may be essential in addressing maladaptive metacognition and improving psychological outcomes. The striking bilateral occipital hypometabolism observed in KS likely reflects neurodevelopmental and neurohormonal disturbances rather than classical degeneration; the occipital cortex, central to visual processing, often shows reduced FDG uptake in Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Lewy body dementias [ 22 – 24 ], and analogous network effects may occur here. Likewise, right gyrus rectus hypometabolism—part of the orbitomedial prefrontal cortex—stems from congenital olfactory bulb agenesis: loss of olfactory afferents reduces sensory-driven activity in connected prefrontal regions, as seen in post‐COVID anosmia patients whose FDG‐PET uptake in the rectus correlates with olfactory scores [ 25 ]. These findings suggest KS’s primary developmental and hormonal deficits induce secondary metabolic downregulation across distributed cortical networks; future work with resting‐state connectivity and metabolomic analyses is needed to clarify these pathophysiological mechanisms. This study has limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences—longitudinal studies tracking metabolic changes during hormone replacement and psychotherapy are warranted. Second, the predominantly male cohort (86.7%) limits generalizability to female KS patients, who may exhibit distinct neuroendocrine-psychosocial interactions. Third, the lack of resting-state fMRI data prevents direct assessment of DMN functional connectivity. Future multimodal imaging integrating [ 18 F]FDG PET, diffusion tensor imaging, and hormonal pulsatility analysis could delineate structure-function-hormone tripartite relationships. Future research should validate these metabolic markers in larger cohorts and explore their utility in personalized treatment algorithms. Fourth, while our FDG-PET study offers insights into neural mechanisms of reproductive health anxiety in KS, FDG lacks neurotransmitter specificity. Applying PET tracers targeting 5-HT1A receptors ( 18 F-FCWAY) [ 26 ]. D2 receptors ( 18 F-fallypride) [ 27 ], or mGluR5( 18 F-FPEB) [ 28 ] hold considerable promise for future KS research. For instance, androgen deficiency may disrupt dopamine-related circuits, while reduced estrogen levels may alter 5-HT1A receptor expression, both of which can be directly visualized using molecular PET imaging. When integrated with FDG-PET-derived metabolic mapping, neurotransmitter-targeted PET could further elucidate the molecular network dysfunctions associated with hormonal dysregulation in KS. Conclusion This study establishes that KS patients harbor a unique neurofunctional signature characterized by DMN-prefrontal-limbic hypermetabolism, which mediates infertility-related psychological distress independently of sex hormone levels. These findings shift the therapeutic paradigm from purely endocrine management to integrated neuropsychiatric interventions.
