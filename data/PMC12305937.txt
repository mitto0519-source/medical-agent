Abstract Background Chronic liver disease (CLD) is a growing global public health issue and ranks as the seventh leading cause of death in Ethiopia. However, regional-level data particularly from the West Arsi Zone are scarce. This study aimed to fill this gap by assessing the burden and key determinants of CLD among adults attending liver clinics, providing essential evidence for targeted interventions. Objective To determine the magnitude and associated factors with medically confirmed CLD among adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing follow-up for suspected or confirmed CLD in three public hospitals in the West Arsi Zone. Methodology An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February to July 2022. A total of 384 adult participants were selected using systematic random sampling. Data were collected through structured interviews and medical record reviews. Multivariable binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with CLD, with adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported. Results All 384 selected participants completed the study, yielding a response rate of 100%. Among them, 60.2% (231/384) were clinically diagnosed with chronic liver disease (CLD), with a higher prevalence observed among males (62%). Background Chronic liver disease (CLD) is a progressive condition marked by sustained liver inflammation, fibrosis, and eventual cirrhosis, contributing significantly to global morbidity and mortality. It accounts for approximately 2.2% of all deaths worldwide and 1.5% of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost annually [ 1 ]. Cirrhosis, the final stage of CLD, is characterized by disruption of liver architecture, widespread fibrosis, and impaired liver function. It leads to complications such as sarcopenia, reduced functional capacity, and increased hospitalizations [ 2 ]. Major causes of CLD include chronic viral hepatitis (HBV, HCV), alcohol abuse, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), autoimmune hepatitis, genetic conditions, and exposure to hepatotoxic drugs or herbal substances [ 3 , 4 ]. While national data highlight the burden of CLD, regional-level data, particularly in the West Arsi Zone, remain scarce. This zone is characterized by cultural practices such as traditional alcohol consumption and widespread herbal medicine use, which may increase the risk of liver damage [ 5 ]. Despite CLD being a leading public health concern in Ethiopia, no prior studies have assessed its magnitude or associated risk factors among adults in the West Arsi Zone—a critical gap in evidence that this study aimed to address. Therefore, this study investigated the prevalence and determinants of clinically confirmed CLD among adults (≥ 18 years) undergoing evaluation for suspected liver disease at gastroenterology and hepatology clinics in selected public hospitals in the West Arsi Zone. Findings from this study are expected to inform targeted prevention and early detection strategies in similar resource-limited settings. Theoretical framework This study was guided by a theoretical framework developed from an extensive review of the existing literature. The framework categorizes factors associated with medically confirmed chronic liver disease (CLD) into three major domains: Socio-demographic and economic factors, which include sex, age, income, educational status, place of residence, marital status, cultural practices, and family support. Behavioral factors, including alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, khat chewing, and level of physical activity. Clinical factors, encompassing viral hepatitis infections, the presence of comorbid conditions, and elevated body mass index (BMI) [ 6 ]. This classification provided a structured approach for identifying and analyzing the determinants of CLD in the study population. Methods Study design and setting An institution-based descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted between February 20 and July 6, 2022, in three public hospitals located in West Arsi Zone, Ethiopia: Shashamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Melka Oda General Hospital, and Negele Arsi Hospital. These hospitals serve as referral centers for the region. Study population Source Population:All adults (≥ 18 years) attending gastroenterology and hepatology clinics for suspected or probable chronic liver disease (CLD). Study Population: Systematically sampled adults on follow-up care who met the inclusion criteria. Inclusion Criteria: Adults aged ≥ 18 years with suspected or probable confirmed CLD who were on follow-up care during the study period. Exclusion Criteria:Patients who were critically ill or had incomplete medical records at the time of data collection. Sample size determination The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula: n = Z 2 * p (1 − p)/d 2 . Where: Z = 1.96 (corresponding to a 95% confidence level). p = 0.5 (assumed prevalence, due to absence of prior data). d = 0.05 (margin of error). n=(1.96)2 * 0.5 (1 − 0.5)/(0.05) 2 =384. Sampling procedure and allocation Proportional allocation was used to distribute the total sample size ( n = 384) across the three selected hospitals based on the estimated number of chronic liver disease (CLD) patients seen at each facility. The formula used for proportional allocation was: ni = Ni/N x n​. Where: ni = sample size allocated to hospital i . Ni = total CLD patient volume at hospital i . N = total CLD patient volume across all three hospitals. n = total sample size (384). Using this formula, the sample was proportionally distributed as follows: Negele Arsi Hospital (130 participants), Shashamane Hospital (127 participants), and Melka Oda Hospital (127 participants). A systematic random sampling technique was employed in this study, where every second patient was selected from the sampling frame (i.e., k = 2k). The first participant was chosen at random from the first two eligible patients, and thereafter, every 2nd patient was included until the desired sample size was reached. This approach ensured an even and unbiased distribution of participants throughout the data collection period. Data collection Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire, reviewed patient medical records, and anthropometric measurements. Interviews were conducted in Afan Oromo and Amharic by three trained BSc nurses under supervision. The tool was adapted from the WHO STEP-wise approach to NCD risk factors and refined based on study objectives and conceptual framework. The questionnaire was developed in English, translated to Afan Oromo and Amharic, and then back-translated to ensure accuracy and conceptual equivalence. A pretest involving 5% of the sample was conducted at Dodola Hospital to assess clarity and reliability. Measurements Socio-demographic variables included age, sex, marital status, education, residence, income, occupation, family support, and family history of CLD. Behavioral variables included: Alcohol consumption &gt; 20 ml/day for ≥ 6 months (as per WHO classification) [ 6 ]. Khat chewing (daily use ≥ 6 months) [ 5 ]. Cigarette smoking (daily use ≥ 6 months). Physical inactivity (no moderate activity ≥ 3 days/week or vigorous activity ≥ 2 days/week) [ 7 ]. Dietary habits (high-fat diet, fruit/vegetable intake). Clinical variables included BMI (kg/m²), comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, HIV/AIDS), viral hepatitis (HBV, HCV), and use of herbal remedies. BMI was categorized as underweight (&lt; 18.5), normal (18.5–24.9), overweight (25–29.9), and obese (≥ 30). Definition of Chronic Liver Disease Medically confirmed CLD (Yes) CLD diagnosis supported by clinical evaluation and at least one of the following. Biochemical markers (ALT or AST &gt; 2× upper limit of normal for &gt; 6 months). Radiologic findings (e.g., ultrasound evidence of fibrosis or cirrhosis). Histological confirmation (when available). Probable CLD (No) Suspected cases based on clinical signs and symptoms, without definitive laboratory or imaging confirmation, following WHO diagnostic criteria [ 8 ]. Data processing and analysis Data were entered using EpiData v4.6 and analyzed in SPSS v25. Descriptive statistics (frequency, mean, SD) summarized participant characteristics. Bivariable logistic regression identified candidate variables ( p &lt; 0.25) for the final model. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of medically confirmed CLD. Variables with p-values &lt; 0.05 at 95% confidence interval (CI) were considered statistically significant. Results Socio-demographic characteristics A total of 384 adults with suspected or probable chronic liver disease (CLD) were included in the study, yielding a 100% response rate. Among them, 231 (60.2%) were medically confirmed CLD cases. Of these confirmed cases, 143 (62%) were male and 88 (38%) were female. The overall sample included 224 (58.3%) males and 160 (41.7%) females. The mean age of the participants was 42.8 ± 13.1 years. A total of 191 (49.7%) were married, 209 (54.4%) resided in urban areas, and 102 (26.6%) had secondary-level education. The mean monthly income was 2613.5 ± 1495.9 Ethiopian Birr (ETB). Detailed distributions are presented in Table 1 . The behavioral and sociocultural characteristics of the study participants Among the 384 participants, 280 (72.9%) reported consuming more than 20 ml of alcohol daily, 110 (28.6%) smoked cigarettes, and 222 (57.8%) chewed khat. Additionally, 280 (72.9%) consumed diets rich in animal fat, while 284 (74%) consumed fruits and vegetables regularly. Herbal medicine use was reported by 216 (56.2%), and 226 (58.9%) engaged in regular physical activity. Family support during treatment was noted by 274 (71.4%). Table 2 shows the full distribution. Factors associated with Chronic Liver Disease Preliminary associations (Bivariate Analysis) Several factors showed initial associations with medically confirmed CLD ( p &lt; 0.25) in bivariate logistic regression: Demographic factors: Male sex, advanced age, urban residence. Behavioral factors: Alcohol consumption, tobacco smoking, khat chewing, physical inactivity, high animal fat diet. Clinical factors: Family history of CLD, viral hepatitis (B/C) infection, comorbid conditions, herbal medicine use, overweight status. Independent predictors (Multivariable analysis) Nine factors emerged as statistically significant ( p &lt; 0.05) independent predictors in the final multivariable logistic regression model (Table 3 ). We present adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals and clinical interpretations: Viral hepatitis (B/C) infection showed dramatically increased odds of developing chronic liver disease (AOR = 18.15, 95% CI: 7.47–44.09), representing a 1,715% higher risk compared to uninfected individuals. This means hepatitis-positive individuals have 18 times greater odds of developing CLD. To illustrate this clinically, while only 3% of hepatitis-negative patients might develop CLD, the probability jumps to 37% among infected patients - a 12-fold increase in absolute risk that translates to a 34% point higher likelihood of disease. Alcohol intake (&gt; 20 ml/day) was strongly associated with chronic liver disease (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 13.53, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 5.50-33.29), indicating that heavy drinkers had 13.5 times greater odds of developing CLD compared to abstainers. This represents a 1,253% increase in odds. In clinical terms, while approximately 5% of non-drinkers developed CLD, the probability rose to 43% among heavy drinkers, reflecting a nearly ninefold increase in absolute risk. Herbal medicine use was significantly associated with chronic liver disease (AOR = 9.84, 95% CI: 4.58–21.13), indicating that regular users had 9.84 times higher odds of developing CLD compared to non-users. This represents an 884% increase in disease odds. In clinical terms, while the baseline probability of CLD was 6% among non-users, herbal medicine users showed a substantially elevated probability of approximately 40% - reflecting a sixfold increase in absolute risk. Family history of CLD was significantly associated with disease risk (AOR = 5.23, 95% CI: 2.25–12.13; p &lt; 0.001). Participants with affected first-degree relatives demonstrated 5.23-fold greater odds of developing CLD compared to those without a family history, representing a 423% increase in relative risk. In absolute terms, while the baseline population risk for CLD was approximately 10%, this probability rose to 39% among individuals with a positive family history - corresponding to a nearly fourfold increase in absolute risk and a 29%-point higher disease probability. Smoking cigarettes was significantly associated with increased CLD risk (AOR = 4.15, 95% CI: 1.70-10.14; p = 0.002). Current smokers demonstrated 4.15 times higher odds of developing CLD compared to non-smokers, representing a 315% increase in relative risk. In absolute terms, while the baseline CLD probability among non-smokers was 8%, this rose to 28% among smokers - corresponding to a 3.5-fold increase in absolute risk and a 20%-point higher disease probability. High animal fat diet consumption was significantly associated with CLD development (AOR = 3.97, 95% CI: 1.67–9.42; p = 0.002). Participants with high animal fat consumption demonstrated 3.97 times greater odds of CLD compared to those with lower intake, representing a 297% increased risk. The absolute risk increased from a baseline of 7% to approximately 23% among high consumers - a more than threefold elevation in disease probability (16%-point increase). Comorbid disease showed significant association with CLD (AOR = 3.58, 95% CI: 1.65–7.77; p = 0.001). Patients with metabolic comorbidities had 3.58 times higher CLD odds than those without, corresponding to a 258% relative risk increase. The absolute risk nearly tripled from 10% in unaffected individuals to 29% in those with comorbidities (19%-point difference). Khat chewing was associated with increased CLD risk (AOR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.13–4.77; p = 0.022). Daily chewers exhibited 2.33 times greater odds of developing CLD compared to non-users, representing a 133% higher relative risk. The absolute probability increased from 12% in non-users to 23% among chewers - nearly doubling the risk (11%-point increase). Overweight status (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m²) was significantly associated with CLD (AOR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.05–6.82; p = 0.038). Overweight participants had 2.68 times higher CLD odds than normal-weight individuals, indicating a 168% increased relative risk. The absolute risk more than doubled from a baseline of 9% to approximately 21% among overweight individuals (12%-point increase). Discussion This study found that the magnitude of medically confirmed chronic liver disease (CLD) among adults with suspected or probable CLD in selected hospitals of West Arsi Zone was 60.2% (231/384). This finding aligns with previous research in sub-Saharan Africa, where a high burden of cirrhosis and advanced CLD has been reported, with more than 50% of patients admitted at late stages [ 9 ]. Of the 231 confirmed CLD cases, 62% were male and 38% were female, indicating a higher prevalence in males. This sex difference is consistent with existing literature, which suggests that estrogen may have hepatoprotective effects, potentially reducing liver fibrosis and slowing disease progression in women [ 10 , 11 ]. However, further research is required to explore hormonal influences on CLD in this population. Having a family history of CLD significantly increased the odds of confirmed disease, which is consistent with studies indicating that certain hereditary liver conditions (e.g., hemochromatosis, Wilson’s disease) can predispose individuals to CLD [ 12 ]. While CLD itself is not generally classified as a hereditary disorder, familial patterns may reflect shared environmental or behavioral risk factors. Alcohol consumption above 20 ml/day was strongly associated with confirmed CLD, consistent with findings from other regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and central India. Chronic alcohol intake leads to hepatic inflammation, steatosis, fibrosis, and in advanced cases, cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The classification of alcohol exposure varies across studies, with some using quantity and duration, while others use screening tools such as the CAGE questionnaire. This variability may account for differences in reported associations [ 6 , 13 , 14 ]. Participants with a history of smoking were also at significantly higher risk of CLD. Smoking-related hepatotoxicity is attributed to the metabolism of nicotine and other toxins in the liver, leading to oxidative stress and inflammation. As with alcohol, differences in classification criteria (e.g., frequency vs. quantity) may contribute to inconsistencies across studies [ 6 , 15 , 16 ]. This study identified a significant association between khat chewing and CLD, in line with findings from Ethiopia [ 5 ]. Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, khat’s hepatotoxic alkaloids may contribute to liver fibrosis and chronic injury. Dietary habits also played a role; participants with a high intake of animal fats had increased odds of CLD. Similar findings have been reported in the United States and elsewhere [ 4 , 6 , 20 ]. Excessive dietary cholesterol may promote hepatic lipid accumulation and contribute to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). As expected, viral hepatitis B and C were among the strongest predictors of CLD, consistent with global evidence [ 8 , 17 , 19 ]. Chronic viral hepatitis causes hepatocellular necrosis, persistent inflammation, and fibrosis, which can progress to cirrhosis and HCC. However, the study faced limitations in documenting the full history of viral hepatitis due to limited access to patient records, which may have affected the precision of this association. Participants with comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, HIV/AIDS, heart disease, hypertension, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) were significantly more likely to develop CLD. These conditions are known contributors to hepatic dysfunction and are consistent with prior studies from Ethiopia and India [ 1 , 2 , 18 , 20 ]. Both disease-related metabolic effects and drug-induced hepatotoxicity may underlie this relationship. Use of herbal medicine was another significant factor. Herbal hepatotoxicity has been reported in various regions, including China, where certain traditional remedies have been linked to liver injury [ 16 ]. The liver’s role in detoxification makes it especially vulnerable to unregulated herbal compounds that may generate reactive metabolites. Finally, being overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m²) was associated with an increased risk of CLD, as compared to individuals with normal BMI. This aligns with research from Ethiopia and India [ 20 – 22 ], and supports evidence that increased adiposity contributes to hepatic steatosis and chronic liver inflammation. Limitations This study is not without limitations. The reliance on self-reported data for behavioral factors (e.g., alcohol intake, smoking) may introduce recall or social desirability bias. In addition, limited access to comprehensive medical records constrained the evaluation of prior viral hepatitis diagnoses. The cross-sectional design also restricts causal inferences. Conclusion The magnitude of medically confirmed chronic liver disease (CLD) was alarmingly high in the study area, indicating a significant public health concern in the West Arsi Zone. This underscores the urgent need for comprehensive, multi-level interventions to curb the burden of CLD. Key strategies should include: Targeted behavioral change programs focusing on reducing alcohol and khat consumption. Community education campaigns promoting awareness of the risks associated with unregulated herbal medicine use. Expanded screening initiatives for hepatitis B and C, especially among high-risk populations. Strengthening hepatitis B vaccination coverage, including hospital-based and outreach efforts. Promotion of healthy dietary practices and physical activity to combat overweight and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). From a policy perspective, stakeholders should prioritize the integration of CLD prevention into existing non-communicable disease (NCD) frameworks and health education curricula. Implications for future research Further studies are warranted to: Assess the toxicity thresholds of commonly used herbal medicines. Explore the longitudinal impact of behavioral and metabolic risk factors on CLD progression. Evaluate the effectiveness of community-level interventions in reducing CLD incidence.
