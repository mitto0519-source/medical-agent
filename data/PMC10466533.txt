A BSTRACT Introduction: Due to shame over discussing menstruation and fear of illness, many adolescent girls with monthly problems never visit their family doctor or gynecologist. The presentation can be delayed as a result. The current study’s goal was to assess the sociodemographic characteristics of adolescent females experiencing menstruation issues and the nature of those problems, and how they were handled. Materials and Methods: The problems faced by the adolescent girls attending the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology were analyzed retrospectively, and all their study characteristics and conditions were evaluated. The descriptive demographics are only represented in the current study. Results: Two hundred-two teenage girls with menstruation issues visited our hospital overall. 64% of them were late adolescents, 96% lived in cities, 89 were unmarried, and 50% belonged to the middle class socioeconomically. Amenorrhea, dysmenorrhea, and irregular menstrual periods affected 86 (61%) people, 38 (27%) people, and 17 (12%) people, respectively. Patients were treated with appropriate counseling and medicinal and/or surgical care. Conclusion: Most of the teenage girls in our study had anemia. Therefore, it is essential to educate people on the importance of nutrition, different menstrual disorders, normal physiology, and the prevention and management of anemia. Adolescent-friendly health care has been attempted and partially developed in India’s governmental and private systems. As a result, counseling and management of adolescent menstrual difficulties must be offered in the current health and medical care systems. I NTRODUCTION Between infancy and adulthood, the physical and mental growth stage known as adolescence is marked by significant hormonal changes. According to the WHO, an adolescent is someone who is between the ages of 10 and 19 and is in their second decade of life.[ 1 , 2 ] Teenage is a significant difficulty for parents, children, and those who are involved with the parenting of the adolescent at this crucial time of identity building and transition from childhood to womanhood. It is defined as vital to welfare physical, psychological, mental, and social changes.[ 3 ] The beginning of menstruation in teenage girls is the most notable shift. The hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis , which regulates cyclical hormonal changes in females, is the cause of menstruation.[ 4 ] Menstruation begins in adolescents, a significant developmental step and a sign of a fully established reproductive system. Menstruation often begins between the ages of 11 and 15. Emotional disturbances, extended menstrual bleeding, dysmenorrhea, and premenstrual syndrome, are common menstrual abnormalities that female adolescents may have after menarche; 75% of girls have some menstrual-related issues.[ 5 ] Numerous studies from around the world, including India, reveal that many adolescent females deal with issues linked to menstruation. It causes a rise in the frequency of absenteeism from work and school. This supports the idea that menstruation issues should be prioritized as a community health problem.[ 6 ] According to a district-level domestic study, one-fifth of women in India experienced issues linked to their menstrual cycles. Women in India who reported menstruation issues reported various issues. Of the 20% of these women who have described menstrual issues, one-fifth were adolescents. Adolescent girls are more likely to experience menstruation-related issues.[ 7 ] Numerous research on menstruation issues has shown that India is experiencing an increase in the prevalence of menstrual issues and the associated school absences. Adolescents frequently experience menstrual abnormalities, which can have a serious impact on their ability to conceive in the future. Premenstrual syndrome, menstrual hygiene, and dysmenorrhea are the main causes of disease burden in teenage girls.[ 8 - 10 ] The purpose of this study was to identify menstruation-related issues that affect teenage schoolgirls attending the tertiary care center. M ATERIAL AND M ETHODS The current study was piloted retrospectively from the department records collected at the tertiary center between April 2020 and April 2022 with the proper approval from our institute’s institutional review board. By using the proper statistical methods, data were evaluated. Both registered participants and those in emergencies made up the study population. Age, education, place of residence, socioeconomic status, type of complaints made, menstruation problem type, and management information were obtained from patients’ socio-demographic data. All teenage girls between the ages of 10 and 19 who visited were admitted to the department for menstrual issues. Teenagers with other gynecological issues were disqualified from the current study. The data collected were presented as descriptive items. R ESULTS Data on 212 adolescent girls who visited during the research period with menstruation issues were gathered. According to Table 1 , the age group of late teenage females between the ages of 17 and 19 had the highest percentage of adolescent girls with menstrual issues, at 64%. 96% of the teenage girls with menstrual issues were from urban backgrounds. The proportion of adolescents experiencing menstruation issues ranged from 6%, in the upper socioeconomic groups, to 50%, in the medium socioeconomic classes. One hundred twenty-six of them, or 89.3%, were single. As seen in Table 2 , dysmenorrhea 60% and amenorrhea 11% were the three most common types of menstrual irregularities (others, 29%). All menstrual issues were more common, but dysmenorrhea was the most common. Of the teenage girls who had dysmenorrhea, 48% and 53% had primary and secondary dysmenorrhea. Secondary dysmenorrhea was caused by ovarian cysts in 28% of the cases, PCOS in 15% of the cases, PID in 8% of the cases, and congenital defects of the reproductive system in 2% of adolescent females. Of the teenage girls with period irregularities, 5% had frequent bleeding, 16% had light bleeding, 21% had infrequent bleeding, and 58% had heavy menstrual bleeding. Among teenage girls who had amenorrhea, 42% also had primary amenorrhea, 14% had Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser syndrome (MRKH) , 14% had cervical agenesis, 15% had a high vaginal septum, and 58% had an imperforate hymen. 59% of adolescent girls had secondary amenorrhea. 61% had polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), and 29% had psychological stress and hypothyroidism. It was also seen that 65% were anemic. There were 59%, 34%, and 7% individuals with mild, moderate, and severe anemia, respectively. Of these, 91% belonged to the middle and lowered social classes. Sixteen of the 20 teenage girls in the high socioeconomic level were anemic. D ISCUSSION Dysmenorrhea, irregular periods, and amenorrhea are the most prevalent menstrual issues in adolescent girls. Menstrual issues should be managed along with appropriate psychotherapy and education.[ 11 - 15 ] The preponderance of the adolescents in the current study was that 64% were in their late teenage years, and most of the girls lived in an urban region. 50% and 96% belonged to the middle socioeconomic class. According to Varghese L et al. ,[ 3 ] the majority of adolescent females with menstrual issues, 51%, were in the mid-adolescent group, 76% lived in an urban region, and 79% belonged to a middle class. In the current research, 65% of the 212 females were anemic. The percentages of people with severe, moderate, and mild anemia were 7, 30, and 59, respectively. According to Thaker RV et al. ,[ 11 ] anemia affected 63% of teenage girls, with severe, moderate, and mild anemia affecting 16%, 33%, and 52% of them, respectively. The incidence of anemia in teenage girls aged 15–9 is reported by NFHS- 5 in 63% and 72.3% of urban and rural areas, respectively. Five out of 91 females who were anemic in the current study, 52% belonged to a lower socioeconomic class and 9% to a higher socioeconomic class, meaning that eight of the nine adolescent girls in the high socioeconomic class were anemic. High socioeconomic classes have plenty of food available, but there is a relative absence of knowledge about nutritional dietary habits. Blood transfusions were provided to seven adolescent girls with severe anemia brought on by AUB. Iron supplementation, dietary changes, and counseling were used to treat adolescent girls with mild to moderate anemia. Menstrual irregularities (38%) and dysmenorrhea (86%), as well as amenorrhea (12%), were all present in the current investigation. 60% of women, according to Goswami P et al .[ 13 ] had menstruation issues. Nearly 6% of females had menstrual issues, according to Thaker RV et al .[ 11 ] research. Dysmenorrhea was reported by Archana R et al .[ 14 ] in 33% of cases. Primary dysmenorrhea and secondary dysmenorrhea were present in 47% and 52% of the 86 teenage girls in the current study who had dysmenorrhea, respectively. Secondary dysmenorrhea was caused by ovarian cysts in 28% of the cases, PCOS in 14% of the cases, PID in 8% of the cases, and congenital defects of the reproductive system in 2% of adolescent females. Teenage girls with PCOS must be treated using a multidisciplinary strategy that includes weight loss, exercise, lifestyle modifications, and medication. Adolescent girls treated with antibiotics, analgesics, and advice to maintain personal cleanliness all had PID in 8% of them. In the current study, 27% of adolescent girls had irregular periods. Of these, 58% experienced severe menstrual flow, compared to 21% who had irregular periods, 16% who had light periods, and 5% who had frequent periods. Stang J et al .[ 6 ] observed that 2.2% of girls had light menstrual bleeding, and 56% of them had HMB. Thirty (79%) patients received hormonal therapy in addition to tranexamic acid/NSAID and anemia treatment. According to Bhalerao-Gandhi A et al .[ 16 ] findings, 66% of females needed hormonal treatment. Seven (41.2%) of the 17 teenagers in the current study who had amenorrhea had primary amenorrhea. Four (57.1%) girls had imperforate hymens, one (14.3%) had cervical stenosis, one had MRKH syndrome, and one had a high vaginal septum. Primary amenorrhea has been documented by Goswami P et al .[ 13 ] in three (27.3%) adolescent girls, with vaginal agenesis in two (66.6%) and an imperforate hymen in one (33.3%). 5% of adolescent girls required surgical intervention. The hematocolpos of four girls with imperforate hymen was drained. Surgery was used to treat one teenager with a high vaginal septum, and the septum was removed. Two-stage surgery was used to treat a girl with cervical agenesis; the first stage involved an examination under anesthesia and diagnostic laparoscopy. A silicone cannula was placed into the lower uterine cavity during the second step of laparotomy to preserve the passage. Both patients now menstruate naturally as a result of their successful treatments. In one adolescent girl with MRKH syndrome, vaginoplasty was suggested. Bhalerao-Gandhi A et al .[ 16 ] described that 4% of females underwent surgical intervention. In the current study, six (60%) of the 10 adolescent girls with secondary amenorrhea also had PCOS, three (30%) experienced test stress, and one had hypothyroidism. Goswami P et al .[ 13 ] (2015) observed secondary amenorrhea in eight teenagers in six (75%), PCOS, one (12.5%), and TB abdomen in one (12.5%) of these girls. Teenage ladies with PCOS received advice on changing their lifestyles and losing weight. Three girls who experienced exam stress were treated through counseling, while a fourth girl with hypothyroidism was referred to a doctor and began receiving therapy.[ 14 - 16 ] C ONCLUSION Several teenagers are still timid and hesitant to visit the adult outpatient clinic. Adolescent counseling and health education clinics are specialized facilities that can help meet their requirements. Adolescent-friendly health care has been attempted and partially developed in India’s governmental and private systems. Therefore, existing health and medical care providers must offer to counsel and manage adolescent menstrual difficulties. As per the current research, the majority of the adolescents were anemic. Therefore, it’s essential to educate people about the value of diet and prevent and treat anemia. Financial support and sponsorship Nil. Conflicts of interest There are no conflicts of interest.
