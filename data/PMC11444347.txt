Background: Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination has been offered in over a hundred countries worldwide (including the United Kingdom, since September 2008). Controversy around adverse effects persists, with inconsistent evidence from follow-up of randomized controlled trials and confounding by indication limiting the conclusions drawn from larger-scale observational studies. This study aims to estimate the association between receiving a quadrivalent HPV vaccine and the reporting of short-term adverse effects and to demonstrate the utility of regression discontinuity design for examining side effects in routine data. Methods: We applied a novel regression discontinuity approach to a retrospective population-based cohort using primary care data from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink linked to hospital and social deprivation data. We examined the new onset of gastrointestinal, neuromuscular, pain, and headache/migraine symptoms using READ and International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision diagnostic codes. For each year between 2012 and 2017, we compared girls in school year 8 (born July/August) who were eligible to receive the vaccine with girls in year 7 (born September/October) who were not eligible. Results: Of the 21,853 adolescent girls in the cohort, 10,881 (50%) were eligible for HPV vaccination. There was no evidence of increased new gastrointestinal symptoms (adjusted odds ratio [OR]: 0.99; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.85, 1.15), headache/migraine symptoms (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.70, 1.01), or pain symptoms (OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 0.95, 1.16) when comparing those eligible and ineligible for HPV vaccination. Conclusion: This study found no evidence that HPV vaccination eligibility is associated with reporting short-term adverse effects among adolescent girls. GlaxoSmithKline, London, UK) was offered to girls aged 12–13 years as a three-dose schedule (0, 1, and 4 months) with the first dose received in school year 8. 3 It was replaced by the quadrivalent HPV vaccine (qHPV) protecting against HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18 (Gardasil; Merck &amp; Co, Rahway, NJ) in September 2012 and the three-dose schedule was reduced to two (0 and 6–12 months) in September 2014. 7 , 8 Suspected adverse effects following vaccination of either bHPV or qHPV vaccine have been reported; these include persistent headache, fatigue, pain, orthostatic intolerance (OI), neuromuscular and gastrointestinal symptoms, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), and chronic/postviral fatigue syndrome (CFS/PVFS). 9 – 17 However, the international HPV vaccine safety review found no consistent evidence of increased risk of adverse effects and an acceptable safety profile and highly favorable risk–benefit ratio of HPV vaccines. 18 Postlicensing surveillance by regulatory health agencies continuously endorses HPV vaccine safety. 19 – 21 A recent review comparing bHPV and qHPV showed that, while bHPV was associated with more systemic adverse events than qHPV, no evidence of a difference in serious adverse events was found. 22 Randomized controlled trials, while considered the gold standard in providing evidence of vaccine efficacy, are often underpowered to examine rare adverse health events. 23 Quasi-experimental designs, such as the regression discontinuity design, have been shown to replicate the results of randomized controlled trials 24 – 27 and have been used to study the effect of HPV vaccination in Canada. 28 , 29 Though underused in epidemiology, 30 regression discontinuity is appropriate where eligibility for treatment or intervention changes sharply at a predefined threshold of continuously measured assignment variables, such as age and school year. 23 Several UK studies have examined the adverse effects of HPV vaccine using observational data and found no evidence of an increased risk of CFS, autoimmune diseases, Guillain–Barré syndrome, or spontaneous abortion after bHPV vaccination, but none have examined the population-level impact of the qHPV vaccination program. 31 – 34 This study aims to use regression discontinuity design to estimate the association of eligibility for the qHPV vaccination program on the incidence of adverse health events among adolescent girls. Any differences in adverse events between those eligible and ineligible to receive the HPV vaccine will be attributable to the vaccination program if all regression discontinuity assumptions are satisfied. This study also aims to demonstrate the utility of quasi-experimental designs applied to routine data for assessing vaccine-related adverse events. METHODS Data Sources Primary care data were obtained from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) GOLD containing pseudonymized electronic patient records from over 700 GPs. The CPRD was linked at the individual level to the Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) containing information on all hospital admissions and patient/practice level Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) scores at the Lower Layer Super Output Area. Study Population To assess the effect of the qHPV vaccine offered from September 2012, we included in the study girls born between 2000 and 2005 (and attending school years 7 or 8 from 2012 to 2017). They were registered at English GPs with at least 12 months of up-to-standard data and with no record of OI/POTS, CRPS, CFS/PVFS, gastrointestinal, neuromuscular, pain, and headache/migraine symptoms in the 12 months before cohort entry. We created six cohorts based on the year of birth (2000–2005), each including girls born in July, August, September, and October. 35 Study Design and Exposure Groups With vaccine eligibility tied to school year 8 and school entry being based on birth date (1 September threshold), this provides an opportunity to apply regression discontinuity design. The month of birth was used to assign exposure to the HPV vaccination program. Girls turning 12 in July or August of a given calendar year will have been admitted to school year 8 beginning in September of that year and were eligible to receive the vaccine. Girls turning 12 in September or October of the same year will have been admitted to school year 7 from September of that year and thus were ineligible to receive the vaccine. The girls were followed up over a 12-month period starting from 1 September (i.e., one full school year). We defined exposure to the qHPV vaccine program based on eligibility to receive this vaccine (intention to treat). The structure of the six cohorts is shown in Table 1 . In a regression discontinuity design, the two groups are likely to be comparable in measured and unmeasured confounders just above and just below the cutoff on the running variable. In order to maximize comparability, we included in our sample girls born on 31 August versus those born on 1 September. We used a window of 2 birth months on either side of the threshold so that any change in their outcomes during the follow-up period can be attributed to the change in HPV vaccine program exposure. Outcomes We compared the incidence of outcomes between vaccine-eligible and ineligible groups during the follow-up period. The adverse outcomes examined were the development of new onset of OI/POTS, CRPS, CFS/PVFS, gastrointestinal, neuromuscular, pain, and headache/migraine symptoms potentially associated with autonomic nervous system disorders identified using READ and ICD-10 diagnostic codes selected in consultation with clinical experts (eAppendix 1; http://links.lww.com/EDE/C174 ). For falsification testing, we have included a negative control (bone fractures) that could not plausibly have been affected by the eligibility for HPV vaccination program but would be subject to similar confounding (e.g., socioeconomic status, region of residence, health-seeking behaviors, and parental education) as the other outcomes. Since algorithms for the identification of outcomes have not been validated, we created narrow and broad definitions to explore the impact of coding specificity and to account for potential misclassification. Baseline Characteristics Baseline characteristics were compared to assess the similarity of the eligibility groups using Pearson’s chi-squared tests, t -tests, or Mann–Whitney tests as appropriate for each baseline variable. Sociodemographic factors included year and month of birth, geographical region, IMD quintile, and broad categories of ethnicity. We quantified health system use by the number of general practitioner (GP) visits before the 1 September threshold within the up-to-standard practice registration period and by the number of hospital admissions. Physical measurements comprised weight, height, body mass index (BMI), heart rate, and systolic/diastolic blood pressure in the 2 years before the threshold. We also compared the presence of any potential contraindications for receiving the vaccine (including bleeding disorders, HIV, and medications affecting the immune system) between eligibility groups. Statistical Analysis As per sharp regression discontinuity design (where there is a fixed unambiguous cutoff defining exposed and unexposed groups), we assumed that all girls born in July and August were eligible to receive HPV and all girls born in September and October were ineligible. It is possible for children from a given cohort to start school at a later time, although this occurs in less than 1% of school children. 36 Vaccine uptake among year 8 girls was high between 2012 (87%) and 2017 (91%) 5 ; hence, misclassification of eligibility for the program is expected to be low. This assumption about nonmanipulation of eligibility to the vaccination program allowed estimation of the effect of the HPV vaccination program on the reported adverse effects using observational data. Actual HPV vaccine impact (per protocol analysis) was not estimable due to the low recording of HPV vaccination in the CPRD (eAppendix 2; http://links.lww.com/EDE/C174 ). We used logistic regression to model the odds of adverse events for eligible versus ineligible groups, generating crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and risk differences (RDs) for each birth-year cohort from 2000 to 2005. The adjusted estimates were adjusted for counts of GP visits and hospital admissions since these are potentially associated with the recording of outcomes in CPRD. We viewed these variables as potentially explaining some of the variability in reporting of adverse outcomes, not as confounders, and were added to improve the power of the study. However, we recognize that these variables could be affected by treatment and are measured posteligibility and thus could potentially be mediators, hence their inclusion as a secondary analysis. We combined the results for each cohort in random effects meta-analysis models using the DerSimonian and Laird method with the estimate of heterogeneity taken from the inverse-variance fixed-effect model assuming year-specific true effect. 37 , 38 The weight for each cohort was calculated based on the number of included participants and the number of events. Heterogeneity between cohorts was explored using the I 2 test ( I 2 &lt; 40% indicating no important heterogeneity between cohorts 39 ). As an alternative overall assessment, crude and adjusted ORs were also obtained for the combined cohort with estimates adjusted for birth year in addition to the frequency of healthcare use. We performed sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the results. We tested the impact of HES linkage by either excluding girls with no linkage or excluding outcomes from HES. Potential outcome misclassification was examined using narrower outcome definitions. We examined the impact of the HPV vaccine dose change by only including girls born from 2000 to 2001 and exposed to a three-dose schedule. The impact of a shorter 6-month follow-up period was examined; a 12-month follow-up is the maximum time available in primary analyses as girls initially ineligible to receive the HPV vaccine become eligible after this period. We performed additional sensitivity analyses on two different populations using the year of birth to assign changes in HPV vaccine program. 1 To estimate the association of the bHPV vaccine introduced in 2008 on the outcomes, we compared girls born in any month in 1996 (exposed to bHPV vaccine program with three-dose schedule) to girls born in 1994 (unexposed to HPV vaccine program). We followed up with girls from these two cohorts for 12 months starting from 1 January in 2009 and 2007, respectively. 2 To estimate the association of the change from bHPV to qHPV vaccine in 2012 on the outcomes, girls born in 2000 (exposed to qHPV vaccine program) were compared to girls born in 1998 (exposed to bHPV vaccine program). We followed up girls from these two cohorts for 12 months starting from 1 January in the years 2013 and 2011, respectively. We assessed the regression discontinuity design assumptions prior to analysis and were found to be plausible (eAppendix 2; http://links.lww.com/EDE/C174 ). 40 Ethical Approval Ethical approval for this study protocol was granted by the Independent Scientific Advisory Committee (ISAC) for Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency database research (protocol number 16_294). This study is based on data from the CPRD obtained under license from the UK Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency. CPRD has been granted multiple research ethics committee approval (05/MRE04/87) to undertake purely observational studies with external data linkages, including hospital admission data. We conducted analyses according to a prespecified protocol (ISAC 16_294). Stata v15 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) was used for data management and analyses. All analysis codes are available in GitHub: https://github.com/UoB-CPRD-Users-Group/analysis-code/tree/main/HPV%20RDA . RESULTS After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 1 ), the study cohort comprised 21,853 female patients from 417 GPs in England; 10,881 (50%) of the patients were born in July and August and were eligible to receive the HPV vaccine within the 12-month follow-up period. There was no evidence of differences in baseline characteristics between eligibility groups in the base study cohort, except for height (0.0078 m; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.0152, 0.0005) and weight (1.0197 kg; 95% CI: 1.9622, 0.0772) that were slightly higher for girls born in July or August (older cohort) (Table 2 ). The small differences in height and weight are likely due to age (not to eligibility for the HPV vaccine) and are unlikely to influence the study outcomes. The number of cases with new onset diagnoses of CRPS, OI/POTS, CFS/PVFS, or new neuromuscular symptoms was insufficient (≤7 events per predictor variable) to detect meaningful differences between eligible and ineligible girls and we did not include them in the analysis (Table 3 ). The adjusted results from meta-analysis model revealed no strong evidence of differences in odds of developing new gastrointestinal symptoms (OR: 0.99; 95% CI: 0.85, 1.15), headache/migraine (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.70, 1.01), pain (OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 0.95, 1.16), or fractures (negative control) (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.74, 1.23) between girls eligible to receive qHPV vaccine and ineligible girls. Adjusted RDs were −0.5 cases per 1000 girls (95% CI: −6.7, 5.7) for gastrointestinal symptoms, −4.1 cases per 1000 girls for headache/migraine (95% CI: −8.1, 0.0), 3.4 cases per 1000 girls (95% CI: −4.3, 11.0) for pain, and −0.6 cases per 1000 girls (95% CI: −3.4, 2.2) for fractures (negative control) and indicate no evidence of reduction or increase in these symptoms within the 12-month follow-up period attributable to HPV vaccination program exposure (Table 4 ). The logistic regression results for the combined cohort encompassing all birth years from 2000 to 2005 revealed similar results (eAppendix 4; http://links.lww.com/EDE/C174 ). Estimates from multiple sensitivity analyses were consistent with the main results. The adjusted ORs with 95% CI from primary and sensitivity analyses are displayed in Figure 2 . When comparing those eligible to receive the bHPV vaccine (born 1996) and ineligible girls (born 1994), adjusted results showed no strong evidence of a difference in odds of developing new gastrointestinal symptoms (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.89, 1.02), headache/migraine (OR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.88, 1.05), neuromuscular symptoms (OR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.59, 1.27), or pain (OR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.90, 1.01). Comparing those eligible to receive qHPV vaccine program (born 2000) to those eligible for bHPV vaccine (born 1998), there was no strong evidence of a difference in odds of developing new headache/migraine (OR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.84, 1.00) or neuromuscular symptoms (OR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.54, 1.20) and a slight decrease in odds of developing new gastrointestinal symptoms (OR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.81, 0.93) or pain (OR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.85, 0.95). Results from all sensitivity analyses, including characteristics for two populations (born 1994 vs. 1996; born 1998 vs. 2000), can be found in eAppendix 3; http://links.lww.com/EDE/C174 . DISCUSSION This population-based observational study measured the impact of the HPV vaccination program (intention-to-treat effects of qHPV vaccine) on the incidence of adverse health events. It showed no evidence in support of the hypothesis that eligibility to receive HPV vaccine has a negative impact on short-term health outcomes for adolescent girls. RDs and their lower and upper limits were small indicating no substantial increase in risk of reporting new symptoms between the comparison groups. We also demonstrated the value of using a regression discontinuity design applied to routine data for assessing age-specific population-level public health programs. HPV immunization coverage for the priming dose in school year 8 was relatively high (average 87%; range from 68% to 92% between local authorities) in England in 2017/2018 and remained stable and consistent from 2012 to 2017. 41 Despite high vaccine coverage, there was no evidence of an increase in adverse effects at the population level. Comparison With Other Studies To our knowledge, this is the first study using regression discontinuity design to investigate a population-based HPV vaccination program’s impact on adverse effects in the United Kingdom and worldwide. The most common systemic adverse reactions to qHPV vaccine in girls reported in prelicensing trials include headache, nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, and arthralgia 42 ; however, these symptoms have only been observed within 15 days postvaccination. While these symptoms have also been reported after vaccine licensing, their incidence and causal link with HPV vaccination remains unclear. To date, none of the population-based studies looked at the association between the HPV vaccine and gastrointestinal, neuromuscular, pain, and headache symptoms within short to medium-term follow-up. Strengths and Weaknesses of the Study The main strength of our study is the use of a population-based database of primary and secondary care data that enabled a large cohort study with a low risk of selection bias. 43 An additional strength is the use of regression discontinuity design that minimizes the potential for confounding bias. We anticipated between-year heterogeneity in true effects due to changes in the number of qHPV vaccine doses, timing of the first dose, timing between doses, and vaccine coverage, therefore we analyzed each birth year separately and combined the results in a random effects meta-analysis model; we adjusted standard errors of year-specific estimates to incorporate a measure of the extent of this variation. However, the analysis using a combined cohort adjusted for cohort effects has yielded almost identical results indicating low variability between birth years. Our research was limited to examining population-level effects of HPV vaccination program, not the effects of the HPV vaccine, due to low recording of vaccination in CPRD (40% compared to &gt;80% actual coverage). Furthermore, data on timing and spacing of administered doses are not consistently recorded and are not available in any central repository or database, including in CPRD. However, under the regression discontinuity design assumptions, we would have seen evidence of differences between those eligible and ineligible to receive HPV vaccine had the HPV vaccine itself affected the outcomes. No evidence of differences in outcome recording between eligibility groups is indirect evidence of no effect of HPV vaccine. Another limitation is the relatively short follow-up time; it was limited to 12 months as the ineligible girls become eligible to receive HPV vaccine once they reach school year 8. The time during the school year that each dose was administered is not known and likely varies between schools and local authorities. Hence, the time between receiving a vaccination dose and the end of follow-up would be variable. Relatively short follow-up time means that the vaccine course might not be complete over the 12-month follow-up period and some adverse effects were reported only after the complete course. Some health effects take time to confirm or have long latency, and not all symptoms may have been reported or recorded. This has the potential to cause bias toward the null; however, in sensitivity analyses for girls born between 2000 and 2001 and eligible to receive three doses within 12 months, estimates were consistent with the main results meaning a tighter dosing schedule was not associated with the change in risk of reporting the outcomes. We chose to investigate symptom groups associated with autonomic nervous system disorders in addition to confirmed diagnoses to increase the chance of detecting potential adverse effects within the follow-up time. Another limitation is potential outcome misclassification. Sensitivity analyses using broader or narrower outcome definitions did not reveal any strong evidence of misclassification and any outcome underascertainment is likely nondifferential. Misclassification of eligibility for the vaccination program might have occurred since summer-born children can have delayed school admissions or can be promoted or held back 1 year from their normal grade; however, the number of such cases is expected to be very low (&lt;0.5% for delayed admissions; 0.1%–0.3% for promotion/retention). 44 , 45 The vaccine uptake rate of around 90% for at least one dose in England suggests that only a small proportion of girls eligible for the vaccine program did not receive any vaccine dose during the follow-up period. This would bias results only if those who had manipulated the school year were also more or less likely to report side effects. The 2-month periods for the exposed and unexposed cohort definitions make these two groups as close as possible in age and other (measured or unmeasured) confounding factors, as per the key idea of regression discontinuity. Using year 6 girls as an alternative unexposed group would mean we are no longer using a regression discontinuity design, which would undermine the potential to make causal inferences. For example, there may be unobserved factors relating to age that also affect the likelihood of adverse health outcomes. Birth date is measured monthly in CPRD. The lack of a more granular birth date affected assumption testing for outcome continuity. We only examined differences between the two groups (eligible vs. ineligible) and could not explore temporal changes and potential jumps in outcome recording/occurrence at the threshold. However, we have found no evidence that the negative control outcome of fractures is associated with eligibility to receive HPV vaccine, indicating that the assumption of no discontinuities in other outcomes may be plausible. Regression discontinuity design addresses confounding affecting individual patients, but it is vulnerable to confounding affecting eligible groups separately. For example, girls eligible to receive HPV vaccine will be the youngest within year 8 and ineligible ones will be the oldest within year 7, which may result in behavior differences. The transition to senior school at year 7 can have a negative impact on mental well-being, while the same factor may no longer be an issue by year 8. 46 Furthermore, individuals who are old for their school year (born in September–October) have different outcomes on a range of variables than those who are young for their school year (born in July–August). 47 Additionally, girls eligible to receive HPV vaccine might be more likely to report known vaccine-associated side effects upon receipt of the vaccine. However, we did not find differences in outcome recording between eligibility groups. To our knowledge, there are no other health interventions affecting eligibility groups separately. Unanswered Questions and Future Research Generalizability of the results to other populations is not known. From September 2019 in the United Kingdom, the qHPV vaccine has also been offered to boys in school year 8, 48 and future research could investigate the adverse effects in this setting with newly available data. CONCLUSIONS This study provides evidence of the impact of HPV vaccination program on several reported adverse effects among adolescent girls within a 12-month period. Our findings show no evidence that qHPV vaccination eligibility increases the risk of reported adverse effects. The study also demonstrates the regression discontinuity design as an important approach for evaluating the impact of age or geographically-based public health interventions, including vaccination programs.
