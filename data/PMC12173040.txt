Objective Previous studies have explored the link between sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption and depressive symptoms, but the mediating role of Mobile Phone Use Addiction (MPUA) remains unclear. This study aims to examine how MPUA mediates the relationship between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms among college students in Northeast China. Methods A prospective study was conducted with first-year university freshmen across three northeastern Chinese provinces. MPUA was assessed using the Adolescent Problematic Mobile Phone Use Self-Rating Questionnaire, and depressive symptoms were measured with the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Generalized linear regression models were used to analyze the relationships, and structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to test MPUA's mediating effect. Results MPUA and depressive symptoms were present in 27.3 % and 36.2 % of participants, respectively. After adjusting for confounders, significant associations were found between SSB consumption (e.g., fruit drinks, tea beverages) and depressive symptoms in both genders. SEM revealed that MPUA accounted for 47.0 % of the total indirect effect in the relationship between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms. Conclusions This study highlights the mediating role of MPUA in the SSB-depression relationship among college students in Northeast China. These findings suggest the need for interventions targeting both mobile phone addiction and dietary habits to improve mental health. Future research should explore the mechanisms and generalizability of these results. In recent years, mental health problems among adolescents have increased, with a notably higher prevalence among females than males ( Diener et al., 2021 ). The one-year prevalence of depression has reached approximately 4 %, and the cumulative prevalence by late adolescence is as high as 20 % ( Shorey et al., 2022 ). Depression during this critical developmental stage not only causes psychological distress but also increases the risk of severe consequences such as mental disorders and suicidal behaviors. University students are particularly vulnerable to depression due to academic pressure, lifestyle changes, financial stress, and career concerns ( Azmi et al., 2022 ). A meta-analysis in 2020 reported a dramatic increase in depression prevalence during the COVID-19 pandemic, with an estimated rate of 34 % (95 % CI: 28–41 %) in the general population ( Salari et al., 2020 ). In China, the overall prevalence of depression is estimated at 26.9 % ( Liu et al., 2022a ), with adolescents showing even higher rates ( Basile et al., 2020 ), highlighting the need for targeted mental health interventions. Among emerging risk factors, sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption has gained attention for its potential impact on mental health. Frequently consumed by adolescents, SSBs are linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases ( Singh et al., 2015 ), and increasingly, psychological distress ( Ugbabe, 2019 ). Although SSB intake has declined in some high-income countries ( Dawes et al., 2020 ; Della Corte et al., 2021 ), it continues to rise in many non-Western nations, including China. A 2014 national survey showed that over 50 % of Chinese adolescents consumed SSBs weekly, and nearly 10 % consumed more than 250 mL daily ( Gui et al., 2017 ). In 27 major cities, nearly half of adolescents reported regular SSB consumption ( Zhang et al., 2018 ). The Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2022) emphasized limiting added sugar intake to under 50 g per day (preferably &lt;25 g), but current estimates suggest average intakes exceed these recommendations, especially among youth ( Yang et al., 2021 ). Another relevant factor is mobile phone use addiction (MPUA), increasingly prevalent among adolescents due to widespread smartphone access. MPUA is associated with academic difficulties, interpersonal problems, and elevated stress ( Geng et al., 2021 ; Mokhtarinia et al., 2022 ). Adolescents are particularly vulnerable due to underdeveloped self-regulation and high exposure to digital media ( Li et al., 2020 ). This study is grounded in the Social Ecological Theory, which considers how individual behavior is shaped by multi-level influences, including personal traits, family environment, and broader societal factors ( Sheerin et al., 2023 ). At the individual level, adolescents' tendencies toward SSB consumption and compulsive smartphone use may stem from reward-seeking behaviors and poor mood regulation. At the interpersonal and community levels, family habits, peer influence, and access to SSBs and smartphones reinforce these behaviors. At the broader environmental level, intense academic pressure—commonly observed in East Asian educational systems—acts as a pervasive stressor that may promote maladaptive coping strategies, such as excessive sugar-sweetened beverage consumption or compulsive smartphone use, which are the focus of this research in relation to depressive symptoms. Despite growing interest in the relationship between SSBs, MPUA, and adolescent depression ( Liu et al., 2022b ; Xu et al., 2023a ; Xu et al., 2023 ), research specifically targeting Chinese adolescents remains limited. Moreover, Northeast China, with its unique geographical and sociocultural context, has been largely overlooked. The region faces harsh winters, reduced physical activity opportunities, and ongoing industrial restructuring. These conditions, combined with population outmigration and educational disparities, contribute to elevated stress and potentially greater mental health vulnerability among adolescents ( Lu and Pang, 2022 ; Zhuang et al., 2021 ). Traditional family values and close community ties may also shape lifestyle behaviors differently than in other regions of China. Therefore, this study aims to examine the associations between SSB consumption, MPUA, and depressive symptoms among college students in Northeast China. The findings are expected to provide valuable insights for regional mental health strategies and contribute to the broader literature on depression. 2 Methods 2.1 Participants The study was conducted between November 2022 and September 2024 across three northeastern provinces in China: Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from the following universities: Jinzhou Medical University and Shenyang Medical College (Liaoning), Yanbian University and Northeast Normal University (Jilin), and Harbin Medical University (Heilongjiang). All five institutions offer both junior college and undergraduate programs. In China, junior college programs typically last three years and award an associate degree, while undergraduate programs span four to five years and lead to a bachelor's degree. Undergraduate students generally meet higher admission requirements and may have advantages in employment and postgraduate opportunities. Eligible participants were those who consented voluntarily, completed the full questionnaire without logical errors, and did not report extreme outliers. Exclusion criteria included refusal to participate, missing data on key variables (e.g., SSB consumption, MPUA, depressive symptoms), logical inconsistencies, and implausible responses (e.g., age 60). Of the 4899 students who completed the questionnaire, 13 were excluded due to invalid responses, resulting in a final sample of 4886 (valid response rate: 99.73 %). Demographic characteristics: 30.5 % were male ( n = 1488), and 69.5 % were female ( n = 3398). Han Chinese made up 89.2 % ( n = 4358), and ethnic minorities 10.8 % ( n = 528). Rural residents accounted for 68.1 % ( n = 3326), and urban residents 31.9 % ( n = 1560). The flow of participant recruitment and inclusion is detailed in Fig. 1 . 2.2 Procedures All research staff received standardized training prior to data collection. A pilot study was conducted to refine the questionnaire based on feedback. The main survey was administered in classroom settings, with clear instructions and written informed consent obtained beforehand. Participation was voluntary, and anonymity was strictly maintained. Participants completed the paper questionnaire independently within 10–15 min under supervision, without discussion. Completed surveys were reviewed for completeness and logical consistency by trained personnel. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinzhou Medical University (Approval No.: JZMULL2022091). 2.3 Measures 2.3.1 Assessment of depressive symptoms Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Chinese version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated tool for screening depressive symptoms over the past two weeks, particularly in adolescents ( Jaalouk et al., 2019 ). The PHQ-9 includes nine items addressing various symptoms, such as “thoughts of self-harm or being better off dead,” scored on a four-point scale: “Not at all” (0), “Several days” (1), “More than half the days” (2), and “Almost every day” (3), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Severity was categorized as: 0–4 (Normal), 5–9 (Mild), 10–14 (Moderate), 15–19 (Moderately Severe), and 20–27 (Severe) ( Kroenke et al., 2001 ). The PHQ-9 showed acceptable reliability in this study (Cronbach's α = 0.671). 2.3.2 Assessment of SSB consumption Participants reported their SSB intake over the past seven days across six beverage types: carbonated drinks (e.g., Sprite™), fruit beverages (e.g., Huiyuan™), tea beverages, energy drinks (e.g., Red Bull™), milk beverages (e.g., Yakult™), and milk tea. Participants reported their consumption using the question: “How many bottles (approximately 500 mL each) of the following beverages have you consumed in the past seven days?” For each category, participants selected one of four responses: “None,” “&lt;1 bottle,” “1–2 bottles,” or “≥3 bottles” (500 mL per bottle). The measure showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.773). 2.3.3 Assessment of MPUA MPUA was measured using the Self-Rating Questionnaire for Adolescent Problematic Mobile Phone Use (SQAPMPU) ( Tao et al., 2017 ). This questionnaire comprises 13 items across three dimensions: withdrawal symptoms, craving, and physical and mental health status. The 13 items of the SQAPMPU are as follows: (1) I always feel that the time spent on my phone is not enough; (2) I feel frustrated, depressed, or irritable when I try to reduce or stop using my phone; (3) Excessive phone use has led to insufficient sleep; (4) I feel annoyed when I have to turn off my phone during classes, meals, or other activities; (5) Sometimes I prefer using my phone rather than dealing with more urgent matters; (6) When my phone has been silent for a while, I start imagining I have missed calls; (7) I feel the need to spend more time on my phone to achieve the same level of satisfaction; (8) When I mistakenly hear my phone ringing or vibrating, I instinctively check it; (9) My leisure time has decreased due to phone use; (10) I often have dreams related to mobile phones; (11) I feel anxious if I don't check my phone for messages or if it's turned off for a period of time; (12) Phone use has directly affected my academic or work performance; (13) I feel helpless or lost without my phone. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “not true at all” to 5 = “extremely true”), yielding total scores from 13 to 65. Individuals scoring at or above the 75th percentile were defined as having MPUA. The SQAPMPU has demonstrated strong reliability and validity, with a Cronbach's α of 0.94 ( Tao et al., 2020 ). In this study, the questionnaire had excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.891). 2.3.4 Sociodemographic variables Collected variables included gender, age, ethnicity, education level (junior college or undergraduate), residence (urban/rural), only-child status, family structure, perceived socioeconomic status, parental education levels, and number of close friends. 2.4 Statistical analysis Generalized Linear Models assessed the relationships between SSB consumption, MPUA, and depressive symptoms. Two models were constructed: Model 1 without any adjustments for confounding factors, and Model 2 adjusted for demographic and other relevant variables to account for potential confounding effects. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) using Mplus 7.4 was employed to test MPUA as a mediator between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms, following the methodology outlined by Xu et al. ( Honglv et al., 2023 ). Model parameters were estimated via maximum likelihood and tested with 5000 bootstrap resamples ( Williams and Mackinnon, 2008 ). Statistical significance was set at p &lt; 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 and Mplus 7.4. 3 Results 3.1 Prevalence and severity of depressive symptoms in college students The prevalence of depressive symptoms among college students was found to be 36.2 %, with 63.8 % classified as normal (no depressive symptoms). Among those experiencing depressive symptoms, the severity distribution revealed mild depression in 26.2 %, moderate depression in 6.3 %, moderately severe depression in 3.0 %, and severe depression in 0.7 %. Table 1 highlights significant differences in the prevalence of depressive symptoms across sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, residence (urban vs rural), and family economic status ( P &lt; 0.05). 3.2 Association between SSB consumption, MPUA, and depressive symptoms in college students In a cohort of 4886 college students, the positive rate for MPUA was 27.3 %, while the consumption rates of six types of SSBs were as follows: carbonated beverages (54.5 %), fruit beverages (53.0 %), tea beverages (52.9 %), energy beverages (52.6 %), milk beverages (53.1 %), and milk tea (52.5 %). The results of the General Linear Model (Model 2), as presented in Table 2 , which accounted for sociodemographic factors and potential confounders, revealed significant associations between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms across all beverage types. Specifically, the consumption of carbonated beverages (β = 0.15; 95 % CI: 0.07–0.23), fruit beverages (β = 0.41; 95 % CI: 0.33–0.49), tea beverages (β = 0.33; 95 % CI: 0.25–0.42), energy beverages (β = 0.39; 95 % CI: 0.31–0.47), milk beverages (β = 0.24; 95 % CI: 0.16–0.32), and milk tea (β = 0.35; 95 % CI: 0.27–0.44) were all significantly associated with depressive symptoms ( p &lt; 0.05). Additionally, MPUA showed a strong association with depressive symptoms (β = 0.34; 95 % CI: 0.33–0.35). 3.3 Mediating effect analysis The latent variable mediation effect model results are summarized in Table 3 . The direct effect coefficient of SSBs on depressive symptoms was found to be 0.35 ( P &lt; 0.01), while the mediation effect coefficient of MPUA was 0.31 (P &lt; 0.01). This yielded a total effect coefficient of 0.66, with the direct effect accounting for 53.0 % of the total effect and the mediation effect contributing 47.0 %. Fig. 2 illustrates the mediation pathway of MPUA in linking SSB consumption to depressive symptoms among college students. All path coefficients were statistically significant (P &lt; 0.01). The model fit indices further supported the acceptability of the mediation model, with Akaike Information Criterion = 179,118.18, Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) = 179,488.34, sample size-adjusted BIC = 179,307.22, Comparative Fit Index = 0.99, Tucker-Lewis Index = 0.99, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual = 0.01, χ 2 = 23,545.93 (P &lt; 0.01), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation = 0.01 (95 % CI: 0.01–0.02). 4 Discussion To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the mediating role of MPUA in the relationship between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms among college students in Northeast China. We found a notably high prevalence of depressive symptoms (36.2 %) among college students, exceeding rates reported in Western China (23.0 %) ( Chen et al., 2023 ), the United States (27.7 %) ( Younes et al., 2016 ), and Europe (19.1–23.4 %) ( Gavurova et al., 2022 ). The prevalence of MPUA (27.3 %) in our sample was comparable to findings from Central China (27.5 %) ( Tao et al., 2020 ). Prior research suggests that sugar, caffeine, and fat content in beverages like milk tea and sugary tea may contribute to adverse mental health outcomes ( Kaiser et al., 2023 ; Min et al., 2023 ). Although some compounds in tea, such as L-theanine and catechins, may offer antidepressant properties ( Unno et al., 2022 ), their benefits may be outweighed by the high sugar and caffeine content in commercially sold beverages ( Brownbill et al., 2020 ). These findings underscore the need to distinguish between traditional tea and modern sugar-laden tea products when assessing their mental health effects, especially in East Asian contexts where both types are commonly consumed. Several biological mechanisms may explain the link between SSB consumption and depression, including reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor, dopamine dysregulation, and heightened systemic inflammation ( Deyama and Kaneda, 2023 ; Shon et al., 2023 ). Additionally, disruption of the gut microbiome and nutrient displacement may contribute to depressive symptoms ( Samuel et al., 2024 ). Crucially, MPUA mediated 47.0 % of the total effect between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms. MPUA is increasingly recognized as a public health concern among youth. For instance, Malaysian students reportedly spend 25 % of their day on smartphones ( Fook et al., 2021 ), and numerous studies have documented associations between smartphone addiction and depression, with longitudinal evidence supporting a temporal link ( Hashemi et al., 2022 ). Gender disparities are also evident; for example, South Korean girls show higher levels of smartphone addiction and depression than boys ( Park and Lee, 2022 ). Evidence from Japan and Shanghai further supports these patterns, while emerging research suggests that excessive sugar consumption may increase the risk of problematic media use ( Liu et al., 2021 ). These findings collectively underscore the significant behavioral and psychological burdens associated with MPUA. SSB consumption and MPUA may share common neural pathways involving dopamine-driven reward systems. Adolescents are especially vulnerable due to heightened reward sensitivity and underdeveloped impulse control ( Steinberg et al., 2009 ). Neuroimaging research further shows that both energy-dense foods and smartphone use activate similar brain regions linked to addictive behavior ( DiFeliceantonio et al., 2018 ). Thus, these behaviors may reinforce each other and collectively elevate depression risk. The Social Ecological Theory provides a valuable framework for interpreting these results. In many East Asian regions, academic demands are generally high, and college students often face significant study burdens, which may affect their lifestyles and mental health. It is also important to recognize that the relationship between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms may vary across cultural and geographical contexts. In Western societies, despite declining SSB consumption, depression rates have continued to rise due to multifactorial influences such as socioeconomic stressors, social isolation, and digital media exposure. Our study, focusing on Chinese college students, reflects a context where both SSB consumption and mental health challenges are increasing, potentially driven by distinct lifestyle and academic pressures. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings beyond the current population. Considering these findings, multi-level interventions are recommended. At the individual level, cognitive-behavioral strategies, digital detox programs, and time management training could mitigate MPUA. At the institutional level, universities should offer mental health services, promote healthy drink alternatives, and establish smartphone use guidelines. At the policy level, public health campaigns and regulations to reduce sugar and caffeine content in beverages could help address both SSB consumption and depressive symptoms. Addressing MPUA directly in parallel with SSB reduction efforts may be essential to improving mental health of college students. 5 Strengths and limitations This study has several notable strengths. First, it utilized a large-scale sample spanning three provinces in Northeast China, ensuring geographic diversity and improving the representativeness of the findings. Second, it is among the first to investigate the mediating role of MPUA in the association between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms among college students, addressing a gap in current research. Third, the use of SEM provided a robust analytical framework to assess complex pathways, with strong model fit indices supporting the reliability of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, causal relationships cannot be established. Future longitudinal studies are needed to explore changes over time and potential causal mechanisms. Second, the data were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias. Although anonymity and data validation procedures were used to reduce these risks, such bias cannot be fully eliminated. Third, the classification of SSBs in this study was based on typical formulations and market availability in China. Certain categories (e.g., tea beverages, milk beverages) may not always contain added sugar and may differ from standardized definitions used internationally. This potential inconsistency could limit comparability with other studies and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, approximately 76 % (3699 out of 4886) of the participants were from medical colleges. Given the possible differences in health literacy, lifestyle behaviors, and stress levels between medical and non-medical students, this overrepresentation may limit the generalizability of our results to the broader college or adolescent population. Future studies should strive to include a more balanced and diverse sample to enhance applicability across different student groups. 6 Conclusion This study revealed a significant association between SSB consumption and depressive symptoms among college students in Northeast China, with MPUA acting as a key mediating factor. These findings highlight the need to address both unhealthy dietary habits and excessive mobile phone use in efforts to improve adolescent mental health. Health professionals and educators are encouraged to incorporate digital and dietary behavior assessments into student mental health screenings and wellness programs. At the policy level, interventions such as limiting the availability of high-sugar beverages on campuses, promoting healthier alternatives, and implementing digital wellness initiatives may help mitigate the risks associated with smartphone overuse and poor nutrition. Together, these strategies can contribute to more comprehensive and effective mental health promotion among college students.
