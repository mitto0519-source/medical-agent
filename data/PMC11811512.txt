Background General anesthesia (GA) in dentistry is crucial for managing patients with special needs, enabling comprehensive care for individuals who cannot tolerate conventional procedures. This study aimed to analyze the demographic characteristics and trends in dental treatments performed under GA. Methods A retrospective analysis of 2,458 dental treatments performed under GA at Dankook University Jukjeon Dental Hospital was conducted between January 2015 and October 2024. Data on sex, age, GA duration, and type of disability were collected and analyzed. Results Of the 2,458 cases, 89.5% (2,199) involved patients with disabilities. Male patients accounted for 60.0% of the total cohort, and 61.3% of patients with disabilities were male. The mean age of patients was 34.1 ± 13.4 years, with younger patients more frequently treated under GA. Intellectual disabilities were the most prevalent (56.3%) followed by autism spectrum disorder (14.6%). The average GA duration was 174.8 ± 61.5 minutes, with longer durations observed in patients with disabilities (177.2 ± 61.1 minutes). Conclusion The increasing demand for GA, particularly among patients with disabilities, underscores the need for tailored guidelines and enhanced education on dental treatment performed under GA. These findings provide a foundation for future policy development. Special-needs dental care encompasses a diverse group of patients, including those with intellectual, physical, developmental, and psychiatric disabilities, as well as individuals with complex medical conditions [ 2 3 ]. For some patients with disabilities, any procedure requiring prolonged sitting and maintaining an open mouth without sudden movements can be challenging [ 4 5 ]. Additionally, individuals with physical limitations, such as those with motor disorders —including Parkinson's disease, cerebral palsy, and uncontrolled tremors— faced significant difficulties in undergoing dental treatment [ 6 ]. For patients with special needs who experience extreme anxiety and fear during dental procedures, or who cannot cooperate due to cognitive impairment, safe and effective treatment may require GA or alternative sedation methods, such as oral, inhaled, or intravenous sedation [ 7 8 9 ]. Among these methods, GA has the advantages of not relying on patient cooperation, achieving a 100% success rate, providing long-lasting effects, reducing the number of hospital visits, and expanding the treatment area [ 10 ]. Therefore, GA is a crucial tool for providing dental treatment to patients with special needs while minimizing physical and psychological stress [ 11 12 ]. However, GA is associated with a risk of complications such as airway obstruction or laryngospasm, due to the loss of consciousness caused by central nervous system suppression and reduced defensive reflexes. Consequently, it should be carefully selected and applied only to cases meeting appropriate indications. According to previous reports, the demand for GA is increasing among patients with special needs [ 11 13 ]. Additionally, there are cases in which patients without special needs choose to undergo dental treatment under GA to experience a more comfortable procedure without pain or discomfort. Given the growing social understanding and awareness of GA, it is essential to examine current GA administration rates and patient demographics. This study retrospectively analyzed the demographic characteristics and trends of patients who underwent dental treatment under GA at Dankook University Jukjeon Dental Hospital between January 2015 and October 2024. These data will be valuable for dental clinics and hospitals targeting patients seeking dental treatment under GA and will help inform future treatment planning and policy development. METHODS 1. Subjects This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dankook University Jukjeon Dental Hospital (IRB number DKUDH IRB 2412-001-001). A retrospective analysis of treatment records was conducted for patients aged 19 years or older who visited the hospital and received dental treatment under GA between January 2015 and October 2024. 2. Methods The study included outpatients with behavioral dysregulation, severely disabled patients, patients whose behaviors could not be managed with sedation due to the need for extensive treatment or lack of cooperation, and patients who requested GA for comfort to avoid discomfort or pain. On the day of the procedure, the patient's readiness for anesthesia was assessed, and it was then induced. In patients who did not cooperate, the induction of anesthesia was more challenging than its maintenance. Mild sedation was therefore initiated with midazolam for patients who were highly resistant to entering the examination room or accepting treatment. [ 3 ]. An anesthesiologist administered the anesthesia. After the patient was seated in the dental chair, anesthesia was initiated and mask induction was performed. The inhalation sequence included a mixture of 50% nitrous oxide, 50% oxygen, and a high concentration of sevoflurane. A standard monitor was applied, and an intravenous catheter was inserted. After confirming the loss of consciousness and providing adequate manual ventilation, rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) was administered prior to nasal tracheal intubation. The depth of anesthesia was maintained using a mixture of 2 vol% sevoflurane, 50% nitrous oxide, and 50% oxygen. Vital signs and entropy were continuously monitored. After the dental procedure, sevoflurane and nitrous oxide were discontinued, and 100% oxygen was administered. Sugamadex (2 mg/kg) was administered to reverse the neuromuscular blockade. Ramosetron hydrochloride (0.3 mg) was administered to reduce the postoperative gag reflex. The patient was extubated upon resuming spontaneous breathing. After administering 100% oxygen, vital signs were monitored, and the patient was transferred to the recovery room once stable. In the recovery room, oxygen was administered through a mask, vital signs were monitored, and the patient was discharged after confirming that they were conscious, coughing, breathing spontaneously, had normal skin color, and could perform more than four movements. We collected 2,458 medical records of patients who received dental treatment under GA, treating each session as a separate case, even if the same patient underwent multiple GA treatments. Disabled patients were identified based on legal disability criteria. Among patients with multiple disabilities, we distinguished between those with “serious disabilities in the field of dentistry” and those with other disabilities. The GA duration was recorded starting from the first 60 min, with additional time counted in 15-minute intervals. Using these records, we conducted a retrospective analysis by categorizing all patients who received dental treatment under GA according to sex, age, GA duration, type of disability, and annual GA usage trends. RESULTS 1. Gender A total of 2,458 cases of dental treatment under GA were recorded, of which 1,475 (60.0%) were male and 983 (40.0%) were female, indicating a higher proportion of males. Patients with disabilities accounted for 89.5% (2,199) of all cases. Similarly, among patients with disabilities, there male population was higher, comprising 1,348 (61.3%) males and 851 (38.7%) females. The distribution of patients treated under GA according to disability type is presented in Table 1 , showing a higher proportion of males across all disability types, except for psychiatric disorders. The annual distribution of sex among patients with disabilities undergoing GA is shown in Fig. 1 . Over the years, the number of patients with disabilities receiving GA has increased, with a consistently higher male-to-female ratio. 2. Age distribution The mean age of all GA patients was 34.1 ± 13.4 years, ranging from 19 to 85 years. Patients aged 20–29 years represented the largest proportion, with the number of patients progressively decreasing with age. Among disabled patients receiving dental treatment under GA, the mean age was 32.6 ± 11.8 years, ranging from 19 to 82 years. This indicates that the overall patient group was slightly older than the disabled group. As with the total group, the number of disabled patients decreased with increasing age. The age of patients treated under GA according to the type of disability is presented in Table 2 . Patients with intellectual disabilities accounted for the largest proportion, with a high concentration in the 20–29 age group. The number of patients with autism spectrum disorders and intellectual disabilities sharply decreased with increasing age. Similarly, patients with multiple disabilities showed a decline in the number of older patients as age increased. The distribution by year and age group for disabled patients is shown in Fig. 2 . The highest number of patients was in the 20–29 age group, followed by the 30–39 age group, with both numbers increasing over the years. Additionally, the number of patients in the 40–49 age group has also been increasing over time. 3. Anesthesia duration The GA duration ranged from a minimum of 60 minutes to a maximum of 480 minutes, with an average of 174.8 ± 61.5 minutes. For disabled patients, the average anesthesia duration was 177.2 ± 61.1 minutes. It was observed that procedures for patients with disabilities required more time. The average GA duration by disability type was highest for psychiatric disorders at 193.1 ± 82.0 min, followed by physical disabilities (188.9 ± 61.0 min), and lowest for multiple disabilities (dental field) at 150.8 ± 41.7 min. Fig. 3 shows the GA duration by disability type. The GA duration according to the age of patients with disabilities is shown in Fig. 4 . The median duration for each age group ranged from approximately 150 to 200 minutes. Outliers exceeding 400 minutes were observed in certain age groups, including 20–29, 30–39, and 40–49 years. Changes in the duration of general anesthesia by year are shown in Fig. 5 using a boxplot. There was no significant change in the duration of general anesthesia over the years. 4. Type of disability The percentages of disability types among patients with disabilities who underwent dental treatment under GA are presented in Table 1 . Intellectual disability was the most prevalent, accounting for 56%, excluding those without disabilities, followed by autism spectrum disorder and multiple disabilities (etc.). The proportion of patients with disabilities treated with GA dental care between 2015 and 2024 is shown in Fig. 6 . Intellectual disability accounted for the largest proportion of patients with disabilities treated with GA and remained consistent over the past 10 years. Autism spectrum disorders showed an overall increasing trend, although the proportion of patients undergoing dental treatment with GA fluctuated. Multiple disabilities (dental field) accounted for a very small proportion of patients treated with GA from 2015 to 2023 but increased sharply in 2024. DISCUSSION Special-needs dental care encompasses a diverse group of patients, including those with intellectual disabilities, physical disabilities, autism spectrum, brain lesions, and psychiatric disabilities [ 3 ]. These individuals often experience extreme fear and anxiety due to their inability to understand the general dental treatment process, leading to noncooperation or refusal. Additionally, even simple procedures without sudden movements can be challenging, making safe dental care difficult or impossible. Therefore, providing dental treatment to patients with special needs poses a significant challenge for dentists. GA is a crucial method for minimizing physical and mental stress while ensuring safe and effective dental care [ 11 12 ]. Recently, there has been increasing interest in conscious sedation and GA, even among patients without specific disabilities, as they seek pain-free and comfortable dental treatment. This study highlights the growing demand for GA in dental procedures, particularly for patients with special needs. From 2015 to 2024, we analyzed 2,458 cases of dental treatment under GA at Dankook University's Jukjeon Dental Hospital, focusing on variables such as sex, age, anesthesia duration, disability type, and annual trends. Among these cases, 2,199 involved patients with disabilities, accounting for 89.5% of all GA cases. The high proportion of patients with disabilities undergoing dental treatment under GA reflects the challenges of behavioral management and low cooperation in this population. Lee et al. reported that patients with disabilities experience more severe dental caries compared to non-disabled individuals, necessitating complex dental treatments [ 14 ]. Sex analysis revealed a higher proportion of male patients (60.0%) compared to females (40.0%) across all cases, with male patients comprising 61.3% of the disability group. This aligns with previous research by Kim et al., which suggested that males with special needs are more likely to require GA for dental procedures [ 14 15 ]. Among all disability types, except for psychiatric disorders, men outnumbered women. The average age of patients undergoing GA for dental treatment was 34.1 ± 13.4 years, ranging from 19 to 85 years, with the largest group being 20–29 years. Patients with disabilities had a mean age of 32.6 ± 11.8 years, ranging from 19 to 82 years. This suggests that GA is required more frequently in younger individuals with disabilities than in the general patient population. Consistent with other studies, we found that dental caries and tooth loss significantly increased in patients with disabilities after their 20s, with rapid progression beyond their 30s, resulting in fewer restored teeth compared to individuals without disabilities [ 14 ]. These findings underscore the need for timely and appropriate dental care to prevent the deterioration of oral health in adults with disabilities. When analyzing the distribution of patients by disability type, intellectual disability accounted for the highest proportion (56.3%), followed by autism spectrum disorder (14.6%), and multiple disabilities (14.3%). Patients with intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder showed a marked decrease in patient numbers with increasing age. For instance, previous studies have reported that 56% of patients aged 20–40 years with intellectual disabilities have periodontitis, increasing to 92.6% in those over 60 years [ 16 ]. Other studies have also noted high levels of periodontitis across all age groups in this population [ 17 18 ]. These findings suggest that the relatively higher number of patients aged 19–49 years with intellectual disabilities undergoing dental treatment reflects fewer missing teeth compared to older patients with disabilities. The total anesthesia time ranged from 60 to 480 minutes, with an average of 174.8 ± 61.5 minutes. For patients with disabilities, the mean anesthesia time was slightly longer at 177.2 ± 61.1 minutes, reflecting the increased complexity of dental procedures for this group. These results align with those of Kum et al., who reported longer GA times for pediatric patients with special needs [ 11 ]. Analyzing anesthesia time by age showed median durations of approximately 150–200 min across disability types, with outliers exceeding 400 minutes observed in patients aged 20–29, 30–39, and 40–49 years. The consistent median duration suggests no significant differences in the procedure duration; however, the outliers likely indicate cases with greater difficulty and complexity. This study categorized disabilities into severe disabilities in the dental field and other types. “Severe disability in the dental field” included brain lesion disorders (mild–severe), severe physical disabilities, severe intellectual disabilities, severe autism spectrum disorders, severe mental disorders, and severe epilepsy disorders. Among GA cases, patients with multiple disabilities (dental field) accounted for only 0.9%, while multiple disabilities (other) constituted 14.3%. This reflects the high demand for GA even among patients not classified as having severe disabilities in the dental field. These findings highlight the need for further research to analyze the types of disabilities and reconsider the scope of severe disabilities in the dental field, if necessary. This study excluded an analysis of the reasons for GA among non-disabled patients due to insufficient information. However, a Canadian study reported that 31.1% of patients with high dental fear expressed a definite interest in sedation or GA for dental treatment, with 54.1% citing cost as the major barrier [ 19 ]. Further research on the reasons for sedation or GA in individuals without disabilities is warranted. From 2015 to 2024, the number of patients undergoing dental treatment under GA has increased steadily. This trend reflects the growing social acceptance of GA as a viable option for dental treatment, consistent with the findings of Jo et al. [ 20 ]. In conclusion, the increasing demand for dental treatment under GA, particularly among patients with disabilities, underscores the need to recognize the high proportion of patients requiring GA despite not meeting the criteria for “severe disability in the dental field.” Developing guidelines for dental treatment under GA, along with strengthening education and healthcare systems, is essential. This study contributes to policy development in this area.
