In a recent published Current Opinion in Sports Medicine-Open , Chaabene, Markov, &amp; Schega make the case for vigorous physical activity (VPA), including high-intensity exercise (HIE) and high-intensity interval training (HIIT), as a priority in future physical activity (PA) guidelines for children and adolescents. Their article provides evidence of the effectiveness of different types of VPA for improving health-related fitness and reducing health risks among children and adolescents. The authors recommend that the “…next generation of PA recommendations should place a greater emphasis on the significance of vigorous physical activity for youth”. In this paper the recommendation for making VPA, including HIE and HIIT, a priority in future PA guidelines for youth is examined. At the outset, I would like to make it clear that I concur with the notion that VPA is very important in promoting health-related fitness and improving health markers among children and adolescents. However, I have questions about exactly what “prioritizing” VPA in future guidelines means and concerns about some of the statements supporting the prioritizing of VPA. Current Opinion article are meant to provoke informed discussion about important topics. Chaabene et al. started the dialogue and provide the basis for a healthy discussion. In this Current Opinion response, the recommendation for making VPA a priority in future PA guidelines for youth is examined. Alternative ideas and priorities are provided. Clarifying Terminology For purposes of this Current Opinion response, sedentary behavior and several different types of PA are identified, and operational definitions are provided (see Table 1 ). The term “youth” (referring to both the singular and the plural) is used throughout to refer to children and adolescents at the recommendation of the journal. Historical Background of Physical Activity Guidelines in Youth Chaabene et al. review the historical background of PA guidelines for youth beginning with the ACSM position statement of 1988 [ 6 ]. Additional historical perspectives are presented here. As late as the 1950s vigorous PA was discouraged for children. For example, the original AAHPER Youth Fitness Test [ 7 ] used in the United States included a 600-yard run/walk rather than longer runs because of concerns about the appropriateness of long runs for youth. In the U. S. it was not until 1978 that the American Academy of Pediatrics endorsed VPA involvement for many who were previously excluded from “full” participation in physical education classes [ 8 ]. In the 1984, an international conference was held to establish physical activity guidelines specifically for adolescents [ 9 ]. The guidelines recommended that adolescents be active daily, or nearly every day, and engage in three or more sessions of activities that last 20 min or more and that require moderate to vigorous exertion. An opinion statement by the American College of Sports Medicine [ 6 ] in 1988 stated, “Until more definitive evidence is available, current recommendations are that children and youth obtain 20–30 minutes of vigorous activity each day (p. 422).” Both statements mirrored the recommendation for adults that focused on continuous aerobic exercise. In their position paper in the President’s Council on Physical Fitness and Sports Research Digest , Corbin, Pangrazi, and Welk [ 10 ] outlined the need for age and developmentally appropriate guidelines specifically for children. They recommended guidelines based on the unique characteristics of children (5–12) shown in Table 2 . In 1998 the first unique physical activity guidelines for children were published in the United Kingdom [ 11 ] and the United States [ 12 ]. The U. S. guidelines were revised in 2004 [ 13 ]. Both the UK and US guidelines recommended at least 60 min of age and developmentally appropriate moderate to vigorous PA (MVPA), selected from a variety of activities, on all or most days of the week. Both sets of guidelines considered the unique characteristics and needs of children (see Table 2 ) and moved away from previous adult-based recommendations calling for continuous bouts of aerobic VPA. Neither specifically included muscle strengthening (MSPA) or bone strengthening (BSPA) guidelines. The guidelines did, however, indicate that extended periods of inactivity (periods of sedentary behavior in current terminology) were discouraged for children. Finally, the guidelines recommended that PA be selected from a variety of activities including MSPA. Both the 2008 American guidelines [ 14 ] and the second edition guidelines of 2018 [ 15 ] recommend 60 min or more of mostly aerobic MVPA daily , as opposed to the “all or most days of the week” standard in earlier guidelines. The American guidelines differed from previous guidelines indicating that the 60 min of MVPA should include VPA, MSPA, and BSPA on at least three days a week. The WHO [ 5 ], in its 2020 guidelines, recommended “…at least an average of 60 minutes per day, mostly aerobic, across the week (p. 1).” The WHO indicates that the evidence does not support requiring daily activity. Similarly, the WHO guidelines recommended that as part of the 60 min of MVPA “vigorous-intensity aerobic activities, as well as those that strengthen muscles and bone, should be incorporated at least three days a week [ 5 , p. 1].” In the American guidelines VPA, MSPA, and BSPA are recommended separately, however, WHO guidelines combine the three. Like the American guidelines, the WHO recommends activities that are enjoyable, offer variety, and are age and ability appropriate. The WHO further notes that “…doing some physical activity is better than doing none and that children and adolescents should start by doing small amounts increasing frequency, intensity, and duration gradually [ 5 , p. 1].” We have come a long way since the 1950s. Adult guidelines that mandated continuous PA and that were applied to children gave way to guidelines created specifically for children. Progressive resistance training (PRE) formerly thought to be inappropriate for youth is now endorsed as one appropriate type of muscle fitness activity [ 16 , 17 ] along with other activities described in PA guidelines. The American Academy of Pediatrics [ 18 ] has indicated that distance running can be performed by children and adolescents as long as appropriate guidelines for participation are considered. To be sure, kids can do VPA, and it should be encouraged. Important, however, are the methods used to encourage VPA among youth. From 1994, when international guidelines for adolescents were introduced [ 9 ], and 1998 when national guidelines for children were introduced [ 10 ], guidelines for children and adolescents were separate. With the advent of American [ 15 ] and WHO [ 5 ] documents, guidelines for children and adolescents were combined. There were no longer different guidelines for each age group. Youth Physical Activity and Sedentary Behavior Patterns Are Too Many Youth Inactive? In their introduction, Chaabene et al. indicate that there is a worldwide “physical inactivity pandemic” as indicated by the “alarmingly deficient” rate of adherence to MVPA guidelines. They cite Guthold et al. [ 19 ] who define inactivity as failure to do at least 60 min of daily MVPA. Based on this definition, Guithold et al. [ 19 ] indicated that 81% of youth worldwide aged 11 to 17 fail to meet the WHO guidelines (19% met the standard). The rate of MVPA adherence is similar to rates in other studies including Hyde et al. [ 20 ] who reported of 15.2% and NYRBSS [ 21 ] that reported 25% of youth met the American MVPA standard. As indicated by Cardon and Salmon [ 22 ], the way in which inactivity is assessed plays a significant role in rates of inactivity among youth. Results reported by Hyde et al. [ 20 ] illustrate that fact. For example, the MVPA standard used by Hyde et al. [ 20 ] was 60 min (that elevate your heart rate and made you breathe hard) on ALL days of the week. Meeting the standard on ALL days of the week is very difficult to achieve. Data from the U. S [ 21 ]. indicate the adherence rate is much higher for MVPA when a five day (44%) vs. seven day (25%) standard is used. When standards other than the MVPA are used, many fewer youth are judged to be inactive. For example, Hyde et al. [ 20 ] report rates of meeting standards for VPA (70.6%), MSPA (50.7%), and BSPA (80.7%). When meeting multiple standards (MVPA, VPA, MSPA, BSPA) is required, the rate of adherence is especially low (11.2%). In addition to the types of assessment, sex (males are more active than females), disability (disabled less active than non-disabled), body composition (obese less active than non-obese), economic status (low income less active than high income), and location (countries have varying activity rates) are among the many factors that affect inactivity status [ 22 , 23 ]. Subgroups within categories vary widely in rates of inactivity from very high to relatively low in comparison to overall rates. In summary the bulk of the evidence supports the statements of Chaabene et al. that too many youth are inactive—fail to meet general MVPA national and international guidelines. It is my view, however, that the rates of inactivity may be overstated. Rates of adherence to MVPA guidelines, for example, vary widely (11.2–44%) depending on the method of assessment. It is difficult to reconcile how only 15.2% percent of youth can be judged as meeting the MVPA standard when 50–80% of youth meet VPA, MSPA, and BSPA standards [ 20 ]. As will be recommended in the final sections of this paper, examination of the method of assessing youth PA seems warranted. Are Too Many Youth Sedentary? In their introduction, Chaabene et al. indicate that “sedentary alternatives” such as too much screen time are prevalent among youth. They cite Boreham et al. [ 24 ] who indicate that youth in the U. S. averaged three and one half to four hours of television time per day. Other sources from the U. S. also provide evidence of high levels of screen time. For example, Zablotsky et al. [ 25 ] report that 80% of youth report engage in screen time for two hours or more hours daily and 50.4% of adolescents had four or more hours of screen time each day. The high levels of screen time do indicate that youth spend hours in sedentary behavior and screen time has become a proxy for sedentary behavior. It is important to add that some forms of sedentary behavior, and even screen time, are necessary as part of the daily activities of youth (e.g., sleep, reading for school, homework, recreation). Also, youth can exhibit long periods of sedentary behavior and still meet PA guidelines. In summary, it seems fair to say that many youth spend too much time in sedentary behaviors. The Benefits of Physical Activity Does vigorous physical activity have fitness and health benefits “…surpassing those of low-or moderate-intensity activity in youth [ 1 , p.1]?” The health benefits of PA cited by Chaabene et al. are improved body composition (e.g., BMI, % body fat, waist circumference), improved metabolic health (e.g., systolic blood pressure), improved bone health (e.g., bone mass), and improved brain health (e.g., cognitive performance, mental health). The PF benefits cited are improved cardiorespiratory fitness and muscular fitness. Based on their review of many articles, Chaabene et al. reach the reasonable conclusion, and I concur, that VPA, including HIE/HIIT, provide benefits for fitness and health for youth that “surpass” those of LPA and/or MPA. It should be pointed out, however, that VPA did not “surpass” MPA for all fitness and health benefits and the evidence showed many benefits for MPA, although sometimes less robust than VPA. The findings are consistent with the existing literature including current PA guidelines for youth [ 5 , 15 ]. In the overview of the research concerning the benefits of VPA, Chaabene et al. cite several studies involving different types of PA, many involving HIE/HIIT. In fact, an HIIT study [ 26 ] is used in the introduction as an example of VPA superiority over LPA and MPA. It is also used to make the case that VPA is more attractive to youth than less intense PA. Five of the nine references for the cardiorespiratory fitness benefits of VPA involve HIIT. Another HIIT study [ 27 ] is used to illustrate the time efficiency of VPA (see next section). Because of the prominence of HIIT in the review of the benefits, HIE including HIIT are considered when discussing VPA and its appeal. Does vigorous physical activity provide fitness and health benefits in less time than low to moderate physical activity? In their Current Opinion piece Chaabene et al. provide evidence that one of the benefits of VPA for youth is time efficiency. That VPA is more time efficient than less intense forms of PA is well documented among adults. For example, American [ 15 ] and WHO [ 5 ] PA guidelines indicate that VPA provides health benefits in half the time (75 vs. 150 min) for MPA. Chaabene et al. offer several citations, including one that involves HIIT [ 27 ], to support their contention that VPA is more time efficient for youth as well as adults. For example, on page 4 Chaabene et al. state that one minute of VPA generates benefits that require 2 to 3 min for MPA. I concur that VPA can produce benefits in less time than less intense forms of PA. However, as the American guidelines indicate, youth are much less time conscious than adults [ 15 ]. When youth find an activity that they really enjoy, the often lose track of time. Is Sedentary Behavior a Threat To the Fitness and Health of Youth? Chaabene et al. offer evidence, in their introduction, that excessive screen time has negative health consequences for youth (e.g., unhealthy body composition, cardiometabolic risks, low physical fitness, and low self-esteem [ 28 ]. Muppalla et al. [ 29 ] provides supportive evidence. We agree that excessive sedentary behavior can be harmful to health and can result in below healthy levels of PF. The Nature of Physical Activity Patterns of Youth Are the PA patterns of youth sporadic in nature involving daily, brief, and intermittent bouts of high-intensity PA? Chaabene et al. state the following: “Youth PA patterns are well-recognized for their sporadic nature, involving daily, brief, and intermittent bouts of high-intensity efforts [p. 6].” Four references are cited. The first [ 30 ], an article about PA assessment, of which I am a coauthor, acknowledges the intermittent nature of PA patterns of youth and the problems that the intermittent patterns create when assessing PA. The article addresses the work of Bailey et al. [ 31 ] that suggests short, intermittent bouts of PA (with frequent rest periods of longer duration) are typical of children and, in fact, may be necessary for normal growth and development. Chaabene et al. are correct in stating that youth PA patterns are typically intermittent in nature. However, their statement that acknowledges only “intermittent bouts of high-intensity ” PA is misleading. A review of the work of Bailey et al. and others is necessary to make the point. The research of Bailey et al. [ 31 ] was cited by Welk and Corbin [ 31 ]. They review the work of Bailey et al. and note that the intermittent nature of PA among children was one of the principal reasons for the 60-minute recommendation for children when the first PA guidelines for children were formulated in 1998. Those guidelines were the first to deviate from adult guidelines that recommended continuous aerobic PA. Because intermittent PA includes burst of PA followed by periods of rest, more time is necessary for children to accumulate PA in a specific time period than for adults who often perform continuous PA. In the classic study by Bailey et al. [ 31 ] children (6–10) were observed for 12 h a day. It is true that short bursts of VPA including high-intensity PA, were part of the self-selected behavior of children and that the bouts were followed by relatively long periods of rest compared to length of PA bouts. The activity patterns of children are sporadic in nature, involving daily, brief, and intermittent bouts of PA. However, the suggestion by Chaabene et al. that the intermittent bouts are primarily of “high-intensity” PA is not reflected in the work of Bailey et al. [ 31 ]. A closer look shows that the intermittent PA of children included VPA bouts averaging three seconds with 95% less than 15 s in duration. Only 0.1% of the VPA bouts lasted one minute or more with none lasting more than 10 min. In addition, PA of children included many intermittent bouts of LPA and MPA averaging 6 s in length (22.5 the longest bout). Of the total time spent in PA was 77.1% LPA, 19.7% MPA, and 3.1% VPA. The results indicated that rest intervals between activities of high intensity were highly variable and much longer than the activity bouts [ 31 , p. 1037]. The intermittent nature of PA among children is well established; however, the suggestion that the intermittent PA is mostly VPA and that it somehow suggests that children prefer VPA bouts is not accurate, at least not based on the research of Bailey, et al. [ 31 ]. The early work of Corbin and Pletcher [ 32 ], that involved filming children in PA, found similar results to those of Bailey, et al. [ 31 ]. Children in self-selected free play performed intermittent bouts of various intensities of short duration followed by rest periods. Wide variation in PA patterns in unstructured play periods were evidenced with some participants being very active and some very inactive. Chaabene et al. cite a second reference Corder et al. [ 33 ] to support their statements about youth PA patterns. The cited article focuses on the assessment of MVPA and does acknowledge the intermittent patterns of youth. However, I find no evidence in the reference to indicate that most intermittent bouts of PA for children are of high-intensity. The third reference cited by Chaabene et al. is the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans [ 15 ] document. The document indicates that “…children are naturally active in an intermittent way, particularly when they do unstructured active play [ 15 , p. 49],” and “They alternate brief periods of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activity with periods of light-intensity physical activity or rest [ 15 , p. 49].” The document also indicates that as children grow into adolescents, their patterns of PA change. Their activity patterns are still intermittent, but that they are able to sustain longer periods of MPA or VPA. There are no statements in the document, that I can find, to indicate that the intermittent bouts of youth are primarily of high-intensity. Finally, the fourth cited reference [ 23 ] is another article about assessment. The authors of the article acknowledge the fact that “…children are less time conscious than adults and tend to engage in physical activities at sporadic times and intensities rather than in consistent bouts [p. 1070]. Nothing that I can find in the paper suggests that the intermittent activity bouts of youth are primarily of high-intensity. In fact, the authors reported heart rate data indicating that youth performed MVP much more frequently than VPA. In summary, the evidence cited by Chaabene et al. does indicate that the activity patterns of youth are intermittent in nature. However, the evidence does not support the notion that the intermittent bouts of PA among youth are primarily of high-intensity. The Appeal of Vigorous Physical Activity In their Current Opinion article Chaabene et al. make the argument that VPA is more appealing to youth than less intense PA. For example, they make the following statement. “Furthermore, unlike in adults, vigorous PA is more attractive for youth. In fact, youth prefer to engage in short, intermittent high-intensity efforts rather than low-intensity ones. Therefore, promoting vigorous PA could improve the adherence rate to PA and mitigate the current physical inactivity pandemic. Considering all the above, we advocate in this Current Opinion paper that the intensity of PA is more appealing than the volume for youth health and physical fitness [ 1 , p.2].” On page six, the authors indicate that the “Accumulating evidence indicates that vigorous PA is more appealing than low or moderate activity to improve various physical fitness and health factors in youth [ 1 , p. 6].” Also on page 6, the authors make this statement: “Notably, unlike in adults, vigorous PA holds a stronger appeal for youth. In fact, youth tend to gravitate towards short, intermittent high-intensity efforts over low-intensity ones [ 1 , p. 6].” In the sections that follow the appeal (attractiveness) of VPA is discussed. Do sports appeal to children and adolescents? Chaabene et al. cite the work of Hyde et al. [ 20 ] to support the notion that sports are a form of VPA that have appeal among youth. Of the 14 activities included as VPA activities by Hyde et al. [ 20 ], 10 were sports and of the 17 activities included BSPA, 10 were sports. Other studies support the popularity of sport among youth. The YRBSS survey, for example, indicates that 52% of American adolescents participated in school or community sports [ 21 ]. In summary there is evidence, including Hyde et al. [ 20 ] and YRBSS [ 21 ], to indicate that self-selected sports, typically involving VPA, are appealing to youth. Of concern is that the proportion of children and adolescents who participate in sports decreases with age [ 33 , 34 ] with a significant drop occurring after the age 13 consistent with the overall decline in MVPA. An American Academy of Pediatrics [ 35 ] report that provides clinical guidance for pediatricians includes concern about burnout, overspecialization, overtraining, and physiologic and psychological concerns with intensive training. Among the nine guidelines for pediatricians concerning youth sport participation is a primary focus on fun and learning lifelong PA skills. What other physical activities appeal to youth? As indicated in the previous section, many of the activities considered when assessing the VPA of adolescents were sports. However, Hyde et al. [ 20 ] indicate that many activities other than sports are appealing to youth including aerobics, bike riding, dance, martial arts, running/jogging, swimming, skateboarding, swimming, walking, weightlifting, yoga, and others. The same survey that was used by Hyde et a [ 20 ]. indicates that many of the non- sports activities listed were among the most popular [ 36 ]. For example, walking, jogging, weightlifting, active video games, dance, bike riding, aerobics and swimming were all in the top 10 participation activities. Basketball and football were the only sports in the top 10. In summary, it is fair to say that MPA and VPA activities other than sports are also appealing to youth. Is vigorous physical activity more appealing to adults than low to moderate physical activity? Multiple statements by Chaabene et al. suggest that VPA is not appealing to adults, but it is to children and adolescents (see the opening paragraph to The Appeal ofVigorous Physical Activity, page 5). Based on his research with colleagues, Petruzello [ 37 ] states that “From an affective perspective, it has been clearly established that as exercise intensity increases, especially at higher levels (i.e., beyond the lactate/ventilatory threshold), affective valence decreases, and the activity becomes increasingly less pleasant/more unpleasant [p. 62].” The increase in negative affect that results as intensity of exercise increases is illustrated by the curved line in Fig. 1 . The figure is provided for illustration purposes and is not based on a specific data set. Petruzzello [ 37 ] further suggests that scholars and practitioners should resist the urge to become attracted to “shiny toys” and the latest fads. He classifies HIIT in the “shiny toy” category and indicates that “…when activity behavior is tracked over time, adherence to such protocols is extremely poor… [p. 62].” Extensive reference materials regarding the efficacy of Petruzello’s comments are provided in his reference [ 36 ] list. The challenge of VPA among adults is also reflected in the fact that the recommended ranges for MVPA for adults in national and international guidelines documents were established based on “compromise between the ‘ideal physiological prescription’ and a ‘manageable behavioural prescription’ [ 38 ].” The cut points, as illustrated in Fig. 2 , were based on the amount of PA that produces the most benefits for an amount of PA that is manageable (not too difficult or unappealing to accomplish) for most adults. The general recommendation for adult population is for 150 min per week of MPA (equal to brisk walking) or 75 min per week of VPA or some combination of the two [ 5 , 15 ]. For weight control higher ranges are recommended (300 min of MPA or 150 min of VPA per week). PA above the recommended range provides benefits in excess of those provided in the recommended range, but the benefits per minute of extra PA are not as great as those for the recommended PA range (see steep slope of the line prior to the recommended range compared to the lower slope of the line after it). The current guidelines are quite different from early guidelines that recommended continuous VPA in bouts of 20 min or more. The salient point is that the experts who developed the current guidelines were aware of the fact that as intensity of PA increases the appeal, especially at the high end, decreases. The most popular forms of PA in the United States are moderate in intensity with walking and swimming among the most popular [ 39 ]. The fact that the most popular activities for adults are moderate in intensity does not mean some adults will not prefer VPA. Adults are abstract thinkers who can assess the value of PA and choose to commit the necessary time to achieve its benefits. For some, the time saving value of VPA is attractive. Others just enjoy the challenge of VPA, including HIE/HIIT. Is vigorous physical activity more appealing for children and adolescents than less vigorous physical activity? As indicated in the introduction to this section, Chaabene et al. make several statements that suggest that youth find VPA more appealing and attractive than less vigorous activities (see introduction to this section). In support of their position, Chaabene et al. cite four references. Three are the same as cited in support of the intermittent nature of PA as discussed in the previous section. In the interest of brevity, each of those articles will not be reviewed again. None of these three references provide evidence that VPA is more appealing than LPA or MPA among youth. On the contrary, in self-selected non-structured PA children display more LPA than MPA. The fourth study cited by Chaabene et al. does provide support for the argument that adolescents find VPA appealing, especially sports [ 20 ], however the study also shows the appeal of other types of MVPA including both VPA and MPA. The evidence provided by Chaabene et al., and in this paper, indicates that youth also find sedentary behavior to be appealing, so much so that they compete with PA for a place in the daily activities of youth. In summary, there is evidence that youth find VPA appealing, especially sports and leisure time activities. There is also evidence that youth find many types of MPA and sedentary behaviors to be attractive, Do youth find HIE/HIIT appealing? In support of their position that VPA has benefits that exceed those of LPA and MPA for youth, Chaabene et al. cite numerous studies including observational studies of self-selected PA and studies of the effectiveness of preplanned exercise programs. They also cite multiple studies of the effectiveness of HIIT and “hard” resistance exercise. Further, “high-intensity effort” is often featured in statements in the Current Opinion article. Chaabene et al. cite two studies in support of the appeal of HIIT. These studies show similar drop-out rates for obese participants in HIIT vs. continuous aerobic exercise programs. In the first [ 27 ], 9 of the 39 obese children (12–18 years of age) dropped out of the 12 week research study, 4 from the HIIT group and 5 from the continuous aerobic exercise group. In the second study [ 40 ], 4 of 19 obese adolescents dropped out of a 4-week study, 2 from the HIIT group and 2 from the continuous aerobic exercise group. The similar drop-out rates for the HIIT and continuous aerobic exercise groups is provided in support of the notion that HIIT is attractive to children and adolescents. Based on the characteristics of youth (see Table 2 ) and other information presented in this paper, it is my view that HIIT is not likely to be a self-selected choice of most children and adolescents. The adherences rates of 77% and 79% for participation in a research study are not surprising. People, including youth, typically honor their commitment to participate in research studies. However, honoring the commitment to participate in a research study does not necessarily imply that youth prefer HIIT as suggested. The information provided in the section on the appeal of VPA among adults indicates a decrease in positive affect as the intensity of PA increases. In my view, there is reason to believe that the same is true for youth. Youth are concrete thinkers who do not see the abstract value of HIE such as HIIT. It is acknowledged, however, that some youth, especially adolescents, may choose to participate in HIE/HIIT programs to improve their fitness in as part of training for participation on sports teams. Coaches of sports teams are well-aware of the value of HIE/HIIT programs for improving fitness and performance of athletes. The fact that youth comply, does not mean that they find them to be attractive. HIE, such as running laps and HIIT such as “wind sprints,” are frequently used to punish non-compliant athletes. Few youth, that I know of, would say that these activities are fun or appealing. In fact, using exercise as punishment is considered to be an inappropriate practice [ 41 ]. That, of course does not mean that it is not used regularly. In my view, one of the main reasons why physical education and PF activities (especially HIE) are often depicted in a negative way on TV and in the movies is the imposition of HIE on youth who do not have an interest in or ability to perform the exercise successfully. In summary, there is little evidence that most youth find HIE/HIIT to be especially appealing and/or that they would self-select this type of activity over other types of PA. Do youth find sedentary behavior appealing? In their introduction Chaabene et al. indicate that “…sedentary alternatives that have arisen over the past two decades due to technological advancements seem to hold greater appeal for young people than PA [1, p. 2.].” One need only look at the child riding in a shopping cart while playing a video game on a smart phone to see that the child is engaged with the game. Effectance motivation [ 42 ] theory helps explain the interest in electronic (digital, video, computer) games (EG). It posits that people, including children, have a need to be effective in their environment. Left to their own resources, they are intrinsically motivated to master skills of all kinds. Central to the theory is the concept of optimal challenge. If a challenge is too easy it will not be enjoyable or interesting. If the challenge is too hard it will result in loss of intrinsic motivation to continue. Optimal challenge enhances intrinsic interest and makes participation pleasant. EG are typically programmed to allow participants to select the level of difficulty consistent with current ability and to modify it if the challenge is too hard or two easy. Over time, as skill levels improve, the difficulty level is adjusted accordingly. In recent years EG that required PA have been developed and have become popular. Active EG, that take advantage of optimal challenge, were listed among the 10 most popular PA among teens in the U. S [ 36 ]. Social media apps on digital devices, primarily smart phones, are also attractive “sedentary alternatives.” Social media provides the opportunity for youth to express themselves, connect with peers, and to be entertained (e.g., watch videos, share photos). The Pew Research Center [ 43 ] in the U. S. indicates that 95% of American teens own smart phones and 97% access the internet daily. Much of the screen time is dedicated to using social media. The fact that 54% of youth say that it would be hard to give up social media illustrates its appeal. Social media apps use artificial intelligence to captivate young users. As we make efforts to promote PA for youth, we could learn from those who design EG and social media apps. In my opinion, we would do well to consider effectance motivation theory and the idea of optimal challenge when promoting PA among youth. Also, in my view, it is fair to question whether VPA, especially HIE/HIIT, is an optimal challenge for youth known to be sedentary or inactive. Would it, for example, be attractive for the kid in the shopping cart? Changes in Physical Activity with Age Does physical activity decrease with age? Chaabene et al. cite studies to illustrate the “…decrease in PA that seems to take place early in life [ 1 , p. 3].” The decrease in activity with age is consistent with many other reports and provides evidence that many people who are active when they are young do not adhere to active lifestyles in later life (long-term adherence). As reported by Chaabene et al. activity levels drop from childhood to adolescence and continue to decline into adulthood. The drop in activity levels with age is of concern and suggests a need for better strategies to promote lifelong PA adherence. However, the fact that activity levels decline with age is not, in itself, an effective argument for prioritizing VPA, especially HIE/HIIT. Tracking Physical Activity from Youth To Adulthood Tracking is defined as “…the persistence of a behavior or state of being over time [ 44 ].” The multiple studies cited by Chaabene et al. support the fact that PA behavior tracks from childhood to adolescence and into adulthood. The bulk of the research on this topic indicates that PA tracks moderately over the short term, but moderately to low over the long term. For example, a study by Kristensen et al. [ 45 ] was cited. Kristensen, et al. concluded “…that physical activity behavior tends to track moderately from childhood to adolescence (p.171).” Kjønniksen et al. [ 46 ], a cited reference in support of tracking, found high short-term tracking from age 13 to 14 (r =.67) and 13 to 15 (r =.56) with moderate tracking from 13 to 16 (r =.45) and from 13 to 18 (r =.40). However, tracking from 13 to young adulthood (age 23) was low (r =.12). Telema et al. [ 47 ], also cited, found moderate to high tracking of PA (primarily sport club involvement) from childhood to adulthood but noted the unique activity patterns of the Finnish population as a reason for the higher tracking coefficients than in other studies. The majority of studies show that tracking is moderate over the short-term to low in the long-term. Telema et al. [ 47 ] suggested more attention should be paid to the tracking of inactivity. A discussion of tracking both active behaviors and inactive behaviors follows. As indicated previously, PA levels decrease with age. Active behaviors track moderately; children who are active tend to be more active in later life than those who are inactive. Ideally, highly active children would maintain high levels of activity as teens and into adulthood. As both Telema et al. [ 47 ] and Corbin [ 44 ] have indicated, tracking inactivity is equally important, if not more important than activity tracking, because of the high number of inactive people of all ages. The research of Telema [ 47 ] indicated that inactivity tracks at approximately the same levels as activity. The moderate tracking of inactivity also means that some inactive people become even less active in later life. However, inactivity tracking also means that some people who are inactive in their younger years become active as adults. Because they are inactive early in life they have more room for improvement. Corbin [ 44 ] argued that we should be as concerned, or more concerned, with “untracking” PA patterns as with activity tracking. Untracking refers to stopping the downward trend in active behaviors that occurs with age and reversing inactive behaviors too common worldwide. Chaabene et al. indicate that, “Altogether, there is strong evidence that both PA and PF track from youth to adulthood, emphasizing the relevance of adopting an active lifestyle at early ages to increase the likelihood of keeping physically active at later ages. This is of utmost importance for health during youth and adulthood alike [ 1 , p. 4].” While I concur that the evidence is strong that PA tracks, the bulk of the evidence indicates that both active behaviors and inactive behaviors track moderately. I also agree, as stated numerous times, that adopting active lifestyles at early ages is important. The fact that active behaviors track, particularly sports participation, supports the notion that being active while young improves the odds of being active later in life. However, “untracking” the decline in PA with age is also important—we need to find ways to keep active kids from becoming inactive adults. The fact that inactivity tracks also makes it imperative that efforts are made to help those who are inactive to get “untracked” and become active later in life. Consistent with current PA guidelines, I submit that helping people to find activities that they enjoy, both moderate and vigorous, is the key. No evidence is provided by Chaabene et al. to indicate that VPA (other than sports) tracks better than LPA or MPA. MVPA Priorities for Children and Adolescents Both the WHO and American PA guidelines for children and adolescents recommend exposing youth to a variety of physical activities for developing optimal fitness, health, and wellness. The WHO [ 5 ] guidelines recommend that youth have the opportunity “…to participate in physical activities that are enjoyable, offer variety , and are appropriate for their age and ability [ 5 , p.1].” The American PA Guidelines [ 15 ] recommend that youth have the opportunity “…to participate in physical activities that are appropriate for their age, that are enjoyable, and that offer variety [p. 8].” Finally, the American Academy of Pediatrics [ 35 ] in its clinical guidance statement about youth sports indicated that “Variety matters! Over 30% of teens drop out of sports. Schools can keep kids playing longer by offering variety of sports and levels of play [p. 3].” Providing youth with opportunities to participate in a variety of age-appropriate activity can be part of a potential solution for “untracking” the decline in PA participation that occurs with age and to “untracking” inactive behaviors. The Physical Activity Pyramid (see Fig. 3 ) classifies popular physical activities by type. Rather than prioritizing one type of PA, it is my view that exposing youth to multiple forms of PA from the pyramid should be a priority. When selecting activities from the pyramid, multiple factors should be considered including age, sex, heredity, maturation level, skill level, fitness level, body composition status, current interests, and previous experience. Chaabene et al. acknowledge that factors such as inadequate skill and size disadvantage can result in negative early PA experiences. For youth who exhibit excessive sedentary behavior (see bottom of Pyramid) and youth who do some PA but who are deemed to be inactive because they do not meet PA guidelines, a step by step approach can be effective. WHO indicates that getting sedentary kids moving is a good start including LPA. As stages of change theory [ 49 ] suggests, behaviors often are adopted slowly and in stages. Left to their own devices it is unlikely that sedentary youth or inactive youth will self-select VPA, especially HIE/HIIT. MPA (step 1 of the pyramid) such as purposeful walking or biking to school are examples of a good options. Fun family activities, outdoor intermittent free play, and/or enjoyable physical education activities that include some MPA and some VPA are other options. Youth who meet the MVPA standard, but not the VPA, MSPA, or BSPA standards, can be encouraged to select from the variety of activities included in Steps 2 to 5 of the pyramid and to balance energy intake with energy expenditure as illustrated at the top of the pyramid. Home, community, and school activities are central to helping youth meet guidelines and avoid sedentary behavior. In schools the use of evidence-based activity management techniques by teachers [ 50 ] can help move youth toward meeting PA standards. Comprehensive school PA programs that provide PA throughout the school day, including before and after school PA opportunities, during school quality physical education programs, and during school classroom exercise breaks, are also effective [ 51 ]. Adolescents develop abstract thinking abilities that allowing them to make informed decisions including learning concepts and principles of PA as well as self-management skills such as self-assessment, goal setting, program planning, self-monitoring [ 52 ]. Evidence-based conceptual physical education programs that teach decision making and allow teens to evaluate different types of PA (including HIE and HIIT) for inclusion in their personal program plan have proven effective in promoting PA into adulthood [ 52 ]. Evidence-based sport education programs [ 53 ] have been shown to be effective in promoting long lasting participation in sports for all ability levels. It is also vitally important to incorporate PA plans such as the WHO Global Action Plan on Physical Activity [ 54 ] to encourage PA behaviors by providing multiple opportunities, creating active environments, and encouraging active transportation. Summary As noted at the outset, Current Opinion articles provide the opportunity for healthy dialogue regarding topics of importance. Chaabene et al. started the discussion. They cite multiple studies, reviews, and meta-analyses to support their position that VPA, including HIE/HIIT, provide fitness and health benefits for youth that exceed those provided by LPA and MPA and that these VPA benefits can be achieved in less time than for LPA and MPA. Both are positions with which I agree. In addition to the fitness and health and time saving value of VPA, Chaabene et al. argue for prioritizing VPA based on the notion that youth find VPA, including HIE/HIIT, more attractive than LPA and MPA. Chaabene et al. and I agree that youth find some types of VPA, especially sport and vigorous leisure activities, to be appealing. Our ideas diverge, however, on several related points. Chaabene et al. state that “…youth prefer to engage in short, intermittent high-intensity efforts rather than low-intensity ones [ 1 , p. 1]. We agree that the patterns of youth in self-selected PA are intermittent in nature. However, I do not find support in the literature for the view of Chaabene et al. that the intermittent nature of PA patterns of youth indicates that they prefer high-intensity efforts . I, contend that the literature supports an interest in all types of PA including LPA, MPA, and VPA. Chaabene et al. also support the idea that youth find high-intensity PA to be especially appealing compared to LPA and MPA. The information in support of the position is not convincing. I contend, as noted previously, that most youth will not self-select HIE/HIIT and that it is not enjoyable except for those who see its value for reaching a goal such as being selected for a sports team. In fact, Chaabene et al., acknowledge the fact that many youth find “sedentary alternative” to be more attractive than PA [ 1 , p.1]. I suggest that we learn from those who program EG and find ways to make PA optimally challenging, fun and enjoyable. Chaabene et al. present evidence to indicate that the rates of adherence to PA guidelines decreases with age. They also cite “tracking” studies that show evidence that youth who are active when they are young are likely to be more active later in life. Both statements are well-documented and provide useful information. At issue is whether the cited facts provide evidence of the position that VPA should be prioritized. I don’t think so. I make the case that “untracking” is an important consideration. For example, I believe that untracking the downward trend in PA as youth grow older should be a priority. I also believe that untracking the low adherence rate among youth is a priority. PA promotional efforts that help youth who are active to stay active and youth who are inactive to become active should be prioritized. I do not question the statement of Chaabene et al. that “… promoting vigorous PA can help mitigate the global issue of low adherence to PA in youth;” however, I do question the notion that VPA, especially HIE/HITT is the most effective solution to untracking sedentary or inactive youth. There is much with which I find agreement with Chaabene et al. I am in favor of encouraging VPA among youth to achieve its many benefits. However, VPA is already prioritized in existing guidelines. I have concerns that “prioritizing” VPA will be interpreted as “doing VPA to youth” rather than helping youth find personal PA patterns that are best suited to their personal needs, interests, and abilities. I am especially concerned about emphasizing HIE/HIIT. As noted earlier, forms of HIE (e.g., running laps, multiple reps of high-intensity calisthenics) and HIIT (e.g., wind sprints) are often used as punishment especially among athletes—a practice that is considered to be inappropriate [ 41 ]. I do not suggest that Chaabene et al. favor such practices. However, I do want to discourage these practices. After reviewing the information presented by Chaabene et al., I have no doubt that they only want what is best for youth, as I do. However, we differ on approaches. I recommend that we prioritize any PA that youth will actually do. The key, I contend, is finding the best type of PA for each person. For some it will be VPA, for others it will be LPA or MPA. Much depends on the starting point. Sedentary and inactive youth will benefit even from LPA. As the current WHO guidelines suggest, “doing some physical activity is better than doing none [ 5 , p. 1]” and “Children and adolescents should start by doing small amounts of physical activity and gradually increase the frequency intensity and duration over time [ 5 , p. 1].” Considerations for Future Guidelines In the spirit of making suggestions that may be of value to those who develop the next generation of PA guidelines for youth, I offer the following. Uniformity Among Guidelines As noted in this paper, different definitions of what constitutes recommended amounts of PA can result in great variations in estimates of guideline adherence. For example, the American guidelines recommend 60 min of MVPA daily, however, the WHO guidelines recommend an average of 60 min per day across the week. Uniformity in recommendations and wording would be helpful, especially for surveys designed to assess PA prevalence worldwide. Realistic Guidelines The first American physical activity guidelines for children [ 12 ] were unique in that they were for children (5–12} and not for both children and adolescents. They were created in response to previous guidelines that were based on adult standards that recommended long periods of continuous PA as opposed to intermittent PA. The first guidelines recommended 60 min of MVPAon all or most days of the week. The current American guidelines required 60 min of MVPA on ALL days of the week. In retrospect, I believe that requiring 60 min of MVPA on ALL days of the week is too restrictive. Some religions, for example, limit activity on holy days. Youth involved in vigorous sporting activities are often urged to take a rest day during the week. In either case, youth are considered to be “inactive” if they take a day off. As noted earlier, rates of adherence to guidelines are much higher when four or five days per week are used as indicators. Using the 2020 WHO standard of an average of 60 min per day across the week seems fairer and may help resolve assessment issues (see next section). Standardized methods of assessing adherence to guidelines Numerous papers and books have been written about the challenges of assessing the PA levels of youth including several cited by Chaabene et al. [ 23 , 30 , 33 ]. Different methods of assessment yield different results. Since surveys are used as the primary method of PA assessment of adherence to guidelines, including a section on methods of survey assessment in future guidelines documents is recommended. As noted above, it is my contention that the seven day a week standard results in overestimates of youth inactivity. I recommend using a different method of assessment. If, for example, the 2020 WHO standard (average of 60 min daily spread across the week) is adopted as a uniform standard, the following question could be asked to determine if the overall MVPA guidelines is met. Beginning with yesterday , record the number of minutes of moderate to vigorous physical activity (equal to or greater than brisk walking) that you performed over the last seven days. The minutes per day could be summed and divided by seven to determine average minutes per day. The current standard requires 420 min of PA per week. Adherence would be indicated if youth met the average daily standard is met. Yet to be resolved is how many days must youth perform PA to meet the “across the week” standard. I also ask the question, Is 420 min per week the right number? 300 min per week is double the number recommended for adults. Would 300 or a number in between 300 and 420 be worthy of consideration? If the expert panel writing future guidelines use the average daily PA standard, they may also want to consider simplifying it by using total minutes of MVPA per week (300 to 420). This would make the guideline for youth similar, in format, to adult guidelines. A disadvantage is that 300 to 420 min per week may be more intimidating than an average of 60 min per day across the week. For Aerobic VPA, MSPA, and BSPA a similar technique could be used. The current American guidelines indicate that each of the three types of PA are “part of” the 60 min per day of MVPA. If this standard is used, the following questions might be considered. Beginning with yesterday , record the number of minutes of vigorous aerobic physical activity (that made your heart beat faster and made you breath harder such as running , or playing a sport) that you performed over the last seven days. Beginning with yesterday , record the number of minutes of muscle strengthening physical activity (such as climbing , body weight calisthenics , resistance training) that you performed over the last seven days. Beginning with yesterday , record the number of minutes of bone strengthening physical activity (such as running , jumping rope , playing a sport that involves jumping or striking) that you performed over the last seven days. If questions like those listed above are used, it would be possible to determine on how many days youth perform each type of VPA and the amount (in minutes). The minutes per day would provide a more precises estimate of aerobic VPA, MSPA, and BSPA than current survey questions. It should be noted that the WHO guidelines do not list each of the three types of VPA separately. Rather, aerobic VPA, MSPA, and BSPA are listed collectively with the indication that they should be performed at least three days a week. If data from different countries are to be compared, a uniform standard and method of assessment would be useful. Note Issues involving overlap among questions about MVPA are acknowledged. For example, many of the activities that build muscle also build bones, activities such as running are included as aerobic VPA but also have muscle and bone strengthening benefits. Creative ideas for resolving this issue are encouraged. Guidelines for helping youth at all stages of the physical activity continuum Various PA documents already acknowledge the need for different guidelines for different subgroups (e.g., youth, adults, older adults, people with disabilities and other conditions). Given the varied levels of PA involvement of youth, additional guidelines for youth who are primarily sedentary, and youth deemed to be inactive because they do not meet current standards for MVPA, seem warranted. As indicated earlier, current guidelines acknowledge that some PA is better than none and that inactive youth start with”… small amounts increasing frequency, intensity, and duration gradually [ 5 , p. 1].” However, providing additional guidance seems warranted. Separate guidelines for children (5–12) and adolescents (13–18) As noted in Table 1 , children are concrete thinkers who exhibit intermittent patterns of PA. Adolescents become more capable of abstract thinking which is necessary to see the value of doing VPA when presented with evidence of the value of participation. Further, adolescents have the advantage of more advanced physical development that allows them to see greater physical benefits as a result of participation in VPA. Given the differences among children and adolescents, separate guidelines may be worthy of consideration. Providing more detail for aerobic VPA, MSPA, and BSPA for adolescents also seems worthy of consideration. Final Words When I began my studies in physical education in the 1950s, my professors regaled us with stories of the free play activities of their youth before the advent of mechanized society. Now, almost 70 years later I frequently hear the same laments from colleagues much younger than me. I have no doubt that many of today’s youth still ride their bikes, walk to school, and engage in unstructured play on a regular basis depending on where they live and what other options are available. I also have no doubt that many youth, as reported by Chaabene et al. are involved in structured sports. Children and adolescents who dream of becoming the next Lionel Messi or the next Serena Williams self-select sporting activities. Others may choose sports to please parents who see their kids as the next Olympic champion or professional athlete. Regardless of the reasons, many youth are involved in sports. This raises the question: “How can so many youth be involved in sports and yet so few youth are judged as physically active?” The default method of determining inactivity is 60 min of MVPA every day. Are youth inactive, however, if they fail to be active everyday but perform VPA (primarily in sports) three days a week? Current survey techniques do not account for the amount of time spent in VPA, MSPA, and BSPA, making their contribution total PA time difficult to determine. In societies where structured sports are prevalent among youth, the normal self-selected free play patterns give way to the structured activities in which youth participate. In these cases, activity may not be daily but certainly do not make participants inactive. It is possible for youth to meet current standards for VPA, MSPA, and BSPA standards and be judged inactive. It is also possible for youth to be judged to be active by the MVPA standard but fail to meet the aerobic VPA, MSPA, and BSPA standards. Children and adolescents may engage in too much sedentary behavior and be active or inactive, depending on the standard used. Many different combinations are possible. In a perfect world, I have no doubt that we would all want youth to meet all four activity standards (MVPA, VPA, MSPA, BSPA) and to engage in limited sedentary behavior. However, as noted in this paper, as we put more stipulations on what constitute PA for youth, the proportion who are judged to be active decreases. I prioritize any form of PA that gets kids moving and that adheres to the HELP philosophy— H ealth and health-related fitness for E veryone, for a L ifetime, in a very P ersonal way (including finding pleasure and joy in PA) [ 48 ].
