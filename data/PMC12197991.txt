Background Adolescent mental health is influenced by family history. Experiences of trauma also convey substantial risk for mental health challenges. Mediation of the association of family history with adolescent mental health by trauma experiences could be actionable and warrants evaluation. We sought to interrogate the mediating role of trauma in the association of family history of psychiatric disorders (FH) with adolescent general psychopathology, accounting for shared environment and genetics. Methods The Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort was a cross-sectional study of participants ages 8 to 21 years with English fluency and in good medical health with characterization from November 2009 to December 2011. The analysis reported here was completed from March 2023 to February 2025. Among 7840 participants, we tested associations of first-degree FH (category count [0–4]: psychosis, mood, suicide attempt, substance use), youth exposure to trauma, neighborhood environment (block-level geocoded socioeconomic indices), and genomic factor of polygenic scores for psychopathologies (depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, schizophrenia, bipolar, cross-disorder) with adolescent general psychopathology modeled as p-factor. Association of FH with general psychopathology was assessed with structural equation modeling, querying for an indirect pathway via trauma, with stepwise accounting of genomics and shared environment, controlling for age and sex. Results Of 7840 participants, 31% had FH and 44% of youths reported trauma exposure. Trauma had substantial direct association with general psychopathology and consistently mediated more than 20% of variance from FH to psychopathology, accounting for neighborhood and genomic predisposition. Conclusions Trauma exposures mediate a substantial portion of association between FH and adolescent psychopathology, an opportunity for transgenerational intervention. Plain Language Summary Two of the strongest influences on adolescent mental health are experiences of trauma and psychiatric family history. Family history may influence mental health in multiple ways—including genetics and shared environment. This study sought to inform how much and in what ways these influences connect. Our results suggest that experiences of trauma are a substantial piece of the relationship between family history and adolescent mental health for some people, even accounting for genetics and shared environment. Trauma could be an important point of intervention to disrupt transmission of mental health challenges across generations. Contributions of FH to youth mental health burden include both genetic and environmental pathways. Genetic contributions of FH have been established and quantified in multiple family and twin heritability studies ( 3 , 4 , 5 ). Nongenetic contributions of FH may include factors that predispose youth with FH to cumulative adverse environmental exposures. Understanding how environmental pathways contribute to the association of FH with youth mental health can have clinical and policy implications, given that environmental risk, as opposed to genetic association, may be modifiable and present actionable points of intervention ( 6 , 7 ). However, it is difficult to disentangle genetic from environmental components in the association of FH with youth psychopathology, and research is warranted on nongenetic components underpinning the risk of FH in genetically informed cohorts. Moreover, the generalized approach is important to illustrate broad relevance across care scenarios including FH domains captured as well as youth psychopathology manifestation. One potentially modifiable environmental risk factor for youth mental health is early-life exposure to traumatic stressful experiences (trauma). Many studies have reported associations of trauma exposure with psychiatric illness, including in adolescents ( 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ). Moreover, caregiver ill mental health has been shown to increase the risk of child trauma exposures ( 13 ). It is also critical to consider the complex network structure of the environment (exposome), whereby individual-level adversity often co-occurs with structural stressors ( 14 ). For example, the likelihood of trauma exposure may be influenced by neighborhood environment such as through frequency of violent crime ( 15 ). We have described the deleterious dose-response association of trauma with adolescent psychopathology in the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC), a sample of genotyped youths with geocoded block-level neighborhood data ( 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ). The PNC included a standardized assessment of FH, which has been linked to increased psychopathology risk in the study participants ( 22 , 23 ). Herein, we aimed to interrogate pathways from FH to adolescent general psychopathology associated with exposure to trauma ( 16 , 17 ), considering neighborhood environment ( 19 ) and genetic susceptibility, quantified by polygenic scores (PGSs) of psychiatric disorders ( 24 , 25 , 26 ). We hypothesized that experiences of trauma would mediate the pathway between FH and general psychopathology in adolescents, even when accounting for neighborhood environment and genetic vulnerability. Methods and Materials Study Participants The PNC is a community cohort of youths ages 8 to 21 years, recruited through the nonpsychiatric pediatric network of the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) ( 20 , 21 , 27 ). Study procedures for the PNC were approved by the institutional review boards of CHOP and University of Pennsylvania. All participants, parents, or guardians provided an informed consent, and minors provided assent. Briefly, mental health phenotyping was completed with a computerized structured interview based on the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia including a module that assessed traumatic experiences that qualify as criterion A exposures for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) ( 21 , 28 ). FH was assessed using the Family Interview for Genetic Studies administered to collateral informants (of participants ages 8–17) and youths (ages 18–21) ( 21 , 22 , 23 , 29 ). Demographic characteristics included were age, sex, and race. Age was included as years at the time of clinical assessment. Sex assigned at birth was reported by either the adult proband (ages 18–21) or collateral informant (of youths ages 8–17). Race and ethnicity are reported as proband or collateral informant–reported participant race (American Indian/Alaska native, Asian, Black, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, more than 1 race, White) and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic). Genotyping data were generated for all PNC participants using the Illumina Infinium or Affymetrix Axiom genome-wide association study (GWAS) platforms ( 20 , 30 ). Measures Family History FH was quantified as the count of psychiatric disorder categories (range 0–4) endorsed among any first-degree relatives (psychosis, mood [depression or bipolar], suicide attempt, substance use) ( 21 , 22 , 23 , 29 ). History of multiple relatives within the same disorder category was counted only by category. Trauma History of trauma was assessed with self-report (participants ages 11 and older) or parent report (participants ages 8–10) of a lifetime (ever) exposure to 8 types of traumatic experiences: 1) attacked by somebody or badly beaten; 2) forced to do something sexual; 3) threatened with a weapon; 4) a natural disaster; 5) thought they or someone close to them was going to be killed or hurt badly; 6) been in bad accident; 7) seen or heard somebody get killed, hurt badly, or die; and 8) been upset by seeing a dead body or seeing pictures of the dead body of somebody they knew well. Consistent with previous work, we created an ordinal count of trauma burden categories of 0, 1, 2, and 3 or more ( 17 ). Neighborhood Environment Neighborhood environment was quantified using dimensionality reduction of block group-level characteristics (e.g., indicators of poverty, employment, as well as population density and vacant lots) of participants’ geocoded home address with positive values reflecting neighborhood assets (see Supplemental Methods 1 ) ( 19 ). Biserial correlation between neighborhood and racially minoritized group comprising American Indian/Alaska native, Asian, Black, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, and more than 1 race was queried consistent with documented patterns of residential segregation in Philadelphia and to address the potential for structural differences in lived exposures ( 31 , 32 ). Latent Genetic Factor PGSs were calculated using GWAS summary statistics as previously described ( 33 , 34 ). Participants were identified as genetically similar to European (EUR) and African (AFR) groups by projection to the 1000 Genomes principal component (PC) space. Bayesian posterior effects were inferred under continuous shrinkage using PRS-CS (see Supplemental Methods 2 ) ( 35 , 36 , 37 ). PGSs were calculated for PTSD, schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and cross-disorder psychopathology for the EUR genetically similar group and PTSD, schizophrenia, and major depressive disorder for the AFR genetically similar group ( 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 ) ( Table S1 ). Each PGS was adjusted for the first 10 within-genetically-similar group PCs. To address genetic overlap within psychopathology ( 43 , 44 ), along with general psychopathology as the outcome variable, we constructed a single latent factor representing composite genetic risk composed of PGSs for PTSD, schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and cross-disorder psychopathology. Primary Outcome: General Psychopathology We calculated a general psychopathology score (p-factor) representing the overall symptom burden ( 8 ). p-Factor was constructed from bifactor analysis of item-wise symptom endorsement from the clinical assessment, as previously described ( 45 ) ( Table S2 ). Statistical Approach Beyond descriptive statistics and correlations to establish variable relationships, 3 structural equation models (SEMs) were used, as our primary multivariable analysis was based on a conceptual model ( Figure 1 ; for extended methods, see Supplemental Methods 3 ). All models controlled for age and sex; model fit was evaluated by both global fit statistics (comparative fit index &gt; 0.95, root mean square error of approximation &lt; 0.06, standardized root mean square residual &lt; 0.08) ( 46 ) and local standardized residuals. First, the base model (FH and trauma as key independent variables and general psychopathology as the dependent variable) was tested to determine whether FH’s relationship with trauma and p-factor was best described by full or partial mediation, applying listwise deletion and using bootstrapping for confidence intervals. The partial mediation approach was selected based on χ 2 and global fit statistics ( 47 , 48 ). Subsequent SEMs were estimated with robust maximum likelihood to protect from violations of multivariate normality and visualized with standardized path diagrams. Next, to account for environmental influences on trauma that are external to FH, neighborhood environment was added as an independent exogenous variable associated with trauma. Consistent with literature and in prioritization of parsimonious models, a direct relationship between neighborhood and p-factor was not included ( 19 ). Finally, to account for genetic influences, a single latent factor representing composite genetic risk composed of PGSs for PTSD, schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and cross-disorder psychopathology was included with direct effects on p-factor in EUR-like participants ( 44 ). Latent factor validity was assessed by composite reliability coefficient as an internal consistency measure. Univariate regressions of general psychopathology related to each PGS (cross-disorder, bipolar, major depressive disorder, PTSD, and schizophrenia) accounting for age and sex were also completed. For PGS available in both EUR and AFR genetically similar groups, independent effects of PGS were assessed simultaneously (not the latent PGS as in main analyses). Analysis was completed from March 2023 through February 2025 in R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation). Sensitivity Analyses Our main analyses used a broad construct of FH (count of psychiatric disorder categories). Thus, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to address the specificity of types of FH. We ran a series of SEMs with a single type of FH (psychosis, mood, suicide, substance use) as the independent variable, maintaining general psychopathology as the dependent variable, trauma as the mediator, and covariates (age, sex) as in main analyses. Results Prevalence of FH and Trauma in the PNC The study population comprised 7840 participants with complete information on FH, trauma exposure, and general psychopathology (see Table 1 ). Participants were between 8 and 21 years of age (median = 14.4 years), and 3778 (48.2%) were female. The most frequent race groups reported were Black ( n = 2694; 34.4%) and White ( n = 4686; 59.8%); 241 (3.1%) of the cohort reported Hispanic ethnicity. Almost 30% of participants were reported to have FH (number of categories, 1 = 22.3%; 2 = 6.7%; 3 = 1.7%; all 4 = 0.2%). A total of 44.4% of the cohort reported exposure to at least one type of traumatic event. Notably, exposure to trauma was more prevalent among youths with FH (52% reported at least one trauma exposure) than youths without FH (41% experienced trauma) (see Table 1 and Table S3 ). Correlations Among Study Variables FH correlated with reported trauma exposure ( r = 0.19, p &lt; .001) (see Figure S1 ) and general psychopathology ( r = 0.26, p &lt; .001). Reported trauma exposure correlated with general psychopathology ( r = 0.51, p &lt; .001). Trauma and psychopathology were associated with age ( r = 0.33, p &lt; .001; r = 0.26, p &lt; .001). Additional pairwise correlations were nonsignificant; all pairwise correlations are presented in Figure S1 . Demographic descriptors (age, sex) were retained for further statistical analyses. Neighborhood environment was noted to have substantial correlation with racially minoritized grouping ( r = −0.96, p &lt; .001); thus, only neighborhood was included in analytical models. Testing the Mediating Role of Trauma in the Path From FH to Adolescent Psychopathology Consistent with our hypothesis, exposure to trauma significantly mediated the path from FH to general psychopathology (partial mediation) ( Table 2 ; Table S4 and Figure S2 ). The estimated indirect effect of FH on general psychopathology via trauma was substantial with approximately 24% of FH total effect on p-factor accounted for by indirect effects via trauma exposure (total effect, β = 0.20, p &lt; .001; indirect effect, β = 0.04, p &lt; .001). To address specificity of this mediation effect, we tested the potential influence of neighborhood environment, which was associated with trauma exposure (β = −0.21, p &lt; .001). Inclusion of neighborhood environment in the model did not change the mediating effect of trauma on the pathway from FH to general psychopathology (total effect, β = 0.19, p &lt; .001; indirect effect, β = 0.04, p &lt; .001) ( Table 2 and Figure S3 ). Genetically Informed Analyses Finally, recognizing the potential role of genetic predisposition within family history, we estimated models that accounted for pleiotropic genomic predisposition as quantified by PGSs of psychiatric disorders. We used a single latent genetic factor that was constructed from multiple PGSs after simplified regression analyses ( Tables S5 and S6 ; Figure S6 ). Genetically informed analyses revealed that while the direct effect of a latent genetic factor on general psychopathology was significant (β = 0.03, p = .04), the total effect of FH on general psychopathology was unchanged (β = 0.19, p &lt; .001), with trauma maintaining a significant mediating effect ( Table 2 and Figure 2 ). A model including covariance between family history and PGSs did not change the effects observed ( Figure S4 ). Of note, this analysis was completed in the cohort subset genetically similar to a EUR referent population to align with GWAS source literature (see Supplemental Methods 2 ); the indirect effect of FH on psychopathology via trauma was consistent in this subset (25% variance accounted for) ( Figure S5 ). To reflect the genetic diversity present within the overall cohort and improve generalizability, a separate analysis was completed using the PGSs available within both EUR-like and AFR-like subsets ( Table S7 ). In this model, the PGSs for PTSD, schizophrenia, and major depressive disorder had less robust direct associations with psychopathology than the latent genetic factor in the previous analysis. Nevertheless, this analysis demonstrated consistent support for mediation by trauma experiences of FH on general psychopathology that was not accounted for by the shared environment ( Figure S7 ). Sensitivity Analysis Alternate conceptualizations of FH were explored. A binary definition of FH of each of the 4 disorders (psychosis, mood, suicide attempt, substance use)—instead of category count (0–4) as in main analyses—demonstrated significant but reduced direct effects of any single FH component on general psychopathology compared with the FH category count variable as used in the primary analysis. For family history of each psychiatric disorder, the estimated indirect effects via trauma ranged from 23% (mood disorder FH) to 31% (psychosis FH) ( Table S8 ). Discussion FH and trauma are both associated with adolescent mental health challenges. We report here that in a large cohort of American adolescents, trauma is more common among youths with family history of psychiatric disorders and that more than 20% of the risk association between FH and adolescent general psychopathology is mediated through trauma exposure. Furthermore, our analyses suggest that the mediating effect of trauma at the individual level is independent of polygenic predisposition and proxies of adverse exposures such as neighborhood environment. This work adds to the evidence on nongenetic pathways from FH to psychopathology ( 9 , 10 , 11 , 49 , 50 ), highlighting the relevance of trauma as a modifiable risk factor in youth with FH. Our results align with the growing recognition of the need to not only mitigate youth exposure to trauma ( 51 ) but also point to the potential benefit of routinely evaluating trauma exposure in youth, particularly those with a family history of psychopathology. Notably, this can provide a clinically actionable point of intervention through early engagement of youth with trauma-informed care ( 52 ). Mental illness is a complex outcome of multiple contributors including genetic and environmental factors ( 53 , 54 ). Our findings suggest that exposure to trauma mediates a substantial path from FH to general psychopathology. This suggests that, in addition to its genetic risk transmission, FH should be considered as part of the exposome ( 14 ). Notably, our findings resonate with the seminal clinical research on adverse childhood experiences, where “living with a household member who was depressed or mentally ill” or who attempted suicide is considered a traumatic environmental exposure ( 55 ). Specifically among parents, mental health conditions can contribute to child mental health risk (i.e., intergenerational transmission) through multiple mechanisms, over and above genetic risk ( 9 , 10 , 11 , 49 , 50 ). Parents with psychiatric conditions may be more likely to reside in underprivileged neighborhoods ( 56 ), contributing to youth mental health risk through structural stressors. Furthermore, psychiatric conditions of parents may also be associated with lower parental monitoring, which, in turn, can increase the likelihood of youth exposure to adversity ( 57 , 58 ). These results extend the extant literature from PNC that has demonstrated association of traumas with psychopathology and neurocognition and specific components of FH with specific domains ( 16 , 17 , 22 , 23 ). This analysis connects these lines of work by combining previously demonstrated associations with a novel framework to emphasize the commonality within FH and trauma-associated predispositions. Our finding that neighborhood disadvantage is associated with more trauma exposure is consistent with previous literature ( 59 , 60 ). It is notable that our population had a strong correlation between neighborhood and racially minoritized grouping, which could be consistent with patterns of residential segregation that have been documented in Philadelphia ( 31 ). Our work adds to the recognition that, even in adolescents, neighborhood disadvantage may portend stress that associates with individual mental health, beyond the influence of family factors ( 61 , 62 , 63 ). The descriptors embedded within our neighborhood measure included area financial indicators, such as poverty and employment rate, and built factors, including population density and vacant lots ( 19 ). Promising interventions suggest that improving built factors, such as vacant lot conversion to greenspace, may serve as meaningful targets for intervention ( 64 ). Investigation of direct relationships between lived environment and adolescent psychopathology is an important future direction for research. Our study highlights at least 2 next steps for translation into clinical practice. First, our results suggest that behavioral health care for adults should integrate patient roles as caregivers and consider direct interventions to bolster protective factors aiming to reduce intergenerational transmission of psychopathology. In particular, identifying and supporting caregivers in the prevention of trauma experiences for their children could disrupt a substantial pathway for risk. Second, although typical medical documentation of family history has been shown to reflect research-derived heritability estimates, variable family history documentation in electronic medical records affirms the need for pragmatic, clinically actionable guidance regarding the use of family history in typical care ( 65 , 66 , 67 ). Our results suggest that simplified reports of psychiatric family history, even without accounting for family size, number of members affected, or a full spectrum of psychiatric conditions, are relevant to youth mental health and should be considered in health care. Our study also has several limitations. First, regarding the reported trauma exposures, salience of trauma exposures may be intrinsically related to general psychopathology. Recollection of perceived trauma has been shown as a primary driver for some long-term effects of exposures, independent of objective documentation of such events ( 68 ). Our measures also do not account for recency, duration, recurrence, or timing as related to the onset of FH of trauma exposures. Second, with regard to genetic predisposition, the latent genetic factor may have limited composite reliability, reflecting potential for low internal consistency in the measure of this genetic construct ( 69 ). Although the latent genetic factor was significantly associated with general psychopathology, characterization of a single genetic factor warrants continued refinement and may be underestimated in the current approach, in particular given the application of adult-derived PGS to adolescent populations. Relatedly, experiences of trauma may, in and of themselves, have some component of heritability as has been previously described ( 70 , 71 , 72 ). Third, despite favorable global model fit, some variable relationships are under- (e.g., trauma and general psychopathology) or overrepresented (e.g., sex and general psychopathology) by our models in the absence of the genetic factor, evaluated by local fit estimation of standardized residuals, supporting the need to better understand the interplay between risk factors for general psychopathology ( Table S9 ). Relatedly, the use of general psychopathology as our primary outcome may fail to reflect domain-specific susceptibilities. Moreover, there is substantial debate about what the p-factor is and how best to model it ( 73 , 74 , 75 ). For example, most mechanistic theories of p are weak and unfalsifiable, and there have been unfair comparisons of bifactor and second-order configurations, where the bifactor always fits better due to its lower parsimony. Nonetheless, the persistent correlation among psychopathology symptoms in a positive manifold provides compelling evidence of its relevance. Furthermore, a bifactor configuration addresses unrealistic constraints of the second-order model ( 76 ). Fourth, although the PNC has a strength in its original design to demographically reflect the Philadelphia region, this study represents a subset that may not be generalizable. Also, analyses completed were inconsistent across genetic similarity groups to reconcile with extant literature with fewer PGSs available for AFR-like than EUR-like. Alternative approaches to PGS generation such as PRS-CSx may be considered in future analysis ( 77 ). Continued expansion and replication of results in different settings and across genetic similarity groups is required to confirm generalizability and advance health equity. This includes adequate attention to environmental exposures across groups, such as neighborhood, to support actionable and generalizable interventions in the future ( 32 ). Conclusions This work demonstrates the importance of reported trauma exposures as a substantial mediator in the pathway between FH of mental health disorders and adolescent general psychopathology, even after accounting for neighborhood environment and genetic predisposition. Prevention through risk counseling in adult caregivers with mental health disorders and identification and intervention in adolescents with FH warrants continued attention as points of clinical actionability.
