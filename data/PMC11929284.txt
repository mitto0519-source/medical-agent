Abstract Background Built environment attributes can facilitate or represent barriers to active commuting to and from school (ACS). Even though parental perceptions of the built environment and their influence on ACS in children have been extensively studied, there is very limited evidence as to whether these perceptions can vary based on the sex of children. Hence, the present study aimed to analyse the relationships between parental neighbourhood perceptions and ACS, differentiating between boys and girls. Methods This cross-sectional study was conducted in Valencia, Spain. The sample comprised 808 children and one of their parents. Parent-perceived neighbourhood environment, ACS, and socio-demographic data were self-reported through a questionnaire using validated scales. Self-organising map (SOM) analysis was used to describe the interrelationships between parental perceptions and child profiles. These analyses were carried out separately in boys and girls. Results Parents of daughters perceived their neighbourhoods as less safe from crime compared to parents of sons. The number of ACS trips was similar between boys and girls. The clustering of the SOM outcomes resulted in seven clusters, both in boys and girls. However, cluster membership had significant effects on ACS in boys, but not in girls. Globally, in the group of boys, the highest ACS levels were positively correlated with the most supportive built environments in terms of residential density, land use mix, recreational facilities, accessibility and walking facilities, traffic safety, pedestrian infrastructure, safety from crime, and aesthetics. However, some of these factors had low scores in clusters with high ACS levels (e.g., low levels of accessibility and walking facilities, and land use mix). Conclusions These findings support the idea that ACS behaviour depends on the interaction of diverse built environment factors. Our results also suggest that parental perceptions had different effects according to the sex of the child, with ACS in boys being more influenced by these perceptions. In the case of girls, it is possible that ACS interventions should emphasise generating child-friendly social environments in addition to strategies to improve the built environment. Future interventions to encourage ACS should consider these sex-specific differences and address parental concerns through improved infrastructure and safety measures. Background Engaging in regular physical activity generates multiple benefits in children, including better physical fitness, cognition and academic achievement, and a reduced risk of diseases such as obesity [ 1 , 2 ]. Unfortunately, the prevalence of insufficient physical activity among children and adolescents is around 80% worldwide [ 3 , 4 ]. According to a recent meta-analysis [ 5 ], active commuting to and from school (ACS) represents an opportunity to increase physical activity levels in young people by contributing, on weekdays, to almost half of the current physical activity recommendations (i.e., 60 minutes of moderate–vigorous physical activity). ACS can also have co-benefits beyond increasing physical activity, contributing to the reduction of healthcare costs or air pollutant emissions resulting from lower dependence on motorised transport [ 6 ]. According to the academic literature [ 7 , 8 ], built environment attributes can facilitate or represent barriers to ACS. Furthermore, parental perceptions of the built environment play a crucial role in determining if children engage in ACS [ 9 – 13 ]. A systematic review carried out in children [ 10 ], found that the main environmental barriers reported by parents were distance to school, traffic, crime-related safety, and neighbourhood walkability attributes. Distance from home to school was the most critical factor in children according to studies other than those included in the systematic review [ 7 , 14 – 16 ]. Parents were less likely to encourage ACS if they perceived the school as being too far, even if the actual distance was within a feasible range for walking or cycling [ 14 , 17 – 19 ]. Higher neighbourhood walkability and good infrastructure for ACS (e.g., adequate intersection density and the presence of pavements, bike lanes, and pedestrian-friendly routes) positively influenced parental perceptions and increased the likelihood of ACS [ 9 , 11 , 12 , 18 , 20 – 22 ]. In addition, perceived traffic safety and safety from crime positively impacted parental decisions about ACS [ 9 ]. Parents often indicated traffic safety, including concerns about road accidents and the presence of highways or roads with a high volume of traffic as a significant barrier to allowing their children to walk or cycle to school [ 10 , 14 , 19 – 21 ]. Concerns about crime in the neighbourhood and the presence of sex offenders also negatively impacted parental decisions about ACS [ 10 , 21 – 23 ]. One of the limitations of studies analysing parental perceptions and ACS in children was related to the low quality of the instruments used to assess neighbourhood attributes [ 10 ]; another was the wide variety of instruments used, which prevents comparison between the results from different studies [ 24 , 25 ]. Despite the fact that parental perceptions of the built environment and their influence on ACS in children have been extensively studied, there is very limited evidence on whether these perceptions vary based on the sex of the child considered. Differences in the perceptions of the environment according to sex may differentially affect the likelihood of a child engaging in ACS. In this sense, in a study of Canadian children [ 26 ], it was found that parental perceptions of traffic safety were negatively correlated with heavy traffic near school only for boys and, while for girls this negative impact was only seen for major street crossings on the route to school. However, both these parental perceptions were only related to ACS in boys, while ACS in girls was unaffected. In another study among Canadian children [ 27 ], boys were more likely to walk to school if parents perceived driving as setting a bad example, while ACS in girls was less affected by parental attitudes and perceptions of the built environment. Hence, this limited evidence prevents us from drawing definitive conclusions on whether parental perceptions influence ACS behaviour differently according to the sex of the child. Therefore, more research on this topic is still required. Thus, the associations between parental neighbourhood perceptions and ACS among male and female children must be separately evaluated in depth by, for example, creating self-organising maps (SOMs). Following a person-centred approach, SOM analysis establishes relationships between a variety of input variables (i.e., parental perceptions) which are mapped to visualise and interpret the findings in terms of child profiles (clusters). Hence, SOM presents the non-linear relationships between the input variables. Previous studies have predominantly employed linear analyses to explore the relationships between built environment attributes and ACS [ 28 ], thereby limiting our understanding of the complex interplay between these relationships. Furthermore, from an ecological point of view [ 16 , 28 ], it is relevant to conduct analyses that consider the interaction between different parental perceptions of attributes of the built environment when ACS is analysed. Indeed, environmental factors that are less favourable for ACS may sometimes be compensated for by others that are more so. The main objective of this current study was to analyse the relationships between parental neighbourhood perceptions and ACS, differentiating between boys and girls and using a cluster analysis of SOM. Additionally, another objective was to examine whether parental neighbourhood perceptions were different depending on whether parents had a son or a daughter. Methods Study design, procedure, and participants This cross-sectional study used data collected between 2021 and 2023 from the Built Environment and Active Children (BEACH) study conducted in Valencia and its metropolitan area, in Spain [ 29 ]. The study area was defined using city administrative boundaries and the Global Human Settlements 2015 urban centres layer [ 30 ]. The size of the area was 204.4 km 2 and included 46 municipalities and a population of 1366934 inhabitants. The BEACH study used the methodology of the International Physical Activity and the Environment Network (IPEN) in Spain [ 31 , 32 ]. The IPEN study was based on the development and use of an international set of protocols aimed at maximising the comparability of built environment attributes and physical activity outcomes between different geographical contexts [ 32 , 33 ]. Children were recruited from 10 primary schools which were non-randomly selected. Spanish primary education consists of six academic school years of children between the ages of 6 and 12 years. Following the IPEN methodology [ 29 , 31 , 32 ], the schools were selected because of their variation in objectively assessed socio-economic status (SES) and the walkability of their neighbourhoods (i.e., census block). Geographic information system (GIS) procedures were used to determine the level of walkability and SES of each neighbourhood [ 32 ]. Each school chose the class groups that would participate in the present study. Data collection occurred during the school year and timing was balanced by season. Inclusion criteria were: children aged 6–12 years; living in the city of Valencia and its metropolitan area; and being able to walk without assistance. Consequently, children younger than 6 years or older than 12 years, those who did not live in the study area and those who had problems walking without assistance were excluded from the study. Different informative meetings were organised at the schools to present the study to parents or legal guardians. An initial sample of 1,341 students agreed to participate in the study (response rate of 55.9%). The students and their parents were then asked to complete paper-based questionnaires. A total of 533 children were excluded, mainly because their parents did not fill in their questionnaire which included different metrics, for example, socio-demographic indicators or their perceptions of the built environment. Other reasons for exclusion were that the questionnaires collected from the parents had missing data or incomplete/inaccurate postal addresses. Therefore, the final sample consisted of 808 children (51.4 % girls; mean age = 10.1 years; SD = 1.1; the number of children of 6, 7 ,8 ,9, 10, 11 and 12 years old were 13, 25, 76, 214, 363, 103 and 14 respectively) and one of their parents ( n = 808 parents; 77.7 % women; mean age = 42.6 years; SD = 5.5). The average number of students per school was 80.8 (SD = 48.2), and the percentage of girls ranged from 43.8% to 61.8% among schools. Measures The questionnaires used in our study to assess ACS as well as parent-perceived neighbourhood environment were previously published [ 24 , 32 ]. These questionnaires are described in detail below. Outcome: active commuting to and from school A paper questionnaire completed by children was used to identify the mode of commuting to and from school. The questionnaire was completed at school in the presence of at least one researcher who helped the children if assistance was required. Previously, this researcher read the question aloud and explained the different possible answers. The following question was used: “In an average school week, how many days do you use the following modes of transport to get to and from school?” Response options were: walking, cycling, skateboarding or similar, public transport or school bus, and car. This question was adapted by IPEN researchers from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [ 32 ]; it had previously been satisfactorily used among youth samples in other research and had good reliability [ 34 , 35 ]. The total number of active trips to and from school per week (walking, cycling, or skateboarding) was then calculated (range = 0–10 trips). Exposure: parent-perceived neighbourhood environment Parents completed the IPEN version of the Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale for Youth (NEWS-Y-IPEN) [ 24 ]. This paper questionnaire was distributed to the children at school to give to their parents. The NEWS-Y-IPEN had good factorial and construct validity [ 24 ]. This instrument was developed to facilitate cross-study comparisons and is based on eight subscales: residential density, land use mix, recreational facilities, accessibility and walking facilities, traffic safety, pedestrian infrastructure and safety, safety from crime, and aesthetics. Table 1 shows the detailed description, response options, and scale scores for the eight NEWS-Y-IPEN subscales. Socio-demographics Parents reported the date of birth and sex of their child and the highest educational level attained by the most educated adult in the household (from 1 = none to 6 = university-level training). As in previous research, educational level was used as an indicator of SES [ 32 ]. Likewise, parents indicated their own sex and age and home postal address. GIS procedures were then used to calculate the street-network distance from the homes of the children to their school (in metres). Data analysis MATLAB R2021a software (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) was used for the data analysis. SOM analysis, which employs competitive, non-supervised artificial neural networks, was used for the study using the SOM toolbox (version 2.0) for MATLAB. The main overall advantages of this analysis was (i) SOM neurons or nodes are ordered into two-dimensional maps according to the distance between neurons in their weight vectors; (ii) the weight vector of a neuron is representative of the characteristics of the participants clustered in said neuron; (iii) it establishes relationships among a great number of variables plotted on maps to improve the visualisation and interpretation of the results; (iv) it is an unsupervised algorithm useful for non-linear models because the relationships between the parameters in biological systems are usually non-linear [ 36 ]; and (v) the analysis did not decrease in power as more input variables or factors were included. In the present study, a data imputation procedure was not applied because SOM analyses are robust to cases with missing values, and the number of participants with missing data was very low. SOM analysis can be divided into three steps [ 37 , 38 ]. First, a neural network is created in which the lattice size (i.e., 13 × 8 neurons in our study) depends on the sample size. The neural network is a set of neurons or nodes ordered into the form of a net. Each neuron or node is a vector with a value for each input variable (i.e., a weight vector). Therefore, each neuron is a vector with 10 values, one for each input variable (i.e., SES, distance to school, residential density, land use mix, recreational facilities, accessibility and walking facilities, traffic safety, pedestrian infrastructure and safety, safety from crime, and aesthetics). The neuron shape was hexagonal, meaning each neuron had six neighbours, one on each side of the hexagon. Secondly, because the neurons start with empty weight vectors, a procedure called network initialisation is required to assign initial values to them. During initialisation, a preliminary weight vector with a value for each input variable is given to each neuron. We employed two methods to initialise the neurons (i) random and (ii) linear. In the former, small random values were assigned to the weight vector of each neuron. In the latter, weight vectors were initialised in an orderly fashion along the linear subspace spanned by the two principal eigenvectors of the input data set. Next, in the third step, the values of the weight vectors of the neurons were adapted to the experimental data. To do that, the network was trained using following equation (Eq. 1): 1 w j n + 1 = w j n + η n h j , i x n x - w j n where w j is the weight vector of the j th neuron, η is the learning ratio, h j,i(x) is the neighbourhood function, x is the input vector (each case or subject in our study), n is the iteration, and i(x) is the winning neuron of the x input vector. Overall, training is an iterative process in which neuron weights are modified. The modification of the neuronal weights in each iteration depends on several factors (described in Eq. 1). First, an input vector (i.e., a case or subject of the study) is presented to the network. Then, the neurons in the lattice ‘compete’ (i.e., the Euclidean distance of their weight vector and the case values are compared) to win the input vectors. The winning neuron is the one that achieves the smallest Euclidean distance between its weight vector and the input vector so that the weight vector of the winning neuron has the closest values to the input vector values. All the neurons in the lattice then adapt their weight values closer to the values of the input case. The magnitude of the adaptation depends on the learning ratio and the neighbour function. The learning ratio has a high value at the beginning of the training process and is gradually reduced as training continues and the neighbour function maximises the adaptation of the winning neuron. The rest of the neurons also adapt their weights according to this function; the further the neuron is from the winner, the smaller the adaptation magnitude. This process is repeated until the training process ends. In our study, we performed two different SOM analyses, one for the sample of boys and the other for the girls. Comparisons between boys and girls in the input variables are shown in table 2 . As can be seen, there were significant differences between them in the safety from crime variable. Because of random factors in SOM training and the different training method setting options (i.e., batch or sequential), the initialisation (i.e., linear and random), and the neighbourhood function (i.e., Gaussian, cut Gaussian, Epanechnikov, or Bubble), 1,600 maps were obtained for boys and girls. The best SOM was selected using the minimum quantisation error between that with the topographic error equal to zero. Cases with similar characteristics were grouped by SOM into the same neurons (i.e., each neuron represented a cluster in itself). However, a cluster analysis was performed to establish larger groups of people. We used a k -means method to test the possibility of setting between 2 to 10 clusters using the neurons weight as cases for the cluster analysis. Thus, groups of neurons sharing characteristics were obtained. This two-steps procedure to obtain participant profiles has been used previously and has shown best results that other one step clustering procedures [ 39 , 40 ]. The best number of clusters was determined using the Davies–Bouldin index and the clusters found are shown in Figs. 1 and 2 . Once each participant was allocated a neuron and cluster, differences between clusters in terms of ACS were computed. These analyses were carried out in Jamovi software (version 2.3.28). Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed with Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner pairwise comparisons. The epsilon square was computed as a value representing the effect size. Finally, the level of significance was set at p &lt; 0.05. Results As shown in Table 2 , parents of daughters perceived their neighbourhoods as less safe in terms of crime compared to parents of sons (2.60 vs. 2.75; p = 0.03). The number of ACS trips was similar between boys and girls (7.41 vs 7.81; p = 0.09). The use of public transport/school bus was also similar between boys and girls (0.29 vs 0.38 trips; p = 0.22), while boys travelled more to school by car than girls (2.23 vs 1.78 trips; p = 0.02). The results for boys are shown in Fig. 1 . Figure 1 A shows the component planes for each input variable of the SOM analysis. K -means analysis revealed that the seven-cluster solution had the lowest Davies–Boulding index result (Fig. 1 B). The representation of the clusters on the neural network is shown in Fig. 1 D while the number of participants allocated to each cluster is reported in Fig. 1 C. Finally, the effects of cluster membership on ACS are shown in Fig. 1 E. As shown, there was a significant effect [χ 2 (6) = 52.3; p &lt; 0.001; ε 2 = 0.14] and the pairwise comparisons indicated that clusters 3, 6, and 7 performed more active trips than clusters 2, 4, and 5. Moreover, cluster 6 performed more active trips than cluster 1. Of note, to recognise which group of traits are related to ACS, the characteristics of the clusters can be easily checked by looking at Fig. 1 A. Considering Table 3 , when comparing the clusters with each other, significant differences among boys were found for all the input variables ( p &lt; 0.05) except for the distance to school ( p = 0.14). For example, cluster C3, which is one of the three clusters with the highest number of ACS trips, comprised boys with a high SES, short distance to school, medium residential density, and high land use mix, recreational facilities, accessibility and walking facilities, traffic safety, pedestrian infrastructure and safety, safety from crime, and aesthetics (Fig. 1 A and Table 3 ). In turn, cluster C4, which was one of the clusters with the lowest number of ACS trips, was characterised by low-to-medium SES, a medium distance to school and traffic safety, low-to-medium residential density, low land use mix, recreational facilities, and accessibility and walking facilities, pedestrian infrastructure and safety, safety from crime, and aesthetics (Fig. 1 A and Table 3 ). The results for girls are provided in Fig. 2 . The k -means analysis reported seven different clusters for girls based on the Davies–Boulding index (Fig. 2 B). The representation of the clusters on the neural network is shown in Fig. 2 D while the number of girls allocated to each cluster is reported in Fig. 2 C. Finally, the effects of cluster membership on ACS can be seen in Fig. 2 E, with no significant effects highlighted [χ 2 (6) = 7.11; p = 0.31; ε 2 = 0.018]. According to Table 4 , significant differences were found in all the input variables when comparing the clusters of the girls with each other ( p &lt; 0.05). Discussion The present findings supported the need to generate clusters or use nonlinear analyses to predict ACS behaviour. The results revealed the existence of seven clusters or built environment profiles in both boys and girls. In the case of boys, significant differences were found in the levels of ACS considering the clusters to which they belonged. However, we found that cluster membership had no significant influence on ACS among girls, despite the existence of significant differences in the characteristics of the built environment between the seven clusters. Considering these results, we hypothesised that, in addition to built environment factors, other factors may have been influencing the ACS in girls. From an ecological perspective, ACS is the consequence of the interaction of factors of the built environment together with psychosocial factors [ 9 , 16 , 28 ]. Previous research has addressed the importance of psychosocial factors in the prediction of ACS and physical activity in youth, such as physical self-efficacy and social support for ACS from peers [ 28 , 41 ]. Indeed, built environment characteristics appeared to have a stronger facilitating influence on physical activity among youth who had more positive support from their close social context [ 41 ]. According to our results, it seems that psychosocial factors were of particular relevance in girls. This idea was in line with the results from a recent study led by Larouche et al. [ 12 ], with parents of Canadian children aged 7 to 12 years, in which it was found that parent-perceived social cohesion in the neighbourhood was positively associated with ACS in girls. Moreover, they found no association between social cohesion and ACS in boys. Based on these findings, these authors suggested that the social environment might play a more important role in encouraging ACS among girls because they may be seen as more vulnerable to criminality than boys. However, we still lack studies in children that have analysed the interaction between built environment factors and psychosocial factors differentiated by sex. Thus, future research should analyse psychosocial factors such as confidence in ability (e.g., self-efficacy), attitudes (e.g., parental attitudes towards ACS), social support (e.g., from parents and friends), and social norms, which are usually and positively related to ACS [ 42 ]. The moderation role of these factors in the relationship between the built environment and ACS should also be evaluated. In this present study, the number of active trips to and from school was similar between boys and girls. This is in agreement with previous research in the Spanish population [ 43 ], although some studies from other countries show other findings. For example, some studies indicated that boys tended to commute more actively than girls [ 23 , 26 , 27 ] while others observed the opposite [ 44 ]. Therefore, it seems that the relationship between sex and ACS depends on the culture of each society [ 23 ]. In the group of boys, the highest ACS levels were found in clusters C3, C6, and C7. The high levels of ACS in cluster C3 can be justified by the perceived supportive built environments (e.g., high walkability and neighbourhood safety) and short distance to school. In relation to distance, it is notable that this factor was not statistically different between the seven clusters for boys. Also, the average distance to school in the group of boys (i.e., 985.4 m) could be considered a walkable distance among urban children [ 7 ]. Thus, it could be stated that distance to school did not seem to be a key predictor among boys in the present study. Interestingly, SES was also high in cluster C3, which does not concur with previous reports in the academic literature, most of which showed an inverse relationship between family SES and ACS [ 45 , 46 ]. It is known that higher household income is usually accompanied by greater access to private motorised transport (especially cars) which in turn, is typically related to fewer ACS trips during the week. In our study it appears that SES did not follow this pattern in cluster C3 or cluster C6. However, in cluster C7 there were low levels of SES, although the environmental characteristics seemed less favourable than those found in other clusters. For example, in cluster C3 there was higher perceived traffic safety or better pedestrian infrastructure and safety. In this scenario, SES would be more or less of a determinant of ACS behaviour in children depending on whether the environmental characteristics were more or less favourable. Therefore, according to our results, ACS improvement programmes based on enhancing attributes of the built environment could be equally effective in both high and low SES environments. In the three clusters in which boys reported more ACS trips (i.e., C3, C6, and C7), the perception of safety from crime was higher when compared to the other clusters. According to previous research [ 23 , 47 , 48 ], a perception of lower safety by parents because of crime along the route to school is associated with lower levels of ACS and independent mobility. Thus, parents preferred their children not to commute to school using active modes meaning that the use of motorised transport increased. According to our present findings, parental perception of safety from crime therefore seemed to be one of the most decisive factors for male children when choosing active modes of transport to school. Overall, our results showed that lower levels of ACS were associated with low levels of traffic safety, pedestrian infrastructure and safety, and safety against crime (see, for example, cluster C4). Hence, considering these present findings and those reported in the literature [ 49 , 50 ], future interventions should try to improve neighbourhood safety and promote ACS by refining pedestrian infrastructure (e.g., with well-marked crosswalks and traffic lights, wide and well-lit pavements, or bicycle lanes separated from vehicular traffic), implementing road safety measures (e.g., speed reduction in school and residential areas or by pedestrianising these areas), and developing specific policies (e.g., foot- and bike-buses and safe school routes). Other environmental factors related to walkability showed high and medium scores in clusters with high levels of ACS, such as cluster C3, in which high scores for land use mix, recreational facilities, accessibility and walking facilities, and aesthetics, and medium residential density were found. These results are in line with expectations, however, some of these factors had low scores in clusters with high levels of ACS, such as cluster C7, where we found low levels of accessibility and walking facilities and land use mix. These results again indicate that built environment variables interact and compensate for each other, explaining why the results for these variables in relation to ACS and physical activity across different studies are mixed. Indeed, with some studies showing predictive power in one direction and others showing the opposite, or even not finding any significant associations [ 9 , 51 , 52 ]. This was the case for variables such as land use mix or residential density [ 22 , 53 ]. The SOM analysis allows the identification of different built environment profiles in relation to ACS. Thus, different groups of children need interventions to promote ACS based on different built environment modifications. In this present study, there were no significant differences in the perceptions of the built environment according to whether the child was a boy or a girl, except for the parental perception of safety from crime. In this sense, parents of daughters perceived their neighbourhoods as less safe in relation to crime compared to parents of sons. Our findings suggest that parents have different perceptions of crime safety based on the sex of their children. This may be because society has more concerns about the safety of girls compared to boys. Nevertheless, although there were clusters (e.g., C1) among girls where the level of crime safety was very low and was significantly lower when compared to other clusters, this appeared insufficient to provoke significant differences in the number of ACS trips. Again, in the group of girls, it seems to be especially necessary to consider psychosocial factors when analysing the built environment and ACS in future research. Study strengths and limitations To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to investigate how parental perceptions of the built environment are associated with ACS by employing advanced nonlinear analysis (i.e., SOM) and differentiating by sex. We also used a standardised metric to assess parental perceptions of the built environment domains (i.e., NEWS-Y-IPEN). Consequently, our results can be compared with other studies using the same tool. A limitation of this study was its cross-sectional design which means that cause–effect relationships cannot be established. Furthermore, this study did not analyse potential psychosocial moderators of ACS. Future studies should therefore incorporate these types of factors when analysing the relationship between the built environment and ACS, especially in the population of female children. Another limitation of this work was the lack of analysis of the impact of parental sex on parental built environment perceptions. Future research should also consider examining the perceptions of the built environment of both parents living in the same household, when possible. Lastly, the present study was carried out in a specific context (i.e., Valencia and its metropolitan area), which makes it difficult to generalise its findings. Conclusions The present findings supported the idea that ACS behaviour depends on the interaction of diverse built environmental factors. In addition, these findings suggest that parental perceptions have different effects according to the sex of children, with boys being more influenced by these perceptions than girls. Parental perceptions of supportive built environments were positively related to ACS in boys. In the case of ACS in girls, the generation of child-friendly social environments, in addition to other measures to improve the built environment, may be necessary. Interventions to increase ACS should consider these sex-specific differences and address parental concerns through improved infrastructure and safety measures.
