Late-life depression (LLD) is a prevalent mental health issue that significantly impacts the quality of life in the elderly population. In recent years, metabolomics has emerged as a novel research tool, offering fresh insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying LLD. This systematic review synthesizes metabolomic studies from January 2004 to March 2024, aiming to identify differential metabolites, explore diagnostic and predictive potential, and investigate aberrant metabolic pathways in LLD. A total of 12 relevant studies were included, revealing differential metabolites such as amino acids and fatty acids, as well as associated metabolic pathways. The study’s findings provide insights into the prediction and diagnosis of LLD, highlighting the multifaceted metabolic disturbances associated with the condition. By synthesizing existing literature, we highlight the importance of metabolomic research in identifying biomarkers for LLD and potential therapeutic targets, addressing current gaps in understanding the pathophysiology of this condition. It is characterized by persistent low mood, loss of interest in activities, and a range of cognitive and somatic symptoms [ 1 , 2 ]. A recent meta-analysis found that the average prevalence of LLD is 31.74%. The prevalence of depression in the elderly is increasing and varies significantly between developed and developing countries [ 3 ]. In China, for instance, the prevalence among the elderly is as high as 20.0%, with women being more affected than men [ 4 ]. This demographic shift towards an aging population necessitates a deeper understanding of the factors contributing to LLD, as well as the development of effective intervention strategies. LLD is a multifaceted condition influenced by a confluence of factors, including physical health, psychological well-being, and social circumstances [ 5 ]. The coexistence of LLD with other physical ailments such as coronary heart disease and cerebral infarction further complicates the condition [ 6 ]. Additionally, the impact of retirement on social status may exacerbate emotional disorders by intensifying feelings of powerlessness in older adults [ 7 ]. Nevertheless, the underlying causes of LLD remain unclear. Therefore, identifying risk factors and predictors of early LLD is crucial for prompt diagnosis and treatment. Biomarkers, as endogenous compounds that reflect the physiological or pathological processes within the body, play a crucial role in elucidating the interplay between environmental factors, biological characteristics, and disease progression in LLD. Neurotransmitters like dopamine and glutamate, as well as pro-inflammatory factors, are known to contribute to the development of LLD [ 8 ]. The NLRP3 inflammasome has been implicated in triggering inflammatory responses that could lead to depression [ 9 ]. Furthermore, genetic factors such as DNA methylation of the serotonin transporter gene (SLC6A4) and gene variants of aromatase (CYP19A1) have been linked to the genetic basis of LLD [ 10 ]. The gut microbiota has also been found to correlate with depressive symptoms in LLD patients, suggesting a potential role in the pathogenesis of the condition [ 11 ]. Exploring these biomarkers will help us gain a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of LLD, which may offer novel approaches for clinical diagnosis and treatment. Metabolomics, as the omics technology closest to the phenotype, is capable of comprehensively analyzing low-molecular-weight metabolites within organisms and intuitively reflecting physiological and pathological changes [ 12 ]. This provides a new perspective for understanding the molecular mechanisms of LLD. In recent years, metabolomics has made significant progress in the study of psychiatric disorders, revealing potential links between various metabolic disturbances and disease progression. This approach may identify new biomarkers by detecting differential metabolites associated with the disease [ 13 – 15 ]. However, whether metabolomics alone can fully elucidate the complex pathological mechanisms of LLD remains an open question. Nonetheless, metabolomics has unique advantages in identifying potential biomarkers and metabolic pathways, and these findings can provide an important foundation for future multi-omics studies and clinical applications. This review systematically analyzes and discusses the metabolomics research on LLD, exploring its application in understanding the pathological mechanisms, assessing its potential value in clinical diagnosis and treatment, and paving the way for innovative approaches to mental health care in the aging population. Methods To ensure the quality of our report, we strictly adhered to the PRISMA guidelines [ 16 ]. Literature search A thorough literature search was conducted across pertinent databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and MENDA, spanning from January 2004 to March 2024. The search criteria incorporated terms such as “metabolome,” “metabolites,” “metabolomics,” “metabolic signature,” “metabolic biomarker,” and “metabolic profile,” in conjunction with “geriatric depression,” “depression in the elderly,” “LLD,” and “late-life depression.” In addition, based on the list of references and relevant reviews of the included studies, we also identified potentially suitable papers and documented the selection process in the article selection flow chart. Article assessments were carried out independently by two researchers, with a third researcher involved in final decision-making when required. Inclusion and exclusion criteria A study is accepted if all of the following criteria are met: (1) The study is for LLD; (2) Metabolomics analysis methods using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), mass spectrometry (MS), high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), gas chromatography (GC), liquid chromatography (LC) and the above combined techniques. Besides, we excluded the following articles: (1) review articles or meta-analyses; (2) conference abstracts or editorials; (3) articles on LLD without a clear diagnosis; (4) non-metabolomics analysis methods; (5) metabolomics analysis of magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). (6) Studies in animals. Quality assessment Given the significant heterogeneity in study designs among the included studies, we conducted a quality assessment. To comprehensively evaluate the evidence quality and methodological rigor of these studies, we employed the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence tool ( http://www.cebm.net/index.aspx?o=5653 ) and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) ( https://www.ohri.ca/programs/clinical_epidemiology/oxford.asp ). The Oxford Levels of Evidence tool is used to determine the quality of evidence for each study, categorizing them into five levels, ranging from high-quality systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials to inferences based on mechanisms, thereby providing clear guidance for clinical practice. The NOS assesses observational studies based on three aspects: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and measurement of outcomes, with a total score of 9 points, where higher scores indicate better study quality. Extraction related information Following a comprehensive review of the full articles, we successfully gathered crucial data encompassing author names, research paper titles, publication dates, and respective journal sources. Additionally, we extracted details on the subjects’ ages, sample group sizes, analytical techniques employed, biological specimens utilized, diagnostic criteria applied, and identification of any distinct metabolites. Statistical analysis The sample characteristics, analytical platforms, species types, study designs, the number of detected metabolites, the number of differential metabolites, and their change trends have been systematically summarized and visually represented using charts. To standardize metabolites with different terminologies but identical chemical structures across studies, all metabolites were linked to their unique KEGG compound IDs (Supplementary Table S1 ). Additionally, the number of studies identifying differential metabolites under different change trends was analyzed. Differential metabolites were analyzed using the Venny 2.1.0 tool ( https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html ), based on biological specimens, analytical platforms, metabolomics approaches, and study designs to identify common differential metabolites across different analytical methods. Metabolic pathway and enrichment analyses were conducted using the MetaboAnalyst online platform ( http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/ ). Significant metabolic pathways were identified based on a p-value threshold ( P &lt; 0.05). Systematic meta-analysis This study focused on differential metabolites (tryptophan and kynurenine) that were consistently reported in more than two publications. We extracted the concentrations of these metabolites from both healthy and LLD groups. Utilizing R (version 4.2.1) and the meta package (version 8.0.1), we cleaned the input data and performed a meta-analysis. The analysis results were visually presented using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4). For heterogeneity assessment, we employed the Q test and I² statistic for quantification. Typically, a p-value greater than 0.1 in the Q test indicates no significant heterogeneity among studies (i.e., homogeneity). The I² statistic measures the proportion of heterogeneity among groups, with an I² value greater than 50% indicating significant heterogeneity. For effect size calculation, we used the standardized mean difference (SMD) measured by Hedges’ method. When no heterogeneity was detected, a fixed-effects model was applied; otherwise, a random-effects model was used to calculate the effect size. The between-study variance in the random-effects model was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, and confidence intervals were calculated using the Hartung and Knapp method when heterogeneity was present. We generated forest plots to display the effect estimates of each study. Subsequently, Baujat plots were created to identify the sources of heterogeneity in the meta-analysis data. Finally, publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test and Begg’s test [ 17 ]. Results Study characteristics This review includes 12 articles [ 1 , 18 – 28 ] on metabolomics of LLD, all of which are clinical studies [ 1 , 18 – 28 ] (Fig. 1 ). Within the 12 studies, 6 studies utilized serum samples, 3 studies used plasma samples, 2 studies employed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and 1 study utilized urine samples. Notably, only two studies employed a non-targeted metabolomics approach, while the rest utilized targeted metabolomics methods. These targeted metabolomics investigations primarily focused on amino acid and fatty acid metabolism. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was the predominant analytical technique used in most articles. Sample sizes in the case and control groups varied from 9 to 1110 individuals, with 5 articles having sample sizes exceeding 100 individuals (Table 1 ). Study quality In this systematic review, a total of 12 relevant articles were included, comprising 10 cross-sectional studies and 2 cohort studies. To ensure study quality, the Oxford Levels of Evidence tool was employed to assess the evidence quality of these articles. The results indicated that 9 articles were rated as Level 3, which are typically based on non-randomized controlled trials or observational studies; the remaining 3 articles were rated as Level 4, which are often case series or case-control studies. Additionally, the NOS was utilized to evaluate the risk of bias in the studies (Supplementary Tables S2). The NOS is a widely used tool for assessing the quality of non-randomized studies, including case-control and cohort studies. According to the NOS assessment, all studies scored no less than 7 points, indicating that the included studies were of high quality overall (Supplementary Tables S3). Analysis of differential metabolites of LLD Among the 12 studies included, a total of 24 differential metabolites were identified, with significant changes observed in their levels in patients with LLD. Of these, 18 differential metabolites showed a downward trend, while 9 differential metabolites exhibited an upward trend. Notably, oleate, kynurenine, and kynurenic acid displayed inconsistent trends across different studies. Additionally, oleate, kynurenine, kynurenic acid, and tryptophan were frequently reported (in ≥ 2 publications) (Fig. 2 ). All metabolic biomarkers were detected in four types of biological samples: plasma, serum, CSF, and urine. Oleate showed inconsistent expression trends between plasma and serum, and kynurenine and kynurenic acid exhibited inconsistent trends between serum and urine (Fig. 3 A). The 24 differential metabolites were primarily detected using two metabolomics techniques: GC (including GC, GC-MS, and GC-MS/MS) and LC (including LC-MS/MS, HPLC, and HPLC-ESI-MS). However, no overlap was observed in the differential metabolites detected by these two methods (Fig. 3 B). Furthermore, all biomarkers were detected using both targeted and untargeted metabolomics approaches, with oleate being the only biomarker detected by both methods (Fig. 3 C). Metabolic biomarkers were categorized based on study design, with 91.7% identified from cross-sectional studies and 8.3% from cohort studies. No common metabolic biomarkers were identified across these studies (Fig. 3 D). In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis to examine the differences in tryptophan and kynurenine in LLD. Tryptophan was reported in four articles and was consistently downregulated in LLD. The heterogeneity test results showed no significant heterogeneity (Q = 5.819, P = 0.1208, I² = 48.44%), and therefore, a fixed-effects model was used for the analysis. The meta-analysis results showed an effect size of -0.61028 for tryptophan, with P = 1.7e−13 (95% CI −0.77255 to −0.448), indicating a significant downregulation of tryptophan in LLD (Fig. 4 A). Neither Egger’s regression test (t (2) = −1.2039, P = 0.3518) nor Begg’s test (Z = −1.3587, P = 0.1742) detected publication bias. Kynurenine was reported in three articles, but the results were inconsistent. The heterogeneity test results showed significant heterogeneity (Q = 53.03, P = 3.06e−12, I² = 96.23%), and therefore, a random-effects model was used for the analysis. The meta-analysis results showed an effect size of 0.44587 for kynurenine, with P = 0.6406 (95% CI −3.0767 to 3.9685), indicating that the results could not be combined (Fig. 4 B). Baujat plot analysis showed that the study by Chojnacki et al. (2020) had the highest heterogeneity in the tryptophan analysis (Fig. 4 C), and removing this study had the greatest impact on the overall effect in the kynurenine analysis (Fig. 4 D). Predictive and diagnostic potential of differential metabolites One study evaluated the potential of differential metabolites and their combinations for the diagnosis and prediction of LLD (Table 2 ). Adachi et al. found that α-aminobutyric acid exhibited a relatively high diagnostic capacity, with a receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC_AUC) of 0.6893 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) ranging from 0.5785 to 0.8002. Moreover, the predictive ability for LLD was significantly enhanced when α-aminobutyric acid was combined with leucine, threonine, hydroxyproline, and histidine, yielding an ROC_AUC of 0.8346 and a 95% CI of 0.7365 to 0.9326. Analysis of metabolic pathways All reported differential metabolites from the 12 included studies were imported into MetaboAnalyst for metabolic pathway analysis and enrichment analysis. The results revealed that eight metabolic pathways were significantly enriched ( P &lt; 0.05, Fig. 5 ). These pathways included glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, glycerolipid metabolism, galactose metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism (Supplementary Tables S4). Notably, the glycerolipid metabolism pathway exhibited the highest impact value of 0.33. Discussion This systematic review comprehensively analyzed the biomarkers of LLD using metabolomics, summarizing the changes in metabolites associated with LLD, the involved metabolic pathways, and the potential value of metabolic biomarkers in the diagnosis and prediction of LLD. The following discussion will focus on the major metabolic pathways. Amino acid metabolism Amino acid metabolism plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of LLD. Paige et al. first employed untargeted metabolomics to analyze metabolites in LLD patients and found that γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), 5-oxoproline, 3-phospho-L-serine and hydroorotate were significantly downregulated [ 25 ]. GABA, a key inhibitory neurotransmitter, is involved in the metabolism of alanine, aspartate, and glutamate. Its reduced levels may lead to neuronal hyperexcitability, thereby affecting emotional regulation and cognitive function [ 29 ]. This finding is consistent with studies in other forms of depression, where GABAergic dysfunction has been implicated in the pathophysiology of the disease [ 30 ]. Additionally, the study by Lyssikatos et al. further highlighted the importance of GABA as a potential biomarker for LLD [ 31 ]. 3-Phospho-L-serine, a key intermediate in serine metabolism, is involved in the synthesis of phosphatidylserine. This is particularly relevant given the role of phosphatidylserine in maintaining neuronal membrane integrity and function, which are critical for cognitive health in the elderly [ 32 ]. Its alterations may influence the onset of LLD through neuroprotection and cognitive function improvement [ 33 ]. Hydroorotate is an intermediate metabolite in the amino acid metabolic pathway and plays a significant role in the synthesis of pyrimidine nucleotides. The disruption of pyrimidine nucleotide synthesis can impair DNA repair mechanisms and mitochondrial function, both of which are essential for maintaining neuronal health and resilience against stress [ 34 , 35 ]. Consequently, hydroorotate may indirectly contribute to the development and progression of LLD by influencing cellular energy metabolism and neuroprotective pathways [ 36 ]. Subsequent targeted metabolomics studies further explored the role of amino acids in LLD [ 1 , 22 ]. Adachi et al. found that α-aminobutyric acid (AABA) was downregulated in LLD patients and could serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker. Although leucine, threonine, hydroxyproline, and histidine did not show statistically significant differences between LLD patients and healthy controls, their combination with AABA achieved a ROC_AUC value of 0.8346, which significantly enhanced the diagnostic capacity for LLD [ 1 ]. This finding underscores the potential of using a panel of biomarkers for improved diagnostic accuracy. AABA and 5-oxoproline are integral to glutathione metabolism, a pathway crucial for protecting cells from oxidative stress damage and maintaining intracellular redox balance [ 37 – 40 ]. Given the increased oxidative stress observed in LLD [ 41 ], the downregulation of AABA may reflect an impaired antioxidant defense mechanism, contributing to neuronal vulnerability and the progression of depressive symptoms. Liu et al. identified significant alterations in the levels of L-threonine, D-methionine, and glycine in LLD patients. Specifically, L-threonine and D-methionine levels were significantly reduced, while glycine levels were significantly elevated [ 22 ]. Glycine, which participates in the metabolism of glycine, serine, and threonine, as well as glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism and glutathione metabolism, acts as a neurotransmitter to modulate the central nervous system. Its altered levels may be associated with neurotransmitter function, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and cognitive dysfunction [ 42 ]. The reduction in L-threonine levels may disrupt neurotransmitter synthesis and neuroprotective mechanisms, further exacerbating cognitive dysfunction in LLD patients. D-amino acids play important roles in higher brain functions and are potential biomarkers for the diagnosis of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Similarly, changes in D-methionine levels may reflect metabolic disturbances and oxidative stress, impacting neuronal function and contributing to the pathogenesis of LLD through multiple pathways [ 43 , 44 ]. Despite Hashimoto et al. not identifying differential metabolites, abnormal expression of glutamine/glutamate (Gln/Glu) was observed in LLD patients [ 20 ]. The Gln/Glu ratio serves as an indicator of the Gln-Glu cycle in glial-neuronal communication, suggesting that this cycle is disrupted in the cerebrospinal fluid of LLD patients. Glu, the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, has a crucial homeostatic balance with its metabolite Gln to maintain neuronal and synaptic function. Elevated levels of Glu relative to Gln can result in excessive neuronal excitation, which may contribute to the development of depressive symptoms. This is supported by studies showing that increased Glu levels in the brain are associated with higher levels of oxidative stress and inflammation, both of which are implicated in the pathophysiology of depression [ 45 ]. Additionally, the imbalance in the Gln-Glu cycle can impair synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter function, further exacerbating cognitive and emotional dysregulation in LLD patients [ 20 , 46 ]. Furthermore, the tryptophan-kynurenine metabolic pathway (TKP) has garnered considerable attention [ 18 , 26 – 28 ]. Tryptophan is primarily metabolized through the TKP, with approximately 95% undergoing kynurenine metabolism and the remaining 5% through serotonin metabolism. The TKP metabolism is typically activated by inflammatory factors, and its downstream metabolites exhibit both neuroprotective and neurotoxic effects. The latter not only impairs neuronal function but also activates oxidative stress, contributing to the pathogenesis of LLD [ 47 , 48 ]. LLD is often accompanied by chronic inflammatory states, and TKP dysregulation appears to be particularly significant in LLD compared to younger-onset depression [ 49 ]. Studies consistently show reduced tryptophan levels and significant alterations in kynurenine metabolism in LLD patients [ 18 , 26 – 28 ]. Heterogeneity, effect size, and sensitivity analyses all support these combined results. However, results regarding downstream TKP metabolites are inconsistent [ 18 ]. For example, Wu et al. found decreased levels of kynurenine and kynurenic acid in serum [ 27 , 28 ], while Chojnacki et al. reported opposite results in urine. Chojnacki et al. also reported elevated levels of xanthurenic acid and quinolinic acid [ 18 ]. Heterogeneity analysis and Baujat plot analysis in studies on kynurenine revealed that the study by Chojnacki et al. had the greatest heterogeneity and the most significant impact on the inconsistency of results, suggesting that differences in study outcomes may stem from inconsistencies in sample types, reflecting different pathophysiological processes. Similarly, studies on kynurenic acid present conflicting results. Comparing the study populations and methods of the two studies, the likely reason for the differences in results is again the inconsistency in sample types. Wu et al. excluded individuals with severe medical conditions or concomitant medications that could affect the central nervous system or immune function, including cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine, and neurological diseases, making the metabolites in serum closer to the physiological state. In contrast, Chojnacki et al. included individuals with comorbidities and medication use, which could influence the changes in urinary metabolites due to diet, drugs, and kidney disease. Future large-scale studies are needed to elucidate the changes in these metabolites. The neurotoxicity of TKP metabolites is particularly relevant in the context of LLD. Quinolinic acid, a neurotoxin, can cause excitotoxicity and neuronal damage when its levels are elevated, which is associated with the development of depressive symptoms [ 50 ]. Similarly, increased levels of xanthurenic acid can disrupt neurotransmitter balance and lead to cognitive dysfunction, a common feature of LLD [ 51 ]. On the other hand, kynurenic acid, a neuroprotective metabolite, has been shown to have anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, suggesting that its reduction in LLD patients may exacerbate neuroinflammation and neuronal vulnerability [ 50 ]. LLD can be categorized based on age of onset into Early-Onset Depression (EOD, onset before age 65 and persisting into or recurring in late life) and Late-Onset Depression (LOD, first onset at age 65 or older) [ 52 ]. Wu et al. found that tryptophan metabolism was more significantly altered in LOD patients, whereas EOD patients exhibited reduced tryptophan levels and marked changes in kynurenine metabolism [ 28 ]. The development of EOD is associated with genetic factors and stress resilience related to personality traits, while LOD, which develops with advancing age, is more likely linked to somatic diseases and neurodegenerative disorders [ 53 ]. Thus, EOD and LOD may have distinct pathogenic mechanisms, leading to different TKP alteration patterns. Moreover, individuals with cognitive impairment among LLD patients show more pronounced changes in tryptophan metabolism [ 27 ]. Chojnacki et al. investigated the sex differences in LLD patients and found that xanthurenic acid was downregulated in female patients, which may be related to sex hormone levels [ 18 ]. McEvoy et al. discovered that LLD patients had decreased serum levels of L-arginine and increased levels of asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) [ 24 ]. This finding is consistent with meta-analyses showing significantly reduced arginine levels in patients with depression [ 54 ]. Additionally, Pervin et al. demonstrated that L-arginine has significant stress-resilience effects against lifespan shortening, cognitive decline, and depression [ 55 ]. Epidemiological studies have also indicated a positive correlation between aging and circulating ADMA concentrations [ 56 ]. In contrast to the decreased plasma ADMA levels found in younger patients with major depressive disorder by Ozden et al. [ 56 ], McEvoy et al. observed elevated serum ADMA levels in LLD patients, which were independently associated with new-onset depression at a 6-year follow-up [ 24 ]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the pathophysiological mechanisms between LLD and younger-onset depression. The elevated ADMA levels may represent a compensatory mechanism to regulate nitric oxide synthase activity in depression [ 24 ], suggesting that ADMA could serve as a specific biomarker for predicting LLD risk and distinguishing depression across different age groups. L-arginine, a semi-essential amino acid, is metabolized by endothelial nitric oxide synthase to produce nitric oxide (NO). ADMA, an endogenous inhibitor of NO synthase, can disrupt the L-arginine-NO metabolic pathway. Recent studies have shown that dysregulation of the L-arginine-NO pathway is closely related to the pathogenesis of major depressive disorder [ 57 ]. Specifically, disruptions in this pathway may increase oxidative stress levels, impair synaptic plasticity and neuronal function, thereby contributing to the onset of depression and causing cognitive impairment [ 55 ]. With advancing age, the role of oxidative stress becomes increasingly prominent, particularly in LLD patients [ 58 ]. Characterized by persistent low mood, cognitive impairment, and comorbid somatic diseases [ 59 ], LLD suggests that arginine and ADMA may play more significant roles in its pathogenesis. Fatty acid metabolism In patients with LLD, oleate was detected in serum by targeted metabolomics and in plasma by untargeted metabolomics, with inconsistent trends in changes [ 23 , 25 ]. These discrepancies may stem from differences in sample size as well as sample processing and the use of anticoagulants. The study by Paige et al. had a small sample size, and its results may have been more influenced by individual variations. Future research should expand the sample size to further validate these findings. Anticoagulants such as EDTA or heparin can interfere with the natural coagulation process, thereby affecting the stability and release of metabolites (including oleate) in plasma and consequently altering the metabolic profile [ 60 ]. Zeng et al. identified oleate as a risk factor for depression through Mendelian randomization studies [ 61 ], a finding consistent with that of Marx et al. [ 23 ]. As a monounsaturated fatty acid, oleate is crucial for regulating the fluidity and stability of cell membranes. Ulmann et al. found that oleate altered the fatty acid composition in the hippocampus and brain of rats with cognitive impairments [ 62 ]. Moreover, neurogenesis depends on the production of oleate [ 63 ], and inhibiting the local conversion of saturated fatty acids to monounsaturated fatty acids in the brain is sufficient to counteract the loss of neural stem cell activity in triple-transgenic Alzheimer’s disease mice [ 64 ], suggesting that oleate may be involved in LLD by influencing neural function. Marx et al. found that total fatty acid levels were decreased in LLD patients, with significant downregulation of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) [ 23 ]. In contrast, Hamazaki et al. reported upregulated levels of arachidonic acid (AA) [ 19 ]. AA, a pro-inflammatory polyunsaturated fatty acid, and EPA, which has anti-inflammatory effects, jointly participate in the regulation of neuroinflammation, neurotransmitter function, and neuroplasticity. Elevated levels of AA can lead to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while reduced levels of EPA can impair the brain’s ability to resolve inflammation, further contributing to the development of depressive symptoms. The imbalance between AA and EPA, particularly elevated AA levels and reduced EPA levels, may contribute to increased inflammation and neurotoxicity, thereby promoting the occurrence of LLD. This imbalance can disrupt the delicate balance of fatty acids in the brain, leading to impaired neuronal function and increased vulnerability to stress and inflammation [ 65 , 66 ]. Additionally, n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFAs) were not associated with the severity of LLD, possibly due to dietary intake of n-3 PUFAs [ 19 , 23 ]. Oleate, EPA, and AA are all involved in the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, which is related to energy storage and signaling, thereby affecting the onset of LLD [ 67 ]. The metabolic disturbances of these fatty acids may exacerbate the pathophysiological process of LLD through multiple pathways, including increased oxidative stress, neuronal dysfunction, and modulation of neurotransmitter balance [ 66 , 67 ]. Carbohydrate metabolism In LLD patients, Paige et al. found that glycerate, citrate, glycerol, and inositol were significantly downregulated [ 25 ]. These metabolites play important roles in carbohydrate metabolism and related pathways. Glycerol and inositol are involved in galactose metabolism, and their metabolic abnormalities may lead to increased oxidative stress and chronic inflammatory states, thereby affecting the onset of LLD [ 68 , 69 ]. Elevated oxidative stress can damage cellular components, while chronic inflammation can disrupt normal neuronal function, both of which are implicated in the onset and progression of LLD [ 9 ]. Glycerate and glycerol are important components of glycerophospholipid metabolism. Glycerophospholipids, as major constituents of cell membranes, are involved in cell signaling, cytoskeletal stability, and energy metabolism. Abnormalities in glycerophospholipid metabolism may weaken neuronal protection, increase neuronal sensitivity to oxidative stress and inflammatory damage, and affect the synthesis and release of neurotransmitters, thereby disrupting emotional regulation [ 70 , 71 ]. Additionally, citrate, glycine, and glycerate are involved in glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism. Their disturbances may reflect imbalances in cellular energy metabolism and increased sensitivity to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Reduced levels of these metabolites can lead to insufficient energy production, which is critical for maintaining normal neuronal activity and synaptic function. These metabolic disorders may contribute to the development of LLD by exacerbating neuronal dysfunction [ 72 , 73 ]. Therefore, abnormalities in these metabolic pathways may exacerbate the pathological process of LLD through multiple mechanisms, suggesting their potential significance in the onset of LLD. Other metabolisms Hashimoto et al. discovered through untargeted metabolomics that ascorbic acid levels were significantly elevated in the CSF of LLD patients with intact cognitive function. This phenomenon may be related to increased transport of ascorbic acid from the blood to the CSF [ 21 ]. This finding is intriguing, as it suggests a potential compensatory mechanism in response to increased oxidative stress in the brain regions of LLD patients. Ascorbic acid, commonly known as vitamin C, is a potent antioxidant that plays a crucial role in maintaining the oxidative balance within and outside cells. Its primary functions include scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and supporting the regeneration of other antioxidants, such as vitamin E. The elevated levels of ascorbic acid in the CSF may reflect an upregulated transport mechanism from the blood to the CSF, possibly as a response to heightened oxidative stress in the brain. This increased transport could be an attempt by the body to counteract the oxidative damage that is often observed in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders, including LLD. Oxidative stress is known to damage cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and DNA, leading to neuronal dysfunction and cell death. In the context of LLD, the elevated ascorbic acid levels may indicate a state of chronic oxidative stress in the brain. This oxidative imbalance can further exacerbate inflammatory responses, as oxidative stress and inflammation are closely intertwined. Elevated levels of ROS can activate microglia, the immune cells of the brain, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and further contributing to neuroinflammation. This chronic inflammatory state can impair synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter function, which are critical for maintaining cognitive and emotional regulation, thereby playing an important role in the pathogenesis of LLD [ 9 , 74 ]. Metabolomics supplements the understanding of LLD The metabolomic features of LLD reveal a complex pattern of metabolic alterations, providing a multidimensional perspective for understanding the pathophysiological changes of the disease. As previously discussed, the metabolism of amino acids, fatty acids, and carbohydrates, as well as the antioxidant effects of ascorbate, primarily influence inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, neurotransmitter balance, and neuronal function through their respective metabolic pathways. These metabolic alterations interact with each other, collectively driving the pathogenesis of LLD and being closely related to its core symptoms. The potential of differential metabolites as biomarkers for LLD This study has uncovered a range of potential metabolic biomarkers in LLD, laying an important foundation for the development of new diagnostic tools and therapeutic targets. Adachi et al. found that AABA, when combined with leucine, threonine, hydroxyproline, and histidine, exhibits a high diagnostic capacity [ 1 ]. Additionally, multiple studies by Wu et al. and Chojnacki et al. focusing on the TKP have revealed significant differences in tryptophan levels among LLD patients [ 18 , 26 – 28 ], suggesting that tryptophan may serve as a biomarker for the early diagnosis and disease monitoring of LLD. These differential metabolites can serve as the basis for developing non-invasive diagnostic tools, which can enhance the accuracy and timeliness of diagnosis and monitor the disease progression and therapeutic outcomes of LLD. By regularly measuring the levels of these metabolites, the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions can be assessed, and treatment plans can be adjusted in a timely manner. Moreover, changes in certain metabolites may indicate potential therapeutic targets. For instance, abnormalities in the TKP may be associated with inflammation and oxidative stress in LLD, and pharmacological interventions targeting this pathway may offer new strategies for treating LLD. Limitation Despite its comprehensiveness, this systematic review has several limitations that warrant further consideration. (1) Some studies had small sample sizes, which may lead to insufficient statistical power to detect significant changes in metabolites. Small sample sizes can also increase the risk of false-negative results, where actual changes in certain metabolites are not detected. Moreover, results from studies with small sample sizes may be more susceptible to random variation, reducing the stability and reliability of the findings. (2) The studies included used a variety of sample types (such as serum, plasma, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine), and different sample types reflect different pathophysiological states of the body. This diversity may lead to inconsistencies in results and thus affect the comprehensive understanding of the metabolic characteristics of LLD. (3) Different studies employed various analytical platforms (such as GC-MS, LC-MS/MS and HPLC), which have significant differences in the detection range, sensitivity, and accuracy of metabolites. For example, GC-MS is suitable for detecting volatile and semi-volatile metabolites, while LC-MS/MS is more appropriate for non-volatile metabolites. This diversity in analytical platforms may lead to inconsistent detection results for the same metabolite across different studies, thereby affecting the comparability of the results. (4) Most of the studies included were cross-sectional, with a generally low level of evidence, mostly grade 3 and 4, which limits the ability to infer causality and establish causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish the temporal relationship between changes in metabolites and the occurrence or progression of LLD. (5) There are gender differences in the incidence of LLD, but analyses of differential metabolites based on gender are insufficient. In addition, patients with LLD at different age stages may exhibit different metabolic characteristics, but related analyses are also limited. Additionally, it is worth noting that this study has uncovered a range of potential metabolic biomarkers and related metabolic pathways in LLD through systematic review and comprehensive analysis. However, the pathogenesis of LLD involves multiple factors, including genetics, environment, and neurobiology, and a single metabolomics study may not cover all relevant mechanisms. Therefore, metabolomics research needs to be combined with other omics technologies (such as genomics and proteomics) to more comprehensively understand the pathogenesis of LLD. For example, genomics can reveal genetic variations related to LLD, proteomics can analyze changes in the expression of key proteins, and metabolomics can provide detailed information on the levels of metabolites. This multi-omics research approach can more comprehensively reveal the pathological mechanisms of LLD and provide stronger support for the development of new diagnostic tools and therapeutic targets. Future research directions and clinical applications The field of metabolomics faces several challenges that must be addressed to fully realize its potential in clinical and research settings. (1) Increasing sample sizes to enhance statistical power and result reliability; (2) Standardizing sample types or conducting rigorous calibration and validation in multi-sample type studies to reduce the impact of sample type differences on results. (3) Using a unified analytical platform or conducting rigorous calibration and validation in multi-platform studies to reduce the impact of technical differences on results. (4) Adopting standardized study designs, such as multicenter cohort studies, to improve the generalizability and reliability of results. (5) Expanding the enhanced analysis of differential metabolites based on gender and age. Meanwhile, integrating metabolomics data with other omics technologies is crucial for providing a comprehensive view of biological systems [ 75 , 76 ]. Conducting multicenter, large-sample studies to validate whether differential metabolites can serve as potential therapeutic targets. Additionally, careful management of data privacy and ethical considerations regarding the use of personal health information in metabolomics research is essential to maintain public trust and ensure regulatory compliance. Despite these challenges, there are significant opportunities for future research, especially in the fields of personalized medicine and disease prevention. By leveraging technological advancements and interdisciplinary collaboration, researchers can enhance the application of metabolomics in clinical settings, ultimately leading to more effective and personalized medical solutions [ 77 , 78 ]. The continuous exploration of metabolomics in various fields, from oncology to infectious diseases, highlights its versatility and potential to significantly advance medical science and public health. Conclusion In conclusion, this systematic review has identified a range of potential metabolomic biomarkers and associated metabolic pathways associated with LLD, providing novel insights into its pathophysiological mechanisms. The metabolites involved in this review mainly include amino acids and fatty acids, highlighting the multifaceted metabolic disturbances in LLD. These findings suggest potential avenues for the development of diagnostic tools and therapeutic targets. However, the need for further validation in larger cohorts and longitudinal studies cannot be overstated. Future research should focus on confirming these biomarkers and exploring their clinical utility in the early diagnosis and treatment of LLD, ultimately aiming to improve patient outcomes and quality of life for this vulnerable population.
