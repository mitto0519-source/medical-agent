To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Abstract Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major global cause of premature mortality. While multiple studies propose CVD mortality prediction models based on regression frameworks, incorporating causal understanding through causal inference approaches can enhance accuracy. This paper demonstrates a methodology combining evidence synthesis and expert knowledge to construct a causal model for premature CVD mortality using Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs). The process involves three phases: (1) initial DAG development based on the Evidence Synthesis for Constructing Directed Acyclic Graphs (ESC-DAGs) framework, (2) validation and consensus-building with 12 experts using the Fuzzy Delphi method (FDM), and (3) application to data analysis using population-based survey data linked with death records. Expert input refined the initial DAG model, achieving consensus on 45 causal paths. The revised model guided selection of confounding variables for adjustment. For example, to estimate the total effect of diabetes on premature CVD mortality, the suggested adjustment set included age, dietary pattern, genetic/family history, sex hormones, and physical activity. Testing different DAG models showed agreement between expert ratings and data accuracy from regression models. This systematic approach contributes to DAG methodology, offering a transparent process for constructing causal pathways for premature CVD mortality. Introduction Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of premature mortality worldwide 1 and become one of the most serious health problems globally. Premature mortality is defined as a death that occurs before the average age of death in a particular population 2 . In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), CVD significantly adds to the growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), accounting for approximately 86% of all premature NCD deaths globally 3 . Many risk factors for CVD and premature CVD mortality have been described. They may refer to unmodifiable risk or inherent biological traits such as age, sex, and ethnicity and modifiable risk or behavioural risk factors including hypertension, diabetes, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and others 4 – 7 . Multiple studies propose models for the prediction of a variety CVD mortality 7 – 9 . However, most risk prediction and causation models in epidemiology are based on additive combinations of risk factors in a regression model framework. The model might not provide a plausible causal representation of how disease or outcome develops. In epidemiological studies, a distinction between prediction and causal approaches should be made to facilitate valid interpretation from the body of evidence. Causal inference approaches, is a method that explicitly considers causality and can describe the influence of variables on a particular disease outcome accounting for a causal pathway between variables 10 . Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) are a useful tool to represent, in a graphical format, researchers’ assumptions about the causal relationships between variables in a causal model 11 , 12 . A DAG shows causal links between variables using arrows connecting nodes. Absence of an arrow between nodes indicates no causal effect. Nodes can be measured or unmeasured 12 , 13 . Any variable that influences at least two others should be included. DAGs use the ‘backdoor criterion,’ a set of rules, to figure out which variables to control for when estimating the effect of one on another. DAGs have attained prominence as essential tools in the field of epidemiologic research, facilitating a comprehensive understanding of fundamental concepts including causation 14 , 15 , mediation 16 , confounding, and selection bias 17 . For example, application of DAGs in identifying the variables that require conditioning to address confounding 14 , 15 , 18 , 19 . In certain scenarios, conditioning on a common effect (collider) variable can introduce bias, even when no bias was initially present in the absence of conditioning 20 . To develop prediction models, we believe that a causal understanding is likely to yield more accurate and reliable predictions compared to those generated using standard statistical methods alone. As DAGs serve as a valuable tool for guiding the selection of confounding variables in a study, drawing a DAGs before analysis is crucial for this purpose. According to key authors in causal inference, theory and ‘background knowledge’ play a central role in DAGs construction 15 , 19 , 21 , 22 . Hernan et al. emphasizes that causal inference from observational data requires prior causal assumptions or beliefs, derived from expert knowledge rather than statistical associations detected in the data 15 . Despite this, to our knowledge, there has been no systematic documentation of the standard methods or protocol employed in constructing DAGs, particularly in epidemiological studies on cardiovascular diseases. A recent contribution by Ferguson et al. introduced a systematic approach to building DAGs based on studies from the literatures, proposing a framework named Evidence Synthesis for Constructing Directed Acyclic Graphs (ESC-DAGs) 23 . However, there remains a need for practical guidance on drawing DAGs, incorporating the experience and knowledge of domain experts. Therefore, this paper introduces a comprehensive methodology that combines the ESC-DAGs guideline with the Delphi method involving expert domains. The Delphi method is a technique used to obtain the most reliable consensus of opinion from a group of experts 24 . Focusing on CVD, specifically ischemic heart disease (IHD) and stroke as the causal model for this study, our aim was to demonstrate a step-by-step process for constructing a causal pathway for premature CVD mortality that integrates both evidence synthesis and expert opinion consensus. The outcome of the paper will propose a systematic development of the causal model for premature CVD mortality and demonstrate how the steps involved will guide researchers in building causal models, particularly in the selection of adjustment variables. Method This study involved integrating evidence synthesis and expert opinion and to construct DAG model, followed by testing the model with available data. Our study comprised three phases: (i) developing the initial draft DAG model based on literature and researcher knowledge, adhering to the ESC-DAGs guidelines 23 ; (ii) obtaining opinion and consensus on the DAG model from the expert domain through the Delphi method, involving two rounds of interviews; and (iii) proposing feasible DAGs models using available data, specifically population-based survey data linked with death records, by comparing model rankings by the expert domain with model accuracy from the data. The flow chart illustrating the steps is presented in Fig. 1 . Generally, Phases 1 and 2 aim to propose a DAG model from evidence synthesis and expert consensus (regardless of data), as a general reference for epidemiological studies on premature CVD mortality. Phase 3 aims to demonstrate the application of this model as a guide for data analysis. This phase proposes the best DAG model from available data in this study, as preferred by experts and for data accuracy. The detailed process for each phase is described below. Phase 1: evidence synthesis for constructing DAGs (ESC-DAG) In this phase, we employed the ESC-DAGs protocol, as proposed by Fugerson (2020) 23 , to develop the DAG model for premature CVD mortality. The ESC-DAGs protocol is structured into four stages: mapping (identifying pertinent variables from existing literature studies; a detailed summary of evidence on CVD mortality risk factors is provided in the supplementary file [Appendix 1 , Tables S1 , S2 ])), translation (establishing relationships between variables), integration 1 (constructing a single DAG with relevant relationships), and integration 2 (grouping similar variables within the DAG). Figure 2 summarizes the ESC-DAGs protocol. This phase was conducted by a research team. From this process, we proposed the first draft DAG model for premature CVD mortality based on the researcher’s knowledge and evidence from the literature. The web-based tool, DAGitty 25 ( http://www.dagitty.net ) was used to draw DAGs. DAGitty is specialized software for drawing and analysing DAGs and providing functions such as identifying minimal sufficient adjustment sets for estimating causal effects 26 . Phase 2: expert opinion and consensus-building First round interview The proposed DAG model, developed by the research team from ESC-DAGs, underwent validation and consensus-building through expert input. In this phase, the Delphi method was employed, involving two rounds of interviews. Twelve experts from diverse backgrounds, including public health specialists, epidemiologists, nutritionist and clinical medicine professionals (endocrinologists and cardiologists), were selected. During the first round of the Delphi method, the researcher individually met with each expert to prevent dominance or power biases that could arise in a collective meeting. In the interview sessions, the researcher initially presented the study’s purpose and provided an explanation of the DAGs, including terminology, key types of variables in the DAGs, and essential concepts (as presented in Appendix 2 in the supplement file). Following this, the researcher presented the draft DAG model for premature CVD mortality from ESC-DAGs, inviting reviewers’ comments and suggestions. This included considerations such as adding or removing nodes or directional paths in the proposed DAG. A structured questionnaire guided the discussion, evaluating suggested variables, alignment with Bradford Hill criteria, and the appropriateness of node combinations. Detailed questions can be found in the supplement file (Appendix 3 ). Second round interview After completing interviews with all 12 experts, the research team collected their feedback. Some causal paths may have appeared contradictory, and others received varied suggestions or may have been solely proposed by individual experts. To address this, a second round of interviews aimed to achieve agreement on each causal path and reach a final consensus. During this phase, the researcher listed all the causal paths from the original ESC-DAGs model and other additional suggestion of path as suggested by each expert panel. The online survey system was developed by the researcher using Google Forms. For each question, a 7-point Likert scale (ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree) was used to indicate the degree of agreement. Each question included an illustration of the DAG to provide clarity to the experts. Fuzzy Delphi method All reviewer feedback from the online survey was extracted and cleaned using R software. The final consensus from all experts was calculated using the Fuzzy Delphi Method (FDM). FDM, introduced by Kaufman and Gupta in 1988 27 , is a modified measurement approach that combines the fuzzy numbering set with the Delphi method 28 . This method, known for saving time and cost in handling questionnaires, allows experts to consistently provide their views 29 . A minimum of 10 experts is recommended for Fuzzy Delphi studies to ensure high uniformity among experts 30 , 31 and including 12 experts in this study aligns with that recommendation. In general, the FDM relies on two essential conditions. First, for the Triangular Fuzzy Number (TFN), the threshold ( d ) must be ≤ 0.2 to signify expert agreement 32 , 33 . Traditionally, if the expert group agreement exceeds 75%, it is considered accepted 28 , 34 . The second condition involves the Defuzzification Process, where the fuzzy ( A ) score value is determined based on the α-cut value of 0.5. If the fuzzy score value ( A ) is equal to or greater than 0.5, the measured item is accepted; if it falls below 0.5, the item is rejected 35 , 36 . These prerequisites form the foundation of the FDM, ensuring a robust and agreed-upon decision-making process. The detailed steps for FDM are as follows: Step 1: Convert Likert scale into a TFN number (m1, m2, m3) as shown in Table 1 . The TFN is expressed in the form (m1, m2, m3), where m1 is the minimum, m2 is the acceptable value, and m3 is the maximum value. This fuzzy number serves as a basis for interpreting linguistic variables as fuzzy numbers, similar to a Likert scale 37 . In this stage, TFN values for each item (causal path statement) were computed by each expert (n1, n2, n3). Subsequently, fuzzy average values (m1, m2, m3) were calculated from all experts to determine threshold values ( d ), expert consensus percentage, defuzzification, and item ranking. Step 2: Calculate the threshold value ( d ) to assess the degree of agreement among experts. The d value is determined using this formula 33 ; a value of d ≤ 0.2 indicates a substantial consensus or agreement among all experts regarding the item 32 , 33 . Step 3: Calculate the percentage of expert agreement or consensus percentage for each item. An item is considered agreed upon if the expert agreement percentage is ≥ 75% 28,34 . Step 4: Perform the Defuzzification Process to determine the fuzzy ( A ) score value using the provided formula. If the fuzzy score value ( A ) is equal to or greater than 0.5, the measured item is accepted 35 , 36 . The calculations for the FDM in steps 1–4 were performed using an Excel spreadsheet. To facilitate this process, the researcher created a template based on TFN specifically designed for a 7-point Likert scale. This template is applicable to other studies if they used TFN based on 7 Likert scale. The Excel template can be downloaded from this link ( https://github.com/shakirarodzlan/Constructing-DAG.git ). The outcomes of the second-round interviews using the FDM are summarized in a table. This table includes a list of all causal paths, their respective TFN, the calculated d and A values for each causal path, and the decision on whether to include or remove the causal path. Utilizing this information, the researcher refined the DAG model, presenting it as the final version derived from evidence synthesis and expert consensus. This proposed model is recommended for epidemiological studies, specifically focusing on the causal pathway for premature CVD mortality in the Malaysia setting. Additionally, based on this DAG model, we propose the minimal adjustment set that should be adjusted to identify the total effect of a certain exposure toward premature CVD mortality, as suggested by DAGitty website tools. Phase 3: testing DAG model with available data Based on the final ESC-DAGs model with expert consensus from phase 2, the researcher proceeded to test the proposed difference DAG models feasible with available data. This involved comparing model rankings by the expert domain with model accuracy from the data. The details of the data source and steps for this phase are described below. Data source and study variables Data for this study were obtained from the Malaysia’s National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) conducted in 2006 38 , 2011 39 , and 2015 40 , linked with mortality records. The study followed individuals aged 18 to 70 for up to 15 years, linking NHMS data to the mortality registry until December 31, 2021, with causes of death classified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision 41 . The outcome variable is premature CVD mortality (ages 30–70), defined as premature mortality following the World Health Organization (WHO) definition 42 . The details description about this data including definition for each variable can be found in our previous work 43 . For the purpose of this analysis, this study includes ICD-10 codes for major CVDs, including IHD (ICD-10 codes I20–25) and stroke (ICD-10 codes I60–69). Factors considered in the DAG included socio-demographic variables (sex, age, ethnicity, location, education level, household income level, occupation, and marital status) and modifiable risk or NCD risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, obesity, abdominal obesity, current smoking, and physical inactivity). For diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidaemia, we classified them as normal, diagnosed, or undiagnosed. Preparation for feasible DAG models with data The researcher initiated the preparation for feasible DAGs models referencing the DAG models from phase 1 and phase 2, and ensuring their compatibility with NHMS data. Three different DAG models were proposed, we drawing it using DAGitty web-based tool. For each model, we set the nodes for diabetes (DM), hypertension (HPT), hyperlipidaemia (HPL) as exposure, and premature CVD mortality as the outcome node to determine the total effect of that exposure toward the outcome. For each model, we draw with different other related exposure nodes, including unmodifiable, behavioural risk factors, and other factors that feasible to adjust with available data (NHMS). DAGitty was used to obtain the adjustment set for different DAG models where different DAG models result in different adjustment sets. The three DAG models, along with their respective adjustment sets, are presented in table for model ratings by experts. Comparing DAG model ratings by experts with data accuracy The same panel of 12 experts who participated in the previous Delphi method was invited to rate the proposed three DAG models. The researcher sought their agreement based on three questions: (1) the validity of the proposed DAG model in representing causal relationships leading to premature CVD mortality, (2) the feasibility of implementing the DAG model in a population-based study, and (3) their agreement on the practicality and suitability of the suggested minimal adjustment set. Ratings were collected on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The order rank was determined using a similar process to TFN, and the A value was additionally ranked to establish the overall ranking for each element (validity, feasibility, and adjustment set) as perceived by the experts. For data accuracy, we utilized the available data (NHMS data linked with the mortality file) to run logistic regression models in R software. Using the suggested adjustment set by DAGitty, we conducted three different logistic models with exposures DM, HPT, and HPL towards the outcome (premature CVD mortality status). Model validity and fitness were assessed using AIC values, area under the ROC (AUC), pseudo R 2 , and confusion matrix (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and precision) for model comparison. The results, including adjusted odd ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for each exposure (DM, HPT, HPL) based on three different adjustment sets, together with results for model validity and fitness, will be presented in a table. Subsequently, we compared the model accuracy between the regression models (data) and the models ranked by experts. Results Phase 1 (DAG model from ESC-DAG protocol) Mapping The initial DAG draft followed the ESC-DAGs protocol, encompassing mapping, translation, and integration. Prior to mapping, we outline framework-related variables for premature CVD mortality, as depicted in Fig. 3 . We carefully identify related variables influencing premature CVD mortality through evidence synthesis, including socio-demographic elements, non-modifiable factors, modifiable risk factors (behavioural and NCDs), treatment and control, and other factors such as environmental conditions and access to medical care that might be causally linked to premature CVD mortality (Appendix 1 , Table S1 , S2 ). Subsequently, we create an implant graph (IG) through mapping, connecting exposures, outcomes, and relevant factors to construct an implied graph, as illustrated in Fig. 4 a. During this stage, the directional arch does not matter; what is important is our confidence in the presence of an association. Translation During the translation stage, we assess whether each posited connection is feasible, thus ‘translating’ the IG into a DAG. Each directed edge in the IG was assessed against three causal criteria based on Bradford Hill’s viewpoints (temporality, face validity, and recourse to theory) and a counterfactual thought experiment. The detailed explanation and results are provided below; Temporality: Following the Bradford Hill criteria, temporality indicates that the effect cannot occur before the cause. For example, premature CVD mortality or any death should not precede any other variable; there should be no arrow pointing from premature CVD mortality. Similarly, unmodifiable factors like age, sex, and ethnicity should not have arrows connecting them, and no other factors should cause these variables. Moreover, temporality suggests that the influence from sociodemographic factors and socioeconomic status (SES) may affect behavioural risk factors, NCD comorbidities, and CVD mortality. Face validity and recourse to theory: During this stage, the researcher assessed face validity in terms of biologic plausibility and evidence supporting each relationship within the model. Implausible relationships were identified, taking into account the temporality for each directed edge. Relevant relationships were retained, while certain implausible ones (directed edges) were removed. For example: (1) directed edges from ethnicity to occupation status, education level, and place of residence were removed; (2) the arrow from sex to alcohol intake and treatment/control was removed; (3) an arrow was added from ethnicity to genetic, and from genetic to family history, and so forth. Counterfactual thought experiment: Fundamentally compares what would happen if everyone in the sample were exposed to the outcome versus what would happen if none were exposed 12 , 13 . For example, we employ a counterfactual thought experiment to assess how diabetes (coded as 1 for diabetes, 0 for non-diabetes) affects CVD events (present or absent). We compare outcomes for all participants when set as diabetes or non-diabetes. The key question is whether the likelihood of a CVD event is the same for potential outcomes under diabetes and non-diabetes. Based on considerations of temporality, face validity, recourse to theory, and causal thinking in the counterfactual thought experiment, we hypothesize that the likelihood of a CVD event (the potential outcomes) would vary between the two counterfactual exposures (diabetes status). Therefore, we retained a directed edge from diabetes to CVD events. All directed edges from the implant graph underwent testing in the counterfactual thought experiment, alongside other causality criteria. Overall, during the translation stage, we defined causal links and dependencies between variables, transforming the implant graph into a DAG (Fig. 4 b). Integration The integration stage included two key processes: Integration 1, where each directed edge from the index was synthesized until exhaustion, allowing for the introduction of new nodes and potential directed edges. At this stage, directed edges not in the index or variables not identified in the literature search but deemed important were considered for addition as new nodes to the I-DAG. For example, we added nodes for other chronic diseases and air pollution, with these new relationships assessed using procedures similar to those used during the translation process. Integration 2 focused on simplifying the initial DAG. In this phase, we combined variables (nodes) with similar input/output or theoretical support. Nodes related to education level, occupation status, income level, and area of residence were grouped into one node labelled as SES. Additionally, nodes for genetic and family history were combined. This simplification aimed to capture the most essential causal pathways and dependencies. The final I-DAG is presented in Fig. 4 c. Phase 2 (Delphi method) First round interview In this phase, the I-DAG from ECS-DAG (Fig. 4 c) that was presented to each expert domain received a few suggestions and comments that improved our DAG. For example, some experts suggested (1) adding a node for hormonal influence as a mediator from sex to CVD and other NCDs, (2) adding a node for cultural norms/belief between the directional arch for ethnicity and alcohol intake, (3) adding a factor for health literacy and other chronic diseases, (4) adding the role for smoking cessation after receiving treatment and having CVD complications that can reduce the probability of premature CVD mortality, and so forth. For each node we added, the translation process, as in ECS-DAG, was applied. The updated DAG after suggestions from experts was presented in Fig. 5 . Second round interview All the suggestions from experts in the first-round interview were collected, and as expected, some causal paths were solely proposed by individual experts, while others received varied suggestions. Therefore, during this second-round interview, our goal was to achieve agreement on each causal path from all experts. All the causal paths from the original ESC-DAG model and additional suggestions of paths as proposed by each expert panel were listed, totalling 80 paths or statements. Based on these causal statements, we obtained agreement from each expert domain using an online survey. As an example, one question asked, “To what extent do you agree that ethnicity influences cultural norms/belief/practice, affecting alcohol use? (Direction: ethnicity → cultural norms/belief/practice → alcohol use).” The detailed set of all 80 questions can be found in the supplementary file (Appendix 4 ). Final consensus from fuzzy Delphi method Eleven out of the 12 experts involved in the first-round interview responded to the online survey during this second-round interview. Table 2 presents the findings of the analysis using FDM, rated by these experts for 80 questions about causal statements. In Table 2 , m1, m2, m3 is average values of TFN where m1 is the minimum, m2 is the acceptable value, and m3 is the maximum value. The threshold value d is used to assess the degree of agreement among the experts. The results reveal that 35 causal statements did not exceed the percentage of expert agreement (&lt; 75%) with a threshold d value ≥ 0.2. We dropped four causal paths from ethnicity mediated by cultural norms/belief toward smoking (Q3), physical activity (Q5), and awareness and screening (Q6) and also the direct causal link between ethnicity and SES (Q7). The suggestion to rename the node from hormonal influence to sex hormone and fat distribution did not get consensus from experts (36.5% agreed), thus we retained the original name hormone influence (sex hormone). Three questions (Q15–17) about the direct causal link between sex and NCDs and CVD (without mediation by sex hormones and fat distribution) did not meet the expert consensus and were expected to be removed. Additionally, five causal paths originating from sex toward behavioural risk (Q19-22) and sex link with different treatment and control (Q18) were removed. Regarding age, five causal paths from age to HPL (Q25), obesity (Q26), dietary pattern (Q31), health literacy (Q33), and awareness and screening (Q34) did not pass expert consensus and were removed from our DAG. Regarding smoking, we removed the path from smoking to DM (Q55) and HPL (Q57), and the mediation effect of dietary pattern from smoking to obesity (Q58) also did not exceed the percentage agreement from experts. Additionally, the node for smoking cessation as a mediator from smoking toward CVD complications was removed. During the first-round interview, one expert suggested adding a causal link between physical activity and dietary patterns, but this causal path did not pass overall agreement from other experts, and it was removed. As a consensus from the majority of experts, we also removed direct causal link of alcohol intake toward DM and HPL (Q64-65) and the factor for air pollution directly linked to CVD events (Q69). We also sought expert agreement regarding the combination of SES from a few nodes (education level, income level, occupation status, and living area) in questions (Q40-42). Although it did not exceed 75% of percentage agreement, most experts agreed on the combination of these nodes as one SES node, rather than a separate node. So, we proceeded with our original proposed DAG presenting SES as a combination. For the question about the combination of genetic and family history (Q35), we decided to retain it, even though the percentage of agreement did not exceed 75%. This decision was made because experts almost unanimously agreed (almost 100%) on the question about the causal link between the nodes (family history + genetic) toward NCDs (DM, HPT, HPL, CVD, and obesity) (Q36-38). Furthermore, the average fuzzy number exceeded 0.5. The updated DAG is presented in Fig. 6 and available online ( https://dagitty.net/mDbAXtYkt ). This represents our final DAG derived from evidence synthesis and expert opinion. Suggested adjustment set As one of our objectives is to propose a DAG model for generalized use in any epidemiological study of CVD in Malaysia, we derived various adjustment sets from our final DAG model (Fig. 6 ) to assess the total effect of each selected exposure variable on the outcome of premature CVD mortality. We utilized the DAGitty web-based tool to suggest minimal adjustment sets. Table 3 shows the suggested minimal adjustment sets if we select certain variables for exposure toward the outcome of premature CVD mortality. For example, if we wanted to estimate the total effect of diabetes on premature CVD mortality, the suggested minimal adjustment set would include age, dietary pattern, genetic/family history, hormonal influence (sex hormones), and physical activity. If we want to estimate the total effect of alcohol intake on CVD mortality outcome, the minimal adjustment set would include either (1) cultural norms/belief, health literacy, or (2) dietary pattern, ethnicity, health literacy or (3) dietary pattern, genetic/family history, health literacy, whenever those variables are measurable. The other suggested adjustment sets can be seen in Table 3 . Phase 3 (test DAG model from expert ranked vs. data accuracy) Model from expert ranked Some suggested minimal adjustment sets from evidence synthesis and expert consensus (Table 3 ) may not be feasible (unmeasured) in our available data (NHMS data). For example, variables such as family history/genetics, dietary patterns, health literacy, access to medical care, CVD (IHD/Stroke), treatment and control, and CVD events were not measured. Thus, based on the DAG model from ESC-DAG (Fig. 4 c) and the final DAG from expert opinion (Fig. 6 ), we proposed three DAG models feasible with our NHMS data. These models are specifically designed to assess the total effect of comorbid NCDs exposures (DM, HPT, HPL) on the outcome of premature CVD mortality. Table 4 shows these three different models with distinct minimal adjustment sets proposed by DAGitty. Adjustments for DAG Model 1 include age, sex, SES (combined node from education and income level), obesity, and smoking. DAG Model 2 adjusts for age, sex, SES (combined node from education, income, occupation, and living area), abdominal obesity, and smoking. DAG Model 3 incorporates age, sex, ethnicity, education, occupation, income, living area (separate node for SES), abdominal obesity, smoking, and alcohol intake. In each model, biasing paths are marked in red. Potential confounding factors or paths that may introduce bias into the estimation of the total effect of exposures (DM, HPL, HPT) on the outcome (premature CVD mortality) are identified. Essentially, uncontrolled paths in the causal network could impact the observed relationship. DAGitty suggests several minimal sufficient adjustments sets for each model. These sets of variables are recommended for inclusion in the analysis to control for confounding. We asked the same experts to rate these models in terms of feasibility, accuracy, and agreement about the suggested adjustment set. Using a similar calculation with FDM based on a 7-point Likert Scale and TFN, we obtained the final ranking of these models. The results from expert ratings are presented in Table 5 . All the models achieved an acceptable threshold d value (&lt; 0.2) for each element (model validity, feasibility, and agreement for the adjustment set). Model 1 (the simplest DAG model) received 100% agreement and was ranked first (based on the average fuzzy score) by experts for the feasibility of implementing this model for a population-based survey but ranked 3rd for validity and agreement for the adjustment set. Model 2 achieved &gt; 75% agreement from all experts in all elements; however, in terms of ranking, Model 2 received the 2nd and 3rd rankings. Model 3 received the first rank for model validity and suggested adjustment set and achieved 91.7% total agreement in terms of model validity (Table 5 ). Model accuracy from available data The baseline characteristics of this data (NHMS data linked with the mortality file) are available in the supplemental file (Appendix 5 , Table S3 ). For each proposed DAG model (outlined in Table 4 ), we conducted multiple logistic regression analyses based on the suggested adjustment set. All the factor variables are available from the data, except for SES. To derive a composite SES measure, we used a regression model with factors such as education level, income status, occupation, and living area. Each factor contributes to the SES score, with weights determined through regression coefficients. DAG Models 1 and 2 share a single SES variable, while DAG Model 3 has a separate SES variable. The summary results for the logistic regression models are presented in Table 6 . Among the three models, Model 3 might be the better model based on several evaluation metrics. It has the lowest AIC value, indicating a good balance between fit and complexity. Model 3 also achieves the highest AUC at 82.02%, indicating better discrimination ability. Additionally, the pseudo R 2 for Model 3 is the highest among the models, emphasizing its superior goodness of fit. In terms of the confusion matrix metrics Model 3 has competitive values for sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, and precision. While the differences are not substantial, the slight improvements in these metrics might contribute to Model 3 being a slightly better choice. Thus, Model 3, with a broad set of adjustments including ethnicity, education, occupation, income, living area, abdominal obesity, smoking, and alcohol intake, stands out as the best choice. Both expert rankings and logistic regression accuracy support its validity in estimating causal model for premature CVD mortality. Discussion DAGs serve as a valuable tool for graphically representing researchers’ assumptions about the causal structure among variables, guiding the selection of confounding variables in a study. The theory and ‘background knowledge’ play a central role in DAG construction 15 , 19 . In the absence of a standardized protocol for DAG construction, this paper proposed a systematic development of a causal model that integrates both evidence synthesis, expert opinion and model assessment, demonstrating a step-by-step process for constructing a causal pathway for premature CVD mortality. Although our DAG model from ESC-DAGs undergoes a systematic process based on evidence synthesis and counterfactual thought from a causal inference approach, it is limited by researcher knowledge. This paper demonstrates that involving expert opinion is necessary to enhance the DAG. However, expert opinions may vary, conflicts may arise, and obtaining their agreement and consensus poses challenges. The FDM aids in reaching consensus on causal paths. Although FDM is commonly used to obtain expert consensus in determining elements for model design, its application in constructing DAG models, particularly in epidemiology, is novel. The capability of fuzzy theory to handle the vagueness factor, utilizing the flexibility of TFNs, motivates us to employ FDM as a consensus-building tool with experts. When using FDM, careful attention to selecting expert domains is crucial. The literature discusses the debate between homogeneous and heterogeneous samples. Existing literature suggests that a diverse panel enhances performance by allowing a broader range of alternatives and perspectives 44 – 47 . However, achieving consensus within a large, heterogeneous sample proves challenging and research suggests that the most reliable Delphi study samples are small, typically involving fewer than 20 participants 46 , 48 . In our study, the inclusion of a somewhat heterogeneous group of 12 experts, with diverse backgrounds from public health and clinical expertise, aligns with the literature. While no causal model is perfect, combining evidence synthesis with expert opinion proves superior to constructing it solely by the researcher. If this DAG were a valid representation of causality, it would suggest that very few of the measured variables actually need adjustment in the premature CVD mortality model. Referring to Table 3 , our final DAG model serves as a valuable guide for researchers in selecting adjustment variables, where different minimal adjustment sets are suggested for difference selected exposures towards the same outcome (premature CVD mortality). Thus, in assessing the effects of different exposures, some adjustments may cause more harm than good. Furthermore, it challenges the common practice in clinical research of reporting ‘independent risk factors’ after adjusting for several variables through regression 49 , considering them as causal. In epidemiology, selecting adjustment variables involves two main approaches: background knowledge-based and statistics-based. DAGs play a crucial role in the background knowledge approach, serving as a visual representation of causal assumptions and aiding in identifying adjustments for confounding and biases 11 , 14 , 26 , 50 . In this study, we utilized the DAGItty web-based tool 25 , employing an algorithm based on the backdoor criteria concept, to determine the suggested minimal adjustment set. This set comprises covariates that, through adjustment, stratification, or selection, minimize bias when estimating the causal effect of the exposure on the outcome 26 . For example, revisiting the DAG in Fig. 6 , the minimal adjustment set for the dietary pattern towards premature CVD mortality (Table 3 ) could be either: (1) alcohol intake, ethnicity and health literacy, (2) alcohol intake, genetic/family history and health literacy, or (3) cultural norms/belief and health literacy. Each set is sufficient because it closes all biasing paths and leaves the causal path open. Additionally, DAGItty is capable of accommodating scenarios where variables in the adjustment set are unmeasured. For example, if genetic and family history is unmeasured, DAGItty provides options 1 or 3 for adjustment without the unmeasured node. However, if the measured variable for health literacy is absent (needed in all three options), the adjustment set is insufficient, and biasing paths are open. DAGItty will suggest “no adjustment set found.” This example highlights the importance of establishing causal assumptions (drawing the DAG) before data collection and analysis to ensure that important variables for adjustment are not overlooked. Traditionally, experiments are the gold standard for investigating causal relationships. However, questions about the causal mechanisms of diseases often cannot be studied in randomized trials, leading us to rely on observational studies. Some of these studies use available secondary data. To make valid causal inferences from observational data, addressing confounding is crucial. In phase 3 of this study, we show how to suggest realistic models using available data. We compare models ranked by experts with their accuracy from the data. In this phase, we try to draw the DAG, with the minimal adjustment set that could be captured in our in-hand data. Both expert rankings and accuracy results from data (logistic regression model) support its validity to estimate a causal effect for premature CVD mortality. This demonstration highlights that while challenges with unmeasured variables exist, estimating the causal effect is not impossible. Another approach commonly employed in situations involving unobserved confounders is the Instrumental Variable Regression method. DAGitty can find instrumental variables but this technique depends on linearity assumptions. Information about instrumental variables and instructions on how to use them with DAGitty can be found in the existing literature 26 , 51 , 52 . Limitation No matter what method used to build a DAGs from observational studies, the true causal structures may be missed since no causal model is perfect. However, combining both literature-driven DAGs and expert-driven DAGs promotes a more robust and realistic representation of the causal model under study. Additionally, another limitation of this study is that the observational data (NHMS) used in Phase 3 involved a reliance on self-reported data for key variables such as known DM, HPT, HPL, smoking, and physical activity. This reliance may introduce recall bias or underreporting, potentially impacting the accuracy of our causal model. Furthermore, while this study utilized a panel of 12 experts, which meets the minimum requirement for Delphi studies, a larger panel could provide a more robust consensus and a broader range of expertise. Additionally, cultural, socioeconomic, and healthcare system differences may limit the generalizability of the DAG model to other settings. The iterative refinement process based on expert input may also lead to overfitting, reducing the model’s applicability and accuracy in different populations. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings of our study. Other than that, this paper is limited to focusing on how to construct DAGs, suggest selecting adjustment variables, and apply this model using available data. DAGs have broad applicability, widely used to understand the impact on causal effect estimates when different variables are conditioned upon—for example, mediation 16 , confounding, selection bias, and collider bias 17 . A crucial aspect to emphasize is the concept of collider bias, a common concern in epidemiology that can be challenging to identify. Collider-conditioning bias occurs when the association between an exposure and an outcome is altered by conditioning on a third factor influenced by both the exposure and the outcome 20 . Drawing DAGs can assist in identifying collider bias and facilitating researchers in avoiding or correcting for such bias. Therefore, further research could extend the construction of DAGs from evidence synthesis and expert opinion as a basis for exploring collider bias, or other specific topics such as selection bias, interaction, or effect modification within the conventional DAG framework. Conclusion In summary, our step-by-step process for constructing a causal pathway for premature CVD mortality offers a systematic, practical, and transparent approach, contributing to DAG methodology. Emphasizing the importance of theory, ‘background knowledge,’ and expert domain input in DAGs construction, our proposed model provides an alternative to traditional variable selection in adjusting for analysis. Researchers can use this approach as an additional tool for selecting adjustment variables when analyzing epidemiological data. By combining ESC-DAGs and expert opinion, our approach may stimulate further debate on how researchers can use DAGs for enhanced population health research.
