ABSTRACT Data addressing safety concerns related to potential drug interactions between cannabis‐derived products and pharmaceutical medications in the pediatric population are lacking. In this study, we retrieved case reports through a published literature search using PubMed and spontaneous reporting data using the Food and Drug Administration's Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) to identify potential cannabis– and cannabinoid–drug interactions in individuals younger than 18 years old. To evaluate the published case reports, we used the Drug Interaction Probability Scale (DIPS), a 10‐item questionnaire designed to discern the causal relationship between a potential drug interaction and adverse drug reactions (ADRs). FAERS reports were deduplicated and analyzed to gather information regarding patient demographics, associated drugs, nature of the ADRs, outcomes, professions of the reporters, and reporting timelines. Seven published case reports and 9142 FAERS ADRs reports were included in the final analysis. Based on the findings, caution is warranted when cannabis or cannabinoids are used in combination with prescribed medications, including methadone, everolimus, fluoxetine, and paroxetine. Cannabinoids may inhibit drug‐metabolizing enzymes, including several cytochrome P450s, leading to increased drug exposure and potentially, an increased risk for ADRs. Introduction Compared with adult cannabis users, children and young adults who use cannabis are at increased risk of neurologic harm, including memory difficulties, psychosis, and cannabis use disorder, due to continued brain development in these age groups [ 1 ]. In the United States, medical marijuana is commonly prescribed for pediatric patients with epilepsy, autism spectrum disorder, and spasticity, among other conditions, [ 2 ] whereas recreational cannabis is currently illegal for individuals aged less than 21 years due to the potential for neurologic harm. Nonetheless, in 2021, approximately 2.7 million adolescents aged 12–17 years reported using cannabis recreationally [ 3 ]. Despite the high cannabis use among adolescents, data regarding potential interactions between cannabis and pharmaceutical drugs and the resulting potential harm remain lacking. The two most widely studied constituents in cannabis are the cannabinoids cannabidiol (CBD) and delta‐9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (THC). CBD is metabolized primarily by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4, [ 4 ] whereas THC is metabolized primarily by CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 [ 5 ]. As such, inhibitors of these enzymes can increase cannabinoid systemic exposure and risk of neurologic harm. Both cannabinoids, particularly CBD, have also been reported to inhibit these enzymes in vitro and in vivo [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ]. Accordingly, using cannabis while taking pharmaceutical drugs can lead to increased systemic exposure to the object drug and potential toxicity. For example, the antiseizure medication, clobazam, is converted to the active metabolite N ‐desmethylclobazam by CYP3A4, which is converted to an inactive metabolite by CYP2C19. When CBD is co‐consumed with clobazam, circulating concentrations of both clobazam and N ‐desmethylclobazam increase, leading to increased adverse events compared with the absence of CBD [ 9 ]. Ultimately, drug interactions with cannabis or cannabinoids can lead to heightened adverse events, negatively impacting a young person's mental, social, and school performance. The objective of this study was to increase knowledge and awareness of potential cannabis– or cannabinoid–drug interactions in pediatric individuals due to the high risk of harm. The aims were to (1) conduct a literature search to review potential pediatric cannabis– and cannabinoid–drug interactions discussed in case reports, (2) complete a causality assessment from the case reports, and (3) search the US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database to identify potential cannabis– and cannabinoid–drug interactions that had been voluntarily reported. Results highlight the need for continued research on this critical public health problem. 2. Methods 2.1. Case Reports Analysis A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed from the start of the database (1966) to July 2023 using an established query (Appendix S1 ). The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) case reports only, (2) available in English, (3) person was less than 18 years old, and (4) a potential adverse event from a cannabis– or cannabinoid–drug interaction was discussed. An example search string is shown below. The search strategy consisted of using ‘AND’ logic with three components: (1) case report, (2) drug interaction, and (3) a comprehensive list of cannabis‐related products. The full string can be seen in Appendix S1 . Sample Search String: Case Reports [PT] AND “drug interactions”[All Fields] AND (“ Cannabis sativa ” [All Fields] OR “CBD” [All Fields] OR “Hemp” [All Fields] OR …). Next, the Drug Interaction Probability Scale (DIPS), a 10‐item questionnaire, was used to assess the epistemological probability of a causal relationship between a potential drug interaction and an adverse event for each case report by two pharmacists (Appendix S2 ) [ 10 ]. The DIPS tool evaluates factors such as metabolic pathways, the potential for metabolism and transporter inhibition or induction, standard pharmacokinetic parameters, concentration–response relationships, and indicators of therapeutic response and adverse events. DIPS scores are categorized as follows: &lt; 2 (doubtful), 2–4 (possible), 5–8 (probable), and &gt; 8 (highly probable). Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved with a third reviewer. 2.2. FAERS Evaluation Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) associated with cannabis or a cannabinoid voluntarily reported to FAERS from January 2004 to June 2022 were extracted using both Latin binomials and common names while accommodating character variations (Appendices S3 and S4 ) [ 11 ]. A four‐step algorithm was used to remove duplicates from the ADR report: (1) singling out cases with identical identifiers but different versions; (2) identifying cases referencing the same literature citation (because many FAERS reports include a citation), along with the reported drugs, reactions, age, and sex; (3) applying the World Health Organization's likelihood‐based vigiMatch algorithm to discern FAERS cases marked as duplicates; [ 12 ] and (4) visually inspecting the remaining cases to filter out duplicates. Following deduplication, a descriptive analysis was conducted on the cannabis‐ or cannabinoid‐related cases to elucidate details regarding patient demographics, associated drugs, nature of the ADRs, outcomes, professions of the reporters, and reporting timelines. FAERS reports that specifically declared “drug interaction” as the preferred term were closely analyzed. 3. Results 3.1. Case Reports Analysis The PubMed search revealed seven pediatric case reports that met the inclusion criteria. Tables 1 and 2 describe the case reports analysis for potential cannabis– and cannabinoid–drug interactions and the corresponding DIPS scores. Table 1 provides an overview of the case reports exhibiting a probable or possible DIPS score, whereas Table 2 includes case reports with a doubtful DIPS score. The case reports comprised individuals aged between 6 and 17 years. The included case reports were published between 1997 and 2022. Only two had a previous credible report of the same interaction occurring in humans. 3.2. FAERS Evaluation Figure 1 shows the inclusion and exclusion criteria for FAERS reports included in the analysis. The total number of reports extracted from the database for cannabis products (based on a list of product names shown in Appendix S3 ) and CBD products (shown in Appendix S4 ) was 8674 and 468, respectively. These two lists also contain names that include THC. We included these products because we could not determine if the names, which seemed to be the result of a standardization process by the FDA, were hierarchical or were combination products. The FDA was contacted regarding this issue of cannabis and CBD strings including THC, but response was not received. After deduplication and filtering for reports of interactions in pediatric patients, seven reports related to cannabis remained. Table 3 describes the FAERS reports for cannabis– and cannabinoid–drug interactions in adolescents. Most of the reports originated from the United States (268/404; 66.3%) and consisted of individuals aged between 13 and 17 years (335; 82.9%). Physicians (165/404; 40.8%) and consumers (102/404; 25.2%) were the most common reporters. The top ten reported ADRs included drug abuse (22.0%), completed suicide (10.4%), toxicity to various agents (9.2%), substance abuse (7.4%), cardiac arrest (6.9%), overdose (6.9%), respiratory arrest (6.7%), somnolence (5.7%), fetal exposure during pregnancy (5.7%), and cardiorespiratory arrest (5.7%). Frequently reported outcomes included serious‐important medical event (209/404; 51.7%), hospitalization (164/404; 40.6%), and death (131/404; 32.4%). Seven cannabis FAERS reports mentioned “drug interaction” as the preferred term (Table 3 ). Regarding the cannabinoid reports from FAERS, 16/17 (94.1%) originated from the United States and 14/17 (82.4%) and were reported by consumers. Most of these reports involved children aged between 1 and 12 years (11/17; 64.7%), while adolescents 13 to 17 years represented 5/17 (29.4%) reports. The top three ADRs were seizure (23.5%), headache (17.6%), and insomnia (17.6%). Common outcomes included serious‐important medical event (8/17; 47.1%), hospitalization (6/17; 35.3%), unknown (5/17; 29.4%), required intervention to prevent permanent impairment/damage (3/17; 17.6%), life‐threatening (2/17; 11.8%), death (1/17; 5.9%), and disability (1/17; 5.9%). 4. Discussion Our literature review of case reports identified several potential cannabis– or cannabinoid–drug interactions with plausible mechanisms, with three reports having a probable DIPS score suggesting causality. These reports included potential interactions between CBD and methadone, everolimus, and fluoxetine. A brief summary of what is known about each possible interaction is described below. Further scientific investigation is required to obtain a thorough understanding. It is imperative that clinicians provide patients with comprehensive education regarding these interactions while closely monitoring the incorporation of other drugs and natural products, including botanical dietary supplements. Methadone is an opioid prescribed commonly for opioid use disorder and pain in minors and adults. Accidental methadone poisoning is often a concern in adolescents because ingestion of small doses (0.5–1 mg/kg) could lead to toxicity and death [ 28 ]. A case report of a 13‐year‐old female who was taking methadone for chronic pain due to metastatic cancer suffered from an interaction between methadone and CBD (Table 1 ). The patient reported increased fatigue and sleepiness. Her serum methadone concentration was 271 ng/mL 2 days following cessation of CBD, then subsequently decreased to 125 ng/mL 14 days postcessation. Methadone is metabolized principally by CYP 2B6, with minimal contribution by CYP3A4 [ 15 , 29 ]. Given that prior in vitro studies have shown CBD to be potent inhibitor of CYP2B6, this case report raised concern for a potentially severe interaction between CBD and methadone [ 5 , 6 , 17 ]. Everolimus, a chemotherapeutic agent, exhibited a DIPS score of 6 (probable). Based on the case report in a 6‐year‐old female, CBD reportedly increased everolimus blood trough concentrations; however, the exact mechanism for the purported interaction remains unclear [ 22 ]. To our knowledge, this case report is the first to mention a potential interaction between CBD and everolimus. There was one probable case report indicating a potential interaction between the antidepressant fluoxetine and CBD. One report involving fluoxetine and CBD had a DIPS score of 5 (probable), with adverse effects that included insomnia, increased agitation, hyperactivity, vocal yelling, and worsening obsessive–compulsive disorder. A potential mechanism of this interaction is that CBD inhibited the metabolism of fluoxetine by CYP2C9 [ 30 ]. Fluoxetine is also a potent inhibitor of CYP2D6 [ 31 ]. However, CBD does not inhibit CYP2D6 in vivo, so a plausible mechanism remains unknown [ 16 ]. To our knowledge, this case report is the first to describe a CBD‐fluoxetine interaction in adolescents. Potential interactions between cannabis and other antidepressants (desipramine, nortriptyline, paroxetine, and sertraline) were prevalent in seven case reports. These interactions resulted in numerous adverse effects, such as confusion, hallucinations, and irritability, which could cause performance issues during school and social activities. Although the reports had DIPS scores ranging from doubtful to probable, similar adverse effects were noted. With respect to the FAERS analysis, seven of the cannabis reports contained “drug interaction” as the preferred term. The drugs reported included pain medications, antipsychotics, and antiepileptics. Combinations of these drug classes with cannabis results in psychosis and seizures (Table 3 ). One report included paroxetine as the primary suspect drug potentially interacting with marijuana that caused hypotension. Another report contained paroxetine as a secondary suspect drug with hypotension as the preferred term. A case report in PubMed also mentioned hypotension when a 15‐year‐old female co‐consumed paroxetine while smoking marijuana [ 18 ]. Although the DIPS score was 1 (doubtful), an interaction between paroxetine and marijuana may be cautioned, as concomitant use can be life‐threatening and lead to hospitalization according to the FAERS outcome codes. To our knowledge, data suggesting a cannabis–paroxetine interaction in adolescents are nonexistent, warranting further investigation. There were no CBD reports from FAERS that mentioned “drug interaction” as the preferred term. As alluded above, several in vitro and some clinical studies have demonstrated that both CBD and THC inhibit multiple CYP enzymes; however, CBD appears to precipitate drug interactions more commonly than THC in clinical settings [ 19 ]. Factors including route and frequency of administration of a given cannabis product, as well as the dose of CBD and/or THC contained in the product, influence the likelihood of experiencing adverse interactions. Whether one of these cannabinoids alone is less likely to precipitate an interaction compared to a combination thereof is not known. 5. Limitations We sought to conduct a comprehensive literature search of PubMed. However, a broader search using other bibliographic databases such as EMBASE might have identified additional case reports. The DIPS score is reflective of the data available in the case report and could change if more data are provided. Reports from FAERS are anecdotal and, while useful for signaling potential issues, do not necessarily imply a causal association. Additionally, we were unable to determine which component of cannabis (e.g., CBD vs. THC) is the main precipitant of a reported interaction, due to the ambiguous and varied descriptions of the cannabis products in the reports. 6. Conclusions Cannabis– or cannabinoid–drug interactions can be harmful and life‐threatening in the pediatric population, prompting the impetus for this study. Using PubMed and FAERS, potential interactions involving methadone, everolimus, fluoxetine, and paroxetine were identified. Further investigation is needed to help address the lack of knowledge on these interactions, as well as lack of clinical decision support specific to this population. Author Contributions Maryann R.
