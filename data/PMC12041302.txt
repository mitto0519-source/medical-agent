Abstract ABSTRACT Background Suicide is a leading cause of death among adolescents, yet most adolescents who report suicidal ideation do not attempt nor die by suicide. To prevent injury and death, it is thus vital to explore factors potentiating the transition from suicidal ideation to suicidal attempt, especially in high-risk groups like sexual and gender minority (SGM) adolescents. Guided by an “ideation-to-action” framework, we explored risk factors for suicide attempt (ie, “painful and provocative experiences”) among adolescents reporting suicidal ideation by SGM status. Methods Data were drawn from the cross-sectional 2022 Minnesota Student Survey and restricted to 10 561 adolescents reporting past-year suicidal ideation (52.3% SGM; 47.7% non-SGM). Analyses first assessed prevalence of exposure to painful and provocative experiences (ie, non-suicidal self-injury, adverse childhood experiences, bullying) by SGM status. Then, in multivariable logistic regression models, analyses probed associations between these risk factors and past-year suicide attempt. Results Among adolescents reporting recent suicidal ideation, a higher proportion of SGM adolescents reported past-year suicide attempt than non-SGM adolescents (26.7% vs 21.6%, respectively). All examined painful and provocative experiences were more commonly reported among SGM adolescents. In analyses restricted to SGM adolescents, non-suicidal self-injury, sexual abuse by a family member, and bullying demonstrated the largest effect sizes of associations with suicide attempt. Discussion Guided by an “ideation-to-action” framework, this research identifies risk factors potentiating suicide attempt in SGM adolescents reporting recent suicidal ideation. Structural and clinical interventions focused on mitigating exposure to painful and provocative experiences may help to prevent suicide among SGM adolescents. WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS This study leveraged data from a statewide, population-based health survey to study risk factors potentiating past-year suicide attempt among adolescents who reported past-year suicide ideation. This study showed that SGM adolescents experience a substantially higher prevalence of all assessed risk factors including non-suicidal self-injury, general and SGM-relevant bullying, and adverse childhood experiences. Among SGM adolescents reporting recent suicidal ideation, this study pinpointed several risk factors that heightened risk for suicide attempt with largest effect sizes for non-suicidal self-injury and sexual abuse by a family member. HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY Given their heightened exposure to risk factors potentiating suicide attempt, SGM adolescents reporting suicidal ideation are a priority population for targeted screening and intervention efforts. Mitigating exposure to adverse childhood experiences including abuse and neglect in the home as well as school bullying are vital strategies to prevent the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts among high-risk adolescents. Introduction “Ideation-to-action” frameworks posit that the development of suicidal ideation and the progression to a suicide attempt are distinct processes. 1 Some research suggests that “painful and provocative experiences,” involving exposure to violence and injury (eg, non-suicidal self-injury), may play a pivotal role in the progression from “ideation-to-action” by desensitising one to the fear associated with pain and death. 2 3 Across studies, exposure to painful and provocative experiences differentiates people who attempt suicide from those who only ideate about suicide. 4 Sexual and gender minority (SGM) adolescents report higher rates of suicide attempt 5 as well as painful provocative experiences 6 7 compared with non-SGM peers, but research leveraging an ideation-to-action approach in this population remains scarce. One study of 129 gender minority adolescents and young adults observed that painful and provocative experiences were significantly positively associated with lifetime suicide attempt history, even when controlling for suicidal ideation. 8 Another study involving 258 SGM young adults reporting recent suicidal ideation found that identity-based victimisation predicted suicide attempt over a 2 year follow-up period. 9 Similarly, qualitative research involving SGM young adults who made near-fatal suicide attempts indicated discrimination, bullying, and childhood abuse as significant factors in the progression from suicidal ideation to action. 10 Yet, this existing research has mostly relied on non-probability sampling approaches, which can introduce selection bias and may overestimate the prevalence of suicidal outcomes and victimisation. 11 Further, previous research has not compared between SGM and non-SGM samples; and most previous research has focused on young adults despite adolescence being a critical period for SGM identity development and the onset of suicidality. To overcome these limitations, we utilised data from a statewide, population-based, cross-sectional survey of adolescents. We restricted the sample to adolescents experiencing recent suicidal ideation and pursued three study aims: first, we evaluated the prevalence of painful and provocative experiences, including SGM-relevant risk factors, among SGM and non-SGM adolescents; second, we assessed differences in associations between these risk factors and recent suicide attempts by SGM status; and third, among SGM adolescents, we quantified the degree to which these risk factors differentiated those who made a recent suicide attempt from those who did not. Methods Sample Data were acquired from the 2022 Minnesota Student Survey, an anonymous, state-sponsored, population-based survey administered every 3 years during school hours to students in fifth, eighth, ninth and 11 th grades. 12 All primary and secondary schools in Minnesota are invited to participate; in 2022, 70% of Minnesota public school districts participated. The current study included eighth, ninth, and 11 th grade students who reported “yes, during the last year” to a question asking, “Have you ever seriously considered attempting suicide?” (N=12 343). Analyses were restricted to those with complete data for all studied variables (N=10 561). Demographic characteristics for the final sample are presented in table 1 . SGM respondents make up a small majority (52.3%) of the analytic sample. This is consistent with nationally representative survey data showing that SGM adolescents are estimated to account for 25% of the U.S. high school population and report three times greater prevalence of past-year suicidal ideation than non-SGM adolescents. 5 13 Sexual orientation, sex at birth, and gender identity Sexual orientation was assessed with a single question, “How do you describe your sexual orientation?” Sex at birth was assessed with the question, “What was your sex assigned at birth (as on your original birth certificate)?” and gender identity was assessed with the question, “What is your gender identity (mark ALL that apply)?” Respondents were classified as SGM if they indicated a non-heterosexual sexual orientation and/or if they reported a discordant sex at birth and gender identity. Painful and provocative experiences Non-suicidal self-injury Non-suicidal self-injury was assessed with the question, “During the last 12 months, how many times did you do something to purposely hurt or injure yourself without wanting to die, such as cutting, burning, scratching or bruising yourself on purpose?” and was coded as a binary variable flagging any exposure to past-year self-injury. Adverse childhood experiences Respondents reported if they currently live with an adult who drinks too much alcohol; has ever been incarcerated; uses illegal drugs or abuses prescription medications; has a mental illness; is verbally abusive; has ever been physically abusive; and/or has ever been physically abusive towards other adults in the home. Respondents also indicated lifetime exposure to sexual abuse by a family member as well as by non-family members. Binary variables flagged exposure to each assessed adverse childhood experience. Bullying Bullying was assessed with two distinct survey items. The first item asked students to indicate, on a scale from 1 (never) to 5 (every day), how frequently they had experienced each of the following types of bullying over the past 30 days: physical violence, threats, spreading rumours, sexual comments, and purposeful exclusion. The second item asked students to report, on the same 1-to-5 scale, how often they had been harassed or bullied in the past 30 days for identity-based reasons, including sexual orientation (“Because you are bisexual, gay, lesbian, asexual, pansexual, queer, or because someone thought you were”); sex or gender (“Your sex or gender (being male, female, transgender, genderqueer, genderfluid)”); and gender expression (“Your gender expression (your style, dress, or the way you walk or talk)”). Based on responses to these items, we created a three-level bullying variable with the following labels: (1) No bullying . Students did not report experiencing any bullying in the past 30 days. (2) Bullying unrelated to SGM identity . Students reported being bullied in the first item but did not indicate that the bullying was related to their sexual orientation, sex or gender, or gender expression. (3) SGM-relevant bullying . Students reported being bullied in the first item and attributed this bullying to their sexual orientation, sex or gender, or gender expression. Using a three-level approach allowed us to assess the role of SGM-relevant bullying as a painful and provocative experience separate from other kinds of bullying (ie, those unrelated to SGM identity). Suicide attempt Past-year suicide attempt was assessed through a question which asked, “Have you ever attempted suicide?” We created a binary variable flagging respondents who responded affirmatively to “yes, during the last year.” Demographics Respondents reported age, race/ethnicity, region (ie, Twin Cities metropolitan area vs greater Minnesota), and the number of books in their household when the survey was administered (a federally recommended 14 proxy for socioeconomic status used in prior research 15 ). Analytic plan First, we calculated frequencies and proportions and employed a χ 2 test to compare the prevalences of painful and provocative experiences between SGM and non-SGM adolescents. Second, we conducted a multivariable logistic regression model investigating the association between painful and provocative experiences and past-year suicide attempt. We incorporated interaction terms (each painful and provocative experience×SGM status) to examine whether some risk factors may differentially facilitate risk in SGM and non-SGM adolescents. Third, we examined associations between each painful and provocative experience and past-year suicide attempt in SGM adolescents only. Regressions controlled for sexual orientation, sex at birth, gender modality (transgender vs cisgender), age, race/ethnicity, region, and the number of books in the respondent’s household. Results Prevalence of painful and provocative experiences and past-year suicide attempt A greater prevalence of SGM adolescents reported past-year suicide attempt than non-SGM adolescents (26.7% vs 21.6%; p&lt;0.001). A significantly higher proportion of SGM respondents, compared with non-SGM respondents, endorsed each assessed painful and provocative experience ( table 2 ). Notably, 81% of SGM adolescents reported past-year non-suicidal self-injury, more than two-thirds experienced SGM-relevant bullying, and approximately one-third reported a history of parental incarceration, physical abuse in the household, and sexual abuse by a non-family member. Association between painful and provocative experiences and past-year suicide attempt by SGM status A multivariable logistic regression model incorporating interaction terms (each painful and provocative experience×SGM status) to assess associations with past-year suicide attempt revealed that, after adjusting for covariates, the only significant interaction term was for bullying ( online supplemental table 1 ). To probe this interaction, we constructed separate multivariable logistic regression models in SGM and non-SGM respondents assessing the covariate-adjusted role of bullying on past-year suicide attempt. These models showed that exposure to bullying, whether for reasons unrelated to SGM identity or for SGM-relevant reasons, was associated with approximately 60% greater odds of suicide attempt among SGM adolescents. Among non-SGM adolescents, the associations were not statistically significant ( figure 1 ). Risk of past-year suicide attempt among SGM adolescents with suicidal ideation Results of the multivariable logistic regression restricted to SGM adolescents are presented in table 3 . After adjusting for covariates, odds of past-year suicide attempt were significantly elevated among those who reported bullying of any kind, past-year non-suicidal self-injury, and several adverse childhood experiences including physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal abuse, parental incarceration, and living with someone with a mental illness. The largest effect sizes were observed for non-suicidal self-injury, sexual abuse by a non-family member, and bullying. Discussion In a sample of adolescents with past-year suicidal ideation, the current study reveals that SGM adolescents are more likely to report past-year suicide attempt and exposure to painful and provocative experiences. Consistent with previous research in SGM young adults, 8 , 10 these findings support a strong association between painful and provocative experiences and the progression from suicidal ideation to action in SGM adolescents. The study presents several limitations that require careful consideration. First, the cross-sectional design employed prohibits the establishment of causal relationships. Additionally, grouping SGM adolescents as a singular category may overlook within-group variation, such as between cisgender and transgender adolescents. Lastly, while the large, population-based nature of the study sample is a strength, the MSS is limited to the state of Minnesota which may limit generalizability. Our findings have theoretical, clinical, and structural implications. Given greater odds of both suicidal behaviour and exposure to painful and provocative experiences, further development and application of ideation-to-action frameworks is especially pertinent to addressing the disproportionate risk of suicide faced by SGM adolescents. With regards to clinical practice, SGM adolescents experiencing suicidal ideation should be screened for exposure to painful and provocative experiences assessed herein, including abuse and neglect in the home, non-suicidal self-injury, and bullying. 16 17 Structurally, policies and programmes that reduce and respond to bullying (eg, inclusive anti-bullying campaigns 18 ) and adverse childhood experiences (eg, social equity policies 17 ) may help to prevent suicidal behaviour among SGM adolescents.
