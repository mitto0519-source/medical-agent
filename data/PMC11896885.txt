ABSTRACT Background and Aims Their close contact with the cokes paediatricians a key group of healthcare professionals for the dissemination of environmental health (EH) notions and the protection of children and families. However, international studies show that their knowledge and competences in EH matters are limited. Methods We recruited a sample of 293 paediatricians (80% women, mean age 47 years) working in Catalonia (Spain). The participants filled in a battery of questions that allowed us to describe EH knowledge and competences and study the role of certain potential predictors on these two EH constructs. Results We found that nature relatedness predicted both EH knowledge and competences. Those interested in environmental issues scored higher in EH knowledge, while older participants reported higher competence levels. We could not confirm the predictive role of other sociodemographic (e.g., gender) or psycho‐environmental variables (e.g., worry about environmental concerns). Conclusion Our results underscore the need to focus on training younger professionals and suggest that fostering psychological connections with nature and the environment might be a suitable approach. Introduction Children are especially vulnerable to environmental risks such as air pollution, noise or climate change. It has been estimated that a quarter of deaths under 5‐years old could be avoided with a cleaner, healthier environment [ 1 ]. Some authors have highlighted the role that paediatricians and other healthcare professionals may play in the dissemination of environmental health (EH) notions; such as the encouragement of protective behaviours (e.g., prevention and management of vector‐borne diseases) and the tackling of relevant exposures affecting the community (e.g., endocrine disruptors [ 2 , 3 ];). However, the international literature shows that these professionals receive scarce specific training [ 4 , 5 ], show low levels of EH knowledge [ 6 , 7 , 8 ], and reveals a a very limited presence of EH matters on their daily practice [ 9 ]. Moreover, current scientific evidence cannot inform about what factors may be useful in predicting EH knowledge and competences so we don't know yet what makes some professionals develop higher interest, knowledge, and abilities than others. To address this gap, efforts have been made to develop tools to adequately measure EH knowledge [ 10 , 11 ]. Vrotsou and colleagues (2023) developed a comprehensive 33‐item tool to measure EH knowledge (EHKQ‐HP). This easy‐to‐use unidimensional questionnaire, which showed good internal consistency, can be a useful resource for evaluating the EH knowledge levels of healthcare practitioners. Apart from that, it could also be used to test the impact of EH training on that type of knowledge as well. 1.1. Predictors of EH Knowledge and Competences Previous studies have described EH knowledge in different samples of health professionals or students [ 8 , 12 , 13 ] with some of them detecting statistically significant differences between professional profiles (e.g., paediatricians, general practitioners, nurses, etc.) [ 2 , 7 ]. However, and to the knowledge of the authors, no systematic approach to study its potential predictors has been carried out. From a theoretical perspective, it could be expected that sociodemographic variables such as age and gender might play a predictive role. Older practitioners could show greater EH knowledge due to their extensive professional experience. Or it could be the opposite, with younger practitioners demonstrating enhanced knowledge of EH matters due to the increased presence of such topics in university degrees [ 4 ]. One could argue that women may be more knowledgeable in EH given their tendency to show stronger environmental attitudes and display pro‐environmental behaviours [ 14 ]. Besides, practitioners living with kids could show greater EH awareness due to their parenting and caring responsibilities [ 15 , 16 ]. Therefore, the expectation of women showing greater environmental concerns and knowledge would be also supported by the fact that the vast majority of one‐parent families in Spain are led by women (81%;) according to official data [ 17 ]. Environmental psychologists also work with variables that could be related to EH knowledge amongst health professionals. Nature relatedness is a personality trait that reflects an individual's connection with nature and other living beings on earth [ 18 , 19 ]. This construct gathers the affective, cognitive and physical aspects of the relationship that individuals hold with nature and has been related to environmental risk perceptions [ 20 ]. Another relevant component of environmental attitudes is environmental concerns, defined as the degree of worry about environmental issues (e.g., greenhouse gas emissions, climate change or the extinction of species) [ 21 ]. Our hypothesis here is that those healthcare professionals with stronger environmental attitudes, more specifically those more psychologically connected with nature and more concerned about environmental problems, would show higher EH knowledge scores than those with weaker connection. In a similar vein, we expect those having a greater interest in EH matters to score higher than those who display lower interest in such issues. Given the need of advancing our understanding of EH literacy in paediatricians, we aimed at describing environmental health knowledge of a sample of Catalan paediatricians and perform a first exploration of potential predictors. In line with the above reviewed literature this set will be comprised of sociodemographic (e.g., age or gender) and psycho‐environmental variables (e.g., nature relatedness). 2. Methods In the epigraphs that follow, we describe the features of this cross‐sectional study in which we recruited a sample of Catalan paediatricians and collected the study variables via an online survey. 2.1. Sample and Data Collection Strategy The study sample was accessed through mailing lists and official communication media by the Catalan Paediatrics Association. The study information text contained a link to the online questionnaire, which was developed with the Google forms app and stored in Google Drive. The ethics of the study were approved by the Gipuzkoan Sanitary Ethics Committee (ref. JES‐ENC‐2021‐01). Research was conducted between June and November 2023 and all participants gave informed consent before taking part in the study. 2.2. Instruments The online questionnaire included the following variables. 2.2.1. Sociodemographic and General Information Participants were asked to indicate their age, gender, professional profile, previous EH experience (i.e., whether the participant had directly worked or collaborated in the EH area), participation in a working group on environmental paediatrics, and whether they lived with children or teenagers at the time of data collection. The professional profile categories were: 1) Primary care paediatrician, 2) Hospital paediatrician, 3) Paediatric surgeon, 4) Paediatric resident, and, 5) Paediatric surgery resident. 2.2.2. Interest Towards EH and Use of Environmental Clinical History The first section of the questionnaire gathered information about the overall attitude of the participants towards EH and the use of environmental clinical history (ECH). More specifically, participants had to indicate their interest on EH matters using a 1 to 10 (minimum to maximum) scale. We also included a question about the ECH use in their clinic practice (yes/no) and its frequency on a 0 (never) to 4 (almost always) Likert scale. 2.2.3. EH Competences Drawing on previous works [ 2 , 22 ] we measured the following EH competences: 1) the evaluation of the main environmental exposures affecting a given individual or community that might result in medical illnesses or conditions, 2) the identification of the main exposures that may be associated with respiratory, neurologic, metabolic or neoplasm conditions, 3) the delivery of EH training on the main environmental risks, 4) the identification of environmental exposures existing in different scenarios (e.g., public space, schools, households, etc.), and 5) the identification of the actions needed to address environmental risks in said scenarios. Each participant had to indicate the degree they felt able to conduct such actions using a 0 ( Strongly disagree) to 4 (Strongly agree) Likert scale. The scale showed a very good internal consistency ( α = 0.91) and the five items were averaged for statistical analyses. 2.2.4. EH Knowledge To measure EH knowledge, we resorted to the 33‐item long EHKQ‐HP scale [ 11 ]. The items included in this tool are distributed in six knowledge areas, namely children environmental health (four items, e.g., the Paediatric population is more vulnerable to environmental exposures due to its biological immaturity ), environmental toxicology (five items, e.g., substances such as dioxins, phthalates and Bisphenol A act as endocrine disruptors ), environmental pollution (six items, e.g., the general population is more affected from asbestos exposure than the occupational population ), drinking and recreational waters (five items, e.g., Cryptosporidum parvum and Giardia lamblia are protozoi in contaminated drinking water that produce gastroenteritis) and physical agents (four items, e.g., noise is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease and generates psychological annoyance ), zoonosis and vector borne diseases (five items, e.g., Rodents transmit salmonellosis, leptospirosis and haemorrhagic syndromes caused by Hantaviruses ), climate change and urban exposures (four items, e.g., urban greenness has been associated with improvements in attention and stress reduction) . Each item presents a statement that has to be classified as true, false, or do not know. For the needs of the present study, “do not know” replies were considered as lack of knowledge and were thus counted as false. All items were treated as binary (correct/incorrect) and correct replies were given 1 point, meaning that the total score would range from a minimum of 0 to maximum of 33 points. In this study, the scale obtained an adequate reliability index (KR‐20 = 0.79). We also included a complementary measure of perceived EH knowledge. Participants had to report whether they were able to give a precise definition of Environmental Health using a yes/no answer format. 2.2.5. Nature Relatedness We measured nature relatedness with the scale developed by Nisbet and colleagues [ 18 , 19 ] The five items include in the scale were presented in a 0 (Totally disagree) to 4 (Totally agree) Likert scale. The scale showed very good internal consistency ( α = 0.89) in this study. 2.2.6. Concerns About Environmental Problems We invited participants to rate the level of concern that several environmental problems generated in them. We used the list developed by Miller and colleagues [ 21 ] which presents eight different environmental problems such as plastic pollution, climate change or habitat loss. Participants had to indicate their level of concern by using a 0 (Not concerned) to 4 (Very concerned) Likert scale ( α = 0.83 in this study). 2.3. Statistical Analyses Study variables were first analysed by means of descriptive statistics such as means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous data and frequencies and proportions for categorical data. Composite measures of EH competences, nature relatedness and environmental concerns were obtained after averaging the individual items scores and its reliability was assessed with the Cronbach's alpha index calculated with the Cronbach() function within Cronbach R package [ 23 ]. To study the potential predictive value of the study variables onto EH knowledge and competences we first developed a Direct Acyclic Graph (DAG [ 24 , 25 ]) reflecting the hypothesis described in the introduction (Figure 1 ). We then used the procedure described in Ankan et al. [ 26 ] to test the DAG testable implications, which we would consider unmet if their associated r coefficient was larger than 0.20 and had a p ‐value below the 0.05 threshold. This means that the original DAG did not specify a relationship between two variables that actually exists in the data set. To correctly apply the d ‐separation criterion to select the minimal adjustment set of covariates, which is the final aim of the DAG validation process, unspecified relationships need to be included in a new version of the DAG by adding the corresponding arrows. These are links between study variables that were not initially considered by the researchers but that are present in the data set and therefore can bias the model estimates if not accounted for. If those specifications were not added to the new version of the DAG and we used the previous DAG to select the minimal adjustment set, the effect estimates might be biased because not all the biasing paths would be effectively blocked. This process required the use of functions included in R packages dagitty [ 27 ] and lavaan [ 28 ]. After detecting previously unspecified relationships between variables by means of the said testable implications (see Supplementary Table 1 ), we amended our initial DAG (see Supplementary Figure 1 ). Then we determined the sufficient adjustment sets for each of the linear regression models to be fit, and which are described in epigraph 3.2. Estimates with associated p ‐values &lt; 0.05 were considered as statistically significant. We used the lm() and confint() function within the base R package stats [ 29 ] to fit the models. 3. Results 3.1. Description of the Sample The study sample was composed of 293 paediatricians, of whom 236 (80%) were women, with a mean age of 47 (SD = 12.78) years. As can be seen in Table 1 , most participants worked in Primary Care (57%). Even though almost a half of the sample was able to define EH, only 10% used ECH. Interest in EH was moderately high and competences were medium to low. Out of 33 potential points in the EH knowledge scale, participants scored 20.76 (SD = 4.80) on average. Participants showed high scores for nature relatedness and environmental concerns. 3.2. Predictors of EH Knowledge and Competences in Catalan Paediatric Practitioners Following the procedure described in the data analysis section, we checked the testable implications of the study DAG. Two previously unspecified relationships between study variables were found; age was related to EH competences, and gender to age (see supplementary Table 1 ). After updating the DAG (see Supplementary Figure 1 ), we extracted the covariate adjustment sets to estimate the association between study variables and both EH knowledge and competences. Results of these analyses can be found in Table 2 . EH knowledge was only, and positively, predicted by interest in EH. We also detected a p &lt; 0.10 association between NR and knowledge in the expected direction. However, we did not find statistical evidence of the predictive value of age, gender, living with kids and environmental concerns. In the case of EH competences, higher scores were associated to being older and more connected to nature. Variables such as gender, lives with kids, environmental concerns and, more relevantly, EH knowledge, were not associated to EH competences. As an additional analysis, we checked whether the expected relationships between NR, EC and interest on EH that were displayed in the DAG held true. As showed in Supplementary Table 2 , NR positively predicted EC and interest in EH. In a similar vein, EC also revealed itself as a positive predictor of interest in EH. 4. Discussion In this study we aimed at describing the environmental health knowledge of a sample of Catalan paediatricians and made a first exploration of potential predictors. In our sample, we found moderate to high levels of interest in EH matters paired with low to moderate levels of EH knowledge and competences, which is consistent with previous studies [ 2 , 8 , 12 , 13 ]. Similarly, we saw that only a 10% of the surveyed paediatricians used environmental clinical history on a regular basis. We measured environmental attitudes and described high scores of nature relatedness and worry for environmental issues. To our knowledge, this is the first study in the literature in which a set of potential predictors of EH knowledge and attitudes has been tested. This set comprised not only sociodemographic variables, such as gender and age, but also some psycho‐environmental variables that we thought could explain EH knowledge and competences scores. The results of this analysis revealed that EH knowledge was higher in those with higher psychological bonds with nature and more interested in EH matters. Additionally, we saw that older participants and those with higher nature relatedness reported higher EH competence scores, so training opportunities should be focused on young residents and paediatricians and activities that could foster nature connectedness could be implemented. In the introduction of the manuscript, we postulated that age could be related to EH knowledge and competences via two potential ways; 1) greater – although still limited – presence of EH matters in the curriculum of health degrees (i.e., meaning younger paediatricians will score higher), or 2) older practitioners showing a better performance due to learning and experiences during longer professional careers. Our results are more compatible with the latter expectation, but further research is needed to confirm this finding. However, we did not find any support for many of the theoretical expectations expressed in the study DAG. For instance, neither women nor participants living with kids scored higher in EH variables. Also contrary to our expectations, greater awareness or worry about environmental matters did not directly translate in higher EH knowledge and competences, although an indirect effect through interest in EH matters was found. Finally, and to our surprise, more knowledgeable participants – as measured with the EHKQ‐HP tool – did not report higher competence levels in EH‐related tasks. 4.1. Study Strengths and Limitations This study has made a relevant contribution to the specific literature because we conducted a first study of potential predictors of EH knowledge and competences. Our results could inform not only future studies in the field but also inspire tailored interventions to increase the presence of EH matters in primary care settings and in the curricula of medicine and other health degrees. However, our work is also affected by some limitations. Despite the fact that our sample is not that different in terms of age and gender to the 2,238 members of the Catalan Paediatrics Association (70% are female, mean age 48 years), given that we used a convenience sample of voluntary participants, self‐selection and desirability biases might have had an impact on our results. Moreover, we resorted to a sample of a single health professional profile (e.g., paediatricians) residing in Catalonia and therefore generalizability to other profiles and probably other locations might be compromised. 5. Conclusion We screened EH competences of paediatricians working in Catalonia, Spain, and saw that despite their interest in EH matters, only a reduced proportion of professionals conducted EH activities such as the environmental clinical history exploration in their regular clinical practice and that EH knowledge and competences levels were low to moderate. Young residents and paediatricians scored lower in EH competences, which invites to make them the main target of training opportunities. Nature relatedness predicted EH competences, so this is a clear enhancement area. Further interventions are needed to ensure an optimal children's health and well‐being.
