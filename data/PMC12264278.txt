Abstract The main purpose of the study was to examine the associations between health-related physical fitness (PF) and screen time in school-going youth. A convenient sample of 3224 children and adolescents aged 10–14 years from 10 schools was recruited to participate in the study. The Health-Behaviours in School-aged Children questionnaire was used to assess the level of screen time during schooldays and weekend days in television (TV) viewing, playing video games (VG) and using electronic devices (ED). Muscular fitness (sit-ups in 30 s and standing broad jump), cardiorespiratory fitness (the 20 m shuttle run test) and motor competence (the 10 × 5 m shuttle run test) were tested to determine the level of PF. Raw data for each test were transformed to quartiles (independent variable), while the outcome variable was not meeting the screen guidelines (&gt; 2 h/day) for each of the screen time behavior. The average total screen time was 9.1 (2.9) h/day. Youth who did not meet the screen guidelines performed significantly poorly than those who met with the guidelines, respectively. A graded inverse association between each PF test and not meeting the screen time guidelines was found. Individuals in upper quartiles of cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness, and motor competence were less likely not to meet the screen time guidelines, compared to their peers in lower quartiles. Similar associations were observed, irrespective of sex. This study suggests that higher PF levels are strongly associated with less time spent in front of screens in a large sample of children and adolescents. From a public health perspective, enhancing muscular and cardiorespiratory fitness may be an adequate method to reduce screen time and re-allocate it to a more active lifestyle. Introduction Health-related physical fitness (PF) is a multifaceted construct that has been consistently associated with the ability to perform physical activity (PA) 1 . Since PF covers several components of muscular and cardiorespiratory fitness, flexibility and body composition 2 , it is considered a powerful marker of health 3 . Indeed, PF in childhood and adolescence has been shown to track well into adulthood 3 – 5 , suggesting that a higher level of PF established at a young age is a cornerstone for having less cardiovascular disease 6 , metabolic disorders 7 , and mental health conditions 8 and all-cause mortality later in life 9 – 11 . PF is determined by genetic and environmental factors 12 . Although genetic factors may moderately to largely influence the level of PF and often remain unchanged 13 , environmental shifts from traditionally active to more sedentary lifestyle have been observed in the last four decades 14 , 15 , especially in children and adolescents 2 , 16 . Sedentary behavior (SB) is also a significant determinant of health in children and adolescents 17 . It is defined as ‘any waking behavior characterized by an energy expenditure ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents while in a sitting, reclining or lying posture’ 18 . Spending more time in various SBs, like TV viewing, playing video games (VG) and using electronic devices (ED), has become a widespread public health problem associated with negative health-related outcomes, which is especially prevalent in children and adolescents 19 . A 24 – hour movement behavior is based on movement activities done during the day 20 . It is considered as an essential approach to for implementing interventions and health – policy strategies to decrease the time spent in SBs and increase the engagement in PA and/or sleep. Based on the Viable Integrative Research in Time-Use Epidemiology (Virtue) initiative 20 , when the time in one behavior decreases, it becomes substituted with more time spent in other behaviors. However, the nature of PF and SB and their co-existence is relatively unknown during childhood and adolescence. Because the level of PF is steadily declining 15 , and the time spent in SBs is rapidly increasing 20 , it is worthwhile to examine the relationship between the two constructs for future interventions. Although the framework often refers to a sufficient level of PA being positively associated with PF 21 , previous studies of meeting the guidelines for screen time and higher level of PF have shown conflicting findings 22 – 31 . Although some previous studies have shown that spending less time in front of screens has been associated with higher PF levels 24 , 25 , several have confirmed no significant associations 22 , 23 , 26 – 28 . For example, a study by Aggio et al. 25 . found that children who spent ≥ 2 h/day in screen time were 2.35 times more likely to become cardiorespiratory unfit at 2-year follow-up. Despite negative associations between screen time and cardiorespiratory fitness being shown in a study by Arango et al. 24 , the magnitude of these cross-sectional associations remained trivial to small. On the other hand, the majority of previous studies has shown null association between various components of PF 22 . In a study by Chen et al. 22 , odds ratios tended to be around 1.00 for cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness, speed and agility, and flexibility, when the outcome measure was spending ≥ 2 h/day in front of screens. Although Bai et al. 23 . indicated that youth who did not meet screen time guidelines had 57% higher odd for having low cardiorespiratory fitness, the association remained non-significant. Similar findings have been observed elsewhere, where youth who followed the recommendations for screen time vs. those who did not perform equally well in cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness tests, and flexibility 26 . Thus, the magnitude and the direction of the association between screen time and PF has yet to be discovered. Moreover, the majority of previous studies have used screen time as a proxy of overall time spent in SBs, while no existing evidence has been provided for splitting screen time into more specific behaviors, like watching TV, playing video games (VG), or using electronic devices (ED). By examining multiple screen time behaviors, health – related policy makers would be able to tackle the most common behavior related to PF and reallocate it with more favorable movement patterns beneficial for health and well – being in youth 30 , 31 . Also, evidence suggests that children and adolescents spend more time using mobile phones than watching TV or using other electronic devices 16 , which can be a good starting point for leveraging more active lifestyle and higher levels of physical performance. Therefore, the main purpose of the present study was to examine the associations between self-reported screen time (TV viewing, playing VG and using ED) and objectively measured PF in a large sample of children and adolescents. Based on previous findings, we hypothesized that meeting the screen time guidelines in all categories of ≤ 2 h/day 18 was associated with higher levels of PF. Materials and methods Participants and study design Participants in this observational, cross-sectional study were a convenience sample of children and adolescents aged 10–14 years (mean ± SD, age = 10.8 ± 1.2 years, 47.5% boys) recruited from 10 primary schools. The schools were located near the city main center and were considered urban. At the first stage, 15 schools were selected to participate in the study, of which 10 agreed to proceed further with the measurements. At the second stage, we randomly selected two classes which represented one age group (two classes of 10-year-olds, two classes for 11-year-olds…), totaling 10 classes from each school and 100 classes from all schools in the study. Thus, 3454 boys and girls were approached at the beginning of the study. The completion criteria were children and adolescents without any history of locomotor and mental disabilities, and the presence of injury, that would potentially distance them from testing, and those who regularly attended physical education (PE) classes. Of them, 205 participants had injuries affecting the upper or lower extremities, and 25 did not attend PE classes due to personal reasons. Thus, the final sample consisted of 3,224 children and adolescents (response rate = 93.3%). Before the initial testing, all children and adolescents and their legal parents were required to provide written informed consent and assent to participate in the study and the second consent to use the obtained data strictly for scientific purposes. Also, the procedures undertaken in the study were anonymous by giving a specific code number to each participant, according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Finally, participation was entirely voluntary, and study protocols were approved by the Ethical Committee of the Henan University. Screen time To assess screen time, we used a reliable and valid questionnaire derived from the Health-Behaviour in School-Aged Children (HBSC) project, a World Health Organization initiative focusing on young people’s health and well-being 32 . The questionnaire showed good psychometric properties in Chinese children and adolescents 33 and was used in a similar sample 22 . The participants had to respond to the following screen time questions related to TV viewing, playing VG and using ED: (1) “How many hours did you spend watching TV or movies in your leisure time on weekdays and weekend days over the past week, respectively?”, (2) How many hours did you spend playing VG in your leisure time on weekdays and weekend days over the past week, respectively?, and (3) How many hours did you spend in activities using ED in your leisure time on weekdays and weekend days over the past week, respectively 22 ?. The responses were categorized as ‘none’, ‘1 hour’, ‘2 hours’, ‘3 hours’ and ‘&gt;3 hours’. To obtain overall screen time for every specific behavior, screen time during weekdays and weekend days were calculated using the following formula: Based on the Canadian recommendations 34 , for every screen time behavior, we dichotomized the participants into those who met (≤ 2 h/day) vs. those who did not meet (&gt; 2 h/day) the screen time guidelines. Health-related PF Muscular fitness was assessed by using the two tests composed of standing broad jump and sit-ups in 30 s. Both tests are universally accepted methods to assess the level of lower limb muscular strength 35 and repetitive strength of the trunk 36 . Standing broad jump was performed by a vigorous push off where participants jumped as far forward as possible using both legs, keeping knees and arms parallel to each other in frontal position, and landing in an upright position without losing the balance 35 . The test consisted of three trials, of which the best was taken as the final score in centimeters (cm). Sit-ups in 30 s tested the repetitive strength of the trunk, where the participant needed to be seated on the floor with straight upper part of the body and hands being placed behind the neck, and knees be in the position of 90°. After the signal, the movement was characterized by heading forward with the upper body with elbows touching the knees, while hands kept staying behind the neck during the exercise and returning to the initial position 36 . The number of correct sit-ups in 30 s was recorded. The 10 × 5 shuttle run test evaluated motor coordination, lateral speed and agility. The protocol was based on completing a 50 m distance from a standing start, where each participant needed to run back and forth five times 35 . The final score was recorded in sec. Finally, to assess the level of cardiorespiratory fitness, the 20 m shuttle run test was applied 35 . Participants were instructed to run back and forth on a 20 m course by touching the lines at the end of the course, while pacing according to prerecorded sound signal from a tape. The pace of the sound signals gradually increased by 0.5 km/h each minute from a starting speed of 8.5 km/h 37 . When a child could not hold the pace with the sound signals, the test was over, and the final score was recorded as the number of shuttles completed during testing. All tests were examined performed by the same instructors and using the same equipment to omit the measurement error and potential bias. Confounding variables Sex (boys vs. girls), age (10–14 years old), BMI and peak height velocity (PHV) were entered into each model as covariates. Sex and age were self-reported, and BMI was calculated from objectively measured height (cm) and weight (kg) using the following formula: [weight (kg)/height (m) 2 ]. Based on the age of the participants and biological rationale that entering puberty would affect physical performance 15 , we calculated maturity offset from the equation proposed by Moore et al. 38 . Testing procedure All tests were completed in the same calendar year between April and June in the morning hours (around 10:30 h), with the average temperature between 22 and 24° C and humidity of around 65%. Before the testing had begun, participants were instructed not to eat heavily and to have a fasting period of at least 2 h before the first measurements. On the first day of testing, self-reported measures of sex and age and height, weight and muscular fitness tests were measured. The resting period between each test was around 15 min to avoid fatigue. On the second day, cardiorespiratory fitness was done, followed by completing the HBSC screen time questionnaire. Statistical analysis Analyses performed in this study were conducted using Statistical Packages for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 (IBM production company, Chicago, Il). Frequencies (N) and percentages (%) and mean with standard deviation (SD) were used for basic descriptive statistics. Differences between children and adolescents who met screen time guidelines (≤ 2 h/day) vs. those who did not (&gt; 2 h/day) in PF components were analyzed by univariate analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted by sex, age, BMI and maturity offset. Next, the associations between screen time (&gt; 2 h/day as the category of interest) and each PF component were examined by a set of logistic regression models. First, numerical values in each test were transformed into quartiles from the 1 st (the lowest) to the 4th (the highest). Quartile 1 was put as a reference category in each model for all PF tests. Next, we run a logistic regression to examine the odds of not meeting the guidelines for screen time based on each quartile. The same procedure was done separately for TV viewing, playing VG and using ED, respectively. Models were adjusted for the same covariates, and the significance was set at p &lt; 0.05 and it was two-sided. Results Basic descriptive characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1 . Roughly, the same prevalence of children and adolescents by sex was observed. Most of the study participants were categorized as being in the ‘normal weight’ category, opposed to ‘overweight/obese’ individuals. The average time in front of screens was significantly above the recommended level of ≤ 2 h/day, and most children and adolescents (81.5% for TV viewing, 80.3% for playing VG and 69.4% for using ED) did not follow the screen time guidelines. Table 2 examines differences in PF components between children and adolescents who met vs. did not meet the screen time guidelines for a certain behavior. Youth who spent ≤ 2 h/day in front of screens exhibited significantly better results in all PF tests, compared to their peers, who did not meet the guidelines ( p &lt; 0.001). Boys performed better in cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness, and agility/speed/flexibility across all screen time behaviors, compared to girls ( p &lt; 0.001). Table 3 shows the associations between PF components and not meeting the recommendations for TV viewing. Children and adolescents in upper quartiles of muscular fitness (‘sit-ups in 30 sec’ and ‘standing broad jump’), motor competence (‘shuttle run 10 × 5 m’) and cardiorespiratory fitness (‘20 m shuttle run’), were gradually less likely not to meet the recommendations for TV viewing.. The associations between PF components and not meeting the recommendations for playing VG are shown in Table 4 . Similar to not meeting the recommendations for TV viewing, children and adolescents in upper quartiles of muscular fitness (‘sit-ups in 30 sec’ and ‘standing broad jump’), motor competence (‘shuttle run 10 × 5 m’) and cardiorespiratory fitness (‘20 m shuttle run’) had more chance to adhere to screen time recommendations for playing VG. Table 5 shows the associations between PF components and not meeting the recommendations for using ED. Children and adolescents in upper quartiles of muscular fitness (‘sit-ups in 30 sec’ and ‘standing broad jump’), motor competence (‘shuttle run 10 × 5 m’) and cardiorespiratory fitness (‘20 m shuttle run’) were less likely not to adhere to screen time guidelines related to using ED. Similar gradual decreases for not meeting the recommendations in both boys and girls for TV viewing, playing VG and using ED were observed. Discussion Based on the main purpose of the study to examine the associations between self-reported screen time and objectively measured PF in a large sample of children and adolescents, the study includes the following findings: (1) children and adolescents who meet screen time guidelines exhibit better results in all PF components, compared to those who do not meet the guidelines, irrespective of the type of screen time behavior; (2) those in upper quartiles in all PF components have lower odds of not meeting the screen time guidelines in all behaviors. This study shows strong associations between higher levels of muscular and cardiorespiratory fitness, and motor competence and spending ≤ 2 h/day in TV viewing, playing VG and using ED, which is in accordance with previous studies 24 , 25 , 38 , 39 . Like our findings, a 2-year follow-up study by Aggio et al. 25 showed that school going children, who engaged in high screen time during the day (&gt; 2 h) were 2.35 times more likely to become unfit at follow-up, defined by the FITNESSGRAM Lower Healthy Fitness Zone cut-off value. However, when the same associations were applied at follow-up, high screen time was not significantly associated with low PF 25 . Another study indicated that total screen time and TV viewing were associated with both waist circumference and BMI, when taking cardiorespiratory fitness into account 24 . Another study showed that children meeting vs. not meeting the screen time guidelines exhibited higher values in relative grip strength adjusted by body weight, while no significant differences in other PF components were observed 26 . However, the direction of these findings did not occur in previous studies conducted among the similar sample of children and adolescents 22 , 23 , 26 – 28 . Specifically, a study by Chen et al. 22 indicated that meeting the guidelines for screen time was not significantly associated with general, cardiorespiratory and muscular PF, speed, agility and flexibility. Youth who spent ≤ 2 h/day in screen time were 2% and 4% more likely to meet general and cardiorespiratory PF, while the odds for muscular fitness, speed, agility and flexibility decreased by 4%, 10% and 5%, respectively 22 . The data from the NAHNES National Youth Fitness Survey 23 highlighted that not meeting the screen time guidelines was associated with a 52% greater odd for having lower cardiorespiratory fitness level, compared to those in the “Healthy Fitness Zone”, although the association was not statistically significant. When the outcome measurement unit was transformed into metabolic equivalents (METs), high screen time was associated with a 42% higher odd for being in a “Needs Improvement Zone” category (non-significant) 23 . In a Japanese study by Tanaka et al. 26 , a null association between screen time and cardiorespiratory fitness (20 m shuttle run), muscular fitness (absolute hand grip strength) and flexibility was found. The same observations were found in Chilean children, where boys and girls who spent ≥ 2 h/day in screen time had similar values in cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness, irrespective of academic achievement 28 . Based on the available literature, it seems that the independent association between screen time and PF is not clearly understood. Although some studies have confirmed that not meeting the screen time guidelines is associated with higher PF 24 , 25 , 38 , still most of the evidence has shown no significant association between the two constructs 22 , 23 , 26 – 28 . Interestingly, by combining screen time with other movements (like PA), studies have consistently shown that the level of PF increases 23 . One potential mechanism is that higher PA levels, especially vigorous-intensity PA, are strongly associated with PF, yet the associations disappear with screen time among youth. In that way, positive associations between PA and PF remain, even though a child does not meet the recommended level of screen time 22 . This would imply that PA interventions based on duration and intensity may be more important in enhancing the level of PF, opposed to screen time. This has been supported by previous literature 22 , 23 , where meeting the PA guidelines alone significantly increases the odds for high PF, while combining PA with screen time only slightly affects the association with PF 22 . Also, studies using compositional data analysis have highlighted the importance of MVPA rather than screen time for increasing PF levels 29 – 31 , indicating that PA can eliminate the adverse health effects of excessive screen time, although independent associations between screen time and PF are yet to be confirmed. Another discrepancy between the findings may be due to the different measurement methods. Although we based our findings on a relatively large sample of children and adolescents and objectively measured PF, and the time spent in front of screens was assessed by a validated questionnaire 34 , self-reported measures may lead to underestimation of screen time and different proportion of children meeting the screen time guidelines. Based on these proportions, the accuracy of statistical power in logistic regression models can be potentially different. However, the prevalence of children and adolescents, who did not meet the screen time guidelines was between 70% and 80%, which is in line with previous estimates 22 , 23 , 26 , 34 . Therefore, the associations obtained in this study may be generalizable to other populations of youth. This study is not without limitations. We were unable to examine the associations between screen time and PF in a longitudinal design, therefore the causality of the existing associations remained unknown. Second, the time spent in different domains in front of screens was assessed by using a self-reported questionnaire. Although such methods have been used to establish the prevalence of meeting the screen time guidelines in children and adolescents 33 , 34 , self-reports often rely on a subjective perception, which may be favorable towards a certain behavior. Although we conducted a study among a large sample of children and adolescents, selecting a sample using a convenient approach might have led to a measurement error. Finally, we were unable to test reliability and validity properties of health-related PF tests. However, previous evidence has included these tests as appropriate to assess the level of cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness, and motor competence in children and adolescents 35 , 40 . In the context of previous studies, practical implications of this study denote that PF, which include cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness, and speed/agility are strongly associated with screen time in a large sample of children and adolescents. That is, youth who do not meet the recommended level of screen time distinguished between TV viewing, playing VG or using ED (&lt; 2 h/day) are more likely to be categorized in lower quartile of each PF component, even after adjusting for various sociodemographic covariates. Thus, by having higher levels of PF may help to reduce the time spent in different screen time behaviors. Although a cross-sectional design is highlighted as a limitation, this study adds a new information about inverse and strong associations between screen time and PF. Since the data are collected in school-based settings, schools may serve as potential avenues to enhance the level of PF during in-school PE classes or breaks, and out of school programs, as effective strategies to target the reduction in screen time during the day. Despite these advantages, future research is proposed to use objective methods (actigraphy or tracking monitors) to assess the level of screen time during a follow-up period, to establish causal associations between the two constructs. Conclusion We examined the associations between health-related PF and screen time in children and adolescents. The findings of the study supported the hypothesis, that youth in upper quartiles of each PF component would be approximately 60 to 95% less likely not to meet with the different screen time aspects of TV viewing, playing VG or using ED, compared to those in lower quartiles. The magnitude of the association in each screen time domain with cardiorespiratory, muscular and motor competence health-related PF indicated strong and inverse relationship. Our data confirm a hypothesis that higher level of PF may be associated with less time spent in front of screens. Thus, the findings suggest that PF may have protective effects towards the excessive screen time in school-going children and adolescents.
