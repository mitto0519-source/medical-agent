ABSTRACT Introduction Suicide among Black youth has become a public health concern. To better understand the rapid increase in Black youth suicide, we aimed to determine whether there had been changes in the risk correlates and their impacts on suicidality among Black youth over the past three decades. Methods We analyzed four suicide‐related outcomes—ideation, planning, attempts, and attempt‐related injuries—using Black youth's responses from the 1991–2021 Youth Risk Behavior Survey (survey‐adjusted n = 31667.12). Potential correlates considered were demographics, sexuality, injury, violence, bullying, diet, physical activity, mental health, substance use, school connectedness, and exposure to community violence. Correlate‐by‐year interactions were included to allow for time‐varying effects of factors. Missing data were imputed five times, and logistic LASSO regressions were used to select important factors. Results Rates of suicidal ideation decreased from 22% (1991) to 12% (2005), then increased to 22% (2021). Suicide planning and attempts had similar trends, whereas rates of injuries remained stable (ranging from 2% to 4%). Correlates identified for all four outcomes were similar and consistent over time, including violent behaviors, substance use, body image concerns, and sex. All correlates' effects remained unchanged over time. Conclusion Although trends of suicidality in Black youth changed over time, correlates associated with them remained stable. Introduction Youth suicide has become a global public health concern (Abraham and Sher 2017 ). In the United States, rates of suicide among individuals aged 10–24 years have been steadily increasing over the past two decades (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC] 2024 ), making suicide the second leading cause of death in this age group in 2019 (CDC 2023a ). Among all racial groups, suicide rate increases the fastest among Black youth: the rate of suicide almost doubled in the past two decades, rising from 3.05 per 100,000 to 5.99 per 100,000 from 2001 to 2020 (CDC 2023a ). From 2003 to 2017, the steepest increase was observed in the 15‐ to 17‐year age group (Sheftall et al. 2022 ). Although the overall rate of suicide is lower among Black individuals compared to White individuals, data between 2001 and 2015 indicate that the suicide rate among Black children aged between 5 and 12 years is almost twice that of their White counterparts (Bridge et al. 2018 ). The COVID‐19 pandemic has further exacerbated racial disparities: despite an overall increase in suicide deaths among US youth, non‐Hispanic Black youth, along with non‐Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native youth, were disproportionately impacted (Bridge et al. 2023 ). Similar upward trends for suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) among Black youth have also been observed in the past two decades (Ivey‐Stephenson 2020 ; Lindsey et al. 2019 ; Price and Khubchandani 2019 ; Sheftall and Miller 2021 ). From 1991 to 2017, a significant increase in suicide attempts was observed for Black youth, as well as a significant increase in suicide attempt–related injuries for Black boys (Lindsey et al. 2019 ). From 2001 to 2017, significant increases were observed across all spectrums of STB, including suicidal ideation, suicide planning, suicide attempts, and suicide attempt–related injuries (Price and Khubchandani 2019 ). In response to these alarming trends, the Congressional Black Caucus published Ring the Alarm: The Crisis of Black Youth Suicide in America in December 2019, to call for the pressing need for focused research in this population. Since then, researchers have developed multiple theoretical frameworks to explain the risk of suicide among Black children and youth. Some noticeable ones include a structural racism and suicide prevention systems framework adapted from the socio‐ecological model (Alvarez et al. 2022 ), an integrated model of interpersonal–psychological theory of suicide and intersectionality theory (Opara et al. 2022 ), and a framework integrating intersectionality theory and minority stress theory (Opara et al. 2023 ). Theories of STB suggest that risk and protective factors are multilevel and interactive (Windle 2004 ). For the general youth population, factors associated with STB include biological factors (e.g., gender), psychological factors (e.g., emotional and behavior dysregulation), clinical factors (e.g., mental health problems, prior STB), cognitive factors (e.g., suicide acceptability), and sociocultural factors (e.g., race, being a sexual minority, being bullied at school, being cyberbullied, carrying a weapon, academic grades, grade level, and weight perception) (Beautrais 2002 ; Benton et al. 2021 ; Joe et al. 2007 ; Richardson et al. 2024 ; Sheftall et al. 2022 ; Windle 2004 ). According to the suicide frameworks for youth of color, Black youth are also exposed to risk factors that are unique to their culture and experience. These factors include sociocultural factors such as stigma, acculturation, racial socialization, and environmental factors such as health care infrastructure, socio‐economic hardships, and exposure to racism and community violence (Bailey et al. 2017 ; Molock et al. 2023 ; Mukolo and Heflinger 2011 ). Along with technological development, new risk behaviors, such as vaping and being cyberbullied, have emerged over the past two decades. Tobacco smoking has been identified as a risk factor for Black youth suicidality (Gilreath et al. 2012 ). However, the recent generation of US high school students uses vaping devices more frequently than smoking cigarettes (Johnston et al. 2020 ; Welty et al. 2023 ). In addition to risky behaviors, the social and political climates have shifted drastically in recent years, exposing Black youth to conditions that may worsen their mental well‐being. The pandemic has disproportionately impacted people from marginalized communities (Banks 2022 ; CDC 2022 ). On top of it, the murder of George Floyd and other Black Americans during the first year of the pandemic heightened anti‐Black racism, further elevating the stress and mental health challenges among Black youth (Eichstaedt et al. 2021 ; Osman et al. 2024 ). Whether the changes in risky behaviors and environment contribute to the increase in Black youth STB or alter the impacts of already known factors remains unstudied. Understanding the evolution of the risk factors for STB over time can provide a dynamic view of the underlying mechanisms that drive the rapid increase in Black youth STB. The goal of this study was to examine whether the correlates, as well as the impact of the correlates, for Black youth suicidality changed over the past three decades. Specifically, we analyzed data from a nationally representative survey, the Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS), which measured demographics and various health‐related behaviors and experiences, as well as STB, in high school students since 1991. As the survey evolved, new variables (e.g., use of electronic vapor product) were added to identify emerging issues. The YRBS data have been used to conduct trend analysis of STB (Lindsey et al. 2019 ; Price and Khubchandani 2019 ), as well as to identify risk correlates for STB in Black youth from a list of preselected variables based on general youth or conceptual models for Black youth (Opara et al. 2023 ; Richardson et al. 2024 ). To our knowledge, this is the first study that jointly evaluated all the questions collected by the YRBS, as well as changes in their effects over time. The results of this study provide critical information on the shift in underlying mechanisms that may have driven the steep increase in Black youth STB over the past decade, which can inform future prevention strategies aimed at reducing STB in this population. 2. Methods The study utilized the 1991–2021 combined national high school data from the Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) (CDC 2023b ). The YRBS is a survey instituted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) that tracks health‐related behaviors and experiences that can lead to poor health in high school students (CDC 2023b ). To collect a nationally representative sample of US high school students, the YRBS implemented a three‐stage stratified cluster complex survey design. Since the first survey in 1991, the YRBS has collected data biannually from more than 5 million high school students in the United States. The 1991–2021 YRBS combined data were constructed based on the 2021 survey. Survey responses from previous years were mapped to the questions in the 2021 questionnaire. Missing data were generated if the questions were not in previous questionnaires. This study restricted the sample to Black youth only (survey‐weight adjusted n = 31667.12). We considered four STB variables as outcome variables: suicidal ideation (Q26), suicide planning (Q27), suicide attempt (Q28), and injury (Q29). Suicidal ideation was measured by the question “During the past 12 months, did you ever seriously consider attempting suicide?” Suicide planning was measured by the question “During the past 12 months, did you make a plan about how you would attempt suicide?” Suicide attempt was measured by the question “During the past 12 months, how many times did you actually attempt suicide?” Suicide attempt–related injury was measured by the question “If you attempted suicide during the past 12 months, did any attempt result in an injury, poisoning, or overdose that had to be treated by a doctor or nurse?” All four variables were dichotomized into “yes” or “no” responses for analysis. Since suicide attempt–related injuries can only happen to those who had attempted suicide, to avoid inconsistent responses and to accurately calculate the prevalence, we manually coded the answer to attempt‐related injuries to “no” for those who did not have any suicide attempts (answered “no” to suicide attempts). We considered 73 potential correlates: students' demographic characteristics (i.e., age, sex, grade, sexual identity, and sex of sexual contacts), sexual behaviors (Q57–Q64), driving behaviors (Q8–Q11), injury and violence (Q12–Q22), bullying (Q23–Q24), diet and physical activity (Q66–Q81), mental health (Q25, Q85), alcohol, tobacco, and drug use (Q30–Q56), preventive health care (Q82–Q84), and sleep (Q86–Q87). All correlates except for age, grade, sexual identity, and sex of sexual contacts were dichotomized into “yes” or “no” responses for analysis. The combined data contain two types of structural missing data: (1) conditional branching and (2) questions added in later years' surveys. To handle missing data due to conditional branching, questions dependent on conditional branching were excluded from the analysis. To handle missing data due to newly added questions, we included only interaction terms between the factor and dummy variables of the year that the factor was collected. For instance, if a question had been collected since 2015, only interaction terms between the question and the dummy year variables 2015, 2017, 2019, and 2021 were included. Observations missing all four outcomes were dropped from the analytical data set (21.08%). Missing data in the remaining data set (independent variables) were imputed using multiple imputation, a general approach to handling missing data with reliable point estimates and standard errors when data are missing at random (Groenwold et al. 2012 ; Hughes et al. 2019 ). Specifically, we used random forest models to impute missing values and generated five imputed data sets for analysis. The complex survey design was incorporated in both the descriptive summary and the modeling process. Logistic regression with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regularization was used to identify the trends of correlates for each outcome variable. LASSO regression was employed as a model selection strategy to select important correlates. Owing to its inherent performance of L1 regularization, LASSO regression can effectively diminish certain coefficients to zero, thereby excluding them from the model. This property renders LASSO regression a highly suitable approach when dealing with data sets that have a large number of features. The trends of factors were captured through allowing interaction terms between correlates and year indicators. Since we have multiple imputed data sets, the Grouped Adaptive LASSO model was fit to uniformly select an identical set of variables across multiple data sets. Factors selected by the Grouped Adaptive LASSO model were then used to fit a final logistic regression model for the final results. All data management and analyses were performed using the R programming language (R Core Team 2023 ). Missing data were imputed using the “MissForest” package. Grouped adaptive LASSO models were fit using the “miselect” package. The final logistic regression models were fit on multiple imputed data sets using the “mice” package. This study was reviewed and approved as exempt by the Institutional Review Board at Weill Cornell Medicine. 3. Results The survey‐adjusted number of respondents in each survey year, along with the demographic distributions, that is, age, sex, grade, sexual identity, and sex of sexual contact(s), are summarized in Table 1 . Most of the students in the surveys were between 15 and 17 years. The male‐to‐female ratio remains relatively stable over the years. The distribution of grade level (Grades 9, 10, 11, and 12) started off relatively even and biased toward lower grade levels starting in 1999. Sexual identity and sex of sexual contact(s) were only collected from 2015 onward. During the years when the data were collected, most participants identified as heterosexual (75.55%–86.55%). For sexual contact, most students (about 90%) reported that they only had opposite‐sex partners or that they were never engaged in sexual activity. Distributions and missing data patterns of other correlates and outcome variables are summarized in Tables S2 and S3 , respectively, of the Supporting Information . Figure 1 summarizes the trends of survey‐weight adjusted prevalence of suicidal ideation, suicide planning, suicide attempts, and suicide attempt–related injuries from 1991 to 2021. Suicidal ideation and suicide planning exhibit similar trends: the prevalence decreased from 1991 to 2005 (22.24% to 12.19% for suicidal ideation; 14.81% to 9.59% for suicide planning) and started to increase afterward, with the steepest increase occurring after 2017 (14.72% [2017] to 21.64% [2021] for suicidal ideation; 12.85% [2017] to 17.73% [2021] for suicide planning). The prevalence of suicide attempts remained stable until 2005 (varying between 6.59 [1991] and 9.53 [1995]) and started to increase afterward, also with the steepest increase occurring after 2017 (9.75% [2017] to 14.49% [2021]). The prevalence of suicide attempt–related injuries remained stable over time, varying between 1.76% (1991) and 3.72% (2015). The LASSO models selected no factor by year interactions (except for screen time ≥ 3 h per day by Year 2021 for suicidal ideation, which was not significant in the final model). Therefore, all factors were included in the models as main effects only (Table 2 ). Correlates associated with suicidal ideation were: being in a physical fight (OR = 1.51, 95% CI [1.42, 1.61]), having ever tried cigarette smoking (OR = 1.32, 95% CI [1.23, 1.42]), currently smoking cigarettes (OR = 1.77, 95% CI [1.63, 1.93]), having had their first drink of alcohol before 13 years (OR = 1.49, 95% CI [1.40, 1.59]), currently drinking alcohol (OR = 1.25, 95% CI [1.17, 1.33]), describing themselves as slightly or very overweight (OR = 1.22, 95% CI [1.13, 1.32]), currently trying to lose weight (OR = 1.28, 95% CI [1.19, 1.38]), and being female (OR = 2.51, 95% CI [2.35, 2.68]). All correlates identified were positively associated with the outcome. Correlates associated with suicide planning were: being in a physical fight (OR = 1.55, 95% CI [1.45, 1.66]), currently smoking cigarettes (OR = 1.86, 95% CI [1.70, 2.04]), having had their first drink of alcohol before 13 years (OR = 1.53, 95% CI [1.42, 1.65]), currently drinking alcohol (OR = 1.26, 95% CI [1.17, 1.37]), having tried marijuana for the first time before 13 years (OR = 1.40, 95% CI [1.26, 1.56]), describing themselves as slightly or very overweight (OR = 1.15, 95% CI [1.05, 1.25]), currently trying to lose weight (OR = 1.30, 95% CI [1.19, 1.41]), and being female (OR = 2.44, 95% CI [2.27, 2.63]). All correlates identified were positively associated with the outcome. Correlates associated with suicide attempts were: rode with a driver who had been drinking alcohol (OR = 1.24, 95% CI [1.13, 1.35]), being in a physical fight (OR = 1.89, 95% CI [1.74, 2.06]), currently smoking cigarettes (OR = 2.43, 95% CI [2.19, 2.70]), having had their first drink of alcohol before 13 years (OR = 1.48, 95% CI [1.35, 1.62]), currently drinking alcohol (OR = 1.24, 95% CI [1.13, 1.37]), having tried marijuana for the first time before 13 years (OR = 1.70, 95% CI [1.51, 1.92]), currently using marijuana (OR = 1.16, 95% CI [1.04, 1.28]), currently trying to lose weight (OR = 1.32, 95% CI [1.22, 1.43]), and being female (OR = 2.72, 95% CI [2.49, 2.97]). All correlates identified were positively associated with the outcome. Correlates associated with suicide attempt–related injuries were: being in a physical fight (OR = 1.87, 95% CI [1.62, 2.16]), currently smoking cigarettes (OR = 2.29, 95% CI [1.96, 2.68]), having had their first drink of alcohol before 13 years (OR = 1.30, 95% CI [1.12, 1.51]), having tried marijuana for the first time before 13 years (OR = 1.78, 95% CI[1.51, 2.11]), and currently using marijuana (OR = 1.56, 95% CI [1.32, 1.83]). All correlates identified were positively associated with the outcome. 4. Discussion This study aimed to investigate trends in and correlates for suicidal ideation, suicide planning, suicide attempts, and suicide attempt–related injuries among Black high school students in the United States from 1991 to 2021, using data from the YRBS. We found that the prevalence of suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts initially decreased from the 1990s to the early 2000s; however, it started increasing since 2005. The trend of suicide attempt–related injuries remained stable over time. Despite the changes in prevalence, correlates we identified from a list of risky behaviors of adolescents, as well as the impact of these factors, remained unchanged over time. Correlates we identified were very similar across all four outcomes, which fall into the following categories: violent behaviors (being in a physical fight), substance use (past or current alcohol, tobacco, or marijuana use, riding with a driver who had been drinking alcohol), body image concerns (self‐perceived overweight; trying to lose weight), and sex (being female). The steepest increase in suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts was observed from 2019 to 2021, indicating that the COVID‐19 pandemic has had a profound impact on the mental health of adolescents. The overall youth suicide rate increased during the pandemic, with Black youth being disproportionally affected (Bridge et al. 2023 ). This period had been particularly challenging for Black youth, who have faced not only the pandemic's health risk but also economic instability and the psychological impact of racial injustices (Banks 2022 ; CDC 2022 ). The murders of George Floyd and other Black Americans coincided with the first year of the pandemic, exposing Black youth to “dual pandemics” (Osman et al. 2024 ). These multifaceted stressors have contributed to a noticeable rise in mental health problems among Black adolescents (Eichstaedt et al. 2021 ; Osman et al. 2024 ), underscoring the need for extra support during this period. Black youth face unique barriers when seeking help for mental health problems (Planey et al. 2019 ). Culturally informed programs need to be developed to help them and their caregivers overcome the stigma toward mental illness. Healthcare systems also need to make culturally matched mental health providers available, affordable, and accessible for them. The gender disparities in adolescents and young adults with STB have been reported by many studies (Miranda‐Mendizabal et al. 2019 ; Wunderlich et al. 2001 ), and our study reported consistent findings for Black youth. In fact, among all the factors being evaluated, being female was the strongest correlate positively associated with suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts (OR = 2.51, 2.44, and 2.72, respectively). The prevalence of STB is very low in children under 10 years old, but increases dramatically during the transition to puberty (Nock et al. 2013 ), especially among girls (Owens et al. 2020 ). In general, girls enter puberty earlier than boys, and Black girls enter puberty earlier than White or Latina girls (Biro et al. 2006 ; Ramnitz and Lodish 2013 ). Therefore, the gender disparities in suicidality might be exacerbated among Black youth. However, further analysis is needed to evaluate this hypothesis. Observed gender disparities may also be caused by differences in methods, intent, ascertainment, psychopathology, and psychosocial reasons (Afifi 2007 ; Beautrais 2002 ), as well as differences in exposure to trauma, emotional regulation, and the acknowledgment of problems. For instance, girls are more likely to experience sexual abuse than boys (Finkelhor et al. 2014 ; Finkelhor et al. 2024 ), which is a known risk factor for suicidality (Abraham and Sher 2017 ; Wunderlich et al. 2001 ). Girls also have higher rates of internalizing disorders such as depression and anxiety, both of which can elevate the risk of STB (Soto‐Sanz et al. 2019 ). However, boys have higher rates of externalizing behaviors such as violence and substance use (Beautrais 2002 ), which may lead to more lethal methods of attempting suicide and partially explain why the gender differences vanished for suicide attempt–related injuries, even though the suicide attempt rate was higher among girls. The association between substance use and suicidality in general youth and young adults has been well documented (King et al. 2001 ; Sher and Zalsman 2005 ; Wilcox 2004 ). One study (Sheftall et al. 2022 ) also documented this association in Black youth. Our study again indicated this association. A previous study also showed that suicide planning in Black youth is positively associated with their fathers having an alcohol problem (Quinn et al. 2022 ). Therefore, family‐oriented education (Quinn et al. 2022 ) for both youth and their parents on the association between substance use and suicidality, and efforts to prevent or delay the first use of substance in youth might be beneficial to reduce the risks of both suicidality and substance use disorders in both youth and their parents. Physical fighting, as a type of violent behavior, was identified as a risk factor for suicidal behaviors for adolescents of various demographics (Juan et al. 2010 ; Li et al. 2022 ; Litwiller and Brausch 2013 ; Stack 2014 ), including Black adolescents in the United States (King et al. 2001 ). Adolescents involved in violent behaviors have an increased capability to overcome the physical pain and psychological fears that prevent people at risk from engaging in suicidal behaviors (Litwiller and Brausch 2013 ). Black youth are at a higher risk of exposure to violent behaviors than White, Latinx, or Asian youth (Wright and Fitzpatrick 2006 ) because a greater proportion of them come from low‐income families residing in under‐resourced neighborhoods, exposing them to higher levels of community violence and gang activity (Molock et al. 2023 ; Opara et al. 2022 ). Black youth are also disproportionately affected by race‐based bullying and harassment at school (Fisher et al. 2000 ), as well as experiencing racial discrimination in society, both of which serve as chronic sources of trauma, which, if left untreated, can increase the risk of future other‐directed violent behaviors (Harford et al. 2018 ; Neller et al. 2005 ). Because of these reasons, it is important to develop culturally and contextually tailored interventions at the family‐, school‐, and community levels to reduce physical fighting in Black youth. Body image concerns, self‐perceived being slightly or very overweight, and currently trying to lose weight were associated with suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts. These results are consistent with previous research on adolescent development and the association between self‐esteem and mental health (Brausch and Gutierrez 2009 ; Litwiller and Brausch 2013 ). Adolescence is a critical period for the development of body image (Voelker et al. 2015 ). On the one hand, body dissatisfaction can lead to low self‐esteem (O'Dea 2012 ), which is bidirectionally associated with poor mental health (Moksnes and Reidunsdatter 2019 ). On the other hand, discrimination based on weight increases the risk of intentional self‐harm and suicidal behaviors among adolescents (Sutin et al. 2018 ). However, according to our study, body image concerns were not associated with suicide attempt–related injuries, indicating that even though negative body image is associated with an increased risk of suicidality, the severity of these suicidal thoughts and behaviors might be relatively low. Nevertheless, schools and families should still raise awareness of the association and screen youth with body image concerns or who have rapid weight loss for timely intervention. In addition, for reasons such as historical redlining and systemic racism in the United States, Black people are more likely to live in neighborhoods lacking healthy food outlets (Bailey et al. 2017 ; Gailey and Bruckner 2019 ; Li and Yuan 2022 ; Shaker et al. 2023 ), elevating their risk of being overweight and obese. Upstream food policy and program interventions, such as building community gardens and fresh markets and promoting healthy eating habits in Black neighborhoods, may also play a role in risk reduction through helping them cope with weight gain or body image concerns. The identified correlates align with existing theories of STB and reflect shared vulnerabilities. The developmental psychopathology frameworks group behavior problems into externalizing and internalizing symptoms. Externalizing symptoms include violent behaviors and are strongly associated with substance use (Pedersen et al. 2018 ). Internalizing symptoms, on the other hand, include anxiety and depression (Marengo et al. 2018 ; Pedersen et al. 2018 ). Both body image concerns and being female are linked to a higher prevalence of experiencing internalizing symptoms (Marengo et al. 2018 ). According to the dynamic diathesis‐stress framework of developmental psychopathology, both internalizing and externalizing symptoms are bidirectionally associated with STB (Windle 2004 ). Multiple sets of factors, including biological, dispositional, and family factors, can contribute to the expression of internalizing and externalizing symptoms either directly or indirectly through poor coping of stressful events (Windle 2004 ). Internalizing and externalizing symptoms can also contribute to suicidal ideation through moderating its relationship with emotional dysregulation (Clapham and Brausch 2024 ). Many studies revealed racial disparities in adolescent suicidality. However, very few studies have exclusively studied suicidality in Black youth, despite it being the racial group with the fastest increase in suicidality. The strengths of our study included using a nationally representative sample with a large enough sample size and high response rate (low 60% range since 2015) (Mpofu et al. 2023 ). Most of the correlates we examined were collected in all survey years, allowing us to track the temporal trends of the correlates. Furthermore, since many substance use and violent behaviors are interrelated, LASSO regression allows us to select correlates that jointly affect the outcomes, providing us with a more complete picture of the complexity of the suicidal process that cannot be revealed from studying only independent associations. 4.1. Limitations Regardless of the above‐mentioned strengths, our study has several limitations. First, as a self‐reported survey, data collected by the YRBS may suffer from response bias (Furnham 1986 ). Second, during the data preprocessing step, many potential correlates were dropped because of the extremely low prevalence rate (Table S1 ). The dropped correlates might not be evaluated statistically but still might be clinically important. For example, it has been well documented that sexual‐minority youth have an elevated risk for STB (Di Giacomo et al. 2018 ; Poštuvan et al. 2019 ); however, since sexual identity was not collected until 2015, the overall low prevalence of the variable prevented us from examining its association with the outcomes. Other dropped correlates that have been documented as risk factors include being bullied (Holt et al. 2015 ), dating violence (Nahapetyan et al. 2014 ), and illicit drug use (Ammerman et al. 2018 ). Similarly, we were also unable to study recognized risk factors not collected by the YRBS, such as gender identity (Toomey et al. 2018 ), mental disorders (Bilsen 2018 ), socioeconomic status (Glenn et al. 2020 ), and family factors (Bilsen 2018 ; Glenn et al. 2020 ; Toomey et al. 2018 ), as well as race‐related factors including perceived racism (Carney‐Knisely et al. 2024 ), increase in suicide acceptability (Joe et al. 2007 ), and exposure to police violence (Carney‐Knisely et al. 2024 ). Third, we did not consider any interaction effects between correlates in this study. The interplay between different correlates could further reveal the complex dynamics between these correlates and suicidality. For example, one study found mediation effects of substance use and violent behaviors on the relationship between bullying and suicidal behaviors (Litwiller and Brausch 2013 ). Evaluating the interaction effects between correlates can be a future direction of this study. Fourth, because each YRBS survey was evaluated cross‐sectionally with questions assessed during different time frames (e.g., outcome variables were assessed in the past 12 months, whereas substance‐related variables were assessed over the lifetime or in the past 30 days), the findings of this study were purely associative and cannot be inferred as causation. Fifth, most of the potential correlates we considered were dichotomized responses, limiting our ability to study their impact by severity of exposures and reducing the statistical power of the analysis. This may partially explain the small to medium estimated effect sizes of most correlates. Nevertheless, it is important to carefully balance statistical significance with the small to medium effect sizes when making interpretations and drawing conclusions for future research and policy recommendations. 5. Clinical Implications Our study highlights key risk factors: being female, engaging in violent behaviors, having substance use problems, and experiencing body concerns that significantly heighten the risk of suicidality among Black youth. These findings carry several important clinical and policy implications for professionals. First, clinicians can adapt standardized suicide risk assessments to culturally informed, gender‐sensitive ones that explicitly screen for body image dissatisfaction, violent behaviors, and substance use among Black adolescents. Specifically, it would be helpful to capture the lived experiences of Black girls, who face unique societal pressures related to body image and identity. Second, given that the co‐occurrence of multiple mental and behavioral health challenges may be rooted in traumatic experiences among Black youth, trauma‐informed and integrative treatment approaches are urgently needed. Therapists should simultaneously address mental health disorders, substance use, and behavioral issues in a coordinated manner. For example, trauma‐focused therapies that target underlying stressors can reduce youth using maladaptive coping strategies such as engaging in fighting, drinking and using drugs, or even suicidal behaviors. More importantly, the effectiveness of these interventions depends on therapists being trained in cultural humility to work effectively with youth from different cultural backgrounds. Finally, there is a pressing need for policy investment in school‐ and community‐based mental health programs specifically tailored to the needs of Black youth. Policies should also prioritize upstream prevention efforts, including expanding access to healthy food, implementing violence prevention strategies, and reducing the availability of illicit substances in affected communities. 6. Conclusion Using data from a nationally representative survey, we observed an upward trend in the prevalence of suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts among Black high school students since 2005. Despite the changes in prevalence, correlates we identified from a list of risky behaviors of adolescents, as well as the impact of these correlates, remain unchanged over time, indicating that the increase in prevalence might be driven by other factors not captured by this study. The COVID‐19 pandemic might explain the steep increase from 2019 to 2021. However, it could not explain the increase from 2005 to 2019. Another potential explanation for the change in suicidality could be the shift in the prevalence of the correlates. Most of the correlates identified in this study became less prevalent over time; however, changes in the prevalence of other known correlates not assessed in our study, including exposure to racism and police violence, suicide acceptability, and mental health comorbidities, still need to be examined. Further research is needed to understand why, despite the steady decrease in the prevalence of suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts from the late 1990s to early 2000s, all trends have gone upward since 2005. Such investigations are crucial for developing targeted interventions and prevention strategies that are sensitive to the unique experiences of Black adolescents. Author Contributions M. S.
