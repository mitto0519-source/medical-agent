ABSTRACT Pharmacogenetics is a promising strategy to facilitate individualized care for patients with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). Research is ongoing to identify the optimal genetic markers for predicting outcomes to antidepressant therapies. The primary aim of this systematic review was to summarize antidepressant pharmacogenetic studies to enhance understanding of the genes, variants, datatypes/methodologies, and outcomes investigated in the context of MDD. The secondary aim was to identify clinical genetic panels indicated for antidepressant prescribing and summarize their genes and variants. Screening of N = 5793 articles yielded N = 390 for inclusion, largely comprising adult (≥ 18 years) populations. Top‐studied variants identified in the search were discussed and compared with those represented on the N = 34 clinical genetic panels that were identified. Summarization of articles revealed sources of heterogeneity across studies and low rates of replicability of pharmacogenetic associations. Heterogeneity was present in outcome definitions, treatment regimens, and differential inclusion of mediating variables in analyses. Efficacy outcomes (i.e., response, remission) were studied at greater frequency than adverse‐event outcomes. Studies that used advanced analytical approaches, such as machine learning, to integrate variants with complimentary biological datatypes were fewer in number but achieved higher rates of significant associations with treatment outcomes than candidate variant approaches. As large biological datasets become more prevalent, machine learning will be an increasingly valuable tool for parsing the complexity of antidepressant response. This review provides valuable context and considerations surrounding pharmacogenetic associations in MDD which will help inform future research and translation efforts for guiding antidepressant care. Summary What is the current knowledge on the topic? ○ Pharmacogenetic (PGx) studies in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) often fail to produce replicable gene‐outcome associations within antidepressant medication trials. However, many pharmacogenetic panels are implemented in clinical settings and promise guidance toward tailored antidepressant selection. What question did this study address? ○ This study primarily sought to characterize existing PGx research in MDD patients and provide a summarization of all drugs, genes, outcomes, and patient populations represented in the literature. Secondarily, this study characterized genes and variants included on clinical panels and contrasted this with the literature. It analyzed study methodologies used and the current state of evidence to guide future research endeavors toward personalized antidepressant therapy. What does this study add to our knowledge? ○ This study adds a full summary of the current evidence behind PGx studies in MDD along with a comparison to clinically implemented panels. This illuminates sources of heterogeneity in research methods within this field and identifies methodological approaches, including machine learning and multimodal data integration, which show promise for future research endeavors. How might this change clinical pharmacology or translational science ○ This study provides a launching point from which future researchers can conduct effective PGx studies in MDD, with the aim of improving personalized medicine for patients with MDD. 1. Introduction Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a complex illness and a leading cause of medical disability worldwide [ 1 ]. Management of MDD is achieved primarily through pharmacological and psychosocial approaches [ 2 ]. Typically, only one‐third of depressed patients treated pharmacologically achieve the primary goal of symptomatic remission following their initial treatment due to lack of efficacy or side effect burden [ 3 ]. To increase the likelihood of drug efficacy and reduce the risk of adverse events, pharmacogenetics (PGx)—the study of genetic contributions to variation in drug outcomes—has emerged as a compelling strategy for guiding antidepressant selection [ 4 , 5 ]. A large portion of the population—estimated at 80%—carries functional variation in genes involved in mediating the effects of commonly prescribed drugs, including first‐line antidepressants [ 6 ]. However, there is mixed evidence supporting the clinical benefit of PGx testing in MDD [ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ]. Consequently, antidepressants are largely still prescribed using artisanal trial and error approaches, and finding the optimal treatment often takes several medication trials over the course of many months [ 15 , 16 ]. Efforts are ongoing to better understand which genes are most predictive of antidepressant outcomes [ 2 ]. The set of important genes likely comprises both those with pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic mechanisms. Antidepressants likely exert their pharmacodynamic effects by increasing extracellular concentrations of monoamine neurotransmitters (e.g., serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine), or by promoting synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis [ 17 ]. The pharmacokinetics of antidepressants, on the other hand, are largely governed by Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) drug metabolizing enzymes [ 18 ]. Of the more than 50 CYP450 genes, CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 are the best studied in psychiatry [ 19 ] and should be considered when making therapeutic recommendations for the antidepressants venlafaxine and citalopram, respectively, according to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [ 20 ]. In addition to the FDA, pharmacogenetic organizations including the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) [ 21 ], Dutch Pharmacogenetic Working Group (DPWG) [ 22 ], and Pharmacogenomics Knowledge Base (PharmGKB) [ 23 ] curate lists of potentially drug‐impacting genes from peer‐reviewed literature and clinical guidelines. CPIC recently assessed five genes ( CYP2D6 , CYP2C19 , CYP2B6 , SLC6A4 , and HTR2A ) and recommended three ( CYP2D6 , CYP2C19 , and CYP2B6 ) to inform prescribing of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs, a class of first‐line antidepressants) [ 24 ]. There have been numerous reviews on the utility of genotype‐guided care compared to standard care in MDD [ 14 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ], on candidate genes and their associations with antidepressant therapies [ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 ], and on pharmacogenetic associations with specific drug classes in MDD [ 36 ]. However, no review has summarized the methodology and results of pharmacogenetic studies in patients with MDD across the spectrum of studied genes and available antidepressants. Therefore, this review aims to address this gap and answer the following questions: (i) which antidepressants have been evaluated in pharmacogenetic contexts, and to what degree are reported associations antidepressant‐specific or drug class specific? This is critical to understand, especially given differences in drug metabolism even among members of the same drug class [ 19 ]. (ii) What genes have been evaluated for potential impact on each antidepressant? (iii) Which variants on such genes have been evaluated, and do these have functional consequences? (iv) How have researchers defined the outcomes to antidepressant treatments, and what degree of heterogeneity in these definitions (and the timepoints at which outcomes were assessed) exists among studies? (v) What statistical and methodological approaches have been utilized in this field (i.e., what datatypes and covariates have researchers accounted for in explaining pharmacogenetic associations in MDD)? (vi) What heterogeneity exists within patient cohorts (including what age groups, such as pediatric, adult, and geriatric have been evaluated)? Given the importance that geographic ancestry may play in depressive presentation and antidepressant therapy outcomes [ 37 ], population characteristics of the articles included in this review were characterized in depth in a companion review by Jackson et al. (simultaneously submitted with this manuscript for peer‐review. Currently In Press ). This systematic review was conducted to answer the preceding questions. Promising variants in genes outside of those recently reviewed by CPIC ( CYP2D6 , CYP2C19 , CYP2B6 , SLC6A4 , and HTR2A ) and the replicability of their associations with treatment outcomes were summarized. As a secondary aim, this review also identified current clinic pharmacogenetic panels offered for MDD, summarized their genes and variants, and compared the literature with the current state of clinical implementation. Identifying robust, replicable genes for guiding MDD pharmacotherapies and the contexts (e.g., patient characteristics, additional biological covariates) in which they are most informative remains a priority for guiding future research and translation efforts. 2. Materials and Methods 2.1. Data Sources and Search Strategy A comprehensive search was conducted to identify PGx studies in patients with MDD. The search comprised six databases and was performed on June 16, 2022. No date limits were applied to the search. Animal studies were excluded. Results were limited to English Language. Databases used (and their coverage dates) were Ovid MEDLINE(R) (1946 to Present and Epub Ahead of Print, In‐Process &amp; Other Non‐Indexed Citations and Daily), Ovid Embase (1974+), Ovid Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (1991+), Ovid Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (2005+), APA PsycInfo (1967+), and Scopus via Elsevier (1788+). The search strategy was designed and conducted by a medical librarian (LH) with input from the study's investigators (CWG, LJ, APA). The aim was to broadly capture PGx studies to comprehensively characterize the field, so no restrictions on therapeutic outcomes were applied in the search strategy. Controlled vocabulary supplemented with keywords was used. The final strategy listing search terms used and how they were combined is available in the appendix. See Table S1 for full search strategy. All resulting study abstracts were screened for inclusion (KD, JB), and articles were eliminated if they were: (i) duplicate records, (ii) case studies, (iii) systematic reviews, or (iv) irrelevant studies. The full text of remaining studies was screened to eliminate those which: (i) did not include patients with MDD, (ii) were a review or editorial, (iii) did not study genes, (iv) did not include a therapeutic outcome, or (v) otherwise failed to meet the inclusion criteria of assessing genetics in the context of pharmacotherapy outcomes in MDD. Any disagreements were adjudicated with the help of a third reviewer (CWG, LJ, or APA). 2.2. Analysis of Study Methodologies Quality assessment was completed using the Ottawa Newcastle Assessment [ 38 ] and the Cochrane Assessment [ 39 ]. Following intended use, Ottawa Newcastle assessment was used for the following study designs: cohort studies, multicenter studies, retrospective studies, and open‐label studies. Cochrane assessment was implemented for studies with a randomized controlled design. Of the N = 390 articles collected from full text review, N = 29 were assessed through Cochrane. Quality of all other articles was assessed using Newcastle by KD and JB, with review by CWG and LJ. 2.3. Study Characterization The following information was extracted from each study: (i) study population size and age group (e.g., pediatric, adolescent, adult, elderly, geriatric), (ii) antidepressant drugs investigated, (iii) genes investigated, (iv) top variants investigated, (v) therapeutic outcome(s), (vi) outcome assessment timepoint, (vii) data types used and corresponding methodological approaches toward analysis, and (viii) significance of gene‐outcome association (binary yes vs. no) for top variants. Following data extraction, top variants were defined as variants occurring in the 10 most frequently studied genes, as determined from study counts. Next, the rate of replicability with which these variants associated with efficacy and side effect outcomes was determined. The scope of top variants was defined as those occurring in the top 10 studied genes given the large quantity of genes that were identified in the search. Top studied genes excluded the aforementioned five genes ( CYP2D6 , CYP2C19 , CYP2B6 , SLC6A4 , and HTR2A ) which have been previously reviewed in depth [ 24 ]. Notably, as ethnic background may significantly impact MDD presentation and antidepressant therapy outcomes [ 37 ], ethnic background of study populations was characterized in depth in a companion review by Jackson et al. (simultaneously submitted with this manuscript for peer‐review. Currently In Press ). 2.4. Identification of Pharmacogenomic Panels and Information Extraction Given a lack of consensus database containing all available clinical pharmacogenomic panels for MDD, a Google search was undertaken to identify panels for individualizing antidepressant therapies by KD and JB, with review by CWG and LJ. Of note, academic laboratories offering testing only for patients in their hospital or health care system are likely not represented due to the lack of external advertising. This search was also supplemented with a search of the Genetic Test Registry [ 39 ] to assess for additional academic laboratories. Google search terms included: “pharmacogenomic panels”, “Major Depressive Disorder”, “antidepressants”, “psychiatry,” and “PGx testing for MDD”. Panels were included if their websites were in English. In instances where a company or organization provided multiple tests for this purpose (e.g., Mayo Clinic CYP2C19 test, CYP2D6 test, psychotropic panel test) the most comprehensive panel is listed. This search was updated at the completion of the manuscript writing to include all panels identified on May 23rd, 2023. As most panels were not specific to antidepressant medications but rather were advertised for assisting with prescribing a wide range of medications, panels were retained if they offered pharmacogenomic support for at least one of the following classes of antidepressant drugs: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), norepinephrine‐dopamine reuptake inhibitors (NDRIs), monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), and atypical antidepressants. Details of panels including intended use and availability of genomic variants were extracted from company websites. 2.5. Scientific Evidence Pertaining to Genes Included on Commercial Pharmacogenomic Panels Genes which were included on pharmacogenetic panels were characterized by the frequency with which they were included on panels and by functional descriptions according to the PharmGKB database. Specific genetic variants for top genes were also characterized by the frequency with which they were included on panels, when made available on websites of the respective panel designers. The genes and variants which were most frequently included on panels were assessed in terms of the scientific literature; outcomes that these variants were studied in the context of (e.g., efficacy outcomes, adverse events) and the proportion of significant associations with respective outcomes was reported. Significance was determined as defined by each study's authors. 3. Results 3.1. Search Output and Final Dataset The search yielded N = 5793 studies whose abstracts were screened for inclusion. Following initial abstract screening, N = 708 records were eligible for full text screening. After full text screening, N = 390 articles were left for inclusion in the systematic review (See PRISMA flow diagram, Figure 1 ). The large majority ( N = 389/390) of articles comprised primarily adult populations (≥ 18 years of age), compared with only one article which studied PGx of antidepressants in depressed adolescents exclusively. Of the studies including adults, ( N = 14) focused on elderly or geriatric populations (&gt; 60, &gt; 65 years of age, depending on study definition). One study focused on geriatric adults &gt; 80 years of age. The sample size of the MDD cohorts ranged from 10 to 9531 individuals (median 181). 3.2. Analysis of Study Methodologies Quality assessment was completed for 327 nonrandomized studies using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale [ 38 ]. Of these, N = 243 studies (74.3%) were rated as having good quality, N = 8 (2.4%) had fair quality, and N = 75 (23.0%) had poor quality. Of these studies, N = 232 (70%) were representative of the underlying populations from which they were drawn, and N = 280 (85.6%) had adequate follow‐up (Table S2 ). An additional N = 29 articles (all randomized trials) were assessed for quality using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool [ 39 ]. Of these 29 randomized trials, N = 14 (48%) studies were rated as having low overall risk of bias, and the remaining studies had high overall risk of bias (Table S3 ). 3.3. Literature Study Characterization 3.3.1. Antidepressants Investigated SSRIs, including citalopram ( N = 99), fluoxetine ( N = 89), paroxetine ( N = 80), and escitalopram ( N = 68), were the most frequently studied antidepressant drugs (Figure 2 ). Of note, these numbers are reflective of the number of publications, not distinct patient cohorts, as the same cohort or portions of the same cohort may have been studied in multiple publications with respect to different treatment outcomes. The next most common treatment context ( N = 61) was naturalistic designs or cohorts comprising a variety of different antidepressants. In these analyses, results were aggregated across antidepressant drugs and drug classes. SNRIs were the next most frequent class studied, followed by an ‘other category’ including multiple classes of less frequently studied antidepressants, such as MAOIs, NDRIs, atypical antidepressants, and tetracyclic antidepressants. To lesser frequency, TCAs have also been studied in pharmacogenetic contexts in MDD; the most frequently studied TCA was nortriptyline ( N = 17 studies). Combination antidepressant regimens were the rarest of classes studied, and included citalopram + bupropion, escitalopram + bupropion, citalopram + buspirone, venlafaxine + mirtazapine, nefazodone + pindolol (Figure 2 ). 3.3.2. Genes Investigated Multiple genome‐wide ( N = 18) and exome‐wide ( N = 2) studies were published. The remaining studies included candidate variant or gene analyses on N = 344 distinct genes (ranked by frequency of study inclusion in Table S4 ). The most frequently studied gene was a serotonin transporter, SLC6A4 , appearing in N = 91 studies, followed by a serotonin receptor HTR2A ( N = 42 studies). Given that these have been recently reviewed by CPIC [ 24 ], we did not presently characterize the specific associations between variants and pharmacological outcomes in these genes, nor in CYP2D6 , CYP2C19 , or CYP2B6 , which were the fourth, sixth, and 60th most characterized genes in the context of antidepressant pharmacogenetics, respectively (Table S4 ). The third most frequently studied gene was brain‐derived neurotrophic factor BDNF ( N = 41 studies) (Table 1 ). Of the N = 344 genes, 62% ( N = 212) had been studied in the context of antidepressant outcomes in only one published manuscript, and 17% ( N = 57) appeared in only two reports (Table S4 ). 3.3.3. Variants Investigated Excluding variants in the aforementioned five genes which have been recently reviewed [ 24 ], the most frequently studied variant ( N = 35 studies) was the nonsynonymous variant rs6265 (Val66Met, also known as NM_001709.5 :c.196G&gt;A) in BDNF (Table S5 ). The next most frequently studied variant was rs4680 Val108/158Met (also known as NM_000754.4 :c.472G&gt;A, p.Val158Met) in COMT ( N = 21 studies). The synonymous variant rs1045642 (3435C&gt;T, also known as NM_001348946.2 :c.3435T&gt;C, p.Ile1145=) in ABCB1 ( N = 20 studies) was next, followed by a nonsynonymous triallelic position rs2032582 (2677G&gt;T or 2677G&gt;A, also known as NM_001348946.2 :c.2677T&gt;G, p.Ser893Ala, and c.2677T&gt;G, p.Ser893Thr, respectively) in ABCB1 ( N = 18 studies), then the intronic variant rs1800532 (commonly referred to as 218A&gt;C, otherwise known as NM_004179.3 :c.803+221C&gt;A) in TPH1 ( N = 17 studies), and rs6295 (−\1019C&gt;G, also known as NM_000524.4 :c.‐1019G&gt;C) in HTR1A ( N = 14 studies). Different nomenclature systems, including the Human Genome Variant Society [ 40 ], Database of Short Genetic Variation [ 41 ], star (*) alleles [ 42 ], or metabolizing phenotypes, were utilized across the literature to describe genetic variants. 3.3.4. Outcomes to Antidepressant Therapies Investigated Outcomes to antidepressant therapies that were studied in the context of pharmacogenetics are summarized in Table 2 , which contains outcome categories and the two most frequent definitions (see Table S6 for complete table). Identified outcomes, derived from the phrasing of study authors, were the following: response, remission, score change in depression rating scales, adverse events, pharmacokinetic/blood draw parameters, and other outcomes, and there were multiple outcome definitions for each category. Studies frequently evaluated more than one outcome. The most frequently studied outcome was antidepressant response ( N = 219 of 390 studies [56.2%]), which had 17 distinct definitions in the literature. The most frequent definition of response was a 50% score reduction in symptoms captured by one of five distinct clinical rating scales (Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology Clinician‐Rated [QIDS‐C], Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [HAM‐D], Montgomery‐Asberg Depression Rating Scale [MADRS], Clinical Global Impressions Scale, and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale). The most prevalent definition was according to the HAM‐D, at 56.6% of studies evaluating antidepressant response. Additional definitions of response included 40% reductions or 60% reductions in clinical rating scale‐derived scores, or scores of less than a finite cutoff on a rating scale (Table S6 ). The next most frequently studied outcome was remission ( N = 190 studies [48.7%]), typically defined by achieving a score threshold (the value of which varied across studies) on one of multiple scales (HAM‐D, QIDS‐C, MADRS, Beck Depression Inventory, or Geriatric Depression Scale). Score change as a continuous measure was an additional efficacy outcome reported by 167 studies (42.8%). Adverse event outcomes were investigated less frequently than efficacy outcomes ( N = 85 side effect studies [21.8%]). There were 28 distinct scales or definitions used to measure various side effects, the most prevalent of which was the Udvalg for Kliniske Undersogelser Side‐Effect Rating Scale (UKU) at N = 20 studies evaluating side effects (23.5%). Pharmacokinetic parameters related to pharmacogenetics, such as antidepressant half‐life or plasma serotonin, were studied in N = 39 (10%) of manuscripts. Finally, other outcomes ( N = 34 studies [8.7%]) included outcomes, such as nonresponse to two consecutive antidepressant treatments ( N = 2 studies), sustained response ( N = 6 studies), or early partial response ( N = 2 studies). Outcomes were most frequently measured up to 6 weeks ( N = 125 studies, or 32% of studies) or 8 weeks ( N = 86, 22%). The next most frequent final outcome assessment timepoints were 12 weeks ( N = 55, 14%) or 4 weeks ( N = 52, 13%) (Figure 3 ). The maximum duration of study was 156 weeks (3 years) [ 43 ]. 3.3.5. Data Types and Methodological Approaches Employed by Studies Data types and corresponding methodological procedures employed by studies are outlined in Figure 4 . The most common approach toward investigating pharmacogenomic associations was simple associations between genetic variant(s) and outcomes ( N = 205 studies). Both candidate variant studies (investigating one or a few variants alone) and genome‐wide association studies and exome‐wide association studies (which investigate associations between single variants and outcomes, repeated across the entire genome or exome) are examples of this approach. These studies utilized techniques, such as linear and logistic regression, Pearson's chi‐squared tests, Wald tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, Kruskal–Wallis test, Pearson's or Spearman's correlation tests, student's T ‐tests, Fisher's exact tests, Wilcoxon Rank‐Sum tests, Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or repeated‐measures ANOVA. Less common approaches ( N = 1 study) were classification and regression tree (CART) and TreeNet gradient boosting approaches. When data were available, these studies largely adjusted for potentially relevant covariates, such as age, baseline depression severity, marital status, professional status, number of prior MDD episodes, depressive characteristics (e.g., presence of melancholic features) or biological sex. In naturalistic cohorts where patients received one of multiple available antidepressant drugs, the drug was also frequently included as a covariate. Sixty‐six percent of studies using these approaches found at least one significant pharmacogenomic association with an antidepressant therapy outcome, with significance defined according to study authors. The next most common approach toward uncovering pharmacogenomic associations was to consider haplotype and allelic associations with outcomes. Seventy‐five percent of such studies found at least one significant association between variants and outcomes. Such analyses accounted for interactions between multiple variants and analyzed variant haplotype blocks or metabolizing enzyme star (*) alleles comprising multiple variants. This category of analyses also included studies that integrated multiple variants that were selected a priori based upon experimental, theoretical, or computational analyses and integrated into a unified model for outcome prediction using approaches that included multivariate regression models or supervised machine learning methods [ 44 , 45 , 46 ]. Analyses by demographic subgroups (e.g., ancestry, biological sex) or clinical or pharmacological (e.g., depressive characteristics and comorbidities) subgroups were the next most frequent analysis strategies, each producing 84% significant associations. Depressive subgroup characteristics in studies which stratified accordingly were defined in several ways, including by personality dimensions captured by the Tridimensional Personality Questionnaire (TPQ) or by comorbid disorders (e.g., anxiety). These utilized the aforementioned statistical approaches within subgroups as well as linear mixed effects models, two‐way ANOVAs, and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). These studies also used machine learning approaches including CART, support vector machines, random forest, extreme gradient boosting, and adaptive boosting [ 47 , 48 ]. This was followed by variant associations which interacted with additional sources of non‐imaging biological data such as methylation status of selected genes [ 49 , 50 ], concentrations of drugs or drug metabolites [ 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 ], or concentrations of endogenous proteins or metabolites in plasma, serum, or urine [ 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 ] ( Supporting Information ). Of note, whereas some studies analyzed each data modality individually, others integrated information from different data sources (e.g., genomics and metabolomics) in one unified model. Such studies often utilized machine learning approaches, including supervised machine learning models, unsupervised machine learning models, and network analyses [ 63 , 66 ]. These techniques enable the detection of complex interactions among multiple data points which may impact multifaceted outcomes, such as antidepressant response [ 2 ]. The next most prevalent methodological approach employed was gene‐level and pathway‐level associations with outcomes. Pathways investigated included neurotrophic, serotonergic, neuroplastic, and second messenger pathways. Given the integration of multiple variants across potentially multiple pathways, studies in this category often relied on machine learning approaches, such as random forest, gradient boosted decision trees, neural networks, recursive partitioning, learning vector quantization, and penalized regression for the prediction of antidepressant outcomes [ 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 ]. Finally, there were seven analyses which integrated variants with imaging data, and all seven found significant associations with outcomes [ 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 ]. Imaging methodologies included functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), positron emission tomography (PET) measurement of serotonin transporter occupancy, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). 3.3.6. Associations Between Top Studied Variants and Outcomes Associations between top variants and treatment outcomes are represented in Figure 5A (excluding variants in the aforementioned five genes which have been recently reviewed, SLC6A4, HTR2A, CYP2D6, CYP2B6 , and CYP2C19 ). When aggregated according to study treatment strategy (e.g., cohort prescribed citalopram, cohort prescribed any SSRI, naturalistic cohort comprising multiple antidepressant treatments), the study count for each variant ranged from one to ten studies for efficacy outcomes, and one to three studies for adverse event outcomes. Only one variant, which had been studied in multiple publications, had 100% replicability of significant associations across studies ( GNB3 rs5443 [C825T] associated significantly with efficacy outcomes in 2/2 studies pooling subjects taking multiple antidepressants). The next highest rate of association replicability was 60% ( ABCB1 rs2235040 associated significantly with efficacy outcomes in 3/5 studies pooling subjects taking multiple antidepressants). All remaining pharmacogenetic associations achieved rates of replicability at 50% or below, as reflected by color intensity in Figure 5 . The top studied variants were BDNF rs6265 (Val66Met) and ABCB1 rs1045642 (C3435T), which were both studied in the context of efficacy outcomes in cohorts comprising patients taking one of multiple available antidepressants, in 10 and seven publications, respectively. Of the 10 publications studying BDNF rs6265 (Val66Met) in patient cohorts comprising multiple antidepressant drugs, three [ 78 , 79 , 80 ] found a significant pharmacogenetic association with efficacy outcomes. One of these found an association with treatment response such that Val66Met heterozygous patients responded better compared to Val/Val homozygous patients, especially in Asian populations [ 80 ]. This association was specific to treatment response (with response being defined as at least 50% decrease in Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM‐D) or Montgomery and Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) total score) and did not replicate when considering symptomatic remission (defined as a final HAM‐D total score of 7 or less) in that study and another [ 78 ]. The third study found a significant association between rs6265 and symptomatic remission at 6‐months of antidepressant treatment in late‐life depression, but not at 3‐months of treatment, indicating that associations with remission may be age and duration specific [ 79 ]. The associations between BDNF rs6265 and efficacy outcomes had also been evaluated in cohorts comprising patients taking one of multiple available SSRIs, as well as individually in the context of specific SSRIs (escitalopram, citalopram, paroxetine, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine) and non‐SSRI antidepressants (venlafaxine, duloxetine, milnacipran, desipramine, and mirtazapine). A portion of the pooled SSRI and specific SSRI studies found significant associations between rs6265 and efficacy outcomes, whereas none of the non‐SSRI studies found significant associations. There were small numbers of non‐SSRI studies (one per drug, except for venlafaxine, with two studies). Only one of the seven studies found a significant association between ABCB1 rs1045642 (C3435T) and efficacy outcomes in cohorts which pooled subjects prescribed multiple different antidepressants [ 81 ]. Significant associations were also found between this variant and efficacy outcomes in treatment‐specific cohorts of patients prescribed escitalopram or venlafaxine [ 82 , 83 ]. One study found that the association with remission to escitalopram could be modeled using a haplotype block comprising five ABCB1 variants (rs1882478‐rs2235048‐rs2235047‐rs1045642‐rs6949448). This study also found that T–T–T‐C‐C was associated with a slower remission rate to escitalopram treatment [ 82 ]. Another study suggested that carriers of rs1045642 TT remitted to venlafaxine at a rate of 73.3%, compared with 12.5% for CC carriers. That study also found that the ABCB1 C3435T polymorphism exerts pharmacokinetic effects and may influence the dose of escitalopram needed to remit, with TT homozygotes requiring lower doses than CT or CC carriers [ 83 ]. In considering associations between genetic variants and adverse events following antidepressant treatment, the nonsynonymous triallelic ABCB1 variant rs2032582 (2677T&gt;G/A), resulting in Ser893Ala/Thr, was the most widely studied variant that was most significantly associated with adverse events in cohorts comprising multiple drug classes (Figure 5B ). This variant was studied in the context of multiple adverse events, including those captured by the Dosage Record and Treatment Emergent Symptoms Scale (DOTES) (mental side effects, neuromuscular symptoms, anticholinergic/GI symptoms, cardiovascular symptoms) following amitriptyline therapy [ 84 ], suicidal ideation in multiple drug classes [ 85 ], Frequency, Intensity, Burden of Side Effects Rating (FIBSER) Scale in multiple drug classes [ 86 ], sexual dysfunction specific to SSRIs [ 87 ], and venlafaxine‐induced akathisia [ 88 ]. Although no significant association existed between rs2032582 and SSRI‐induced sexual dysfunction or amitriptyline‐induced DOTES side effects, a significant association existed between venlafaxine‐induced akathisia and the Frequency, Intensity, and Burden of Side Effects Rating Scale (FIBSER) side effects and suicidal ideation in patients prescribed multiple drug classes. Regarding suicidal ideation, the carriers of the minor T allele had a higher rate of treatment‐increasing suicidal ideation compared with other genotypes [ 85 ]. 3.4. Pharmacogenetic Panels and Information Extraction Thirty‐four pharmacogenetic panels with potential utility in aiding MDD pharmacotherapy treatment were identified in the Google and Genetic Test Registry searches (Table S7 ). In total, these panels genotyped variants in 143 distinct genes. For details on panels, including genes, publicly available variants, and indications, see Table S7 . These panels had a broad range of indications for mental health and other common diagnoses. For example, panels were advertised for “Depression and other psychiatric disorders”, “Common mental health indications”, or “Multiple therapeutic classes including antidepressants”. Additionally, panels were often not specific to one or multiple antidepressant drugs—rather, they were often advertised as assisting with prescribing for multiple drug classes and indications. Most panels (91%, N = 31) had publicly accessibly information on the genes they contained. Panels varied regarding the numbers of genes included, ranging from one gene to 57 genes, with a median of 13 genes. Details on how such genes were used, for which therapeutic treatments, and which treatment outcomes, was largely unavailable. The scope covered by some panels was broad (e.g., Pain &amp; Mental Health panel, from Inagene covering multiple psychiatric medications) while others were specific (e.g., Sinochip Diagnostics CYP2C19 test). Panels often included a mixture of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic genes (Table S8 ). The most common genes included on clinical panels and their frequencies of appearing on panels can be found in Table 3 . Of the eight most common genes, seven belong to the cytochrome P450 drug metabolizing enzyme family. Additionally, COMT , MTHFR , SLCO1B1 , UGT2B15 , and VKORC1 , all with pharmacokinetic metabolism or transport functions, were represented among the most common genes. Pharmacodynamic genes including HTR2C , OPRM1 , HTR2A , DRD2 , and GRIK4 were also among the top 15 most included genes. Of the panels which specified their genes, 29% ( N = 9) made publicly available all genotyped variants. These panels varied regarding how many variants they genotyped, ranging from two variants on two genes (Antidepressants and Antipsychotics Pharmacogenetics Test from Unilabs Genetics) to 150 variants across eight genes (Psych HealthPGx Panel by RPRD). The most common variants genotyped include CYP2C19*2 , CYP2C19*17 , CYP2C19*3 , CYP2D6*4 , and CYP2D6*41 on eight panels (Table S7 ). The CYP2C19*2 , CYP2C19*17 , and CYP2C19*3 result in decreased, enhanced, and decreased enzyme function, respectively, and comprise the Tier 1 variants for CYP2C19 [ 89 ]. Meanwhile, CYP2D6*4 and CYP2D6*41 result in no function and decreased function, respectively, and are likewise represented among Tier 1 variants for CYP2D6 [ 90 ]. 4. Discussion This work provides a comprehensive characterization of the pharmacogenetics of antidepressant therapies for patients with MDD. This includes a summarization of the genes, variants, antidepressants, datatypes/methodologies, and therapeutic outcomes studied in MDD, as well as the genes and variants that have been implemented on clinical panels for guiding antidepressant selection. This work reveals heterogeneity within the research landscape, including in the definitions of therapeutic outcomes, in the timepoints at which such outcomes were assessed, in patient population characteristics, in the inclusion of mediating biological covariates, and in cohorts that pool patients prescribed various antidepressants or antidepressant classes. Like others [ 91 ], this review finds low rates of replication of associations between variants and antidepressant treatment outcomes. The outlined heterogeneity, combined with practical difficulty in adequately powering larger genetic studies, may underlie the failure of promising pharmacogenetic markers in MDD to generalize across studies. Toward the goal of identifying methodological approaches that may improve the generalizability of findings, this review summarizes the strategies implemented and their success rates. Finally, this work highlights potentially promising variants associated with adverse event and efficacy outcomes in an antidepressant‐specific manner. Genes which play pharmacokinetic roles (e.g., CYP450 family members, ABCB1 drug efflux pump, Catechol‐O‐Methyltransferase COMT ) and pharmacodynamic roles (e.g., serotonin transporters, serotonin receptors) in antidepressant outcomes were represented among the top studied genes in the literature. The genetic variants most frequently studied in the context of efficacy outcomes (e.g., ABCB1 C3435T, BDNF Val66Met) had mixed evidence underlying their associations with outcomes, with some studies finding significant associations while others did not. In fact, all but two variants had rates of replicability with efficacy outcomes at 50% or less. For adverse event outcomes, one variant, ABCB1 G2677A, achieved full replication across studies, with 3/3 studies finding significant associations [ 85 , 86 , 88 ]. ABCB1 encodes P‐glycoprotein, an ATP‐dependent transmembrane transporter of xenobiotics including antidepressants [ 92 ]. Thus, individual differences in ABCB1 activity or expression influence drug bioavailability, which may explain the impact of this variant on antidepressant adverse events. The remaining variants studied in the context of adverse events achieved replication at rates of 66% or lower. Heterogeneity across studies in outcome definition and assessment timepoint may contribute to the low rates of replication of pharmacogenetic markers. As no universally accepted biomarker exists to quantify depressive severity, scientists and clinicians rely on standardized rating scales, such as the QIDS‐C or HAM‐D to assess symptomatology. This review finds that treatment response and symptomatic remission, the most common definitions of efficacy endpoints, are defined across the literature using six different rating scales, sometimes with different thresholds to define improvement. This review also finds variation in the timepoint(s) at which outcomes were assessed—final assessments most commonly occurred 6‐ or 8‐weeks following treatment initiation, but a large portion of studies (&gt; 50 each) made a final assessment at 4‐ or 12‐weeks. Interestingly, multiple studies which followed patients for longer durations found that some pharmacogenetic markers had an ability to predict long‐term (&gt; 1 year) but not short‐term (6 or 8 weeks) outcomes to multiple classes of antidepressants [ 43 , 79 ]. The extent to which pharmacogenetic markers are beneficial for short‐ vs. long‐term outcome prediction warrants additional research. This review also found that a large proportion of antidepressant studies are not drug‐specific but rather pool patients taking one of multiple antidepressants—the fifth most common cohort was pooled subjects prescribed a variety of antidepressants ( N = 61 studies). This pooling approach is useful toward increasing statistical power or identifying potential shared mechanisms among various antidepressants or classes of antidepressants. However, the resulting gene‐outcome associations with this approach are not drug‐specific and cannot address “which antidepressant amongst several options would most benefit my patient”? Given different mechanisms of action across drug classes, and even within a single drug class, this may contribute another source of heterogeneity and explain low rates of replication across studies. Design of future analyses should therefore prioritize investigation of pharmacogenomic associations in a drug‐specific manner. This work also demonstrates that the addition of relevant biological or clinical variables to complement genetic variant(s) improved the ability to predict therapeutic outcomes. This is reflected by the proportion of studies achieving a significant association with outcomes—analyses considering only single variants using simple statistical approaches reported a smaller proportion of significant pharmacogenetic associations compared to studies which consider additional features (e.g., biomarkers, pathway‐level data, imaging data) through more complex statistical approaches including machine learning. For example, studies which utilize machine learning approaches to integrate genes involved in different neurotransmission pathways (e.g., monoamine, glutamate, or GABA) or consider genes alongside neuroendocrine and clinical variables achieved higher predictability of antidepressant outcomes [ 45 , 67 , 93 ]. In this regard, machine learning appears to be a helpful tool toward integrating large or complementary data sources to improve predictions of antidepressant response. Although genetic influence on pharmacological outcomes can account for a portion of individual variation in drug therapy outcomes, additional factors should also be considered. One such consideration is phenoconversion, or the conversion to a different metabolizer status (e.g., extensive to poor metabolizer) based on environmental factors or drug–drug interactions [ 94 ]. Phenoconversion has been described in depressed patients prescribed antidepressants metabolized by CYP2D6, including venlafaxine [ 95 ]. Additional variables to consider include patient exposure to marijuana‐derived compounds, such as tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), given the increasing prevalence of marijuana use in MDD [ 96 ] and the interactions between THC, CBD, and cytochrome P450 metabolizing enzymes [ 97 ]. Ultimately, the therapeutic predictions generated by clinical pharmacogenetic panels should be contextualized alongside patient‐specific environmental factors. This review revealed that current clinical PGx panels more frequently include CYP450 drug metabolism markers than pharmacodynamic markers. However, in many cases, the variant content of the panels was not publicly available. Furthermore, many panels offered support for multiple drugs and diagnoses without indicating which genes and/or variants are utilized for each drug and for each indication. Therefore, presently, the ability to compare between clinical implementation and scientific evidence underlying variant‐outcome associations in MDD is limited. Testing laboratories should be encouraged to provide greater transparency to aid clinicians, patients, and researchers in understanding the underlying scientific evidence of their tests, especially when combinatorial algorithms are in use. This work has limitations. This review included data from PGx associations at all published timepoints, yet did not do a characterization across all studies on whether variants may associate with outcomes differentially by timepoint, which may influence results [ 98 ]. As this work reviewed summary‐level data across cohorts, it was unable to assess for baseline depression severity, sex, or age‐based differences in reported pharmacogenetic associations with outcomes. There is rationale for follow‐up studies to investigate potential pharmacogenetic differences based on baseline depression severity, sex, and age [ 99 , 100 , 101 ]. This review did not conduct an analysis on whether outcomes to various antidepressants may differ by genetics in a dose‐dependent manner, as dose was assumed to vary across individuals in a cohort by clinical judgment; a study on how dosage in response to PGx has been modified and to what degree of success represents a further avenue for future assessment. The number of previous failed antidepressant trials, a measure of treatment resistance, was not presently extracted or summarized. This may be an important future consideration, as evidence suggests that the benefits of genotyped‐guided care may increase for patients with previous antidepressant trial failures [ 102 , 103 ]. We aimed to identify all available panels through a Google search and a search of the Genetic Testing Registry. However, it is possible that these panels may not be fully representative of tests used for patients as academic laboratories may be performing testing but not advertising broadly. Finally, whereas the search strategy employed was comprehensive, there is no guarantee every article of relevance was detected and included. In conclusion, this work systematically reviewed published studies evaluating the pharmacogenetics of MDD, compiling associations between promising genes and a myriad of outcomes to antidepressant therapies in a drug‐specific manner. This review also summarized publicly available data from clinical PGx panels, highlighting a gap in insight into clinical academic laboratory practices. Evaluation of methodological approaches utilized across studies highlighted the promise of machine learning for integrating variants across multiple biological pathways in one unified model, as well as utilizing multimodal data to identify robust gene‐outcome associations in MDD. It is likely that these approaches, which are adept at handling large and complex datasets, are especially helpful for patients with MDD given the intricate interplay between biological and environmental factors in influencing outcomes to antidepressant therapies. Finally, this review delineates sources of heterogeneity which may underlie the low rates of replicability of pharmacogenetic associations in MDD. Identifying these sources serves as a starting place for future research efforts aimed at improving the replicability of findings. Establishing clear contexts around which genes are useful in predicting antidepressant response will help lead us to the next generation of clinical panels for guiding antidepressant treatment selection in MDD.
