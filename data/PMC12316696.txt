See: https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ Abstract Background: Both cerebral blood flow (CBF) and fractional anisotropy (FA) are altered in bipolar disorder (BD). We sought to investigate the unexplored CBF–FA association in relation to youth BD. Methods: We recruited youth with BD, high-risk youth with a family history of BD, and healthy controls. Using 3 T magnetic resonance imaging, we measured global and regional grey matter CBF using arterial spin labelling and we measured regional FA using diffusion tensor imaging. We explored the CBF–FA association across groups, between groups, and by sex. Analyses of the BD group further examined mood effects. We conducted region-of-interest analyses of global CBF and FA, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) CBF, and cingulate FA. Results: We included 59 youth with BD, 19 high-risk youth with a family history of BD, and 47 healthy controls. There were no significant CBF–FA associations in the overall sample. Within the BD group, higher ACC CBF was associated with higher cingulum FA (β-weight 0.30, p = 0.03), with larger effect sizes among females and asymptomatic participants. Among controls, higher global CBF was associated with lower global FA (β-weight −0.40, p = 0.05). Within-sex analyses in the overall sample revealed a small effect size association between ACC CBF and cingulum FA among females only. Limitations: Our small sample size limited power, and our cross-sectional observational design precluded directional inferences about CBF–FA associations. Conclusion: We found preliminary evidence that ACC CBF was positively associated with cingulum FA among youth with BD, which may be driven by asymptomatic female participants. These associations may relate to differences in cerebral metabolism or white matter perfusion. Introduction Bipolar disorder (BD) is a mood disorder characterized by substantial symptomatic burden, comorbidity, and functional impairment. 1 , 2 Early onset of the illness has been found to manifest more severely and is often characterized by longer and more frequent mood episodes, a nonremitting course of illness, and comorbidities. 3 , 4 Reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in white matter (WM) is one of the most replicated neuroimaging findings among adults and youth with BD and their unaffected relatives. 5 –7 Fractional anisotropy describes the preferential diffusion of water in the direction of fewer barriers, with high values in intact neuronal tracts and lower values where WM pathology is present. In healthy people, FA declines with age in frontal brain regions. 8 , 9 Both adults and youth with BD show decrements in FA in similar regions. 6 , 10 In studies involving people at familial risk for BD, regional FA (e.g., corpus callosum, superior longitudinal fasciculus, posterior thalamic radiation) is highest in controls, lowest in people with BD, and intermediate in those with familial risk of BD. 7 , 11 Several putative mechanisms may underlie deficits in WM integrity, including genetic susceptibility, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation. 10 , 12 – 14 Another potential explanation for reduced FA in BD is cerebral blood flow (CBF). In healthy adults, higher CBF is associated with higher FA and lower diffusivity. 15 , 16 The few studies that have examined the association of CBF and FA in psychiatric populations have yielded mixed findings, including no relationship between CBF and diffusion measures in major depressive disorder (MDD) and BD, 17 , 18 and a positive relationship between CBF and FA in MDD. 19 However, this topic remains understudied, and there is a particular gap in knowledge regarding CBF and FA associations (CBF–FA) in BD and among youth. Cerebral blood flow describes the rate of blood volume supplied per unit mass of brain tissue, with higher CBF in grey matter (GM) than in WM by virtue of GM’s higher vascular density and underlying brain metabolism. 20 In adults with BD, there is replicated evidence of reduced CBF in the cingulate gyrus, frontal, and anterior temporal regions during depression and mania. 21 Our group has previously reported evidence of higher global and regional GM CBF among youth with BD overall, compared with healthy controls. 22 –24 Among youth with BD, lower regional GM CBF is associated with greater severity of depressive symptoms, 25 while higher CBF in temporal and precentral regions has been observed in euthymic patients. 26 In a study on familial risk for BD, unaffected individuals and their affected siblings shared changes in CBF with mood provocation in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and orbitofrontal cortex that were not previously observed in healthy individuals without family history of mood disorders. 27 Although reduced FA and anomalous CBF in BD are replicated findings, little is known about the association between these neuroimaging metrics. Similarly, there is a gap in knowledge regarding the association of FA and CBF among youth. We therefore set out to examine the relationship between FA and CBF in a sample of youth with BD. Given that BD is one of the most heritable psychiatric illnesses, with markedly increased risk among first-degree relatives, we included a high-risk comparison group of youth with first-degree family history of BD, in addition to a healthy control group, to gain insights into potential biomarkers of BD risk. 1 , 28 , 29 Using arterial spin labelling (ASL) and diffusion tensor imaging, we sought to investigate the relationship between CBF and FA across the entire sample and between groups. Because vascular dysfunction and reduced FA are independently observed in BD, we hypothesized that CBF–FA associations would be significantly stronger among youth with BD than healthy controls, with the high-risk group intermediate between these groups. 2 , 10 , 30 , 31 Methods Participants We recruited youth with BD who met Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria for BD-I, BD-II or other specified bipolar and related disorder (akin to BD not otherwise specified) from a subspecialty clinic. High-risk participants could not have BD, MDD, psychosis, or autism. We recruited youth with no lifetime mood or psychotic disorders, no recent anxiety disorders, and no first- or second-degree family history of BD or psychotic disorder from the community. We excluded participants if they were unable to provide written informed consent, or if they had cardiac, autoimmune or inflammatory illness; neurologic or cognitive impairment; substance dependence in the previous 3 months; or contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). All participants and their parent or guardian provided written informed consent. Study data were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). 32 , 33 Clinical procedures and measures Psychiatric diagnoses were confirmed using the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children, Present and Life Version (K-SADS-PL), 34 based on DSM-IV criteria (sample recruited in 2012–2019; DSM-5 became available only in 2016). Current and lifetime symptoms of depression and mania were assessed using the K-SADS Depression Rating Scale and K-SADS Mania Rating Scale. 35 Current mood was defined by depression and mania scores from the worst week in the past month, with patients characterized as symptomatic if they had Depression Rating Scale scores of at least 13 or Mania Rating Scale scores of at least 12. 34 Diagnosis of other specified bipolar and related disorder was established using the criteria defined in the Course and Outcome of Bipolar Illness in Youth study. 36 Diagnoses were determined using all available information, including youth and parent interviews and any available medical records. Clinical judgment was applied when conflicting information arose. Diagnoses were confirmed by a consensus meeting with a licensed child and adolescent psychiatrist following completion of the K-SADS-PL interview. Pubertal status was determined using the self-reported Pubertal Developmental Scale. 37 Body mass index was calculated and adjusted for clothing. 38 Global functioning was evaluated using the Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS), with higher scores indicating higher levels of functioning. 39 Image acquisition We acquired brain images using a 3.0 T Philips Achieva MRI scanner with an 8-channel head receiver coil. We obtained high-resolution T 1 -weighted images for co-registration. Structural scans were acquired using fast-field echo imaging (repetition time [TR] 9.5 ms, echo time [TE] 2.3 ms, inversion time [TI] 1400 ms, field of view 240 mm × 191 mm, spatial resolution 0.94 × 1.17 × 1.2 mm, 256 × 164 × 140 matrix, scan duration 8 min and 56 s). We collected pseudo-continuous ASL images to derive CBF measures. Before ASL imaging, we acquired phase contrast angiography scout images to visualize vascular anatomy. We obtained ASL images with single-shot, 2-dimensional echo-planar imaging (TR 4000 ms, TE 9.7 ms, 64 × 64 × 18 matrix, spatial resolution 3 × 3 × 5 mm, 1650 ms labelling duration, 1600 ms post-label delay for the most inferior slice, 30 control–tag pairs, scan duration 4 min and 8 s). We acquired ASL reference images with a TR of 10 seconds to establish initial magnetization for CBF quantification. Diffusion-weighted data were acquired using a single-shot, spin-echo echo-planar imaging sequence following the anterior–posterior commissure (TR 9150 ms, TE 55 ms, 32 gradient directions, b value of 1000 second/mm 2 , flip angle of 90°, field of view 224 mm × 224 mm, 52 3-mm-thick slices, 128 × 128 matrix, scan duration 6 min and 27 s). We also acquired 7 non-diffusion-weighted images. Image processing We processed images using FMRIB Software Library (FSL) tools. 40 Processing of T 1 -weighted images involved skull-stripping, co-registering to ASL space and standard space, normalizing, and segmenting into GM and WM. We co-registered ASL images to a reference volume. We then estimated CBF from differences in consecutive control and tag images. Images with excess head motion were identified automatically and removed to optimize CBF image quality. 41 Estimates were converted to absolute units (mL/100 g/min), and CBF maps were smoothed with a 5-mm smoothing kernel. We extracted CBF values from these maps. Specifically, we estimated mean global CBF as the average across GM using a mask of voxels with more than 80% probability of belonging to GM. We also extracted mean CBF values from a mask of the ACC, restricted to GM voxels and referred to as ACC CBF. Masks were defined using the Harvard–Oxford cortical atlas in FSL in 2-mm standard space. Diffusion data were preprocessed with eddy-current correction, brain extraction, and diffusion tensor calculation steps to produce FA maps. We used the WM JHU-ICBM atlas to create a mask of the cingulum for extraction of regional FA from the FA maps. We extracted mean WM FA and cingulum FA values from skeleton versions of the FA maps using the Enhancing Neuroimaging Genetics Meta-Analysis (ENIGMA) pipeline; 42 these measures are referred to as global FA and cingulum FA, respectively. Statistical analysis We conducted statistical analyses using SPSS version 26. We assessed the normality of continuous demographic and clinical variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For 3-group comparisons across the BD, high-risk, and control groups, we analyzed normally distributed continuous variables using analysis of variance (ANOVA), with assumptions of homogeneity of variance assessed using the Levene test. We analyzed non-normally distributed continuous variables using the Kruskal–Wallis test. For comparisons of clinical characteristics between the BD and high-risk groups, we employed independent samples t tests for normally distributed continuous variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. We compared categorical variables across the sample and between BD and high-risk groups using χ 2 tests. Tests were 2-tailed and used an a priori significance threshold of p less than 0.05. We employed multiple linear regressions to examine the relationship between global CBF and FA measures, as well as between ACC CBF and cingulum FA, across the whole sample and within groups. All primary analyses included age and sex as covariates. Collinearity diagnostics were performed to ensure the assumptions for multicollinearity were met. Exploratory analyses further examined CBF–FA associations related to sex and mood states within subgroups of BD participants (i.e., asymptomatic and symptomatic, female and male) using linear regressions. We used a significance threshold of 0.05 (2-tailed) for all analyses. Given the small sample size and preliminary nature of the study, we did not correct for multiple comparisons as the goal was to generate novel insights to guide future research. Ethics approval This study was approved by the research ethics boards at Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, where the study was conducted, and at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health, where analyses were performed. Results Demographic and clinical characteristics We recruited 125 English-speaking youth participants (aged 13–20 yr), including 59 with BD, 19 at high risk, and 47 controls. Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1 . Participants with BD were significantly older than high-risk participants, whereas those with BD and controls did not differ significantly. There were no significant between-group differences in sex. The BD group also had a significantly higher percentage of participants who identified as White than controls. Within the BD group, 24 (40.7%) participants were asymptomatic, 16 (27.1%) were experiencing a mixed episode, 17 (28.8%) were experiencing a depressed episode, and 2 (3.4%) were experiencing a hypomanic or manic episode. Clinical characteristics for youth with BD and high-risk youth are presented in Table 2 . Whole-sample CBF–FA correlations Within the whole sample, there was a nominal positive association between ACC CBF and cingulum FA ( r = 0.22, F 3,121 = 2.00, p = 0.1). Findings for global CBF did not approach significance. Within-group CBF–FA correlations Within the BD group, ACC CBF was significantly associated with cingulum FA ( r = 0.38, F 3,55 = 3.16, p = 0.03) ( Figure 1 ). The association between global CBF and FA within the BD group did not reach significance ( p = 0.1). Among controls, there was no significant association between ACC CBF and cingulum FA ( p = 0.3), whereas greater global CBF was nominally associated with lower global FA ( r = 0.40, F 3,43 = 2.76, p = 0.05). There were no significant global ( p = 0.2) or ACC ( p = 0.6) findings in the high-risk group. Exploratory analyses Given the limited literature in this area, we conducted exploratory analyses to parse heterogeneity in the association of CBF with FA within the BD group ( Table 3 ). The association between ACC CBF and cingulum FA was evident among asymptomatic BD participants ( r = 0.39, F = 4.00, p = 0.058) but not symptomatic BD participants ( r = 0.09, F = 0.26, p = 0.6). This finding appeared to be driven by asymptomatic females with BD ( r = 0.51, F 1,16 = 5.63, p = 0.03; Figure 2 ). Discussion We investigated the association of global CBF, a measure of metabolism and vascular heath, with regional FA in relation to youth BD. Partially in line with our hypothesis, we observed a positive association between ACC CBF and cingulate FA in the BD group, particularly among asymptomatic females. In the control group, a counterintuitive finding of a negative CBF–FA relationship was observed. Although we did not detect a CBF–FA association in the high-risk group, the subsample was small. This work provides preliminary findings of CBF–FA associations among youth with BD and emphasizes the importance of sex differences and mood states in understanding this relationship. The significant association between higher ACC CBF and higher cingulum FA may in part be explained by a vascular–bipolar link. Specifically, BD is associated with premature and increased cardiovascular disease, and with vascular dysfunction. 43 Among the most consistent neuroimaging findings in adult BD is an increased rate of WM hyperintensities, which are considered a consequence of small vessel disease. In contrast, a large study involving youth with BD found no differences in the prevalence of WM hyperintensities compared with healthy controls. 44 Such hyperintensities appear on T 2 -weighted images during normal aging and are reflective of WM integrity deficits. 45 Age-related WM deficits are attributed to vascular ischemia; however, it remains unclear whether reduced blood flow is a cause or consequence of brain tissue damage. 46 Decreased CBF has been shown to be associated with WM integrity deficits among older patients with minor ischemic cerebrovascular events. 46 , 47 Together, these studies suggest that vascular dysfunction precedes structural changes in WM. Thus, CBF is a potential intervention target for preventing WM injury and subsequent cognitive decline. Changes in CBF and FA are linked to neurodevelopmental events. Cerebral blood flow decreases over a 10-year span of maturation from adolescence to adulthood. 48 This decline is reflective of the pronounced synaptic pruning, coupled with a decline in energy utilization, that occurs during adolescence. 49 Myelination starts early in life and escalates during adolescence, 49 reflected by an increase in FA during this period in development. 50 Together, synaptic pruning and myelination reconfigure brain connections into their adult patterns. 49 Reduced FA in youth with BD may be indicative of abnormal maturation processes. 51 Polygenic risk for BD has been associated with altered WM and GM structure in youth, 52 supporting the proposed mechanism of genetic susceptibility as the underlying cause of neurodevelopmental changes in BD. Although reduced CBF is the most consistent finding in BD during depression and mania, 21 we have previously reported elevated CBF among youth with BD, 22 –24 speculating that this is related to increased energy demands in the context of mitochondrial dysfunction. A recent study from our group investigated the relationship between retinal vascular caliber and CBF among youth with BD, reporting a positive association between these neurovascular phenotypes and further highlighting the vascular–brain connection in BD pathophysiology. 53 Future studies powered to parse the effects of factors such as symptomatic status, BD subtype, comorbidity, and treatment are warranted to further evaluate the CBF–FA association in this population and to identify subgroups where the association is especially pronounced. In healthy adults, studies have shown strong associations between WM microstructural integrity and GM cortical thickness in connected regions. 54 , 55 Moreover, higher global CBF is associated with higher global FA among healthy adults. 15 Interestingly, we found the opposite pattern among controls, albeit at a nominal level of significance, whereby higher CBF predicted lower FA. One study reported that higher WM CBF was associated with lower FA, which may be explained by microstructural WM organization rather than the neurovascular coupling seen to regulate GM perfusion. 56 Specifically, the myelin sheath acts as an insulator, increasing the efficiency of electrical signal propagation while restricting ion exchange to the nodes of Ranvier and thus significantly reducing the energy required for sodium–potassium adenine triphosphatase pumps. Hence, a possible mechanism is that energy demands are inversely correlated with the extent and density of the myelin sheath, and thus blood supply is also inversely correlated with FA. 56 However, this is a tentative, nominal finding that requires replication in larger samples of youth. In exploratory analyses, we found that the association between higher ACC CBF and higher cingulum FA within the BD group appeared to be driven by euthymic females. Studies involving adults have shown differential mood and CBF associations based on sex. A positron emission tomography study involving older individuals with subthreshold depressive symptoms showed more widespread decreases in CBF, with higher depression scores in frontotemporal regions, in men than in women. 57 An ASL study further linked perfusion to depression severity in adults with MDD, with more pronounced negative correlations observed among females. 58 Our group recently examined sex differences in CBF among youth with and without BD. 59 Although there were no significant sex-by-diagnosis interaction effects on CBF, exploratory within-sex analysis revealed elevated CBF in the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex of females with BD versus controls, with no differences in CBF observed among males versus controls. 59 There are mood-related differences, both in terms of mood state and dimensional symptom severity, in CBF among adults and youth with BD. 21 , 25 Our group has found that lower regional CBF is associated with greater depression symptom severity among youth with BD, 25 , 26 and that regional CBF is elevated among euthymic youth with BD compared with healthy controls. 23 , 24 , 26 Therefore, mood effects on CBF may obscure any association between CBF and FA among symptomatic youth with BD, which may explain in part why findings were most prominent among euthymic females with BD. The present findings highlight differential CBF–FA associations related to symptomatic status and sex and provide motivation for investigating these variables and subgroups in future studies on BD. Limitations Despite the relatively large overall sample size for a single-site neuroimaging study focused on youth BD, analyses were not powered to detect small effect sizes, and power considerations were especially relevant for the small high-risk group. This may explain the statistical insignificance despite the medium-to-large effect size demonstrated by the CBF–FA associations observed in the high-risk group. Literature that has compared the 3 groups using these brain measures is scarce, and future studies employing larger high-risk subgroups will be particularly valuable for exploring high-risk youth as an intermediate phenotype between youth with BD and controls. Exploratory analyses conducted within BD subgroups (e.g., by mood status, sex) were similarly underpowered and should be validated in future larger samples. This study used a cross-sectional design, precluding inferences regarding the direction of observed associations or the effects of symptomatic status and developmental changes over time. As we opted for a clinically representative sample, heterogeneity in terms of BD subtypes, comorbidities, family psychiatric history, and medication may have affected the observed findings. Again, larger future studies are warranted to evaluate for subgroups based on these characteristics to identify youth for whom CBF–FA associations are especially prominent. Conclusion This study provides initial evidence that higher regional CBF is associated with higher regional FA among youth with BD, suggesting that vascular health may be related to the preservation of WM microstructure early in illness. Findings underscore the potential value of examining differences by symptomatic status and sex in relation to CBF–FA associations and point to the benefit of integrating mitochondrial function indices to guide mechanistic inferences. Identifying vascular contributions to WM integrity could inform early risk stratification efforts and novel treatment strategies targeting vascular health and oxidative stress. More broadly, framing BD as a systemic illness may help reduce stigma and encourage earlier treatment engagement. Although this work focuses on BD, it informs broader models of processes that may be applicable across psychiatric and neurologic disorders. Future larger, longitudinal, repeated-measures studies, along with advanced imaging techniques such as neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging, are needed to characterize developmental trajectories, establish temporal relationships, and better explore potential causal pathways. Such designs will improve interpretability and identify developmental patterns and subgroups of youth with BD for whom CBF–FA associations are most relevant.
