Abstract Background Obesity is associated with a high risk of diabetes mellitus (DM); therefore, obesity-related indices are strongly associated with DM. This study evaluated the association of obesity indices, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and a body shape index (ABSI), with DM in Korean middle-aged and older adults. Methods Data from three population-based cohorts (Ansan and Ansung, Health Examinee, and Cardiovascular Disease Association Study, derived from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study) were analyzed. Logistic analysis was used to evaluate the association of BMI, WC, WHR, and ABSI with DM, after adjusting for covariates according to sex and age. The integrated discrimination index (IDI) and category-free net reclassification improvement (cfNRI) and area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were analyzed by age group to investigate index-specific model performance. Results Among the 160,585 participants, 13,846 had DM (6,837; men [11.86%] and 7,009 women [6.81%]). Age increase was associated with an increased prevalence of DM: 2,339 (4.01%), 5,313 (8.74%), and 6,194 (14.93%) in the 40–49, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years age groups. After adjusting for covariates, the odds ratios (OR) for DM of WHR and WC were higher than those of the other indices in every age group. However, the OR for WHR, WC, and BMI decreased with age in both sexes. ABSI showed steady and slightly increasing ORs with increasing age although the ORs in both sexes were generally low for DM. For IDI and cfNRI, WHR had the highest values among all age groups. The AUC of the ROC curve showed that the WHR had the highest value in all age groups. Conclusion The WHR had the strongest association with DM, but was not a good DM index in older people. Therefore, age-related index criteria for DM, especially in women, were needed for the effective prevention and management of DM. Introduction Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major public health concern worldwide. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), three out of four people in low- and middle-income countries have been living with DM, and their number is predicted to increase to 783 million by 2045 [ 1 ]. DM is among the most prevalent diseases in Korea as well. According to the Korean Diabetes Association, 13.8% of people aged over 30 years and 27.6% of people older than 65 years had DM in 2018 [ 2 ]. In South Korea, the proportion of people aged over 65 years is expected to increase from 15.1% in 2019 to 16.8% in 2021 [ 3 ]. As the proportion of older adult increases, the importance of studying their public health risks increases. Diabetes can be diagnosed by three criteria: glycated hemoglobin, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) [ 4 , 5 ]. These tests are undertaken using blood samples, which involves a need to visit the clinic, obtain blood samples, wait for the clinical test results, and incurs a cost. This long and costly process makes people hesitant to visit the clinic, which makes disease prevention and management more difficult. DM is associated with several risk factors, including age, sex, obesity, and lifestyle [ 6 – 8 ]. Among these risk factors, obesity has various indices that can be measured anywhere. Obesity is normally defined by the body mass index (BMI); however, several other indices are also used to define obesity, including waist circumference (WC), waist-hip ratio (WHR), and a body shape index (ABSI). Focusing on specific obesity indices such as BMI, WC, WHR, and ABSI is essential for understanding their associations with DM. Numerous studies have demonstrated significant links between these indices and DM, reinforcing their relevance in clinical assessments. ABSI is particularly noteworthy because, unlike other indices that may decrease after a certain age, it consistently increases with age for both sexes, making it a valuable measure for evaluating ongoing diabetes risk as populations age (additional file Figure S1 ). Many studies have assessed the association between anthropometric indices and chronic diseases, such as diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and mortality in the general population [ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 – 13 ]. A previous study examined the association of obesity-related cardiometabolic risk factors in the Bangladeshi population and showed that the WHR, WC, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and body fat percentage (BF%) were more strongly associated with cardiometabolic risk factors than the BMI was, specially ORs were highest for WHR for DM for both sexes [ 9 ]. The Z-score of the log-transformed ABSI (LBSIZ) has been assessed with regard to its association with CVD in Koreans [ 14 , 15 ] and the LBSIZ was shown to have a stronger association with CVD than with other indices, including the BMI and WC. Several studies have compared and analyzed obesity indices to determine the mortality risk [ 16 – 18 ] and DM risk [ 9 , 10 , 14 , 15 , 19 ]. As obesity is associated with a high risk of DM, obesity-related indices are strongly associated with DM [ 10 , 20 ]. A cohort study in Iran showed that the WHR is a better index for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) than the body adiposity index (BAI), BMI, and WC, showing differences between men and women, and even the WHR was generally found to be a more appropriate index for T2DM than the others [ 10 ]. Other studies have compared several indices, including the BMI, WC, WHR, and ABSI, to assess their association with DM [ 21 – 23 ]. This study investigates the complex association between obesity indices and DM across different age groups, emphasizing the necessity of understanding how these relationships vary by sex. Prior research has demonstrated that BMI significantly influences the prevalence of type 2 diabetes, with notable variations by age and ethnicity. Specifically, finding indicate that mean BMI and diabetes prevalence vary both age and sex underscoring that the relationship between BMI and diabetes is not uniform [ 24 ]. Moreover, the study has shown that WHR serves as a critical risk factor for DM, particularly among older women, highlighting the importance of sex difference in these associations [ 25 ]. A study conducted in Korea further supports this notion by revealing differential associations among body composition measures—such as fat mass index (FMI), BMI, and muscle mass index (MMI)—with DM, emphasizing the need to consider both age and sex in these analyses [ 8 ]. This evidence supports the need to explore age- and sex- specific associations to develop targeted interventions and deepen our understanding of the distinct impact of obesity on diabetes risk. Expanding our focus in this area is essential for creating effective public health strategies to varied populations. The previous studies have shown a simple association between the obesity index and DM or provided a comparison of the obesity indices for the association with DM. The previous study examines the weight weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) as a predictor of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and non-accidental mortality in East Asian populations, particularly Chinese individuals. The results showed that WWI has significant positive associations with diabetes risk, outperforming other adiposity measures in predicting cardiovascular disease and mortality [ 26 ]. However, only a few studies obesity indices for DM based on sex- and age- specific group have been conducted in Korea. This study aimed to evaluate the association between these indices and DM and compare the indices for predicting the prevalence of DM according to age and sex in Korean middle-aged and older adults. Methods Data sources and study population We evaluated the associations between the indices and the prevalence of DM in different age groups (40–49, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years) based on data from the three cohorts, including the Ansan/Ansung Study, the Health Examination (HEXA) study, and Cardiovascular Disease Association Study (CAVAS), from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) [ 19 ]. The KoGES was launched to investigate the genetic and environmental etiologies of common complex diseases in Koreans. The KoGES aims to form a genome epidemiological platform for research using a health database and biobank [ 19 ]. Informed consent for data collection, including interviews and examinations, was obtained from participants and trained health workers. Core questionnaires, physical examinations, and clinical investigations, including those on sociodemographic status, lifestyle, and psychological stress were collected from all cohorts [ 19 ]. Eligible participants aged over 40 years at baseline were recruited from the National Health Examinee Registry. The Ansan/Ansung study recruited 10,030 participants from 2001 to 2002, the HEXA study recruited 173,202 participants from 2004 to 2013, and the CAVAS study recruited 28,337 participants from 2006 to 2011. CAVAS and Ansung represented for rural, and HEXA and Ansan represented for urban area. This diverse geographic distribution, encompassing both urban and rural populations, allows for a comprehensive analysis of health outcomes across different living environments, providing insights into how geographic and demographic factors may influence disease prevalence and risk. A detailed description of these cohort studies has been provided previously [ 19 ]. Baseline recruitment was used for each cohort in this study. The three studies included 211,569 participants who were older than 40 years. Among the 211,569 participants, we excluded 50,984 participants who were older than 80 years and those with missing data for variables, such as indices, clinical information, and anthropometric values (Fig. 1 ). In total, 160,585 participants were included in the analysis, including 57,653 men (35.90%) and 102,932 women (64.10%). Anthropometric indices In this study, four anthropometric indices were used to evaluate associations with DM, namely the BMI, WC, WHR, and ABSI. The ABSI was defined as WC/(BMI 2/3 *height 1/2 ) [ 27 ]. BMI was measured using a machine, and for participants who did not have BMI values, we calculated it using weight and height as the body weight (kg) divided by the height squared (m 2 ). For WC and hip circumference measurements, the Ansan and Ansung study measured these three times, and we calculated the average; the HEXA study measured this parameter once; and the CAVAS measured it twice; therefore, we calculated the average. The height and weight were measured once in all the studies. We dichotomized each index with each criterion as normal and high, as follows: 25 kg/m 2 for the BMI and 90 cm in males and 85 cm in females for the WC according to the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity (KOSSO) [ 28 ], 0.9 in men and 0.85 in women for the WHR according to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition [ 29 ], and 0.08 m 11/6 /kg 2/3 in men and 0.073 m 11/6 /kg 2/3 in women for the ABSI as reported previously [ 30 ]. Diabetes mellitus and covariables DM was defined based on a doctor’s diagnosis, the use of antidiabetic treatments, and/or high fasting blood sugarglucose (&gt; 126 mg/dL). The questionnaires for the diagnosis of DM and antidiabetic treatment included yes or no questions that were answered by the participants themselves. In this study, demographic and clinical information, including age, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), current smoking (yes/no), current drinking (yes/no), education level (less than elementary school, middle and high school, and more than college), and income level (low, middle, and high as &lt; 1,500,000, 1,500,000–4,000,000, and &gt; 4,000,000 South Korean Won (KRW) per month, respectively), was used. The participants were divided into three groups, as follows: 40–49, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years. SBP and DBP were used to calculate the average and were measured several times. In this study, smoking and drinking variables were classified dichotomously as yes or no in present. The studies covered different periods of data collection, from 2 to 10 years: 2 years for the Ansan and Ansung study, 10 years for the HEXA study, and 6 years for the CAVAS. Moreover, the number of study sites varied: two sites for the Ansan and Ansung study, 39 sites for the HEXA study, and 11 sites for the CAVAS. To adjust the model, we included age, sex, year of sample collection, collection site, education level, income level, smoking, drinking, TC, and SBP. Statistical analysis We evaluated the associations between obesity-related indices and DM for the three age groups and by sex. We analyzed categorical variables as frequencies and proportions, and continuous variables as the mean and standard deviation (SD), or median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences in the prevalence of DM for categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, whereas continuous variables were analyzed using the two-sample t-test. We used multi-adjusted logistic analysis to evaluate the odds ratios (ORs), with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), of the prevalence of DM for the BMI, WC, WHR, and ABSI in sex- and age-specific groups, with the normal group as the reference. Model was adjusted for variables, including age, sex, collection year, collection site, education level, income level, current smoking, current drinking, TC, and SBP. TG, HDL-C, FBG, and DBP were not included in the final model since those variables did not distinctly change the estimates. To demonstrate the discrimination of the model, we analyzed the integrated discrimination index (IDI) and category-free reclassification improvement (cfNRI) for each index by sex and age group. We compared the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to compare the indices according to sex and age. The AUC of the ROC curve indicated the accuracy of each index for predicting the prevalence of DM. A comparison of the AUC of the models with and without the index indicated an improvement in the predictive accuracy. The AUC of the ROC curve for BMI, WC, WHR, and ABSI for the prevalence of DM by age group. Model 2 was added to each index of Model 1. We compared the AUCs of the models to show the incremental contribution of other indices. Those methods were used for the investigation to improve the model performance for each index by sex and age group [ 31 ]. Data analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Results Characteristics of the participants Table 1 shows the anthropometric, clinical, and lifestyle characteristics of the participants according to their age and prevalence of DM. The mean age of the 211,569 participants was 53.26 (SD 8.44) years. Participants were divided into three age groups: 40–49 years (58,332; 36.32%), 50–59 years (60,768; 37.84%), and ≥ 60 years (41,485; 25.83%). With age, the number and percentage of participants with DM increased: 2,339 (4.01%) in the 40–49 years, 5,313 (8.74%) in the 50–59 years, and 6,194 (14.93%) in the ≥ 60 years groups. Most of the variables differed significantly across all age groups between the normal and DM groups. The drinking variable showed no significant difference between the 40–49 and ≥ 60 years groups. The education and income level variables in the ≥ 60 years group did not show any difference. Among the variables that significantly differed, participants with DM were more likely to have higher age, BMI, WC, WHR, ABSI, SBP, DBP, TG, and FBG. In contrast, HDL-C levels and nonsmoking variables were higher in participants without DM. Age-related association of indices with the prevalence of DM Figure 2 presented the adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI for the association between each index and DM prevalence by age group. Using the normal group as a reference, every index showed a higher ORs in the high group. WHR had the highest OR (OR, 2.36; 95% CI, 2.16– 2.58) in 40–49 years group, and WC had the second highest OR (OR, 2.30; 95% CI, 2.16–2.58). The ORs of the WHR were highest among indices for each respective age group. With age, the ORs decreased for the BMI, WC, and WHR, whereas the ABSI showed almost steady or slightly increasing ORs with increasing age despite having the lowest ORs (Fig. 2 and Table S1 ). Even after stratification by sex, all indices showed a significantly increased risk of DM by age group for both sexes, and the WHR indicated the highest increased risk of DM for all age groups, although the ORs decreased with increasing age. The ABSI resulted in the lowest risk for almost all age groups, but showed an almost steady or slightly increasing OR with age (Fig. 2 and Table S2 ). AUC difference for each index between age groups Table 2 showed that the highest AUC in Model 2 was for the WHR in the 40–49 years group, followed by WC, BMI, and ABSI. The AUC difference between Models 1 and 2 was significant ( P &lt; 0.0001) for all indices; between Models 1 and 2, had the largest difference in AUC (0.030) was noted for WHR in the 40–49 years group. Overall, the AUC differences decreased as age increased for all indices. In Model 2, the largest difference in the AUC (0.069) was identified in BMI between the 40–49 and ≥ 60 years groups; however, the WC, WHR, and ABSI showed similar differences of 0.065, 0.062, and 0.053, respectively. In men, the AUCs were slightly lower than the overall values for all age groups; however, the AUCs for women were higher than the overall values for all age groups. The trends in both men and women were similar, with AUCs decreasing with increasing age. For both sexes, the WHR in the 40–49 years group had the highest AUC (male: 0.668, female: 0.727). The AUC values between models 1 and 2 significantly differed in most groups, except for the ABSI in the 40–49 and 50–59 years groups in men (P: 0.212 and 0.069; additional file Table S3 ). Reclassification of each index by age group The IDI showed positive values for all age groups ( p &lt; 0.05), indicating that adding each index to Model 1 was significantly better than the model without the index. Unlike the OR and AUC results, the IDIs in the 40–49 year group did not have the highest values for all indices. The WHR had a higher IDI in each age group than the other indices, and the WHR in the 50–59 year group had the highest IDI (0.013; Table 3 ). After sex-specific stratification, the trend was similar to the overall trend, with positive values and lower p-values for all age groups. In both sexes, WHR had the highest IDI in all age groups, and between men and women, men had a lower IDIs in the 40–49 and 50–59 year groups than women; however, the IDIs of men ≥ 60 years were higher than those of women (additional file Table S4 ). The cfNRI showed a trend similar to that of the ORs, and decreased as age increased. Similar to that in the OR and IDI, the WHR had the highest cfNRI among the indices in every age group, whereas ABSI showed similar and increased cfNRI values with age. BMI in the 40–49 year group had a higher cfNRI than the WC; however, the cfNRI of BMI and WC in the ≥ 60 year group crossed over because the cfNRI of the BMI decreased sharply as age increased (Table 3 ). Overall, the WHR group had the highest cfNRI among all age groups for both sexes. Women generally had higher cfNRIs than men in all age groups, except for the ABSI in those &gt; 60 years. In the 40–49 year group, women had a much higher cfNRI than men; however, the cfNRI was similar between men and women in the ≥ 60 year group because the cfNRI of women decreased sharply as age increased (additional file Table S4 ). cfNRI decreases with increasing age similar to the ORs; however, the IDI showed that the 50–59 year group had higher values than that of the 40–49 year group for every index. The IDIs of BMI and WHR decreased in participants ≥ 60 years whereas the IDI of WC in this group was the same as that in the 50–59 year group; however, the ABSI increased more in the ≥ 60 year group. Similar to the OR, the IDI and cfNRI were higher in women than in men. The WHR group had the highest IDI and cfNRI in both sexes among all the age groups. Men had a trend similar to that of the overall IDI, whereas women had a decreasing IDI for BMI and WC. In the cfNRI in men, the BMI, WC, and WHR decreased with age whereas the ABSI showed an increasing cfNRI, even with age. Discussion In this study of 160,585 participants (DM: 13,846 and normal: 146,739), we compared the associations between the four indices, BMI, WC, WHR, and ABSI, and the prevalence of DM by age- and sex-specific groups (Fig. 2 ). Our findings indicated that WHR had the strongest association with DM prevalence in every group, although this association weakened with age for most indices, including BMI, WC, and WHR. Subgroup analyses revealed that while women generally exhibited higher ORs than men across most age groups, ORs for both sexes decreased sharply with increasing age. Overall, indices incorporating waist circumference, such as WC and WHR, demonstrated higher ORs for DM compared to other indices, while ABSI showed low but steady ORs across all age groups. Additionally, for a comprehensive analysis, we evaluated the associations between each index and DM using the AUC of the ROC curve and reclassifications, including IDI and cfNRI. These results suggest that waist circumference-related indices are critical for assessing DM risk in both men and women. Several studies had compared the association between these indices and mortality or chronic diseases. The ABSI had a stronger association with all-cause mortality for type 2 diabetes than for the BMI or WHR [ 18 ]. A recent previous study still showed the association between WHR and diabetes. In the people with diabetes, abnormal WHR had a higher prevalence than normal WHR [ 12 ]. In a rural Chinese population, an increase in the ABSI and body adiposity estimator (BAE) resulted in an increased risk of T2DM in a comparison of ABSI, BAE, BMI, WC, and WHtR [ 22 ]. One study showed an association between obesity-related indicators and the prevalence of chronic diseases, including DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, using data from the sixth Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (Sixth KNHANES), wherein the BMI was found to be a better index of chronic disease [ 32 ]. Our study showed that the WHR and WC, which included a waist circumference factor, had stronger associations with the prevalence of DM than the other indices (ABSI and BMI); moreover, the WHR had the strongest association with the prevalence of DM, and the result persisted even in the sex- and age-specific stratified analyses. Another study compared the association between the obesity indices, WC, BMI, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and WHR, and DM [ 33 ]. The results were similar, as the indices, including waist circumference were more closely related; however, the difference from our study is that the WHR also including a waist circumference factor, did not show a close relationship. Some studies had reported differences in the associations between anthropometric indices and diseases by age and sex [ 8 , 11 , 34 ]. A cross-sectional study showed an association between body composition and DM in age- and sex-specific groups, indicating that sex- and age-specific groups are linked to DM with different indices: the fat mass index and muscle mass index [ 8 ]. Another previous study with participants over 60 years of age showed that the WC had the strongest association with DM in males and that BMI had the strongest association with DM in female participants [ 11 ]. Another study reported the effects of major metabolic risk factors on CVD and DM according to age groups [ 34 ]. Unlike previous studies, we obtained different results; specifically, the WC and WHR had similar ORs in 40–49 years old participants, but the ORs associated with WC fell more sharply with an increasing age than the ORs associated with WHR, especially in females. Although the ORs of women were higher than those of men for all indices and age groups, except the OR for BMI in the ≥ 60 year group, the WHR had the highest ORs in every age group for both men and women. Except for the ABSI, the other three indices decreased with increasing age in both male and female participants. These results indicated that the indices are not sufficiently associated with DM in older adults. In the 40–49 year group, men had markedly lower ORs than women; however, the ORs of women decreased sharply with age in terms of BMI, WC, and WHR. Unlike the other three indices, the ABSI had a different pattern in both men and women, with a steady or slight increase with age. The association between age and the ABSI could explain these different patterns as shown in additional file Figure S1 , which indicated that all index trends displayed that men had higher values than women from the 40th to 60th age groups; however, the differences became smaller and women had higher values after the 70th age group. This association created the BMI paradox, in which a higher BMI was better than a low BMI for chronic diseases and mortality in older people [ 11 ]. WC and WHR showed similar associations with a half-parabolic pattern in men but increased continuously in women. On the other hand, although ABSI also showed a cross between males and females at approximately 70 years, the values in both sexes increased continuously with age (additional file Figure S1 ). WHR showed the highest improvement after adding the index in all age groups, and WC had the second-highest AUC. This result indicated that WHR had the strongest association with DM. Even after stratification by sex, the WHR had the highest AUC in both sexes IDI and cfNRI. IDI and cfNRI decrease with increasing age like ORs’ results, which indicated that the model performance deteriorates as increasing age for BMI, WHR, and WC. ABSI showed as increasing cfNRI, even with age; however, the value is lowest in all age groups, which indicated that the model with ABSI did not improve. While our study provides important insights into the associations between obesity-related indices and the prevalence of DM in Korea, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other contexts, such as different populations or regions. For instance, a previous study on Chinese adults highlighted the increasing incidence of type 2 diabetes among younger populations, demonstrating a significant association between BMI and diabetes risk across various age groups. This study found that the impact of BMI on diabetes incidence was particularly pronounced in younger adults, indicating that age and regional factors can influence the relationship between obesity and diabetes [ 35 ]. Our findings, which emphasize the strength of waist circumference-related indices like WHR and WC in predicting DM risk, may not directly translate to populations with different lifestyle, dietary, or genetic factors, as seen in the Chinese cohort. The variation in obesity patterns and diabetes prevalence across cultures suggests that while waist circumference remains a critical indicator, the specific associations and their implications may differ. This study had several strengths. First, we had a large sample size of middle-aged and older people, which could be stratified by several subgroups. This study possibly had the largest sample size to show sex- and age-specific associations between obesity-related indices and the prevalence of DM in South Korea. Second, we stratified the people with diabetes into three groups and stratified age group-specific analyses showed that the associations between the indices and the prevalence of DM changed with age. Finally, we analyzed the IDI and cfNRI to compare the models with and without each index. Several studies had compared the AUC of the ROC curve; moreover, we analyzed the practical value of discrimination to improve by comparing the IDI and cfNRI. Despite these strengths, this study had some limitations. First, because we used three cohorts, the research periods were different and long. The Ansan/Ansung cohort study was performed between 2001 and 2002 (2 years), the CAVAS study between 2007 and 2014 (8 years), and the HEXA study had been performed between 2004 and 2013 (10 years). The CAVAS and HEXA had some overlapping periods, but the Ansan and Ansung cohorts had been assessed in many different years. Second, because this study was cross-sectional, we could not conclude whether the indices and DM are causally related. Third, older participants were not included. Due to limited participants, we excluded those aged &gt; 80 years. Therefore, the age of the oldest group in this study was 60–79 years. However, the cohorts had been followed up until now, and the participants were older at follow-up. Further studies should be performed with a more recent follow-up to evaluate the association or causality between the indices and diseases in the old-old group, aged &gt; 80 years. Fourth, our study was the use of dichotomization to categorize obesity-related indices. While this approach was chosen for its alignment with widely recognized criteria that are easily understood by the public and healthcare professional, it may oversimplify the complexities of obesity and obscure subtle variations in risk by collapsing continuous data into binary categories. Future studies should address the limitations of our study, particularly the use of dichotomized indices, by exploring the implications of continuous measured for assessing diabetes risk. Investigating obesity- related metrics in diverse populations could provide deeper insights into their associations with diabetes prevalence. Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to establish casual relationships and better understand how these indices interact over time, especially in older adults. By incorporating a wider range of demographic factors and employing more delicate analytical methods, future studies can enhance our understanding of diabetes risk and inform targeted management and prevention strategies. Conclusions This study showed that, among the four anthropometric indices of BMI, WC, WHR and ABSI, the indices with a waist circumference factor, which included the WHR and WC, had strong associations with the precedence of DM in general. However, the association weakened with increasing age in both men and women, but especially so in women. These findings suggested that age-dependent cutoff points and a proper index for DM in older adults need to be developed.
