Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) has caused significant damage to the poultry industry globally, including in Japan. To identify farm-level risk factors for HPAI infection while considering potential confounding and correlations among variables, an additive Bayesian network (ABN) model was applied. This case–control study analyzed outbreaks in layer and broiler farms during the 2022–2023 HPAI season in Japan, selecting 69 infected farms as cases and 361 uninfected farms located within a 5-km radius as controls. The ABN model incorporated four variables: HPAI infection status, flock size, production type, and coverage of surrounding water bodies. Results indicated that layer farms, farms with large flock sizes, and those situated near extensive water bodies faced a higher risk of HPAI infection. Notably, being a layer farm increased risk both directly and indirectly, due to their tendency to maintain larger flocks. These findings highlight the importance of reinforcing biosecurity measures on farms with these characteristics to prevent future HPAI outbreaks. In Japan, HPAI outbreaks on poultry farms have also increased. A record high of 52 outbreaks was reported in 2020, and HPAI has continued to occur annually since then 4 . During the 2022–2023 season, a total of 84 poultry farms in Japan were affected by HPAI outbreaks caused by the H5N1 subtype (Fig. 1 ), marking the highest number of outbreaks recorded as of 2025 5 , 6 . The affected farms included 58 layer farms, 3 growing layer farms, 11 broiler farms, 1 breeding chicken farm, 7 duck farms, 1 quail farm, 2 emu farms, and 1 guinea fowl farm. A notable characteristic of this season was the high proportion of affected layer farms and the predominance of farms with large flock sizes 6 . Previous studies have identified geographic location and flock size as potential risk factors for HPAI infection on chicken farms 7 – 9 . In Japan, farms with large poultry populations and those located near water bodies are at a high risk of HPAI infection 10 , 11 . Notably, layer farms constituted a large proportion of affected farms during the 2022–2023 season (58 out of 84, 69%), suggesting that layer farms may be particularly vulnerable to HPAI outbreaks. Most of the affected layer farms housed more than 100,000 birds, whereas none of the affected broiler farms did 6 . This observation implies a possible interrelationship between production type (layer vs. broiler) and flock size, raising the concern that one may act as a confounder, making the other appear as a spurious risk factor. To better understand the risk factors associated with HPAI outbreaks, further research is needed using analytical approaches capable of accounting for such interrelationships and potential confounding effects. Additive Bayesian network (ABN) analysis is a multivariate method that enables the assessment of risk factors while accounting for the associations among them. As an extension of the generalized linear model (GLM), the ABN approach employs a graphical modeling technique using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to represent relationships among variables, enabling the identification of both direct and indirect associations 12 , 13 . These capabilities make ABN particularly useful for evaluating risk factors in infectious disease epidemiology, where variables are often interrelated in complex ways 14 , 15 . In this study, we used the ABN model to investigate risk factors associated with HPAI outbreaks in Japan during the 2022–2023 season, with particular emphasis on addressing potential interrelationships among variables. Results Descriptive statistics for each variable among the 69 HPAI-affected farms and the 361 control farms are presented in Table 1 . Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) identified the coverage of water bodies within a 1-km radius as the variable most strongly associated with HPAI infection, based on the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC) (Supplementary Fig. S1 ). Therefore, this spatial scale was used to define the Water variable in subsequent analyses. Generalized additive model (GAM) results indicated a nonlinear relationship between flock size (Size) and production type (Type) (Supplementary Fig. S2 ). Based on the GAM plots, flock size was categorized into three groups: small (Size S, &lt; 7,000 birds), medium (Size M, 7,000–90,000 birds), and large (Size L, &gt; 90,000 birds). As Water demonstrated a linear relationship with the other variables, it was treated as a continuous variable in the ABN analysis. The ABN analysis identified the optimal DAG describing the relationships between HPAI infection and associated risk factors (Fig. 2 ). The risk of HPAI infection was higher in large-scale farms (log odds ratio: 1.73 [95% credible interval: 1.12; 2.37]), layer farms (1.59 [0.86; 2.40]), and farms with a high proportion of surrounding water bodies (0.63 [0.32; 0.96]). Production type was associated with flock size: layer farms were more likely to be categorized as large-scale farms (1.88 [1.16; 2.7]), whereas broiler farms were generally of medium size rather than large or small (− 1.92 [− 2.71;−1.33]). As shown in Fig. 2 , the link strengths (LS) between Size L, Water, and Type, and HPAI infection were relatively high (15.17–21.15%) compared to the link strengths between flock size (Size M and Size L) and Type (4.71–5.53%), suggesting that the associations with HPAI infections were stronger (Table 2 ). Discussion In this study, ABN analysis was used to investigate the risk factors associated with HPAI outbreaks on chicken farms in Japan during the 2022–2023 season, while accounting for the complex interrelationships among variables. The results indicated that the risk of HPAI infection was higher in farms with a large-size flock, layer chickens, and high coverage of surrounding water bodies (Fig. 2 ; Table 2 ). Production type was associated with HPAI risk both directly and indirectly through flock size, as layer farms tended to maintain flocks of more than 90,000 chickens. The LS between production type and HPAI infection (15.17%) was greater than that between production type and flock size — medium (5.53%) and large (4.71%)—suggesting a strong direct association between production type and HPAI risk. Conventional multivariable approaches, such as GLMs and GLMMs, cannot simultaneously assess these associations while accounting for the interrelationship between flock size and production type. In contrast, the ABN model enabled the concurrent estimation of the relationships between these factors and HPAI risk, demonstrating that both factors were interrelated and directly associated with infection risk. These findings support the conclusion that layer farms and large-scale farms are at higher risk of HPAI outbreaks. However, it should be noted that the DAG derived from ABN analysis does not imply causality 16 . Further analyses incorporating additional risk factors are necessary to better understand the causal mechanisms underlying HPAI outbreaks. This study demonstrated that being a layer farm increased the risk of HPAI infection both indirectly—through its association with larger flock sizes—and directly, as revealed by the ABN analysis (Fig. 2 ; Table 2 ). In Japan, most layer farms are structurally equipped with belt conveyors for eggs and feces that are often exposed to the external environment, which may facilitate the entry of pathogens into poultry sheds. Bertran et al. 17 experimentally showed that layer chickens (White Leghorns) are more susceptible to highly pathogenic H5N2 viruses than crossbred White Plymouth Rock × Cornish broiler chickens. These breed-specific differences between farm types may contribute to varying levels of susceptibility to HPAI outbreaks. Additionally, the higher HPAI risk at layer farms may be attributable to differences in production-cycle duration. Layer chickens typically remain on the farm for about 1.5 to 2 years before shipment, whereas broilers are sent to market at just 7–8 weeks of age. Even if the virus enters a flock, a farm may not be identified as a “case farm” if infected birds are shipped out before noticeable clear signs of disease, such as clusters of dead birds or a sudden spike mortality are observed. The longer production cycle at layer farms may therefore contribute to their elevated HPAI risk. As noted by Artois et al. 9 , the relationship between production type and HPAI risk is influenced by multiple interacting factors and thus requires analytical approaches capable of capturing such complexity. Building on this insight, this study employed an ABN model to elucidate the interrelated risk factors for HPAI infection, highlighting both the direct and indirect effects of production type. While few studies have specifically assessed whether layer farms are at greater risk, our findings contribute to bridging this knowledge gap by providing a more integrated perspective on how production type is linked to HPAI infection. In addition, the proximity of water bodies and large flock size were identified as significant risk factors for HPAI infection, consistent with previous studies conducted in Japan and other countries 8 , 10 , 11 , 18 . Regarding water bodies, ducks—natural reservoirs of influenza A viruses—may contaminate surrounding environments with HPAIVs, increasing the likelihood of transmission to nearby farms. In large-scale farms, frequent access by staff and vehicles may heighten the risk of virus introduction, and the presence of a large number of susceptible birds may further increase the likelihood of an outbreak. Flock size was categorized using cutoff values determined from a GAM analysis (Supplementary Figure S2 ), rather than by equal quantiles. Consequently, the distribution of farms across categories was imbalanced, with the smallest group (Size S) including only 35 of the 430 farms (8.1%, Table 1 ). Although Size S was not associated with other variables in the final DAG (Fig. 2 ), this small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect such associations. Therefore, we refrain from drawing definitive conclusions regarding the effect of small-scale farming on HPAI infection risk. This study has several limitations. Notably, it did not assess certain risk factors previously reported to be associated with HPAI infection. For example, insufficient disinfection of boots, inadequate farm zoning, and the presence of nearby paddy fields and infected farms have been identified as risk factors for HPAI outbreaks 8 , 19 – 21 . Future risk assessments should incorporate these and other relevant factors to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of HPAI infection risk. Despite these limitations, this study clarified that, among layer and broiler farms, layer farms, farms maintaining large flocks, and those located near numerous water bodies are at a higher risk of HPAI infection. Furthermore, layer farms may increase the risk both indirectly—through their tendency to house large flocks—and directly, independent of flock size. Farms with these characteristics should prioritize strengthening biosecurity measures to prevent HPAI outbreaks. Methods Farm selection This case–control study was conducted on chicken farms affected by HPAI between October 2022 and April 2023. Of the 7,867 poultry farms in Japan, the major production types are layer farms ( n = 3,385), which raise egg-laying chickens, and broiler farms ( n = 3,488), which raise chickens for meat production; by contrast, there are only 786 chicken farms of other production types and 208 farms of other avian species (based on non-public data). As securing control farms in those categories using the method described below is challenging, this study focused exclusively on layer and broiler farms, excluding duck farms, quail farms, emu farms, guinea fowl farms, growing chicken farms, and breeding chicken farms from the analysis. Case farms were defined as farms where HPAIV infection occurred during the epidemic period, with a total of 69 farms included. Previous studies have demonstrated that HPAI infection risk varies regionally, likely due to differences in environmental factors influencing the distribution of wild waterfowl, the natural hosts of HPAI 22 , 23 . To minimize confounding from such regional heterogeneity and focus on farm-level risk factors, control farms were selected within a 5-km radius of each case farm. This spatial constraint was intended to ensure comparable exposure to regional HPAI risk and to reduce spatial autocorrelation arising from broader environmental and region-specific poultry farming characteristics. The 5-km distance corresponds to the estimated foraging range of mallards ( Anas platyrhynchos ) and northern pintails ( Anas acuta ), two of the most common waterfowl species in Japan 24 , 25 . Each case farm and its corresponding control farms were assigned the same cluster ID, which was later used as a random intercept in the ABN model, as described below. If case farms were located within 5 km of each other, those case farms and their shared control farms were assigned the same cluster ID. Farms with fewer than 1,000 chickens were excluded from the analysis, as they were presumed to be noncommercial operations. Additionally, epidemiologically related farms—defined as farms where chickens were culled as a precautionary measure due to a management link with infected farms—were excluded because their HPAI infection status was uncertain. Consequently, 361 farms were selected as controls for the 69 case farms, comprising a total of 44 clusters. Five control farms fell within the 5-km radius of two different case farms and were therefore assigned to both clusters. Data collection Three factors previously suggested as risk factors for HPAI infection in Japan and other countries were investigated in this study: production type, flock size, and presence of water bodies in the vicinity 8 , 10 , 11 . Furthermore, farm coordinates, flock sizes, and production types were obtained from the Japanese farm database maintained by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Fisheries of Japan. The coverage of surrounding water bodies was calculated using the High-Resolution Land Use and Land Cover Map ver. 23.12 (2022), produced by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency 26 . Statistical analysis Four variables were used in the ABN analysis: HPAI infection status, production type (Type), flock size (Size), and water coverage (Water), as described in Table 1 . The water coverage surrounding each farm and its optimal spatial range for calculation were determined beforehand. To identify the optimal range, GLMMs were fitted using a binary response variable indicating HPAI infection status. In these models, Water was included as the explanatory variable, with cluster ID incorporated as a random intercept. The radius for calculating Water was varied from 100 to 2,000 m in 100-m increments. The radius corresponding to the lowest AIC value was selected for use in the ABN analysis. Since Water exhibited a positively skewed distribution across all ranges, a log 10 transformation was applied prior to the GLMMs. The linearity between the continuous variables (Size and Water) and the other variables was assessed using GAMs with a random intercept for cluster ID 27 . When GAM plots indicated a nonlinear relationship, the corresponding continuous variable was categorized based on the plot, with the cutoff defined as the point where the fitted curve crossed zero on the y-axis. Variables showing a linear relationship were retained as continuous variables in the ABN analysis 28 , 29 . As the distribution of Size was positively skewed, log 10 -transformed values were used in the GAMs. The ABN analysis was performed with a random intercept for cluster ID. The data used for the ABN analysis are shown in Supplementary Table S1 . The optimal DAG was identified using a score-based algorithm. An exact search was conducted to find the optimal network based on the Bayesian approach, selecting the network with the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion score 14 , 30 . Network structures considered theoretically implausible were restricted according to prior assumptions, listed in Table 3 . Continuous variables were standardized to a mean of zero with equal variances 31 , and categorical variables with more than two levels were included as dummy variables in the ABN model. Posterior estimates, including coefficients and 95% credible intervals, were obtained for each parameter in the optimal DAG. LS was also calculated and represented by the thickness of arcs in the DAG. LS represents the reduction in uncertainty about a child variable when the state of a parent variable is known, conditional on the states of all other parent variables 32 . All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.1.2 33 . Specifically, the analyses for GLMMs, GAMs, and the ABN model were performed using the “lme4” 34 , “mgcv” 35 and “abn” 36 , respectively.
