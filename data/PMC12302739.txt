Abstract Background Adolescence represents a sensitive developmental window for brain maturation, particularly for executive functions, which are crucial for goal-directed behavior and long-term mental health. While physical activity is associated with brain health, the potentially differential impacts of specific ball games on executive cognitive processes remain insufficiently examined. Objective This study aims to explore the sport-specific associations of eight commonly investigated ball games, selected from a broad literature base, on adolescent executive function, through a comparative analysis across distinct cognitive domains. Methods A systematic review and network meta-analysis were conducted following searches in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science for studies published between January 2000 and March 2025. Eligible cross-sectional studies assessed executive function using one or more of five validated cognitive tasks: Go/No-Go Task, Stop-Signal Task, Stroop Task, Trail Making Test, and N-back Task. The analysis compared adolescents participating in baseball, handball, tennis, badminton, basketball, volleyball, soccer, or table tennis with non-athlete controls. Reaction time was selected as the primary outcome. Effectiveness rankings were established using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), and network consistency and potential publication bias were evaluated using standard diagnostic plots. Results Thirty-two studies were included. Across all five tasks, adolescents involved in ball sports demonstrated significantly better executive function performance than non-athletes. Relatively higher SUCRA rankings were observed for: table tennis and baseball in the Go/No-Go Task (80.0%, 79.6%), soccer in the Stop-Signal Task (89.3%), volleyball in the Stroop Task (99.1%), table tennis in the Trail Making Test (95.4%), and basketball in the N-back Task (76.2%). However, not all sports were represented across every task, and these rankings should be interpreted with caution. No substantial publication bias was identified. Conclusion Participation in ball games was associated with sport-specific differences in executive function performance among adolescents. These findings highlight potential links between structured physical activity and neurocognitive outcomes, supporting its consideration in youth health promotion strategies aimed at fostering brain development, mental resilience, and lifelong cognitive health. Introduction Adolescence is a pivotal period for brain development, characterized by heightened neuroplasticity and the rapid maturation of executive functions such as inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and working memory [ 1 – 4 ]. These core cognitive abilities underpin academic achievement, emotional self-regulation, and adaptive social behavior. As adolescents face increasing cognitive and emotional demands, identifying effective, developmentally appropriate strategies to foster brain health and psychological resilience is of critical importance. Physical activity has been widely acknowledged as a non-pharmacological means of enhancing cognitive function. Among various modalities, team-based ball sports—including basketball, soccer, and table tennis—are proposed to provide cognitively enriched environments that merge aerobic intensity with real-time decision-making, perceptual-motor coordination, and complex social dynamics [ 5 , 6 ]. In contrast to repetitive endurance exercises such as running or cycling, ball sports tend to place greater demands on attentional control and cognitive adaptability, challenging the brain in ecologically valid, unpredictable scenarios [ 7 – 9 ]. These characteristics suggest they may be particularly suitable for promoting executive function in youth [ 10 – 14 ]. Executive functions are typically assessed through validated experimental paradigms that target distinct cognitive domains. The Go/No-Go Task evaluates inhibitory control, the Stop-Signal Task assesses response inhibition latency, the Stroop Task examines interference control, the Trail Making Test measures cognitive flexibility and task-switching, and the N-back Task probes working memory updating [ 15 – 19 ]. Together, these tasks provide a comprehensive framework for quantifying brain-based responses to physical activity and investigating the neurocognitive effects of sports participation. The rationale for expecting differential cognitive outcomes across ball sports is grounded in several sport science and cognitive neuroscience theories [ 1 , 2 ]. First, the open vs. closed skill classification suggests that open-skill sports (e.g., soccer, basketball) demand higher levels of cognitive flexibility, decision-making under uncertainty, and rapid environmental adaptation, whereas closed-skill sports (e.g., table tennis, badminton) emphasize pre-planned motor execution and anticipatory control [ 20 – 22 ]. Second, the concept of sport-specific cognitive load highlights that each sport imposes unique attentional, perceptual, and tactical challenges, which may selectively engage distinct executive processes [ 9 , 12 , 14 ]. Third, neuroimaging evidence has shown divergent activation patterns across sport types, implicating networks involved in inhibitory control, working memory, and attentional regulation [ 23 – 25 ]. These perspectives support the hypothesis that different ball sports may exert domain-specific effects on adolescent executive function. Nevertheless, existing evidence is constrained by several methodological limitations. Many investigations assess a limited range of sports, rely on single-task cognitive measurements, or suffer from high heterogeneity in sample characteristics and outcome reporting [ 5 , 20 , 26 ]. Furthermore, little is known about whether specific ball sports may selectively benefit particular components of executive function—a key question for optimizing sport-based cognitive interventions in adolescents [ 23 ]. To systematically explore this question, the present review adopts a PICO-informed approach. It focuses on adolescent populations (Population), exposed to one of eight widely practiced ball sports (Intervention), compared with either passive control groups or participants in other sports (Comparator), with executive function measured by five standard cognitive tasks (Outcome) [ 5 , 15 ]. To address these gaps, the present study conducts a network meta-analysis (NMA) of cross-sectional research published from January 2000 to March 2025. Although a wider range of sports could be considered in principle, this study focuses on eight commonly practiced ball games—baseball, handball, tennis, badminton, basketball, volleyball, soccer, and table tennis—for which the existing literature provides sufficiently robust data and validated cognitive outcome measures. This selection enhances methodological consistency and allows for reliable comparisons across cognitive domains. By leveraging Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) values, this analysis aims to explore potential sport-specific differences in cognitive performance. Findings from this study are expected to inform targeted, evidence-based physical activity strategies that support adolescent brain development and long-term cognitive health. Materials and methods Study registration and reporting standards This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews and network meta-analyses to ensure methodological rigor, transparency, and reproducibility [ 27 ]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251036950), outlining the study objectives, eligibility criteria, data extraction procedures, and planned statistical methods. Adherence to these standards minimizes selective reporting and strengthens the reliability of evidence supporting cognitive-enhancing exercise interventions. The PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in the supplementary material. Literature search strategy A systematic literature search was performed in four electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science—covering the period from January 2000 to March 2025 [ 28 ]. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) with an expanded set of free-text terms to capture a comprehensive range of team-based ball sports. In addition to the eight sports ultimately analyzed in this study (baseball, handball, tennis, badminton, basketball, volleyball, soccer, and table tennis), the search also incorporated terms related to rugby, netball, cricket, lacrosse, hockey, and American football to ensure broad coverage and minimize the risk of omitting potentially relevant studies [ 28 , 29 ]. After applying the predefined eligibility criteria—including adolescent samples, the use of validated executive function tasks, and the availability of extractable quantitative outcome data—only studies involving the eight selected sports fulfilled all inclusion requirements. This ensured consistency in cognitive outcome measurement and methodological quality across the included research. Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were used to balance sensitivity and specificity. In addition, reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify further eligible articles [ 29 ]. No language restrictions were applied [ 30 ]. Studies were included if full-text access and extractable quantitative data were available. A detailed example of the PubMed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table S1 . Inclusion and exclusion criteria To ensure methodological consistency, the inclusion criteria for this review were formulated based on the PICO framework, as follows: Population: Adolescents aged 12–18 years who had initiated ball sport participation during adolescence and engaged in regular, structured training. Studies with broader age ranges were included if adolescent training onset was clearly stated. Sport Type: Participation in one of eight commonly practiced ball sports—baseball, handball, tennis, badminton, basketball, volleyball, soccer, or table tennis. These sports were not arbitrarily chosen but were the only ones among a broad initial pool of search results that met all inclusion criteria, including the use of validated executive function tasks and sufficient data reporting. Outcome Measures: Reporting of at least one validated executive function task—Go/No-Go, Stop-Signal, Stroop, Trail Making, or N-back—with quantitative outcome data. Study Design: Cross-sectional design only, as such studies provide valuable insights into long-term engagement effects, especially in the context of limited randomized controlled trials in this area. Study Type: Peer-reviewed original studies with available full texts and complete reporting of relevant data. Exclusion criteria : Studies involving participants clearly outside the adolescent age range or lacking sustained sport training history. Studies involving sports outside the ball games category or mixed-sport interventions where the isolated effect of ball sports could not be determined. Studies lacking any of the five specified executive function tasks. Studies with insufficient or non-extractable outcome data (e.g., no means, SDs, or CIs). Review articles, case reports, conference abstracts, animal studies, or duplicate publications. Study selection and quality control Two independent reviewers conducted the initial screening of titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review. Disagreements were resolved through consensus with a third reviewer. This process ensured methodological consistency and reduced risk of selection bias. Data extraction Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form to ensure consistency. Extracted information included general study characteristics (first author, publication year, study location, and design), sample details (total sample size, participant age range, and group allocation into sport or control), sport-specific details (type of ball sport, training duration, frequency, and intensity), and the type(s) of executive function task(s) used to assess cognitive performance. Risk of bias assessment The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using a modified version of the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) for cross-sectional designs [ 31 ]. The assessment focused on six key domains: (1) transparency of study design, referring to whether research objectives and data collection methods were clearly described; (2) sample selection bias, including whether random selection was applied or if the sample may have been selectively drawn; (3) implementation of blinding for interventionists, outcome assessors, and participants; (4) handling of missing data and the strategies employed to address it; (5) completeness in reporting pre-specified outcomes; and (6) the presence of any potential conflicts of interest or funding-related biases. Statistical analysis The primary outcome was the mean difference (MD) in reaction time on executive function tasks between ball sport participants and non-athlete controls, reported with 95% confidence intervals [ 32 , 33 ]. A NMA was conducted using a Bayesian random-effects model, enabling simultaneous comparisons across multiple sport types. Network diagrams illustrated the comparative structure. SUCRA values were calculated to estimate the relative effectiveness of each sport type in enhancing executive function. Higher SUCRA values were interpreted as indicating relatively greater cognitive benefits among the included sport types. Funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests were used to assess potential publication bias where applicable [ 34 , 35 ]. Results Study selection A total of 4,398 records were identified through database searches in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. After removing 827 duplicates, 3,571 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 3,243 were excluded for not meeting relevance criteria. Full texts were retrieved for the remaining 328 articles. Five studies were excluded due to unavailability of full text. Among the 323 full-text articles assessed for eligibility, 291 were excluded for the following reasons: no control group ( n = 116), insufficient or missing data ( n = 54), non-target sports or multi-sport programs where effects could not be isolated ( n = 59), or publication type (e.g., case reports, reviews) ( n = 62). Ultimately, 32 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the NMA. A flowchart of the study selection process is shown in Fig. 1 . Study characteristics The 32 included cross-sectional studies were published between 2000 and 2025 and focused on eight ball sports: basketball, soccer, table tennis, volleyball, badminton, baseball, tennis, and handball [ 21 , 22 , 24 , 25 , 36 – 63 ]. All studies compared executive function between adolescent athletes and non-athlete controls. Most participants began structured training during adolescence, with training duration ranging from 1 to over 13 years. Weekly training frequency was commonly reported (e.g., 3–5 sessions or over 12 h per week). Cognitive assessments involved one or more of the following tasks: Go/No-Go Task, Stop-Signal Task, Stroop Task, Trail Making Test, and N-back Task, evaluating domains such as inhibitory control, attention, working memory, and cognitive flexibility. A detailed summary of sport type, participant characteristics, and cognitive tasks is available in Supplementary Table S2 . Quality assessment All 32 studies were evaluated using the modified AXIS tool for cross-sectional designs [ 31 ]. Most studies demonstrated clear research objectives, appropriate methodology, and adequate data reporting. Sample selection bias was generally low. However, none of the studies reported blinding of the interventionist, assessors, or participants, which may introduce performance or detection bias. This limitation is partly inherent to the nature of sport-based interventions, where blinding is often impractical or unfeasible due to the visible and participatory nature of physical activity. Small sample sizes in some studies may affect external validity. Pre-specified outcomes were largely reported, and no significant funding-related bias was identified [ 64 ]. Detailed results of the quality assessment are presented in Supplementary Table S3 . Pairwise meta-analysis To assess the direct effects of ball sport participation on specific executive function outcomes, we conducted pairwise meta-analyses for each cognitive task using a random-effects model. The primary outcomes included performance measures from the Go/No-Go Task, Stop-Signal Task, Stroop Task, Trail Making Test, and N-back Task. Pooled effect sizes were calculated as mean differences (MDs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), Z-values, and p -values. Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic, with values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [ 35 ]. Subgroup analyses were performed to further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, stratifying studies by geographic region, training duration, and training frequency (see Supplementary Table S4 ). Substantial heterogeneity was observed in several subgroups, particularly among older participants and those with inconsistent training frequency. To identify moderators contributing to this variability, we additionally conducted meta-regression analyses examining the effects of average participant age and male proportion on intervention outcomes. Results indicated that younger age was significantly associated with greater improvements in Go/No-Go task performance ( p = 0.01), while male proportion significantly influenced the outcomes of the Trail Making Test ( p = 0.044) (see Supplementary Table S5 ). These findings underscore the importance of demographic and training-related factors in shaping cognitive outcomes associated with ball sport participation. Network meta-analysis Bayesian NMA was conducted to evaluate the comparative effects of the eight ball sports across five executive function tasks. Consistency between direct and indirect evidence was assessed via node-splitting tests [ 65 ], and SUCRA values were used to rank the relative effectiveness of the included sports. All models showed good overall consistency and convergence. This analysis included eight types of sports: Baseball, Basketball, Soccer, Table Tennis, Badminton, Tennis, Volleyball, and a non-athlete control group. A NMA method was used to integrate both direct and indirect evidence. According to the node-splitting consistency test [ 67 ], the P -values for all sport comparisons were greater than 0.05, except for two comparisons ( P = 0.003 and P = 0.019), indicating that most of the direct and indirect evidence were consistent, and the model structure was stable (see Supplementary Table S6 ). Discussion Summary of findings This NMA systematically compared the associations between participation in eight popular ball sports and adolescent executive function performance across five standardized tasks. The results indicate that adolescents engaged in ball sports tended to perform better—particularly in the domains of inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and working memory—compared to their non-athlete peers [ 52 , 54 , 73 ]. Task-specific analysis revealed that table tennis was consistently associated with relatively stronger performance in inhibitory control (Go/No-Go Task) [ 54 ]; soccer was linked to better outcomes in response inhibition (Stop-Signal Task) [ 74 ]; volleyball was associated with relatively better performance in cognitive flexibility (Stroop Task) [ 75 ]; table tennis again ranked highly in task switching (Trail Making Test) [ 54 ]; and basketball showed comparatively favorable results in working memory (N-back Task) [ 76 ]. These findings offer preliminary evidence that participation in structured, cognitively demanding ball sports may be linked to differential patterns of executive function performance in adolescents [ 73 , 77 ]. However, such associations were not consistent across all sports or tasks, suggesting that specific training characteristics (e.g., speed, perceptual load, degree of social interaction) may differentially engage neural systems related to executive function. The subsequent sections explore potential mechanisms underlying these sport-specific cognitive patterns. Interpretation of results and mechanism analysis Inhibitory control Inhibitory control—essential for suppressing impulsive or inappropriate responses—is heavily mediated by the ventrolateral and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices [ 74 , 78 – 80 ]. In this study, table tennis, baseball, and soccer were among the sports associated with relatively stronger performance in this domain, as reflected in results on the Go/No-Go and Stop-Signal Tasks [ 74 ]. In table tennis and baseball, athletes must react to high-velocity stimuli and make split-second decisions. This constant exposure to rapid behavioral adjustments may engage the brain’s inhibitory circuits [ 49 , 81 ]. Soccer, meanwhile, introduces a more complex set of stimuli: players must inhibit premature reactions while simultaneously processing strategic, perceptual, and social information [ 10 , 82 – 84 ]. Importantly, inhibitory control in these contexts is shaped not only by individual decision-making but also by social dynamics. Team sports like soccer require ongoing behavioral regulation in relation to teammates and opponents, which may recruit neural regions involved in socially mediated inhibition [ 6 , 10 ]. At the neurofunctional level, inhibitory control is underpinned by interactions between the prefrontal cortex and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), which plays a key role in error detection and conflict monitoring [ 80 ]. Repeated engagement of this DLPFC–ACC network during reactive and proactive control demands in open-skill sports has been hypothesized to support more efficient top-down regulation [ 85 ]. Sports such as table tennis and soccer, characterized by unpredictable, fast-changing environments, have been linked to broader and more dynamic patterns of neural activation than closed-skill exercises [ 52 , 54 ]. Although these three sports differ in structural format—from individual (table tennis) to small-group (baseball) to team-based (soccer)—they share core cognitive demands that may underlie the observed associations with inhibitory performance. These shared elements include: (1) rapid, externally paced stimuli; (2) high error cost, necessitating the suppression of premature actions; and (3) frequent opportunities for adaptive responses based on immediate feedback [ 51 , 52 , 54 , 74 ]. Table tennis, though an individual sport, involves intense sensorimotor integration and extremely short latency between stimulus and response, which might be particularly relevant for engaging reactive inhibition [ 54 , 59 ]. In contrast, soccer and baseball embed inhibitory demands within broader social and strategic contexts, requiring regulation of actions in anticipation of others’ behavior rather than solely in response to external cues [ 53 , 56 , 86 ]. This may help explain why handball—despite its structural similarity to soccer—was not associated with comparable inhibitory performance. One possible explanation is that handball’s play structure includes more pre-organized offensive patterns and fewer spontaneous reactive scenarios per time unit. Additionally, spatial constraints in handball may limit the variability and unpredictability of stimuli, thus potentially reducing engagement of inhibitory circuits relative to more open-field sports [ 55 , 63 , 87 ]. Taken together, these patterns suggest that it is not the individual-versus-team distinction per se that is critical, but rather the intensity, immediacy, and variability of stimulus-response demands, in combination with strategic complexity and interpersonal coordination [ 9 , 12 , 14 , 57 ]. In summary, the combined cognitive demands of real-time sensory processing, decision-making under time pressure, and socially coordinated behavior in sports like table tennis, baseball, and soccer may be associated with more frequent recruitment of prefrontal regulatory networks involved in inhibitory control [ 52 , 54 , 66 , 74 , 82 ]. Cognitive flexibility Cognitive flexibility—the capacity to adapt behavior in response to changing tasks or environments—is rooted in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) functioning [ 75 , 88 – 90 ]. This ability allows individuals to switch strategies, reallocate attention, and manage task interference in dynamic settings [ 78 ]. Sports such as volleyball, table tennis, and handball are thought to involve frequent cognitive shifts, as athletes navigate rapidly evolving game contexts [ 91 ]– [ 87 ] [ 91 ].– [ 87 ]. In volleyball, for instance, athletes must adjust defensive formations and offensive strategies in real-time, often within seconds of the opponent’s movement [ 75 ]. Similarly, table tennis players analyze spin, trajectory, and placement, adjusting their tactics continually [ 54 , 73 ]. Such environments may be associated with increased engagement of adaptive flexibility mechanisms, as they involve continuous strategic adjustments under time constraints. The frequent switching of attention and responses has been hypothesized to contribute to the activation of neural systems involved in task switching and strategic control [ 37 , 92 ]. The combination of decision-switching frequency, unpredictable stimuli, and immediate feedback has been linked to the engagement of real-world cognitive control pathways [ 77 , 93 , 94 ]. However, despite functional similarities, the observed outcomes related to cognitive flexibility varied across these sports. For example, volleyball was associated with relatively better performance, while handball—although also an open-skill, team-based sport—did not show comparable associations in this analysis. One possible explanation may involve the degree of positional rotation and tactical asymmetry inherent to volleyball, where each play entails a restructuring of roles (e.g., from blocker to setter to spiker), potentially requiring greater levels of attention shifting and motor-cognitive adaptation [ 75 , 91 ]. Handball, in contrast, appears to involve more consistent player roles and less dramatic functional shifts. While it does involve attentional agility, the complexity and variability of in-game decisions may be relatively lower compared to volleyball, where transitions between backline and frontline positions occur within milliseconds and gameplay is less pre-structured [ 55 , 63 , 87 ]. Moreover, task-switching efficiency involves not only the DLPFC but also coordinated activity in parietal areas (for attentional shifts) and the ACC (for conflict monitoring) [ 89 , 90 ]. This broader fronto-parietal control network has been implicated in flexible cognition, especially under rapidly changing, context-sensitive demands [ 88 , 92 ]. Open-skill sports may engage neural mechanisms similar to those recruited in laboratory tasks such as the Stroop and Trail Making Tests [ 54 , 77 ]. Therefore, sport-specific differences in flexibility-related performance may reflect factors such as (1) the diversity of motor roles, (2) the strategic unpredictability of in-game scenarios, and (3) the level of cognitive switching required within seconds. Volleyball and table tennis, with their rapid shifts and fine-grained tactical demands, may offer contexts that more frequently engage this cognitive domain [ 14 , 75 , 87 , 89 , 91 ]. Importantly, if such associations are replicated in future studies, flexibility-related cognitive engagement in sport settings may correspond to broader functional benefits, given prior evidence linking flexibility with academic learning, emotional regulation, and problem-solving in adolescents’ daily lives [ 3 , 78 , 88 , 95 ]. Working memory Working memory involves the active maintenance and updating of task-relevant information, primarily mediated by the DLPFC and parietal cortices [ 96 – 98 ]. This function underpins reasoning, decision-making, and complex multitasking [ 99 ]– [ 100 ]. In this analysis, basketball was associated with relatively better working memory performance, as reflected in N-back Task results. This sport entails continuous real-time information processing—tracking teammates’ positions, interpreting opponent movements, and adjusting plays—often under high time pressure [ 86 , 101 , 102 ]. Players must rapidly integrate new sensory inputs with stored information to update tactical decisions [ 76 ]. Basketball also involves extensive teamwork, where rapid coordination and information sharing may place increased demands on working memory [ 63 ]. Frequent task switching—such as transitioning between offensive and defensive roles—requires the flexible reactivation and reconfiguration of task-relevant information [ 41 , 103 ]– [ 104 ]. Notably, certain structural aspects of basketball appear to align with the cognitive operations involved in N-back tasks, which involve continual monitoring, refreshing, and manipulation of information. Basketball players often must remember what occurred just seconds earlier—who was where, what strategy was attempted—and integrate that with present stimuli to plan immediate actions. This form of sequential updating resembles the operational demands of N-back paradigms, suggesting that basketball may engage similar memory processes more frequently than other sports contexts [ 89 , 96 , 98 , 101 , 102 ]. In contrast, while other team sports like soccer or handball also involve complex scenarios, they may emphasize longer planning horizons and slower tempo buildup. Soccer, for instance, involves more distributed action over a larger field and typically allows more time for decision-making, which may shift cognitive demands toward strategic planning rather than rapid memory updating [ 83 , 95 , 103 ]. Handball, although fast-paced, includes more fixed play structures (e.g., repeated offensive formations), potentially reducing the frequency and variability of working memory engagement [ 55 , 63 , 87 ]. The complexity of basketball has been likened to high-load cognitive tasks that require continuous updating and set-shifting [ 101 ]. Neuroimaging research shows that such demands not only activate the DLPFC but also involve the frontoparietal and basal ganglia circuits responsible for information prioritization and memory gating [ 96 ]. In team-based contexts, the need to anticipate others’ intentions and adapt strategies under uncertainty may impose dual-task demands on both verbal and visuospatial working memory systems [ 76 , 103 ]. Moreover, the high-density information environment in basketball—where multiple players change position every few seconds—requires constant mental tracking and prioritization, which may help explain its observed association with working memory performance [ 102 ]. Although these associations are compelling, longitudinal and experimental research is needed to clarify whether extended engagement in such cognitively intense sports environments contributes to structural or functional adaptation of working memory systems [ 105 ]. Integrated interpretation of sport-specific profiles In addition to task-specific analyses, a broader synthesis reveals distinct sport-specific cognitive patterns that emerge across executive domains [ 20 , 73 ]. Notably, table tennis and basketball were associated with relatively strong performance on multiple tasks, suggesting potential cross-domain cognitive benefits arising from the interplay of high-speed sensorimotor demands, precision execution, and rapid cognitive switching [ 54 , 75 , 91 , 101 , 102 ]. Similarly, soccer demonstrated consistent associations with both inhibitory control and strategic flexibility, likely reflecting the sport’s high environmental variability, interpersonal coordination, and need for real-time tactical adaptation [ 53 , 83 , 95 , 103 ]. In contrast, sports such as handball exhibited more modest or inconsistent associations, which may reflect genuine differences in cognitive demands or be attributable to methodological variation, such as sample size, participant expertise, or task sensitivity [ 55 , 63 , 87 ]. Certain task-specific anomalies were also observed. For instance, although table tennis was linked to superior performance on the Go/No-Go and Trail Making Tests, it did not show comparable effects on the N-back task [ 36 , 37 , 54 , 74 ]. This discrepancy may be explained by the sport’s emphasis on reactive inhibition and motor anticipation, which align more closely with inhibition and task-switching than with sequential working memory updating [ 54 , 59 , 66 , 98 ]. Alternatively, cultural or institutional differences in task administration practices—especially in studies from East Asia—could have influenced these outcomes [ 23 , 44 , 50 ]. Taken together, these findings highlight that cognitive outcomes are shaped not solely by sport type, but by specific features such as stimulus variability, team versus individual structure, and the temporal dynamics of decision-making [ 14 , 20 , 57 , 84 ]. Recognizing these profiles may inform the development of tailored sport-based interventions that align athletic demands with the targeted enhancement of specific executive functions [ 5 , 77 , 106 , 107 ]. Comparison and contribution to existing literature Contributions and innovations This study is the first to utilize NMA to systematically evaluate and compare the associations between participation in eight ball sports and executive function in adolescents. By evaluating performance across five cognitive tasks, this research extends prior work beyond single-sport or single-task designs, offering a more holistic perspective on sport-related cognitive engagement [ 107 ]. Importantly, the use of SUCRA rankings enables a comparative overview of which types of ball games may be more frequently associated with specific executive domains, thereby providing exploratory insights that could inform the development of more tailored, evidence-guided training strategies [ 108 ]. Moreover, this study proposes a nuanced interpretation of how distinct sport types—despite structural dissimilarities (e.g., individual vs. team, fast-paced vs. strategic)—might engage overlapping neural processes relevant to executive functions [ 14 , 20 , 66 , 73 ]. For example, while table tennis and soccer differ in setting and team format, both place intensive demands on reactive inhibition, suggesting that task-relevant features (e.g., stimulus variability, response latency, social anticipation) may play a more central role than broad sport classifications in shaping executive function profiles [ 36 , 53 , 54 , 66 , 74 , 82 ]. Comparison with prior studies Previous studies have consistently demonstrated associations between physical activity and executive function outcomes in youth, but often lack comparative breadth [ 6 , 26 , 73 , 106 ]. For example, Reinsberger et al. (2024) examined the relationship between basketball and soccer participation and cognitive flexibility, but omitted other sports and executive domains [ 109 ]. The present study builds on this literature by incorporating a broader range of sports and tasks within a unified, quantitative framework. Furthermore, this study offers a conceptual basis for interpreting variability across sport types [ 14 , 20 , 73 ]. While prior research often attributes cognitive differences to general physical activity, our analysis emphasizes the potential interaction between sport-specific characteristics and the demands of particular executive tasks [ 5 , 6 , 26 , 110 ]. This distinction may help explain why structurally similar sports (e.g., handball vs. soccer) are associated with different cognitive outcomes depending on the domain assessed [ 55 , 63 , 83 , 87 ]. Methodological and theoretical innovation In addition to methodological contributions, this study introduces a conceptual model suggesting that the cognitive correlates of exercise participation may be both task-specific and sport-dependent [ 14 , 20 , 73 ]. Different ball sports appear to differentially engage neural systems, depending on their decision complexity, perceptual load, and social structure [ 54 , 83 ]. This framework moves beyond generic physical activity models by proposing a functional alignment perspective, in which cognitive engagement may be optimized when the demands of the sport align with the processing requirements of a specific executive domain (e.g., basketball with updating; volleyball with shifting; soccer with inhibition) [ 23 , 103 ]. Such a theoretical approach may inform the design of more cognitively informed, developmentally responsive sport-based programs. Limitations and future directions Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, most of the included studies adopted a cross-sectional design, which inherently limits the ability to infer causal relationships [ 78 ]. Moreover, the possibility of a selection effect cannot be ruled out—that is, individuals with inherently stronger executive functions may have been more likely to select certain sports, rather than the sports themselves directly enhancing cognitive performance. This issue of reverse causality remains a well-known limitation in observational research and requires cautious interpretation. Future research should therefore prioritize longitudinal and randomized controlled trials to assess both sustained associations and potential causal links between sport participation and executive function development [ 77 ]. Additionally, variability in sample characteristics (e.g., age, gender distribution, training background) and relatively small sample sizes may further limit the generalizability of findings. To enhance external validity, future studies should aim to include larger, more demographically diverse adolescent cohorts and adopt standardized protocols for reporting training duration, frequency, and intensity [ 62 , 95 ]. Finally, it should be acknowledged that the uneven distribution of sports across different executive tasks constrains the scope of direct comparison. Not all sports were represented in every cognitive domain, and as such, apparent differences in performance may be influenced by data availability rather than true variation in effects. This limitation underscores the need for more balanced empirical coverage in future investigations [ 5 ]. In addition, we recognize that all executive function outcomes in the current meta-analysis were assessed exclusively through reaction time data. While reaction time is a widely accepted and meaningful performance metric—particularly in adolescent populations—it does not fully capture the multifaceted nature of executive functions [ 17 ]. Accuracy rates, error patterns, or dual-task interference measures may offer additional insight into cognitive processing and should be incorporated in future studies when data availability allows. Our reliance on reaction time was based on consistency and comparability across studies, but we acknowledge this as a methodological limitation that should be addressed in subsequent research [ 68 ]. Another important limitation pertains to the geographic and cultural distribution of the included studies. A substantial proportion of the data originated from East Asian countries, with notable overrepresentation of sports such as table tennis and badminton. This regional concentration likely reflects patterns of sport popularity, institutional infrastructure, and research output in those settings [ 37 , 54 ]. However, it also raises concerns regarding cultural generalizability. Differences in training systems, educational frameworks, and even task administration procedures may introduce contextual biases that constrain the external validity of our findings. To enhance global relevance, future research should prioritize the inclusion of more geographically and culturally diverse samples, in order to examine whether the observed sport–cognition associations remain consistent across different social and institutional contexts. Addressing this limitation would not only improve the ecological validity of sport-based cognitive research but also contribute to a more inclusive understanding of adolescent neurocognitive development worldwide [ 60 ]. Practical implications and policy recommendations The findings highlight the potential relevance of incorporating cognitively demanding ball sports into school curricula and youth development programs. Sports such as table tennis and basketball—which place high demands on decision-making and working memory—may be particularly engaging for executive function development during adolescence. Promoting such activities has the potential to support dual developmental outcomes: enhancing physical fitness while also being associated with positive cognitive engagement during a critical neurodevelopmental stage. Interpretation of subgroup and meta-regression findings The results of the subgroup and meta-regression analyses provide further insight into the potential sources of variability in the cognitive benefits associated with ball sport participation. Subgroup analyses revealed that participant region, training duration, and training frequency may significantly influence executive function outcomes. For instance, studies conducted in East Asia showed relatively consistent and significant improvements on the Go/No-Go task, whereas studies from Eastern and Western Europe exhibited greater variability, suggesting that cultural or contextual factors may moderate intervention effects. Moreover, groups with higher training frequency (3–5 times per week) generally demonstrated stronger cognitive gains across several tasks, emphasizing the importance of training dose. Meta-regression results further supported the role of demographic moderators. Younger participants exhibited significantly greater improvements in Go/No-Go performance, likely reflecting heightened neural plasticity during early developmental stages. Additionally, a higher male participant proportion was associated with greater improvements in Trail Making Test performance, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and may relate to sex-specific differences in cognitive processing strategies. These findings highlight the complexity of sport–cognition interactions and suggest that age, sex composition, and training parameters should be considered when designing or interpreting ball sport interventions aimed at enhancing executive function. Future studies should consider incorporating these moderators more systematically to better understand for whom and under what conditions ball sport participation may be most beneficial. Conclusion This study indicates that participation in specific ball sports—notably table tennis, soccer, volleyball, and basketball—was associated with domain-specific patterns in executive function performance among adolescents. Each sport showed links to particular cognitive strengths across inhibition, flexibility, and working memory, suggesting that different physical activities may align with distinct cognitive domains. These findings support the rationale for tailoring youth physical activity programs toward cognitively enriched sport environments that engage both motor and executive systems. For instance, table tennis and basketball were associated with stronger performance in inhibitory control and working memory, while volleyball and soccer were linked to relatively higher outcomes in cognitive flexibility and rapid decision-making. Future research should focus on rigorously designed longitudinal studies that systematically evaluate how specific sports relate to distinct neurocognitive profiles over time. Doing so may contribute to the development of more precise, evidence-informed physical activity frameworks tailored to the developmental or therapeutic needs of adolescents.
