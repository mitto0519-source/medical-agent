TITLE: How does parental psychological control influence adolescent depression? A sequential mediation and network analysis of smartphone addiction and sleep disturbance

JOURNAL: BMC Public Health

ABSTRACT:
Background The rising prevalence of depression among children and adolescents has become a critical global public health challenge requiring urgent attention. Emerging evidence suggests that parental psychological control (PPC) may be a significant risk factor for the development of depression in adolescents. Nevertheless, the intricate mechanisms linking parental psychological control to adolescent depression remain largely unresolved. This study aims to explore the association between parental psychological control and depression using a large sample of adolescents, and to determine whether parental psychological control relates to adolescent depression through a sequential pathway involving smartphone addiction and compromised sleep quality. Furthermore, network analysis is employed to identify the core and bridging symptoms within the network of parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep disturbances, and depression in adolescents, providing a basis for targeted interventions. Methods A cross-sectional questionnaire study was administered to 4,416 adolescents (63.56% females; mean age of 16.24 ± 0.92 years) between September and November 2024. Participants completed a battery of standardized measures, including self-reported demographic questionnaires, the Parental Psychological Control Scale, the Short Version Smartphone Addiction Scale, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and the Patient Health Questionnaire 9-Item Scale. Data were analyzed using the SPSS 26.0 and R statistical software packages. Results Our study revealed a significant positive association between parental psychological control and adolescent depression. Parental psychological control significantly predicted both smartphone addiction and poor sleep quality in adolescents. Importantly, smartphone addiction and sleep quality served as a serial mediator in the relationship between parental psychological control and adolescent depression. Network analysis further revealed that three central symptoms—“Energy” (PHQ4), “Love withdrawal” (LW), and “Sleep” (PHQ3)—and two key bridge symptoms—“Smartphone addiction” (SASall) and “Daytime dysfunction” (DD)—emerged as critical connectors linking parental psychological control to depression in adolescents. Conclusion These findings elucidate the mechanisms through which parental psychological control contributes to adolescent depression, yielding significant theoretical and practical implications for adolescent mental health preventive interventions. Specifically, interventions targeting maladaptive smartphone use and poor sleep hygiene, particularly those addressing intermediary symptoms such as daytime dysfunction, may effectively mitigate the risk of depression in adolescents under high parental psychological control. Introduction
In contemporary society, adolescent mental health issues have garnered increasing attention, with adolescent depression emerging as a critical concern in global public health. The latest epidemiological data from the World Health Organization [ 1 ] indicate that 14% of adolescents aged 10–19 worldwide experience mental health problems, with depression accounting for 3.9%. This proportion continues to rise annually. Longitudinal data from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) in the United States reveal that the prevalence of major depressive episodes (MDE) among adolescents aged 12–17 years escalated from 8.1% in 2009 to 15.8% in 2019, with particularly pronounced increases among girls (from 11.4% to 23.4%) [ 2 ]. The situation in China is equally concerning, with the recently published “China National Mental Health Development Report” indicating that the detection rate of depression among Chinese adolescents reaches 24.6%, with 7.4% classified as severe [ 3 ]. Depression severely impairs adolescents’ academic and social function and can also lead to extreme behaviors such as self-harm and suicidality [ 4 , 5 ]. Its negative effects often persist into adulthood, resulting in long-term psychosocial and functional impairments [ 6 ]. Given the substantial impact of depression and its high prevalence among adolescents, research into the risk factors and underlying mechanisms of depression or depressive symptoms in vulnerable populations is essential for developing targeted interventions to protect the mental health of children and adolescents.
Among the many contributors to adolescent depression, the family environment has drawn particular attention for its enduring psychological influence. Parenting style, a core construct within the family microenvironment, plays a pivotal role in the mental health growth of children and adolescents [ 7 ]. Positive parenting style characterized by warmth and mutual trust, is beneficial to enhance individual adaptive capacities and resilience, thereby promoting holistic well-being and reducing the incidence of psychological distress [ 8 , 9 ]. Conversely, dysfunctional parenting practice, such as parent-child conflict and parental marital conflict, can impede the development of a child’s personality and contribute to maladaptive behaviors and affective disorders [ 10 ]. Parental Psychological Control (PPC), defined as the use of covert strategies (e.g., guilt induction, love withdrawal) to invade children’s psychological autonomy and enforce compliance with parental expectations [ 11 ], has been identified as a robust risk factor of adolescent psychopathology [ 12 ]. Recent longitudinal research on Chinese adolescents demonstrates that parental psychological control is not only significantly correlated with concurrent depressive symptoms but also predicts new depressive episodes over time [ 13 , 14 ]. According to Self-Determination Theory (SDT), such controlling behaviors may impede the satisfaction of adolescents’ basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, ultimately disrupting emotional regulation processes and increasing the risk of depression symptoms [ 15 ]. From an attachment perspective, excessive control erodes secure bonds, leaving adolescents emotionally dependent yet unsupported—a contradiction strongly linked to depressive symptoms [ 16 ]. A recent meta-analysis further revealed that, even after controlling for potential confounding factors such as genetics and family socioeconomic status, the negative parenting practices, including parental psychological control, can still independently account for a substantial proportion of variance in childhood depression, with effects being more pronounced in collectivistic cultural contexts [ 17 ]. Despite these robust findings, the psychological mechanisms connecting parental psychological control and adolescent depression remain insufficiently understood.
Smartphone addiction, defined as the uncontrollable and compulsive overuse of smartphones that impairs physical, psychological, and social functioning [ 18 ], is increasingly recognized as one such mechanism. According to the latest CNNIC report [ 19 ], as of December 2024, China’s internet user base has reached 1.108 billion, with 99.7% accessing the internet via mobile phones. Among these users, approximately 1.44 million are adolescents aged 10 to 19 years. International data indicate that over 30% of teenagers experience problematic smartphone use [ 20 , 21 ]. Negative parenting practices, including parental psychological control, are posited as antecedent variables influencing problematic behaviors in children and adolescents [ 22 ]. Several studies report that parental psychological control predicts problematic smartphone use [ 23 , 24 ]. Based on the Social Control Theory [ 25 ], individuals exposed to high levels of parental psychological control are prone to develop compromised self-regulation abilities and eroded self-esteem. This dual impact may weaken their capacity to resist smartphone-related temptations. In turn, excessive smartphone use, is closely associated with depressive symptoms in adolescence [ 26 ]. Excessive smartphone immersion may disrupt adolescents’ normal social patterns, reducing their opportunities for real-world social engagement, thereby intensifying feelings of social isolation and triggering negative emotions [ 27 ]. Longitudinal evidence supports this link, showing that higher levels of smartphone addiction significantly predict the emergence and intensification of adolescent depression [ 28 ]. Eye-tracking studies further demonstrate that smartphone addicts tend to exhibit negative attentional biases characterized by heightened vigilance and difficulty disengaging from socially threatening information, which constitutes a key cognitive signature associated with depressive vulnerability [ 29 , 30 ]. Collectively, based on the aforementioned theoretical and empirical evidence, our study proposes that smartphone addiction may mediate the relationship between parental psychological control and depression among adolescents.
From a family systems perspective [ 31 ], maladaptive parent–child interactions also impair physiological regulation, particularly in the domain of sleep. Research has shown that the more parental rejection, opposition, or neglect children and adolescents encounter, the more their sleep quality deteriorates, with significant decrements in sleep efficiency and continuity [ 32 ]. Conversely, parental care and warmth are positively associated with improved sleep outcomes [ 33 ]. Furthermore, excessive parental control is linked to delayed circadian rhythms and a higher prevalence of evening chronotypes, which in turn exacerbate sleep disturbances [ 34 , 35 ]. Recent empirical evidence confirms that high parental control significantly contributes to adolescent depression through the serial mediation of circadian misalignment and sleep impairment [ 36 ].
As a conserved biological process, sleep plays a crucial role in adolescent neurocognitive development and mental health maintenance [ 37 ]. Optimal sleep quality enhances adolescents’ psychological resilience, helping them cope more effectively with life stressors, whereas sleep deprivation or impaired sleep continuity may compromise this adaptive capacity, leading to various psychological and behavioral problems [ 38 ]. It’s been shown that adolescents with poorer sleep quality tend to exhibit high levels of depression symptoms [ 39 ], and chronic sleep deprivation can further intensify this risk by undermining emotion regulation—particularly the ability to employ adaptive strategies like cognitive reappraisal, ultimately increasing the vulnerability to depression [ 40 ]. A recent meta-analytic study found that subjective sleep quality demonstrates the strongest correlation with depressive symptoms, while sleep duration shows relatively weaker associations [ 41 ]. Supporting these findings, a three-year longitudinal study of 932 Chinese adolescents [ 42 ] confirmed that poor sleep quality in early adolescence not only predicts later depressive symptoms but also plays a crucial mediation role between family intimacy and emotional problems. Thus, in view of the above findings and research evidence, we assume that sleep quality may also serve as a mediator in the relationship between parental psychological control and depression among adolescents.
Based on the foregoing analysis, smartphone addiction and sleep quality may serve as important mediating variables between parental psychological control and adolescent depression. Additionally, there may be associations between smartphone addiction and sleep quality when both are considered as mediating roles. Studies have shown that there is a significant correlation between smartphone addiction and sleep quality, and smartphone addiction can negatively predict sleep quality of children and adolescents [ 27 , 43 ]. Due to the heavy academic workload during the day and the possibility of being prohibited from using mobile phones in the classroom, many adolescents tend to overuse their phones before bedtime, leading to sleep procrastination and deteriorated sleep quality [ 44 ]. A recent large-scale study observed that poor sleep quality plays a key mediating role in the link between problematic smartphone use and adolescent depression [ 45 ]. Adolescents with higher smartphone addiction levels tend to report greater insomnia, fatigue, and emotional distress compared to those with lower addiction levels [ 46 ]. Mechanistically, blue light emitted by mobile phones can significantly inhibits the secretion of melatonin—a hormone produced by the pineal gland that regulates an individual’s sleep-wake cycles [ 47 ]. This inhibition may, in turn, exacerbate delays in sleep onset and disrupt individual circadian rhythms [ 48 ]. Intervention studies have suggested that reducing pre-sleep screen exposure can significantly improve sleep duration and efficiency [ 49 ]. All the above evidence indicates that smartphone addiction and sleep quality may act as sequential mediators in the relationship between parental psychological control and depression in adolescents.
Over the past decade, the majority of research on the comorbidities of depression and sleep disturbances has primarily relied on syndromic assessments using standard scale total scores. This method is rooted in traditional psychopathological theories, which typically emphasize the association of individual symptoms with prototypical symptoms of other syndromes rather than highlighting the differences among individual symptoms themselves [ 50 ]. Importantly, these scores are composed of various individual symptoms, each of which may involve distinct underlying psychoneurological mechanisms. Recently, network analysis has emerged as a novel approach for examining the complex and dynamic changing relationships among individual mental symptoms [ 51 ]. This approach is grounded in the notion that psychological distress consists of an intricate network of interrelated symptoms [ 52 ], with an emphasis on understanding the nature and intensity of the associations among these symptoms [ 53 , 54 ]. Moreover, network analysis has proven to be a valuable tool for elucidating the interconnections between specific symptoms and diverse psychiatric disorders [ 55 ]. It provides a comprehensive and intuitive representation of the intricate relationships among the variables, facilitating a deeper understanding of the interplay between individual mental symptoms—an aspect that is challenging to capture using total scores from standardized questionnaires [ 56 ]. Impressively, network analysis has the ability to capture and visually display both the direct and indirect connections between variables from a data-driven standpoint [ 57 ]. Furthermore, it can identify the central and influential nodes within the network structure, providing critical insights into the key variables that play a pivotal role in the overall network [ 58 ].
Network analysis has been utilized to investigate the relationships among depressive, anxiety, and sleep disturbance symptoms. For example, a network analysis was conducted to explore the interconnections between anxiety, depression, sleep issues, and problematic smartphone use among Chinese college art students. The findings indicated that uncontrollable worry served as the most central symptom, while daytime dysfunction, self-harm or suicidal ideation, abnormal behavior and speech, and sensory fear were identified as significant bridge symptoms connecting these comorbid conditions [ 59 ]. Similarly, another network analysis focusing on the relationship between internet addiction (IA) and sleep disturbance symptoms in a sample of 1,977 university students revealed that “surfing the internet late instead of sleeping” was the most prominent bridge symptom liking these two components factors [ 60 ]. Despite these advances in understanding comorbidities through network approaches, the complex interplay among parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and depression in adolescent populations remains insufficiently understood. Most existing studies have examined these factors in isolation or in limited combinations, with few investigations comprehensively exploring how parental psychological control relates to the triad of smartphone addiction, sleep disturbances, and depression in adolescents. Given the unique developmental characteristics of adolescence—a period marked by heightened vulnerability to parental influences, rapid neurobiological changes, and emerging behavioral patterns [ 61 ]—examining the specific symptom-level connections through which parental psychological control is linked to adolescent mental health, sleep patterns, and problematic smartphone use is particularly important. Network analysis offers a valuable framework for this investigation by allowing identification of central symptoms, bridge symptoms, and the strength of specific inter-symptom connections, thereby revealing potential intervention targets that may be overlooked by variable-centered approaches. Such analyses may shed light on the key symptoms or behaviors that bridge these domains, informing targeted interventions and support strategies to promote the well-being of adolescents.
Taken together, although prior research has established a link between parental psychological control and adolescent depression, the intricate pathways through which parental psychological control contributes to the development of depression in adolescents remain largely unclear. Existing studies have identified several single mediating mechanisms underlying this association, such as loneliness [ 62 ], emotion inhibition [ 63 ], and psychological resilience [ 64 ]. However, few have systematically examined the specific serial mediating roles of addictive behaviors (such as smartphone addiction) and subsequent sleep quality in this relationship. Furthermore, prior research has predominantly relied on traditional, variable-centered approaches utilizing scale total scores, with no studies having yet systematically investigated the symptom-level connections through which parental psychological control relates to adolescent depression, problematic smartphone use, and sleep disturbances, thereby hindering the identification of core intervention targets across these interrelated variables. To fill the research gap, the current study, which used a large sample of Chinese adolescents, aimed to investigate: (1) whether parental psychological control was positively associated with adolescent depression, smartphone addiction, and poor sleep quality; (2) whether smartphone addiction and sleep quality independently mediated the relationship between parental psychological control and adolescents’ depression; and (3) whether parental psychological control positively predicted depression through the chain mediating roles of smartphone addiction and sleep quality. Additionally, network analysis was conducted to identify key bridging symptoms that link different symptom clusters within the parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and adolescent depression network, thus elucidating potential intervention targets across these interconnected domains.

Participants and procedure
A questionnaire-based cross-sectional study was carried out from September to November 2024 at a public secondary school in Hefei, Anhui Province (China). The study employed a using cluster sampling method by recruiting all students from randomly selected classes across Grades 10 to 12. A total of 4,489 students were invited to participate. Prior to data collection, students and their parents or guardians received a detailed explanation of the study and provided written informed consent. Participants were also informed that their involvement was voluntary and they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Subsequently, they were instructed to complete electronic questionnaires assessing demographic characteristics (i.e., gender, age, only child, place of residence, family type, paternal/maternal education level, and family economic status), as well as parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and depression. After screening the data and excluding a small number of responses from participants over 18 years of age or with substantial missing values, a final sample of 4,416 students (63.56% girls, 36.44% boys) was retained for analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 98.37%. The age of the participants ranged from 14 to 18 years, with a mean age of 16.24 ± 0.92 years. Among the participants, 3,571 (80.86%) were from rural areas, 845 (19.13%) from towns or urban areas. There were 271 (6.13%) only children and 4,145 (93.86%) non-only children. In terms of family structure, 89.03% came from intact families, while the remaining participants were from divorced families or families where one of the parents was deceased. The detailed demographic information is shown in Table 1 . This study was reviewed and approved by the ethical review of the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Normal University. All procedures were performed in compliance with the ethical standards laid out in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Parental psychological control scale (PPCS)
This study employed the Parental Psychological Control Scale [ 65 ] to measure participants’ levels of parental psychological control. The PPCS comprised 18 items across three dimensions: love withdrawal (10 items), guilt induction (5 items), and authority assertion (3 items). Each item utilized a 5-point Likert scoring system, ranging from 1 (completely inconsistent) to 5 (completely consistent). The scale score represents the average of all items, with higher scores indicating stronger degrees of parental psychological control. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescent populations [ 66 ]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.95, with Cronbach’s α coefficients for the love withdrawal, guilt induction, and authority assertion subscales being 0.88, 0.91, and 0.87, respectively.

Patient health questionnaire 9-Item scale (PHQ-9)
This study utilized the Chinese version of Patient Health Questionnaire 9-Item Scale (PHQ-9) [ 67 ] to assess adolescents’ depressive symptoms. The scale composed of 9 items, each of which was assessed on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day), with participants selecting corresponding options based on symptom frequency over the past two weeks. Sample items are like “Feeling tired or having little energy”, “Feeling bad about myself or that I am a failure or have let myself or my family down”. The total score is calculated by summing individual item scores and ranges from 0 to 27. Higher scores represent greater levels of depression. Prior research has demonstrated the satisfactory validity and reliability of scale among Chinese adolescents [ 68 ]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.92.

Smartphone addiction scale (SAS-SV)
Smartphone addiction was measured using the Short Version Smartphone Addiction Scale (SAS-SV) developed by Kwon et al. [ 69 ]. The SAS-SV is a validated scale that composes of 10 items in a single dimension. Participants were asked to respond on a 6-point scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). Sample items are like “I won’t be able to stand not having a smartphone”, “I feel impatient and fretful when l am not holding my smartphone”. Scale scores are calculated by summing individual item scores, with higher total scores indicating higher levels of smartphone addiction. This scale has been widely used among Chinese sample and showed good psychometric qualities among adolescents [ 70 ]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.92.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)
Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a self-reported questionnaire that evaluates participants’ sleep quality and disturbances over a one-month period [ 71 ]. This scale consists of 19 items divided into seven subcategories: subjective sleep quality, sleep disturbances, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep latency, sleep duration, hypnotic medication use, and daytime dysfunction. Sample items include “How often during the past month have you had you had trouble sleeping because of this?” and “During the past month, how often have you had trouble staying awake while driving, eating meals, or engaging in social activities?”. Each dimension of the PSQI is scored between 0 and 3, with the total score ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. The Chinese version of the PSQI has well-established validity and reliability [ 72 ]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.84.

Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics for demographic characteristics and scale scores were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 26.0 (IBM). To address potential common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test, as proposed by Podsakoff et al. [ 73 ], was implemented. The exploratory factor analysis (EFA) revealed eight factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first factor accounted for 34.4% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. These results indicate that there was no substantial common method bias in this study. Subsequently, bivariate correlations among study variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation analysis. To investigate the underlying mechanisms linking parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and depression, a sequential mediation analysis was performed using Hayes’ (2013) PROCESS macro (v4.0) for SPSS [ 74 ]. The mediation effects were evaluated with 5,000 bootstrap resamples to generate 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals (CIs). In accordance with established criteria [ 75 ], mediation effects were considered statistically significant if their bootstrap CIs did not include zero.

Network estimation
Network analysis was conducted using R software (v4.4.0), implementing the bootnet and qgraph packages created by Epskamp, Borsboom and Fried [ 76 ]. Within this analytical framework, symptoms are visualized as nodes, while the relationships between symptoms are illustrated as edges [ 53 ]. The network comprised 20 distinct nodes, each representing different symptoms. These nodes included parental psychological control, depression, sleep disturbance, and smartphone addiction symptoms, as outlined in Table S1. Nodes that appear more densely clustered and situated in closer proximity to each other demonstrate stronger and more prevalent interactions with surrounding nodes in the network.
The network models underwent regularization through the application of the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) methodology [ 77 ]. In the process of model selection using the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC), researchers typically configure the tuning parameter to fall within the range of 0 to 0.5 [ 78 ]. Centrality metrics including strength, betweenness, and closeness were calculated to assess the importance of individual nodes within the network [ 79 ]. These calculations were executed using the ‘centralityPlot’ function available in the qgraph package [ 80 ]. The identification of symptoms functioning as connective bridges between other symptoms in the network was accomplished through the application of the ‘bridge’ function from the network-tools package [ 81 ]. Network visualization was performed utilizing the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm [ 82 ]. The R package “mgm” [ 83 ] was employed to estimate the predictability of nodes within the network model.

Estimation of network accuracy and stability
The robustness of the network solution was examined by evaluating edge weight accuracy and centrality indices reliability through the “bootnet” R package. Edge weight accuracy assessment involved calculating confidence intervals (CIs) using nonparametric bootstrapping techniques [ 84 ]. Correlation stability coefficients (CS) were computed to measure the stability of centrality indices across these various subsets [ 85 ]. In evaluating centrality indices stability using CS, the established guideline suggests that values should exceed 0.25, with values surpassing 0.50 being considered optimal [ 76 ].

The chain mediating effects analyses
Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 6) was utilized to examine the sequential mediating roles of smartphone addiction and sleep quality in the association between parental psychological control and depression. Table 3 ; Fig. 1 present the results of chain mediation analysis with gender and age as control variables. The mediation model showed that parental psychological control had a significant total effect on adolescent depression ( β total effect = 0.454, p < 0.001). When smartphone addiction and sleep quality were included in the regression model, parental psychological control significantly and positively predicted both smartphone addiction ( βa 1 = 0.444, p < 0.001) and sleep quality ( βa 2 = 0.226, p < 0.001). Furthermore, smartphone addiction significantly predicted sleep quality ( βa 3 = 0.264, p < 0.001) and depression ( βb 1 = 0.182, p < 0.001). Additionally, sleep quality had a significant predictive effect on depression ( βb 2 = 0.511, p < 0.001). At this point, the direct effect of parental psychological control on depression was diminished ( β = 0.198, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that smartphone addiction and sleep quality not only partially mediate the relationship between parental psychological control and depression, but also have a chain mediation effect on them. Network structures
The analysis of the network consisting of 20 nodes, as shown in Fig. 2 , revealed that a total of 128 out of 190 edges (43.76%) were estimated to be nonzero edges, indicating considerable interconnectedness between these symptoms. The three strongest edges were the between PHQ1 and PHQ4 (“Anhedonia” - “Energy”), GI and LW (“guilt induction,” - “Love withdrawal”), PHQ1 and PHQ2 (“Anhedonia,” - “Depressed mood”). The strongest edges were distributed within the individual communities for parental psychological control, depression, smartphone addiction, and sleep disturbance, as shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1.
Fig. 2 Symptom network of parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep problems and depression in adolescents. In the diagram, symptom nodes with stronger connections are positioned closer to each other
The predictability values of the nodes ranged from 5.6% to 76.3%, with an average value of 48.47%, suggesting that approximately 48.47% of the variability in nodes within the network could be explained by their neighboring nodes (see Table S2). LW (“love withdrawal”) had the highest level of predictability. The HD (“Hypnotic drugs”), symptoms of sleep disturbance, had the lowest level of predictability in the study (Table S2).
Figure 3 illustrates the bridge symptoms of all network nodes in the study. Discussion
The present study provides a nuanced understanding of how parental psychological control contributes to adolescent depression by integrating sequential mediation and network analysis. The.
results revealed that adolescents subjected to high levels of parental psychological control were more likely to experience depressive symptoms. Moreover, parental psychological control was not only directly associated with adolescent depression but also indirectly linked to it through a sequential pathway involving smartphone addiction and subsequent sleep disturbance. Further network analysis identified PHQ4 (“Energy”), LW (“Love withdrawal”), and PHQ3 (“Sleep”) as core symptoms, and SASall (“Smartphone addiction symptom”), DD (“Daytime dysfunction”) as key bridge symptoms linking parental psychological control and adolescent depression. These findings hold profound implications, significantly enhancing our comprehension of how parental psychological control contributes to adolescent depression and the underlying mechanisms involved. They may serve as a guide for relevant institutions to implement measures that further alleviate the prevalence of depression in children and adolescents while promoting their overall mental health.
In this study, we verified that parental psychological control is a significant predictor of adolescent depression, consistent with prior research indicating that the higher an adolescent’s exposure to parental psychological control, the more severe their depressive symptoms tend to be [ 86 , 87 ]. Complementarily, Rogers and colleagues [ 88 ] found that adolescents exposed to parental overprotection, excessive control, or intrusiveness were more prone to depression and anxiety, further corroborating our result. This association can be explained through the lens of self-determination theory [ 15 ], which posits that controlling parental behaviors frustrate adolescents’ basic psychological needs for autonomy and competence. Such frustration, in turn, leads adolescents to internalize feelings of inadequacy and foster maladaptive beliefs (e.g., “I am incompetent”), which directly contribute to the development of depressive symptoms. Collectively, these findings.
reaffirm parental psychological control as a robust risk factor for adolescent depression, highlighting the urgency of prioritizing mental health support for adolescents exposed to such parenting practices in real-world contexts.
The current study also revealed that both smartphone addiction and sleep quality independently mediated the relationship between parental psychological control and depression in adolescents, with sleep quality emerging as the more salient mediator. On one hand, this finding echoes previous studies showing that problematic smartphone use serves as a mediator between negative parenting styles and psychological distress [ 89 ], and that higher levels of parental control elevate the likelihood of sleep problems in children and adolescents, subsequently leading to emotional disorders [ 90 ]. On the other hand, our findings extend the existing literature by demonstrating that sleep quality plays an even more critical role. This stronger mediating effect can be attributed to the fundamental role of sleep in adolescents’ neurocognitive and emotional development. Unlike smartphone use—which varies widely in form and function, sleep constitutes an essential and non-negotiable biological process. Its disruption has been well-documented to pose significant risks to the physical and mental health of children and adolescents [ 91 ]. Specifically, poor sleep quality could adversely affect the secretion of stress- and mood-related hormones [ 92 ], and impair the function of prefrontal cortex, a brain region crucial for emotion regulation [ 93 ], thereby increasing vulnerability to emotional problems [ 40 , 93 ]. The observed stronger mediation effect of sleep quality underscores its unique position as a “physiological bridge” between psychological stress and mental health in adolescents. It also suggests that even in an increasingly digitalized world, core biopsychological processes such as sleep remain pivotal to mental health outcomes. Therefore, strategies aimed at mitigating adolescent depression in the context of high parental control may achieve greater impact by prioritizing sleep health, either alongside or even ahead of addressing digital behaviors.
More importantly, our study identified a sequential mediation pathway linking parental psychological control to adolescent depression through smartphone addiction and poor sleep quality, which is in line with our initial hypothesis. This chain mediating model provides deeper insights into how parental psychological control is associated with depression in adolescents, and extends prior research by empirically delineating a dynamic mechanism whereby controlling parenting fosters adolescents’ behavioral dependency on smartphones, which in turn impairs sleep quality and increases the risk of depressive symptoms. While previous studies have separately examined the roles of smartphone overuse and poor sleep in adolescent mental health [ 24 , 46 ], the present study integrates them into a coherent theoretical pathway, illustrating how external familial stress is internalized through behavioral and physiological channels. This identified pathway aligns with arousal theory [ 94 ], which suggests that high levels of parental psychological control drive adolescents to seek emotional refuge in smartphones. However, such compensatory use may exacerbate pre-sleep physiological arousal and psychological overstimulation, ultimately resulting in difficulty falling asleep and compromised sleep quality [ 95 ]. Prolonged sleep impairment, in turn, may substantially increase susceptibility to emotional disorders such as depression and anxiety [ 96 ]. Notably, these findings carry important implications for practical intervention: for adolescents exposed to high parental psychological control, concurrently addressing maladaptive smartphone use and promoting sleep hygiene could disrupt this detrimental cascade more effectively than single-focused approaches.
To further investigate the interrelationships among parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and depression in adolescents, and to identify potential intervention targets for this vulnerable population, network analysis was applied. The results indicate that the most robust connections are observed within individual symptom clusters rather than crossing between different symptom domains. This observation is consistent with findings from prior network analytic research, highlighting the reproducibility of these patterns across various studies [ 97 ]. Specifically, the strongest direct connections were found between PHQ1 and PHQ4 (“Anhedonia”–“Energy”), GI and LW (“Guilt induction”–“Love withdrawal”), and PHQ1 and PHQ2 (“Anhedonia”–“Depressed mood”). A recent network analysis exploring the complex relationships among anxiety, depression, sleep problems, and smartphone addiction in college students similarly identified strong within-domain connections, with the most prominent edges being subjective sleep quality and sleep latency (PSQI1–PSQI2), and nervousness and uncontrollable worry (GAD1-GAD2) [ 59 ]. This cross-study consistency suggests that psychological symptom networks tend to exhibit modular architecture, with stronger intra-domain connections and relatively weaker inter-domain connections.
Nodes with high expected influence centrality effectively identify symptoms that contribute most to the overall network. The PHQ4 (“Energy”) exhibited the highest centrality, indicating its pivotal role in the symptom system. Given this centrality, interventions targeting energy-related symptoms may produce broader network effects, potentially influencing connected symptoms across depression, smartphone addiction, sleep disturbance, and responses to parental psychological control. Several evidence-based approaches could help restore energy restoration in adolescents, including behavioral activation therapy for improving motivation [ 98 ], regular physical exercise for enhancing energy, reducing depressive symptoms, and improving sleep quality [ 99 ], and adequate nutritional intake of complex carbohydrates, B vitamins, iron, and omega-3 fatty acids for energy metabolism [ 100 ]. Sleep hygiene interventions, such as maintaining consistent sleep–wake schedules and limiting pre-bedtime screen exposure [ 101 ] may also prove beneficial. Given the interconnected nature of these factors in our network model, integrated multi-component approaches warrant further investigation among adolescents experiencing high parental psychological control [ 102 ].
Among the three dimensions of parental psychological control—love withdrawal (LW), guilt induction (GI), and authority assertion (AA)—network analysis identified LW as a core symptom with the highest centrality, suggesting a more pervasive influence than other controlling behaviors. Theoretically, LW is particularly detrimental because it threatens adolescents’ fundamental attachment needs by making parental affection contingent on behavior. Self-determination Theory posits that such basic psychological need frustration cascades into broader difficulties across emotional and sleep domains [ 103 , 104 ]. Empirical evidence indicates that LW exerts stronger negative effects on adolescents’ psychological well-being, internal motivation, and autonomous functioning compared to other forms of psychological control [ 105 , 106 ]. The perceived threat of losing parental love may activate hypervigilance to parental approval, leading adolescents to suppress authentic emotions and develop external locus of control—both recognized risk factors for depression [ 107 ].
Of particular significance, the results of network analysis revealed that smartphone addiction (SASall) and daytime dysfunction (DD) served as key bridge symptoms, playing crucial roles in connecting the networks of parental psychological control and depressive symptoms. Targeted interventions addressing these two bridge symptoms may effectively mitigate the transmission pathways from parental psychological control to depressive symptoms. For smartphone addiction, evidence-based approaches such as setting screen time limits, establishing phone-free time periods, and cultivating alternative healthy activities can be implemented to reduce excessive usage behaviors and enhance self-control abilities [ 108 ]. For daytime dysfunction, sleep hygiene education and behavioral activation therapy can be implemented, including establishing regular sleep-wake schedules, optimizing sleep environments, reducing caffeine intake, and increasing daytime physical activities and social engagement [ 109 ]. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has also demonstrated effectiveness in improving sleep quality and reducing daytime dysfunction among adolescents [ 110 ]. Given the high bridge strength of these two symptoms in the network, successful interventions targeting them can not only directly improve related symptoms but also generate cascading effects by severing harmful symptom transmission pathways, thereby mitigating the negative impacts of parental psychological control on adolescents, improving sleep quality, and ultimately reducing the severity of depressive symptoms. This network structure-based precision intervention strategy provides new theoretical foundations and practical directions for adolescent mental health promotion, as bridge symptoms represent transdiagnostic indicators that serve as crucial targets for specific interventions.
Additionally, it is important to note that the results of this study should be interpreted within the sociocultural context in which the research was conducted. In collectivist societies such as China, parental psychological control is often embedded within interdependent family norms that emphasize obedience, harmony, and filial responsibility. Such control-oriented parenting practices may be socially legitimized and even perceived by parents as expressions of care or moral guidance rather than intrusion. However, from the adolescent’s perspective, these behaviors may still undermine autonomy and emotional security, particularly amid the increasing exposure to individualistic values and digital autonomy in contemporary youth culture. This cultural ambivalence may partially explain why parental psychological control exerts a strong influence on both behavioral regulation (e.g., smartphone addiction) and emotional outcomes (e.g., depression) among Chinese adolescents. Beyond the cultural level, the current findings also have important implications for social and educational practice. The identified sequential pathway—linking parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, poor sleep quality, and depression—suggests that intervention efforts should not only target individual emotional symptoms but also address the broader family and behavioral ecology. School-based mental health programs could include psychoeducational components aimed at promoting autonomy-supportive parenting and digital literacy among both parents and students. In community settings, preventive strategies might focus on improving family communication, regulating pre-sleep screen use, and enhancing adolescents’ sleep hygiene. Integrating these culturally sensitive and system-oriented interventions could help reduce depressive vulnerability and foster psychological well-being in adolescents.

Limitations and future directions
Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the study limits its ability to establish causal relationships between study variables. While the observed associations align with our theoretical model, the data cannot directly assess causality among parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and depression. The directionality of their interrelationships remains uncertain. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are essential to verify the temporal precedence and causality among these variables. Second, all questionnaires utilized in this study are based on self-reporting, which may introduce information bias and weaken the objectivity and accuracy of the data. Future research would benefit from incorporating more objective and sensitive indicators (e.g., actigraphy for sleep quality, usage logs for smartphone activity) to triangulate findings and enhance the validity of the results. Third, given that the study sample was drawn from a single city in Anhui, China, generalizations regarding the conclusions need to be made with caution. Specifically, the findings may not be directly applicable to adolescents from other regions outside Mainland China and other countries. Additionally, caution is warranted when extending the findings to populations outside the study’s target age range, such as children and college students. Future studies involving a more diverse sample of individuals from various regions may be conducted to replicate and expand upon the findings of this research. Finally, our study primarily focused on the impact of parental psychological control on adolescent depression and the underlying mechanisms involved. However, additional factors, such as peer dynamics and biological predispositions, may also influence the relationship between parenting and depression during adolescence. Future research should conduct further investigations into the interplay of various factors and depression to enhance the understanding of its internal mechanisms and provide a scientific basis for more comprehensive mental health interventions for children and adolescents.

Conclusions
In conclusion, this study provides critical insights into the mechanisms through which parental psychological control contributes to adolescent depression. The results revealed that parental psychological control not only directly exacerbates depressive symptoms in adolescents but also indirectly increase depression by promoting their excessive smartphone use, which subsequently undermines sleep quality. Notably, network analysis further identified that PHQ4, LW, and PHQ3 were the core symptoms within the network of parental psychological control, smartphone addiction, sleep problems and depression, and SASall and DD as pivotal bridge symptoms connecting these psychological and behavioral disturbances. These findings significantly advance our understanding of adolescent mental health by demonstrating how maladaptive parenting practices may initiate a cascade of digital overuse and sleep disruption, ultimately culminating in depressive symptoms. The identification of smartphone addiction and daytime dysfunction as central bridge symptoms highlights its potential as a key intervention target. These evidence-based insights establish a strong empirical robust for developing targeted, multi-component interventions to mitigate depression risk during adolescence. Additionally, this study underscores the need for future research to investigate the temporal dynamics of these relationships and assess the effectiveness of interventions targeting the identified pathways.
