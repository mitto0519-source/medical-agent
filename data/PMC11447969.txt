Background Previous studies have indicated that social connectedness can serve as a protective buffer against negative outcomes associated with online victimization. However, the role of social connectedness between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms is still unclear. This study aims to examine the mediating effect of social connectedness on the association between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. Methods A cross-sectional design was utilized, using questionnaires for data collection and multi-stage stratified cluster sampling. The general demographic questionnaire, Nine-Item Internet Gaming Disorder Scale–Short Form, Social Connectedness Scale-Revised and Patient Health Questionnaire Physical Symptoms were used to collect data. We adopted Pearson’s correlation analysis and the PROCESS Macro Model in regression analysis to explore the relationships among Internet gaming disorder, social connectedness and somatic symptoms. Results Internet gaming disorder was positively correlated with somatic symptoms ( r = 0.20, P &lt; 0.001), while network ( r =-0.08, P &lt; 0.001) and real-life social connectedness ( r =-0.31, P &lt; 0.001) negatively affected somatic symptoms. The network social connectedness and the real-life social connectedness played a chain mediating role in the development of Internet gaming disorder to somatic symptoms [95%CI: 0.073, 0.088], explaining 45.25% of the total effect value. The difference of real-life social connectedness and network social connectedness played a partial mediating role between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms [95% CI:0.050, 0.062], accounting for 31.28% of the total effect value. Conclusions Real-life social connectedness, network social connectedness, and their disparity all mediated the relationship between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. Real-life social connectedness acted as a protective factor, while network social connectedness served as a risk factor. Encouraging offline activities and guiding teenagers to use the internet responsibly may help prevent and reduce physical symptoms linked to Internet gaming disorder. According to the latest report from the China Internet Network Information Center [ 1 ], as of December 2021, China’s internet user base reached 1.032 billion, with over 517.93 million netizens engaging in gaming activities, accounting for 52.4% of total internet users. Consequently, there has been a growing focus on Internet gaming disorder. Internet Gaming Disorder was proposed as a mental disorder in Section III of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [ 2 ], and gaming disorder has been acknowledged in the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) as a ‘disorder due to addictive behaviors‘ [ 3 ]. IGD is characterized by a persistent and recurrent pattern of internet gaming involvement despite physical or psychological impairment [ 4 ]. The pooled prevalence of Internet gaming disorder among global adolescents is approximately 8.8% [ 5 ]. Internet gaming disorder can lead to various adverse effects on adolescents; one common consequence being somatic symptoms such as headaches, chest pain, and sleep disorders which significantly impact daily social functioning and may cause considerable mental distress accompanied by heightened psychological stress [ 6 ]. Giuliana Morabito et al. [ 7 ] found that somatic symptoms often coexist with several mental health issues including anxiety disorders, depressive disorders among adolescents. Furthermore, somatic symptoms contribute to substantial personal, health system, and societal costs. In a population-based cohort study conducted in Ontario, young individuals hospitalized due to somatic symptoms incurred per capita costs ranging from $1494-$1843 which were16-20 times higher than their peers without such symptoms aged between five to twenty-four years old [ 8 ]. Understanding the mechanism of Internet gaming disorder affecting somatic symptoms and early intervention may be one of the effective ways to prevent somatic symptoms. Previous studies have demonstrated a reciprocal relationship between Internet gaming disorder and social connectedness, both of which influence somatic symptoms [ 9 , 10 ]. However, the specific role of social connectedness in the process by which Internet gaming disorder affects somatic symptoms remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the interplay among Internet gaming disorder, social connectedness, and somatic symptoms in order to provide adolescents with better prevention strategies and coping mechanisms for managing somatic symptoms. Background Relationship between internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms While moderate online games can provide stress relief, pleasure, and improve visual-spatial ability and motor skills [ 11 ], recent trends indicate that the nature of online gaming has shifted from entertainment to a potential risk [ 12 ]. Excessive engagement in online gaming can have detrimental effects on the physical, mental, and social well-being of adolescents, manifesting as anxiety disorders, musculoskeletal discomfort (e.g., wrist pain), social phobia, interpersonal challenges and so on [ 13 , 14 ]. Previous studies have shown a significant association between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms such as headaches, chest pain, sleep disturbances [ 15 ]. A one-year longitudinal cohort study of 1838 adolescents found that Internet gaming disorder was significantly associated with baseline somatic symptoms (dry eye symptoms, pain) and significantly increased the risk of new onset of these symptoms during the follow-up period [ 9 ]. The concept and impact of social connectedness Social connectedness refers to subjective perceptions of belonging or intimacy with others or social groups formed on the basis of close relationships like parents/family/friends/peers or distant relationships like strangers/society with surrounding world [ 16 ]. When individuals experience a high level of social connectedness, they tend to feel very close to others and perceive others as friendly and approachable, as well as actively participate in social activities and integrate into social groups [ 17 ]. However, when individuals lack social connectedness, they will show a variety of adverse reactions, and even behavioral or health problems, and this is reflected in higher rates of depression and anxiety, increased aggressive behavior, and increased loneliness and somatic symptoms [ 18 ]. With the development of Internet technology, the form of social connectedness has been expanded, including not only real-life social connectedness, but also network social connectedness [ 19 , 20 ]. In contrast to real-life social connectedness, which is established through face-to-face communication, network social connectedness is acquired through virtual interactions. Given the disparities in media and characteristics between network and real-life connectedness, it is imperative to ascertain whether they serve the same purpose; however, current evidence remains insufficient. Research has demonstrated that digital communication technologies such as the Internet can transcend temporal and spatial limitations while fostering social connectedness [ 21 ]. Nevertheless, Daniel Wheatley et al. discovered that excessive use of social networking platforms (more than 4 h per day) can significantly impact health despite its association with network social connectedness [ 22 ]. Relationship between internet gaming disorder, somatic symptoms, and social connectedness The relationship between Internet gaming disorder, somatic symptoms and social connectedness has been a focal point of scholarly research. Excessive engagement in virtual game worlds may result in a weakening of social connectedness [ 23 ]. In a study conducted by Maurice M. Ohayon et al., involving 2,984 US students, it was found that individuals with Internet gaming disorder had a smaller social circle, indicating a bidirectional relationship between increased Internet use and limited social environment [ 15 ]. The Social Convoy Model (SCM) posits that society, individuals, and the bonds formed with friends, relatives, and acquaintances can serve as defenders throughout different stages of life to provide assistance and support for overcoming difficulties and pressures while promoting physical and mental well-being [ 24 ]. Nevertheless, inadequate social connectedness frequently engenders feelings of loneliness in individuals. A qualitative study delved into the loneliness experienced by older adults during the COVID-19 outbreak, uncovering those participants devoid of social connectedness reported heightened levels of self-perceived loneliness [ 25 ]. And the Evolutionary Theory of Loneliness (ETL) predicts that loneliness will have long-term implications for both somatic and mental health, including sleep disturbances, physiological and immune function decline, as well as anxiety and depression [ 26 ]. In summary, Internet gaming disorder, somatic symptoms, and social connectedness are pairwise correlated [ 9 , 15 , 18 ], suggesting a potential intricate relationship among them; however, the evidence supporting their interrelationship remains insufficient. McLoughlin et al. [ 27 ] found that social connectedness can act as a protective buffer against the negative psychological consequences associated with online victimization, and can also effectively reduce the risk of suicide among adolescents. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that social connectedness can influence the impact of Internet gaming disorder on somatic symptoms. Additionally, we included a new variable - the difference between real-life social connectedness and network social connectedness (RSC-NSC), in order to explore potential disparities in their respective roles. We propose the following hypotheses: (1) Network social connectedness will play a mediating role between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms; (2) Real-life social connectedness mediating the influence of Internet gaming disorder on somatic symptoms; (3) Network social connectedness and real-life social connectedness will may have a chain mediating effect between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms; (4) There are differences between network social connectedness and real-life social connectedness among adolescents, and the difference will play a mediating role in the association between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. Methods Study design and participants This is a large sample cross-sectional study spanning the northern, southern, western, and eastern regions of China. The study was carried out from September 2022 to March 2023. A multistage stratified cluster sampling method was used in this study. Specifically, we selected one representative province from each of the four regions of China: Fujian, Sichuan, Jiangsu and Xinjiang. In each province, two schools were randomly chosen—one Junior high school and one senior high school. Subsequently, one class from each grade within these schools was randomly selected, and all students in these classes were surveyed. Individuals met the following inclusion criteria were eligible to participate: (1) adolescents aged 11–19 years who were in school at the time of the study; (2) Voluntary participation in this study and signed informed consent with parents. Exclusion criteria included: (a) verbal communication disorders, and (b) intellectual disabilities. Questionnaires were deemed invalid if completed within 10 min; if respondents indicated “completely untrue” or “untrue” when asked about the survey’s reflection of actual circumstances; or if important basic information was missing from the questionnaire. We fully explained the purpose of the study and obtained authorization and cooperation from all selected schools, and informed consent forms were issued to all adolescents and their guardians to participate in the survey. The questionnaire was issued to the students only after they and their guardians signed informed consent at the same time. The questionnaire was distributed through the online survey platform “Questionnaire Star” ( https://www.wjx.cn/app/survey.aspx ). All the enrolled students were given one class time to complete the questionnaire in the computer classroom of their school, ensuring that everyone had enough time and a relatively quiet environment to participate. According to the Kendall criterion, the number of samples is 5 ~ 10 times the number of items [ 28 ] (70 items), and 20% invalid questionnaires are considered. The sample size should be at least 420. Finally, A total of 12,231 questionnaires were collected, 9,616 valid questionnaires were recovered, resulting in a valid recovery proportion of 78.62%. Measures We used STROBE Checklist for more rigorous study design and improved article quality. Demographic variables Demographic variables included age, gender, dominant hand, residence, family economic situation, parents’ occupation and parents’ education, etc. Nine-item internet gaming disorder scale–short form (IGDS9-SF) The 9-item Internet Gaming Disorder Short Form Scale (IGDS9-SF), developed by Pontes and Griffiths [ 29 ], is a brief psychometric instrument based on the 9 core criteria proposed by DSM-5 to define Internet gaming disorder. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = “never” to 5 = “often”) with total scores ranging from 9 to 45, with higher scores indicating a more disordered game. According to previous studies, “the optimal cutoff score of IGDS9-SF is 32 for the positive diagnosis of Internet gaming disorder in a Chinese context” [ 30 ]. IGDS9-SF has been widely used to assess the symptoms and prevalence of Internet gaming disorder in the general population, and the Chinese version has been verified to have good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.91 [ 30 ]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of IGDS9-SF was 0.932. Social connectedness scale-revised (SCS-R) The Social Connectedness Scale-Revised (SCS-R), developed by Lee RM et al. [ 31 ], was used to assess the social connectedness of the participants. It measures experiences of intimacy in interpersonal contexts and difficulties in establishing and maintaining a sense of intimacy. Ten negatively worded items and the remaining 10 positively worded items are included on the scale. The negatively worded items are reversely scored, so a higher score of each item suggests a greater level of social connectedness and vice versa. The SCS-R can reach a total score comprised between 20 and 120 by using a six-point rating scale with 20 items. Participants were asked to choose the degree to which they agreed or disagreed with a statement on a scale of 1–6, on which 1 indicates “strongly disagree” and 6 indicates “strongly agree”. In this study, the degree of social connectedness in the Internet or in the real-life was investigated by adding “in the online world” or “in the real world” to each item, the Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.949 and 0.951, respectively. The discrepancy between real and online social connectedness was quantified as the difference between the scores of real social connectedness and online social connectedness (RSC-NSC). Patient health questionnaire physical symptoms (PHQ-13) We utilized the PHQ-13, a somatic symptom assessment tool specifically designed for children and adolescents, as referenced in Part III of the DSM-5-TR by the American Psychological Association (APA). This tool is adapted from the PHQ-15, excluding two adult items related to menstruation and sexual intercourse, and has been widely adopted [ 32 , 33 ]. The PHQ-13 questionnaire measures somatic symptoms over the past seven days, encompassing 13 items, each rated on a scale of 0 (not at all bothered), 1 (somewhat bothered), or 2 (bothered a lot). The total raw score is multiplied by 15 and the resulting value is divided by 13 to obtain a proportionally allocated total score [ 34 ]. The severity of somatic symptoms is classified as minimal (0–4), low (5–9), moderate (10–14), and high (15–30) [ 35 ]. We defined subjects with somatic symptoms as those who reported being “somewhat bothered” or “bothered a lot” on the PHQ-13 scale, with higher scores indicating more severe somatic symptoms. The validity of the Chinese version of the PHQ-13 has been verified, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.86 [ 36 ]. In our study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the PHQ-13 was 0.904. Statistical analysis Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0. The Mean and standard deviation (SD) and proportion were used for statistical description. Pearson correlation was used to analyze the association between Internet gaming disorder, social connectedness and somatic symptoms. Process V3.5 in SPSS (IBM. V26.0) was used to analyze the mediating effect of network social connectedness and real-life social connectedness on Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms of adolescents. The bootstrap method was used to estimate the 95% confidence interval with 5,000 repeated sampling, and Two-sided inspection level α = 0.05. Results Demographic characteristics Among 9616 participants, 48.47% were men and 51.53% were women in this study, with a mean age of 14.88 ± 1.67 years (range 11.00 to 19.00). The other demographic characteristics of adolescents are shown in Table 1 . Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis The study found that the total score of Internet gaming disorder was 14.44 ± 6.45 (range 9.00 to 45.00)., with approximately 2.04% ( n = 197) of adolescents being afflicted by this condition. The total scores for network social connectedness and real-life social connectedness were found to be 77.66 ± 11.918 (range 25.00 to 120.00) and 84.89 ± 16.10 (range 20.00 to 120.00), respectively. Furthermore, the score of somatic symptoms was 3.46 [0.00, 8.08] (range 0.00 to 30.00), and a significant proportion (77.14%) of adolescents reported experiencing at least one somatic symptom, while 9.36% experienced severe distress due to somatic symptoms. Prevalent symptoms reported by adolescents included arm, leg or joint pain, headache, fatigue or low energy levels, and sleep difficulties (Table 2 ). The correlation analysis results revealed a positive association between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. Additionally, both Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms exhibited significant negative correlations with network social connectedness, real-life social connectedness, and RSC-NSC (Table 3 ). Mediating effect of social connectedness in the relationship between internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms Based on the chain mediation model, direct effect [95%CI: 0.081, 0.116] and the total indirect effect [95%CI: 0.073, 0.088] were significant ( P &lt; 0.001). The indirect effects of Internet gaming disorder on somatic symptoms via network social connectedness, real-life social connectedness, and both were-0.003 [95%CI: -0.004, -0.001], 0.077 [95%CI: 0.069, 0.084],0,006 [95%CI: 0.004, 0.009]. The mediation effect accounted for 45.25% of the total effect (Table 4 ), the final path model was shown in Fig. 1 . Furthermore, Internet gaming disorder negatively predicted RSC-NSC (β = -0.703, P &lt; 0.001), and RSC-NSC negatively predicted somatic symptoms (β = -0.079, P &lt; 0.001), indicating that RSC-NSC played a partial mediating role between IGD and somatic symptoms [95% CI:0.050,0.062], with an effect share of 31.28% (Table 5 ; Fig. 2 ). Discussion This study, which surveyed 9,616 adolescents from four provinces in China, showed that the prevalence of Internet gaming disorder among Chinese adolescents was about 2.04%, lower than other previous reports [ 37 , 38 ]. This phenomenon could be attributed to the regional distribution of samples and the selection of research instruments. Yuan-Xia Gao et al. observed in a meta-analysis that the prevalence of Internet gaming disorder varied between 0.4% and 41.2% across different countries and regions worldwide, and elevated prevalence rates were reported when utilizing DSM-5 and Short Version of the Game Addiction Scale for Adolescents (GAS-7) criteria, likely attributed to differences in diagnostic thresholds [ 5 ]. King et al. highlighted in their systematic review that among the 32 tools employed for measuring Internet gaming disorder, none exhibited significant superiority over others; however, the IGDS9-SF utilized in our study possesses more robust empirical support for its psychometric properties [ 39 ]. Nevertheless, it is imperative to note that the scale serves solely as a screening instrument and lacks diagnostic validity; thus, these figures should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, our findings indicated that a substantial proportion (about 77.14%) of the participating adolescents reported experiencing at least one somatic symptom, with around 9.36% reporting severe somatic symptoms. Notably prevalent self-reported somatic symptoms included arm, leg or joint pain, headache, fatigue or low energy levels, and sleep difficulties. In the short term, these somatic symptoms can detrimentally impact academic performance and social functioning among adolescents; moreover they have long-term implications for their physical and mental well-being [ 40 ]. A community-based longitudinal follow-up investigation demonstrated that non-depressed adolescents who reported experiencing at least one somatic symptom were more inclined to seek mental health care at a hospital subsequently [ 41 ]. Moreover, our study identified a robust association between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. This aligns with the findings of Niko Männikkö et al. [ 42 ], who discovered a correlation between problematic gaming behavior and psychological as well as health-related issues, including fatigue, sleep disturbances, depression, and anxiety symptoms. Therefore, greater emphasis should be placed on somatic symptoms in adolescents, particularly those with Internet gaming disorder, necessitating targeted interventions for early detection and intervention. Based on the literature review, our study is the first to address the relationship among network social connectedness, real-life social connectedness, Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. An important finding of this study is that network social connectedness and real-life social connectedness play a chain mediating role between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms, which is the main theoretical contribution of this study. Firstly, network social connectedness partially mediates the relationship between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms, supporting the hypothesis 1. Adolescents exhibiting higher levels of Internet gaming disorder tend to experience lower levels of network social connectedness. Different from the negative relationship between network social connectedness and somatic symptoms in the correlation analysis, our mediation model reveals that lower levels of network social connectedness were associated with lower levels of somatic symptoms. This suggests that within the context of Internet gaming disorder and its impact on somatic symptoms, network social connectedness serves as a risk factor. Previous studies have found that if the underlying purpose of social media use is friendship and connection, then network social connectedness plays the same role as real-life social connectedness, such as alleviating anxiety, depression, and improving well-being [ 43 ]. However, our study uncovers an opposing role for network social connectedness among adolescents excessively addicted to online games. The World Health Organization stresses that increased screen time replaces healthy behaviors and habits such as physical activity and sleep habits, and leads to potentially harmful effects such as reduced sleep or circadian reversal, headaches, neck pain [ 44 , 45 ]. Online socializing further increases screen time in adolescents with Internet gaming disorder, which may explain why network social connectedness plays a risk factor between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. Therefore, to prevent and alleviate somatic symptoms in adolescents, it is important to appropriately regulate their internet usage while paying attention to the types of online social interactions they engage in. Encouraging beneficial online media use for social interaction among adolescents is also recommended. Secondly, hypothesis 2 was supported. Real-life social connectedness serves as a partial mediator in the relationship between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms, accounting for 43.02% of the total effect, thereby indicating the dominance of this pathway in the chain mediation model. Heightened levels of Internet Gaming Disorder were found to be inversely correlated with real-world social connections, while diminished real-world social connections were associated with increased somatic symptoms. Excessive engagement in gaming contributes to a diminished sense of connection with reality [ 46 ]. The cognitive perception of oneself as independent and disconnected from others is associated with detrimental implications for health and well-being [ 47 ]. Conversely, a relational framework of belonging and interconnectedness with others, extending beyond personal intimate relationships to encompass broader levels of community and society, appears to be protective and a crucial element of health and well-being [ 48 , 49 ]. Social connectedness in real life can be used as a protective buffer factor for the response to somatic symptoms caused by Internet gaming disorder. It is imperative for educational institutions, families, and society to actively promote and engage adolescents in offline activities such as sports events, regular family gatherings, community initiatives, etc., aiming to facilitate their increased interaction with the real world and foster a stronger sense of belonging and connectedness within society. Finally, there existed another path (IGD →NSC→ RSC → somatic symptoms) (Path 3), explaining 3.35% of the total effect and providing empirical support for hypothesis 3. On the basis of path 1, the effect value of path 3 changes from negative to positive after adding a variable (real-life social connectedness), thereby further elucidating the beneficial role played by real-life social connectedness. Additionally, this study revealed that network social connectedness positively predicts real-life social connectedness, indicating that online social networking may extend into offline interactions and enhance individuals’ sense of belonging in their physical communities. A study found that sharing experiences of physical activity online during COVID-19 isolation can build new relationships and enhance social connectedness [ 50 ]. In summary, it is not only necessary prioritize individuals’ sense of real-life social connectedness but also to assist adolescents in cultivating healthy internet usage habits and promoting safe and amicable online interactions as means to expand their interpersonal networks and augment their connections within offline society. In our study, we found that the real-life social connectedness score of Chinese adolescents surpasses that of network social connectedness, indicating the prevailing influence of real-life interactions on social connectedness. We further analyzed the role of RSC-NSC in Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. The results showed that the difference of real-life social connectedness and network social connectedness played a partial mediating role between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms, and RSC-NSC can buffer the somatic symptoms caused by Internet gaming disorder, supporting the hypothesis 4. A study exploring why regular Twitter users take breaks from the site found that most individuals’ motivations for abstinence periods are due to concerns over the disconnect between internet and real-life [ 51 , 52 ], demonstrating that individuals perceive real-life social connectedness as more important than network social connectedness. A systematic review emphasized that while Internet technology may provide more opportunities for adolescents to seek connectedness, social networks and skills based on real-life, rather than virtual, are still a necessary foundation for them to use technology in beneficial ways [ 53 ]. Therefore, paying more attention to adolescents’ real-life social connectedness may be the main way to increase their sense of social connectedness. Limitation This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, due to the cross-sectional design employed, it is not possible to establish a causal relationship between the variables under investigation. Future research should consider employing longitudinal cohort studies to further explore the associations among Internet gaming disorder, social connectedness, and somatic symptoms. Secondly, reliance on self-report measures in this study may introduce potential biases as adolescents might intentionally conceal information due to fear of stigmatization or provide subjective evaluations. To mitigate these biases, we included a question in the questionnaire asking participants about the extent to which they believed the results reflected their actual experiences. Finally, although our sample encompassed regions across northern, southern, western and eastern China; its distribution was uneven with over 70% of participants from Xinjiang region. Consequently, caution must be exercised when generalizing our findings nationally and future studies are encouraged to include larger samples from other provinces for improved representativeness. Conclusion The level of real-life social connectedness of Chinese adolescents is higher than that of network social connectedness, and real-life social connectedness dominants social connectedness. Real-life social connectedness, network social connectedness and the difference between them all play mediating roles between Internet gaming disorder and somatic symptoms. Placing greater emphasis on the connection between teenagers and real-life, actively promoting and encouraging offline activities for teenagers, and guiding them to make appropriate and reasonable use of the internet for social engagement may prove effective in preventing and alleviating somatic symptoms associated with Internet gaming disorder. Relevant intervention studies can be conducted among adolescents with Internet gaming disorder to facilitate their physical and mental well-being.
