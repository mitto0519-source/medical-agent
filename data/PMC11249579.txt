According to data from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), South Korea ranked first in terms of deaths by suicide in 2020, with 24.1 deaths per 100,000 individuals. 1 This has been the case since 2003, with the sole exceptions of 2016 and 2017. In 2022, according to recent statistics on causes of death released by Statistics Korea, suicide was reported as the most common cause of death among individuals aged 10-19 years, with 7.2 per 100,000 individuals, accounting for 42.3% of deaths. 2 Although the youth suicide rate is around the OECD average (around six per 100,000), it is noteworthy that it is gradually increasing annually. As the number of patients with suicidal thoughts and behaviors increases, clinicians who are not necessarily psychiatrists (even child and adolescent psychiatrists) or mental health professionals are increasingly likely to encounter these patients and need to be prepared. Antidepressant medications are an essential part of a clinician’s arsenal when caring for children or adolescents with suicidal thoughts or behaviors. Clinicians who are unfamiliar with antidepressant usage or who are new to working with patients with suicidal tendencies should be familiar with their side effects and management before prescribing them. In particular, I would like to highlight the most important and potentially fatal side effect associated with antidepressant use, namely increased suicidality. When managing depression in children and adolescents, caution is recommended when initiating treatment using these medications. According to the 2015 NICE guidelines, antidepressants should not be prescribed as the initial treatment for mild depression in children. Furthermore, a four-week monitoring period and 2-3-month period of psychotherapeutic intervention should be considered before initiating treatment with antidepressants, unless there is suicidal ideation or significant coexisting problems. 3 Even for patients with moderate-to-severe depression, antidepressants are not always the first point of call and should not be recommended immediately. Antidepressants should not be prescribed alone without psychotherapy. Medication can be used in situations where psychotherapy is unavailable. However, the prescribing physician should closely monitor changes in the child regularly, as many of the observations made in psychotherapy sessions are not well understood. After a multidisciplinary review, if the child’s depression does not respond to psychotherapy due to family discord, parental psychiatric disorder, or other coexisting factors, other medical alternatives should be considered, as well as adjunctive psychotherapies for the family or parents. If the child does not respond to a particular psychotherapy after four to six sessions, only then should fluoxetine be offered in children aged 12–18 years, which should be considered with caution in children aged 5–11 years. However, evidence for its effectiveness in the latter age group is not well established. Fluoxetine is an off-label indication for children aged &lt; 8 years. In other words, antidepressant usage in children and adolescents should be limited to moderate-to-severe depression when psychotherapeutic interventions are not feasible and when other non-drug interventions have failed. The rigor of using antidepressants in children and adolescents is evidenced by the fact that while more than a dozen antidepressants are currently in development, only a few have been approved by the Food and Drug Association (FDA) in the US for use in children and adolescents. 4 Although the FDA has approved some antidepressants for obsessive compulsive disorder (sertraline for ages 6 and older, fluoxetine for ages 7 and older, fluvoxamine for ages 8 and older, and clomipramine for ages 10 and older) and generalized anxiety disorder (duloxetine for ages 7 and older), only two selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) have been approved for pediatric depression (fluoxetine for ages 8 and older and escitalopram for ages 12 and older). The Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety also warns that “safety and efficacy have not been established in children and adolescents, and it is not recommended to administer this drug to children and adolescents.” Additionally, in short-term studies of children, adolescents, and young adults (aged 18–24 years) with major depression or other psychiatric disorders, antidepressants reportedly increase the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (e.g. suicidal ideation) compared to placebos. To understand this stringent warning regarding antidepressant use among the pediatric population, it is important to first explain the black box warning (BBW) and its context. BBW, also called “boxed warnings,” are the most stringent warning that the FDA can label medication. Only the FDA has the discretion to issue black box warnings, which started in the 1970s. Their name comes from the black line that borders the warning, calling attention to severe adverse side effects, while not being entirely contradictory. Imipramine was the first antidepressant to be approved in 1959 by the FDA for major depressive disorder (MDD) treatment, which established the class of drugs called tricyclic antidepressants. In 1974, the first report on the SSRI fluoxetine was published. Fluoxetine was approved by the FDA in December 1987 and was launched into the market in January 1988 under the trade name Prozac. Issues with BBWs were raised, but were shelved due to the argument that it should only be contraindicated in clinical situations where the risks of drug use clearly outweigh the possible therapeutic benefits. In 1990, Teicher et al. 5 described the first series of six case reports of adult patients taking fluoxetine who developed intense suicidal ideation; however, this was not the first published literature regarding antidepressants and suicidal ideation. The case reports of fluoxetine causing suicidal ideation outlined by Teicher et al. 5 gained attention from the FDA and the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), which ultimately led its manufacturer, Eli Lilly and Company, to reanalyze data from randomized controlled trials on fluoxetine. In 1991, the FDA reported no clear association between fluoxetine and the emergence of suicidality based on pooled data. 6 In May 2003, GlaxoSmithKline reported issues concerning suicidality in pediatric patients taking a drug they had manufactured, paroxetine, to the UK and US regulatory agencies after a reanalysis of previous data. In June 2003, the UK Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) released a statement concerning the use of paroxetine in children and adolescents that explicitly stated that it should not be used to treat depression in individuals younger than 18 years, along with a mandated labeling change which made its use contraindicated in pediatric patients with MDD. While there is some controversy regarding antidepressant-induced suicidality in the pediatric population, it is important to note that the FDA has not placed a contraindication for the use of antidepressants in children and adolescents. Instead, they updated the labeling to warn of suicide risk and encourage prescribers to balance their clinical needs with risk. The FDA recognizes that untreated childhood depression or psychosis can have serious consequences. It is also important to note that no completed suicides were recorded in theses clinical trials. On the one hand, the participants were receiving regular clinical attention and active suicidality was excluded in most studies. Therefore, it may not be appropriate to generalize the results of incomplete suicides to real-world patients. In October 2004, after the FDA black box warning on antidepressants, the New England Journal of Medicine published opinions on both sides of the issue. Epidemiologists and pediatricians who cited the treatment for adolescents with depression study argued that the improvement in symptoms was not significant and that it did more harm than good. 7 Child and adolescent psychiatrists argued that the suicide rate has increased back to where it was before SSRIs were developed. 8 Moreover, the argument that BBWs are more harmful than beneficial is supported by the finding that SSRI prescriptions have decreased and suicide rates have increased in the US; a similar trend has been observed in the Netherlands. 9 However, an argument could also be made that BBWs are more beneficial than detrimental, given reports of a slight decline in prescriptions after BBWs but a significant decrease in suicide rates. 10 The number of antidepressant prescriptions has increased worldwide. This is true for both children and adolescents populations. 11 Many of these prescriptions are for off-label use (off-label, off-license prescribing of licensed drugs occurs when the condition is being treated, and patient demographics (primarily age), route of administration, and duration do not match). 12 A study in Denmark found that 51.1% of the SSRIs were prescribed off-label. 13 The data from South Korea were not significantly different in terms of the proportion of off-label prescribing. 14 In conclusion, the controversy regarding antidepressant-induced suicidality is ongoing and valid. 15 , 16 As suicide in children and adolescents is a publicly sensitive issue, the importance of timely access to treatment in a multidisciplinary setting should be emphasized, as well as the need for clear warnings of increased suicidal ideation and close monitoring when antidepressants are used in children and adolescents by all related medical professionals. At present, no guidelines exist for depression in children and adolescents in Korea, and there are only expert consensus-level drug selection algorithms. 17 Despite this, it is clear that systematic guidelines tailored to the Korean healthcare system are needed in the future. In addition, there is also a need to analyze the relationship between antidepressant prescriptions and suicide using national health insurance data or cause-of-death data.
