Abstract Background The General Aggression Model offers the theoretical basis for this study, which evaluates connections among Dark Traits, Social Anxiety, School Climate, and Cyberbullying. This analysis centers on Chinese vocational school students, a demographic infrequently studied as an integrated system. This research aims to clarify the mediating role of social anxiety in the relationship between dark personality and cyberbullying, as well as the moderating role of school climate. It is expected that the study conclusions can offer insights for preventing student cyberbullying. Methods Data were collected from 1,826 students across six vocational colleges. Participants completed validated scales for Dark Traits, Social Anxiety, School Climate, and Cyberbullying. Hypotheses were tested with structural equation modeling in MPLUS, and model fit was assessed through CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. Results A significant positive correlation was identified between dark traits and cyberbullying behaviors. Social anxiety had a significant mediating effect in the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying behaviors. In addition, perceived school climate moderated the direct path as well as the mediation process between dark traits and cyberbullying behaviors. Conclusions These findings suggest that dark traits contribute to higher levels of social anxiety in Chinese vocational school students, which increases their likelihood of cyberbullying. Moreover, a positive perception of the school climate can facilitate the reduction of social anxiety and lower the incidence of cyberbullying. Introduction Adolescents represent the future of the nation, and the effect of the internet on their lives has drawn considerable scrutiny. According to the Chinese 2021 National Research Report on Internet Usage Among Minors indicates that 38.3% of adolescents have encountered harmful online content [ 1 ], 16.6% have been subjected to online ridicule or insults, 7.0% have been targets or witnessed their friends targeted by online harassment, and 6.1% have had their personal information exposed without their permission. Additional surveys demonstrate the widespread nature of cyberbullying among Chinese adolescents; these studies exhibit 58.7% reporting themselves as victims, whereas 34.8% acknowledge perpetrating it [ 2 ]. More alarmingly, internet use is starting at increasingly younger ages, with over a third of students online before they even begin school, a number that continues to grow annually. In China, vocational school students represent a large demographic, including junior and senior high school graduates, the majority of whom are minors [ 3 ]. In general, these students in China are on average academically low-performing and are often those whose entrance exam grades are too low for enrollment at academic high schools [ 4 , 5 ]. They frequently encounter social stigma, reduced self-esteem, and a higher susceptibility to negative influences due to labels such as “poor academic performance” and “low overall quality”, which can cultivate the development of detrimental values and behaviors [ 6 ]. Their varied educational histories contribute to their placement in an especially vulnerable situation [ 7 ]. Moreover, these students frequently do not receive adequate parental guidance regarding interpersonal relationships and future planning [ 8 ]. The risk of cyberbullying in this group can be amplified by factors including insufficient parental oversight and deficient parent-child communication [ 9 – 11 ]. Therefore, understanding the development and underlying processes of cyberbullying among Chinese vocational school students is crucial for protecting their well-being. Dark Traits and Cyberbullying Behavior The General Aggression Model hypothesizes that aggressive behavior arises from an interaction of personal and situational factors [ 12 ]. Specifically, individual factors such as personality traits and attitudes, coupled with environmental factors such as provocative or hostile stimuli, can induce negative affective states that may lead to aggression [ 13 ]. Dark traits comprise three overlapping yet separable characteristics—Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and narcissism—unified by a common thread of callousness and disregard for ethical principles [ 14 ]. Research indicates a strong relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying, indicating that individuals with higher levels of dark traits are more prone to cyberbullying perpetration [ 15 ]. Studies focusing on adolescents additionally indicate dark traits as strong predictors of cyberbullying; in these, Machiavellianism demonstrates the most significant predictive capability, followed by psychopathy, and then narcissism [ 16 , 17 ]. Therefore, we propose that the dark traits of Chines vocational school students could positively associated with cyberbullying behavior. Mediating Role of Social Anxiety Social anxiety describes the feelings of insecurity, worry, and fear that people experience in novel situations [ 18 ]. A reluctance to engage in direct conversations and a fear of others’ judgment constitute its key features. In the relevant field, the relationship between personality and interpersonal communication has long been a significant topic of concern for scholars. Previous research has shown that vulnerable narcissists are particularly sensitive to what others think of them, and may exhibit a hostile attribution bias. This discrepancy may give rise to strained dynamics and conflicts in interpersonal relationships, thereby undermining relational quality [ 19 ]. A longitudinal data set al.so indicates the influence of personality traits on interpersonal relationships, which showed that individuals with low levels of Neuroticism and high levels of Agreeableness and Conscientiousness tend to have strong interpersonal identities and are better able to maintain interpersonal relationships [ 20 ]. Individuals exhibiting dark traits, represented by negative characteristics such as manipulation and low agreeableness, are often with hostility toward others or avoiding others, and tend to have troubled interpersonal relationships [ 21 , 22 ]. Thus, they often find it challenging to build positive peer connections [ 23 ]. Weak peer relationships can undermine a person’s sense of security, rendering trust challenging and potentially causing them to withdraw from social interaction entirely. This absence of peer support can contribute to the onset of social anxiety [ 24 , 25 ]. Moreover, social anxiety in adolescents is a strong predictor of aggressive behavior. Individuals unable to develop healthy peer relationships, or those facing other stressors, may feel more susceptible, resulting in reduced self-regulation and increased social anxiety, possibly resulting in aggression [ 26 , 27 ]. The General Aggression Model hypothesizes certain traits predispose individuals to high levels of aggression [ 12 ]. For example, a type of high self-esteem produces high aggression, individuals with inflated or narcissists are prone to anger and are highly aggressive when their high self-image is threatened [ 28 ]. Those with dark traits are often egocentric, preoccupied with their image and self-interest. When unable to achieve their goals by manipulating others, their inflated self-perception may be damaged, provoking aggression [ 29 ]. In real life, traditional bullying is closely monitored by schools and society, making it increasingly difficult to perpetrate [ 30 ]. In comparison to the increased challenge of traditional bullying due to such scrutiny, the internet’ s anonymity nurtures an environment that facilitates aggressive behavior [ 31 ]. Online platforms allow individuals to express themselves without the typical constraints of physical presence or standard ethical norms. Therefore, cyberbullying is often simpler to carry out than traditional bullying. In this regard, we propose an indirect effect of social anxiety on the association of dark traits and cyberbullying behaviors, which we will test this study. Moderating Role of Perceived School Climate The General Aggression Model also hypothesizes that like the person factors, situational factors influence aggression by influencing cognition, affect, and arousal [ 12 ]. The existing literature highlights that perceived injustice was positively related to aggression behavior [ 32 ]. For students, schools represent a crucial ecological setting that significantly affects their development. As a fundamental characteristic of any school, the school climate comprises interactions among its members and their shared values; these dynamics, accordingly, influence everyone in the school community [ 33 ]. Research demonstrates a correlation connecting school climate with both internalizing and externalizing behavioral problems. Students perceiving a negative school climate generally report greater anxiety and depression [ 34 ]. In contrast, students who view their school climate positively are better able to lessen the effect of adverse experiences on their mental health, often exhibiting greater psychological well-being [ 35 ]. In addition, negative school climates often feature prevalent peer conflict, an environment which can potentially cultivate the misperception among students that bullying is a means to solve problems or advance self-interest, thus increasing aggressive behavior [ 36 ]. A positive school climate cultivates a supportive atmosphere that promotes student development, students in such environments are more inclined to participate in school activities and form strong interpersonal bonds [ 37 ]. Such an environment contributes to a lower probability of problematic internet use and also effectively limits cyberbullying [ 38 , 39 ]. The Present Research The literature review indicates that despite well-established evidence of an association between dark traits and cyberbullying, research addressing the interrelationships between dark traits, social anxiety, perceived school climate, and cyberbullying among Chinese vocational school students is lacking. Furthermore, some scholars have studied the relationships among attachment anxiety, dark personality, and cyberbullying [ 40 ], but the mediating role of social anxiety between dark traits and cyberbullying has not yet to be empirically tested in an integrated framework. Additionally, the moderating effect of perceived school climate on the association between dark traits and social anxiety as well as the associations between social anxiety and cyberbullying needs to be investigated. The conceptual framework of the present study is presented in Fig. 1 , and the major research hypotheses are developed from this framework: Hypothesis 1 Dark traits were positively associated with cyberbullying behavior among Chinese vocational school students. Hypothesis 2 Social anxiety mediates the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying. Hypothesis 3 Perceived school climate moderates the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying. Hypothesis 4 Perceived school climate moderates the relationship between social anxiety and cyberbullying. Research methods and data collection The research was conducted accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki for experiments involving humans. Participants were recruited through cluster sampling from six vocational schools across Anhui, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu provinces. Homeroom teachers acted as liaisons, first giving a brief, age-appropriate presentation to their classes that covered the study’s purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of response. No material incentives were offered. A total of 2,156 students initially completed the electronic questionnaire utilizing their mobile phones, with this survey occurring during a scheduled after-class activity period. According to several criteria, the questionnaires were removed from the analysis: completion times under 120 s, the presence of missing data from invalid answers, or inconsistencies in reverse-coded items. Participants After these exclusions, the final sample consisted of 1,826 valid questionnaires, which produced an effective response rate of 84.8%. The mean age of the students was 17.89 ( SD = 1.75) years, with an age range from 15 to 23 years. The sample comprised 946 male students (51.8%) and 880 female students (48.2%). In secondary vocational schools, students in grades 1, 2, and 3 account for 545 (29.8%), 388 (21.2%), and 156 (8.5%) respectively. And in higher vocational schools, students in grades 1, 2, and 3 account for 393 (21.6%), 292 (16.0%), and 52 (2.9%) respectively. In terms of internet usage time, 222 participants spent 0–2 h per day, 557 spent 2–4 h, 636 spent 4–6 h, 227 spent 6–8 h, and 184 spent more than 8 h. Measurements Dark traits The Chinese version of the Dirty Dozen scale was developed by Jonason and Webster and later revised by Geng [ 41 ], consists of 12 items on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree), which assess three dimensions: Machiavellianism, Narcissism and Psychopathy. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s coefficient α = 0.853, the subscale coefficients were Machiavellianism ( α = 0.880), narcissism ( α = 0.853), and psychopathy ( α = 0.769). Confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the scale demonstrated good overall model fit (CFI = 0.920, TLI = 0.897, RMSEA = 0.097, SRMR = 0.064). Cyberbullying behavior The Chinese version of the cyberbullying scale was developed by Erdur-Baker and Kavsut and revised by Chu [ 42 ], comprises 14 items on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (Never) to 4 (Always). The scale has a single dimension, and higher scores indicate more severe cyberbullying behaviors. In this study, the Cronbach’s coefficient α = 0.815. Confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the scale demonstrated good overall model fit (CFI = 0.900, TLI = 0.871, RMSEA = 0.077, SRMR = 0.049). Social Anxiety The Interaction Anxiety Scale was developed by Leary and later revised by Peng [ 43 ], consists of 15 items on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). The scale has a single dimension, with higher scores indicating greater levels of social anxiety. In this study, the Cronbach’s coefficient α = 0.932. Confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the scale demonstrated good overall model fit (CFI = 0.957, TLI = 0.946, RMSEA = 0.066, SRMR = 0.028). Perceived school climate The Delaware School Climate Survey-Student (DSCS-S) was created by Bear and revised by Xie [ 44 ]. This scale consists of 31 items on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 4 (Strongly Agree). Higher scores reflect a more positive perception of the school climate. Drawing on research by Xie [ 45 ], this study selected two representative dimensions from the components of “support and structure” as indicators of school climate: teacher-student relationships, peer relationships, fairness of rules, and clarity of expectations. The Cronbach’s coefficient α = 0.953, with the subscale coefficients were teacher-student relationships ( α = 0.898), peer relationships ( α = 0.897), fairness of rules ( α = 0.877), and clarity of expectations ( α = 0.806). Confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the scale demonstrated good overall model fit (CFI = 0.945, TLI = 0.936, RMSEA = 0.083, SRMR = 0.029). Data Analytical Strategies Although we implemented various strategies to mitigate common method bias during the distribution of the questionnaire, such as ensuring anonymous responses, employing reverse scoring for certain items, and incorporating lie-scale questions to control for this bias. The questionnaires were all completed by students, and so the study results could be subject to common method variance bias. Harman’s single-factor test indicated no significant common-method bias: the first factor accounted for 23.15% of the variance, which is less than the critical value of 40%, indicating that this study is not significantly affected by common method bias [ 46 ]. Model analyses proceeded in four main steps. (1) To verify the associations between the predictor and criterion variables were consistent with predictions, bivariate correlations were calculated. Among demographic variables, both gender and internet usage time correlated significantly with major variables. Age also demonstrated a significant correlation with major variables. Therefore, in the following analyses, this study controlled for gender, age, and internet usage time. Both gender and internet usage time were treated as dummy variables in the models. These initial analyses were performed utilizing SPSS 26.0. (2) Following the recommendations of Wu [ 47 ], cluster sampling was utilized to improve modeling efficiency and clarify the relationships among variables. The three dimensions of the Dark traits Scale were aggregated into three latent variable indicators utilizing internal consistency methods. Items from the Social Anxiety Scale and Cyberbullying Scale were grouped based on their factor loadings utilizing a “high-load method”, which organized high-load items and low-load items into three separate sets. We investigate the social anxiety as mediating variable between dark traits and cyberbullying behaviors. (3) To evaluate the moderating effect of perceived school climate, we employed the product indicator (PI) approach. In the first step, we created interaction terms utilizing a paired indicator approach, centering social anxiety and school climate by matching high-load indicators with each other and low-load indicators based on their factor loadings. In the second step, we constructed a structural equation model with dark traits as the independent variable, social anxiety as the mediator, cyberbullying as the dependent variable, and perceived school climate as the moderator. All the above primarily analyses were conducted with Mplus 8.3; (4) Simple slope analyses were undertaken based on the moderated mediation results to further understand how perceived school climate moderated the direct and indirect effects of dark traits on cyberbullying behaviors. Results Descriptive Statistics and Correlation Analysis Table 1 presents the mean scores participants achieved and the correlations identified between the analyzed variables. Significant correlations with each other were demonstrated by the main variables. From the correlational analyses previously presented, a rough foundation for verifying the research hypotheses was established. The Mediating Model First, the direct effect of dark traits on cyberbullying was analyzed. The resulting model demonstrated a good fit: χ² =36.363, df = 8, CFI = 0.992, TLI = 0.986, RMSEA = 0.044, SRMR = 0.024. The analysis demonstrated that dark traits were a significant positive predictor of cyberbullying ( β = 0.309, p &lt; 0.001). Next, incorporating social anxiety as a mediator, the model again exhibited a good fit: χ² =258.284, df = 45, CFI = 0.976, TLI = 0.967, RMSEA = 0.051, SRMR = 0.040. Social anxiety significantly affected cyberbullying ( β = 0.174, p &lt; 0.001), and dark traits significantly predicted social anxiety ( β = 0.294, p &lt; 0.001). The mediating effect, as evidenced by the 95% Bootstrap confidence interval of [0.024, 0.057], confirmed that social anxiety mediates the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying. Testing for the Moderated Mediation Model As presented in Fig. 2 , we controlled for gender, age, and internet usage time in this model. The hypothesized model demonstrated a good fit ( χ² / df = 5.480, CFI = 0.953, TLI = 0.944, SRMR = 0.069, RMSEA = 0.050). Our analysis indicated that dark traits significantly and positively predicted social anxiety ( β = 0.187, p &lt; 0.001), which significantly and positively predicted cyberbullying ( β = 0.187, p &lt; 0.001). The direct effect of dark traits on cyberbullying remained significant ( β = 0.099, p &lt; 0.001), indicating that social anxiety partially mediates the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying. Moreover, the interaction between dark traits and perceived school climate significantly and negatively predicted cyberbullying ( β =–0.204, p &lt; 0.001), suggesting that perceived school climate moderates the effect of dark traits on cyberbullying. Therefore, Hypothesis 3 is supported. Similarly, the interaction between social anxiety and perceived school climate also significantly and negatively predicted cyberbullying ( β =–0.167, p &lt; 0.001), indicating that perceived school climate moderates the relationship between social anxiety and cyberbullying. To further investigate the moderating effect of perceived school climate, we performed simple slope tests on two groups: those reporting a positive school climate (Z ≥ + 1SD) and those reporting a negative school climate (Z≤–1SD). This approach allowed us to appraise the effect of dark traits on cyberbullying across varying levels of perceived school climate (Fig. 3 ). Our findings indicated that in environments where students perceive a low-quality school climate, dark traits significantly and positively predict cyberbullying behavior ( β = 0.115, p &lt; 0.001); whereas, when students experience a high-quality school climate, dark traits exhibit a significant negative predictive relationship with cyberbullying ( β =–0.067, p &lt; 0.001). To further explore the moderating role of perceived school climate, simple slope analyses were conducted for two groups: those reporting a positive school climate (Z ≥ + 1SD) and those reporting a negative school climate (Z≤–1SD). This analysis evaluated the effect of social anxiety on cyberbullying in high and low levels of perceived school climate (Fig. 4 ). The results indicated that when students perceived a low level of school climate, social anxiety had a significant positive predictive effect on cyberbullying ( β = 0.140, p &lt; 0.001). In contrast, when students reported a high level of school climate, the predictive influence of social anxiety on cyberbullying was nonsignificant ( β =–0.023, p = 0.118). Discussion Previous studies have mostly focused on the relationship between dark personalities and cyberbullying among middle school students and college students, with a lack of research on Chinese vocational school students [ 48 , 49 ]. This study, taking vocational school students as the research subjects, enriches the research objects of cyberbullying. Thus, understanding the impact of dark traits on cyberbullying can effectively identify high-risk groups prone to committing cyberbullying and enable timely intervention. This is of utmost importance for the development of adolescents. On the other hand, the results of this study also confirm the validity of the General Aggression Model (GAM) in explaining cyberbullying behaviors. The General Aggression Model posits that personal factors and situational factors can induce aggressive behaviors [ 12 ]. On this basis, this study finds that in addition to the independent effects of personal and situational factors, interaction effects exist between them. Specifically, depending on different situational factors, there are two scenarios: reducing the occurrence of aggressive behaviors and increasing the occurrence of aggressive behaviors. The correlation between Dark traits and Cyberbullying First off, the research results indicate that Dark traits positively associated with Cyberbullying, which is consistent with existing research [ 50 ]. People with Machiavellian traits plan their harmful actions to avoid detection and maintain credibility, therefore, online platforms provide a place for these people to carry out cyberbullying [ 51 ]. For narcissistic people, who are characterized by boasting and being self-centered, cyberbullying is a way to achieve dominance over others and gain a sense of superiority [ 52 ]. Psychopathy is the most common antisocial personality trait. People with psychopathy lack empathy and are more likely to engage in cyberbullying behaviors [ 53 ]. The Mediating Role of Social Anxiety The findings in this study suggest that social anxiety partially mediates the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying. Dark traits are reflected by a belief in one’s own superiority, a tendency toward manipulation and control, impulsivity, and a craving for stimulation, often coupled with antisocial tendencies [ 54 ]. Individuals exhibiting high levels of dark traits are generally considered to have unfavorable characteristics. Research exploring the relationship between dark traits and interpersonal relationships has indicated that individuals with high levels of these traits demonstrate increased hostility and dominance in social exchanges, challenging the conventional understanding of submissiveness; these individuals are often considered disagreeable and tend to experience challenges in peer relations [ 55 , 56 ]. To avoid the challenges of their social realities, vocational school students may seek refuge in the concealed and public spaces of the internet to satisfy their psychological needs. By constructing an idealized online persona, they pursue both gratification and a sense of power over others [ 57 ]. Vocational school students are in a developmental period represented by growing autonomy and self-perception. However, their limited exposure and life experiences can exacerbate self-centeredness and emotional distance. This can render them less accepted among their peers, leading to heightened social anxiety [ 23 ]. Socially anxious individuals tend to have higher levels of loneliness and appear to prefer seeking online social interaction on social media to compensate for their lack of social support experienced in the real world [ 58 , 59 ]. But on internet platforms, the fear of negative evaluation among individuals with social anxiety persists, leading them to perceive online social interactions as risky and potentially exposing them to interpersonal threats and harm from others [ 60 ]. The fear of negative evaluation can trigger individuals’ fear of missing out and rumination, thereby deepening problematic internet use [ 61 ]. In a recent study have confirmed that there is a significant correlation between problematic internet use and cyberbullying [ 62 ]. Cyberbullying behaviors are facilitated as the internet offers a degree of anonymity, which reduces moral inhibitions and social accountability related to bullying [ 63 ]. The Moderating Role of Perceived School Climate This study also found that perceived school climate moderates the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying behavior. Research indicates that individuals with dark traits tend to have a negative perception of their school environment; those with higher levels of dark traits often perceive negative school climate [ 64 ]. When students encounter a negative school climate, they may experience more adverse situations, such as poor teacher-student relationships, unreasonable rules, and increased levels of bullying [ 65 ]. Specifically, negative interpersonal experiences brought about by uncontrollable and unstable environments are likely to impair their emotional security, while attempting to alleviate the discomfort caused by such negative emotions, adolescents are highly prone to engaging in antisocial behaviors [ 66 , 67 ]. At the same time, experiencing these negative events can shift individuals’ attitudes toward aggressive behavior, leading them to believe that their actions inflict little or no harm on others, finally cultivating poor behavioral habits [ 68 , 69 ]. In environments lacking cues—such as online spaces where individuals cannot see others’ real reactions—potential conflicts can trigger cyberbullying behavior. Conversely, a positive school climate buffers against these risks by addressing underlying psychological needs. For students in a positive school climate, they are inclined to adapt better socially and demonstrate more prosocial behaviors [ 70 ]. Additionally, these students are more likely to participate in school activities and express themselves in real-world settings, gaining positive attention and validation from others. This process reduces their sense of loneliness and, in turn, decreases antisocial behaviors accordingly [ 71 , 72 ]. This study also identified that perceived school climate moderates the relationship between social anxiety and cyberbullying. Research indicates that a positive school climate cultivates a “harmonious” and “friendly” environment, making students feel accepted and valued, which encourages interpersonal interactions among them [ 73 ]. Such interactions not only reduce social anxiety by providing real-world social support but also mitigate loneliness and fear of missing out by satisfying adolescents’ needs for belonging and increase school engagement [ 74 , 75 ]. Through these interactions, students can gain insight into others’ true attitudes toward them, helping to reshape their perceptions and reduce the effect of negative social labels, thereby alleviating social anxiety [ 76 ]. In contrast, within a negative school climate, teachers often fail to provide adequate attention and care for students, instead reprimanding and criticizing them frequently without considering their emotions. Such an environment exposes students to chronic pressure, thereby increasing social anxiety levels [ 77 ]. In such environments, students often turn to the internet as a means of escaping real-world pressures, with excessive social media use potentially exacerbating loneliness [ 78 ]. Heightened social anxiety makes them more sensitive to perceived threats in interpersonal interactions, thereby causing negative interpretations of social cues [ 79 ]. Negative information and online violence incidents can further trigger aggressive thoughts and anger, thereby increasing the likelihood of them expressing frustrations through insults or other aggressive online behaviors. Conclusion This study systematically examined the associations between dark traits, social anxiety, school climate, and cyberbullying among Chinese vocational school students. The findings indicate that dark traits are correlated with higher levels of social anxiety, which may be connected to cyberbullying behavior. Although causality cannot be inferred due to the study’s cross-sectional design, the results suggest that fostering a positive school climate in vocational schools could be associated with the development of healthy values, enhanced integration of students into school life and peer interactions, and potentially fewer instances of cyberbullying. Limitations and Future Directions This analysis focused on dark traits that are associated with cyberbullying behavior, while various limitations should be noted. First, the study employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish causality and does not provide insights into the temporal trends of dark traits, social anxiety, school climate, or cyberbullying behavior. Consequently, future research should consider adopting a longitudinal design to better elucidate the relationship between dark traits and cyberbullying behavior. Second, this study did not explore the influence of family socioeconomic status on cyberbullying behaviors, future studies may consider family socioeconomic status to explore broader contextual influences. Third, dark traits, social anxiety, and cyberbullying were measured utilizing self-report scales, which could involve biases, particularly social desirability bias, where respondents may conceal or misrepresent some information to present themselves positively in the survey. Future research could be enhanced through the integration of self-reports and peer reports and a combination of questionnaire studies and experimental studies.
