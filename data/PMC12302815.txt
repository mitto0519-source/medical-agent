For more information: https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/ Does Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) increase the risk of suicidality among children? This article critically examines a notable study by Shahnovsky et al. (2024) , which identifies hyperactivity as a significant predictor of suicide attempts. A detailed analysis of the study reveals three critical gaps: (1) oversight of key risk factors, such as ADHD medications and comorbid disorders – some of which were examined in a related study by the same authors, showing no significant association between ADHD and suicidality; (2) misinterpretation of prior research, inaccurately presenting ADHD medications as protective despite evidence indicating a significantly elevated suicide risk among users; and (3) misrepresentation of the study's findings, which largely suggested that ADHD was unrelated to suicide risk based on both parent and child reports and the two examined components of ADHD. Drawing on an extensive body of research, it is argued that depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation are more likely attributable to the first-line treatments for ADHD than to the diagnosis itself. By situating the study within the broader literature, this article cautions against potential real-life implications arising from its conclusions, such as clinicians prescribing ADHD medications to suppress the allegedly dangerous hyperactivity – medications that may paradoxically increase the risk of suicide. This critique is offered with the utmost respect for the authors’ lifelong dedication to advancing suicide prevention, with the sincere aim of fostering a constructive dialogue to deepen our understanding of the tragic phenomenon of childhood suicide. According to a recent study by Shahnovsky et al., published in the European Journal of Investigation in Health, Psychology and Education on September 24, 2024 (Shahnovsky, Apter, et al., 2024), the answer to this troubling question appears to be yes. The study, conducted among 71 children aged 7–12 (who were referred from emergency departments to a depression and suicide clinic between May 2018 and November 2021), aimed to explore the relationship between suicidality and the two core characteristics of ADHD: inattention and hyperactivity. The children completed the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (CSSRS) and the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), while their parents provided parallel reports using the SDQ. At first glance, the study’s findings, as summarized in the abstract, seem to offer a clear and affirmative answer to the opening question about ADHD and suicidality: “The results revealed that elevated hyperactivity scores, surpassing the ADHD diagnosis threshold, were significantly associated with increased rates of suicidal behavior. Hyperactivity demonstrated a stronger association with lifetime suicide attempts compared to inattention. Moreover, children's self-reported ADHD symptoms exhibited a stronger correlation with suicide attempts than parental reports. This study highlights the critical role of hyperactivity in understanding suicidal behaviors among children with ADHD. It underscores the importance of considering hyperactivity-related symptoms in assessment and treatment approaches for suicidal behavior in this population.” In this article, I discuss the study by Shahnovsky et al. (2024) , a noteworthy effort to explore the relationship between characteristics of ADHD and suicidal behaviors. While the study addresses an essential topic, certain methodological and interpretative challenges undermine its validity and raise concerns about unintended consequences, including the potential to inadvertently increase suicide risk. Therefore, careful analysis and discussion of the study are warranted with the utmost respect for the authors and their dedication to advancing suicide prevention . The analysis and discussion are also essential for understanding the broader complexities surrounding ADHD and suicidality, as well as informing real-life clinical and parental decisions. Despite the significance of these concerns, my formal commentary on this study was rejected by the European Journal of Investigation in Health, Psychology and Education , citing their policy against publishing commentaries. Additionally, the journal asserted that the study’s limitations section sufficiently addressed my criticisms – an assertion I demonstrate throughout this article to be inaccurate (for the complete exchange with the journal, see the Supplementary Material). Given the potential clinical implications of the study’s conclusions, a transparent and critical discussion remains essential. Key oversights in ADHD and suicidality research The analysis of the study by Shahnovsky et al. begins with a close inspection of its methodology, followed by an analysis of its findings later in this article. As implied above, the study focused on testing direct links between characteristics of ADHD (inattention and hyperactivity) and suicide risk (suicide attempts and suicide behaviors). Such an approach, which emphasizes simple, unidimensional relationships between two variables, is accep in pioneering studies exploring phenomena that have not been previously investigated. However, when building on existing literature, it is essential to consider factors that are already well-documented. In the case of the relationship between ADHD and suicidality, two key variables extensively discussed in the literature are the presence of comorbid disorders and the use of common medications prescribed for ADHD ( Nigg, 2013 ). Notably, neither of these critical factors were considered in the study, a limitation that significantly impacts its conclusions. The role of comorbid disorders is explored below, followed by a discussion of the potential impact of ADHD medications. The overlooked role of comorbid disorders Previous studies linking ADHD to suicidality have consistently noted that, in most cases, the risk of suicide is better explained by the presence of other psychiatric disorders ( Agosti et al., 2011 ; James et al., 2004 ). This phenomenon, where another factor provides a more compelling explanation for the observed outcomes, is particularly easy to understand when considering learning disorders (e.g., dyslexia and dyscalculia), which, like ADHD, are common conditions that affect children's behaviors and academic achievements. While these disorders have been linked to poor mental health, depression, and even suicidality ( Fuller-Thomson et al., 2018 ; Wilson et al., 2009 ), few would claim that difficulties with math or reading directly lead to suicidal thoughts. Instead, researchers often propose that more severe psychiatric disorders or emotional distress – stemming from the child’s struggles with academic demands or social environments – are the true drivers of these outcomes. In the case of ADHD, this conflation – attributing suicidality to the disorder’s core characteristics (e.g., hyperactivity) – is well illustrated in a systematic review by Balaz and Kereszteny (Balazs &amp; Kereszteny, 2017). In this review, the relationship between ADHD and suicide risk was frequently mediated by other psychiatric conditions. In fact, the authors of this review previously reported that the correlation between ADHD symptoms and suicide risk was entirely mediated by symptoms of other disorders ( Balazs et al., 2014 ). This finding emerged from a rigorous study they conducted using structured clinical interviews, which are widely regarded as the gold standard for psychiatric research. Such evidence may clarify why even experts who emphasize the 'dangers' of ADHD occasionally acknowledge that “to a substantial extent, this risk [of suicide] is mediated by co-occurring psychiatric conditions” ( Nigg, 2013 ). The contributing role of comorbid disorders (not ADHD) in a parallel study by the authors This issue seems to concern Shanhovsky et al. as well. In the limitations section of their article, they acknowledge that “ the link between ADHD and lifetime suicide attempts found in this study may be influenced by psychiatric comorbidities, such as depression .” While it is true that suicide researchers cannot account for every possible contributing variable, in this case, the authors had already analyzed such comorbid confounders in “ a separate study ,” explicitly mentioned in the same limitations section (albeit in a different context). This separate study, published just four months earlier on May 30, 2024, in the Archives of Suicide Research , hypothesized that “ among children… depression, ADHD, anxiety, and conduct disorders would be associated with STB [Suicidal Thoughts and Behaviors]” (Shahnovsky, Pirogovsky, et al., 2024). However, their findings did not support the hypothesis regarding ADHD. As the authors noted in that sperate study, “ Surprisingly, our results did not render a significant association between ADHD and SI [Suicidal Ideation] among children .” This discrepancy between the two studies raises critical questions about the validity of attributing suicidality directly to ADHD. Why did the authors not integrate their own findings from the earlier study, which was based on overlapping data and essentially highlighted the importance of accounting for comorbidities? Additionally, how should we interpret the current findings of a positive relationship between hyperactivity and suicide risk when, in their previous study, the authors found no significant association between ADHD and suicidality ? These unresolved contradictions underscore the need for greater consistency and methodological rigor in disentangling the complex relationships between ADHD, comorbidities, and suicidality. The overlooked role of ADHD medications A further, and perhaps more troubling, methodological gap in the study by Shanhovsky et al. concerns the potential contribution of psychiatric medications in general, and the first-line pharmacological treatment for ADHD in particular, to the observed suicide risk. Could the reported increased risk in this study be not a consequence of the diagnosis itself, but rather an unintended effect of the stimulant medications commonly prescribed as its treatment of choice ( Bruno et al., 2022 ; McCarthy et al., 2009 )? Is it possible that these medications, designed to suppress the very behaviors of impulsivity and hyperactivity thought to increase the risk, might paradoxically exacerbate it? Misplaced focus on antidepressants Indeed, the limitation section of the article by Shahnovsky et al. acknowledges that they “ did not account for whether the participants were taking medications, despite evidence that antidepressants can have side effects that significantly increase the risk of suicidal ideation and attempts among young people .” They further note that “ those factors were considered in a separate study ,” referring readers to the study discussed above, which they conducted on overlapping data (Shahnovsky, Pirogovsky, et al., 2024). However, this “separate study” only documented the prevalence of certain psychiatric medications and did not investigate their contribution to suicidality risk – a noteworthy oversight in its own right, particularly given the high prevalence of medication use reported in the sample. Moreover, the discussion of antidepressants in the limitations section may be less relevant for children aged 7–12, compared with the potential contribution of stimulants – the first-line treatment for ADHD – which was not mentioned in this section. As documented in the authors’ “separate study,” antidepressants were used by 58.6% of adolescents but only 15% of children. By contrast, stimulants were used by 22.5% of children and 13.5% of adolescents. This suggests that stimulants are more pertinent to the current study, which focuses on the link between hyperactivity and suicidality in young children. Interpreting prior research on stimulants and suicide Not only was this straightforward possibility left unexamined, but the authors also state in their introduction: “It is now well established that significant associations exist between ADHD and suicidal behavior and that stimulant medications may help attenuate this link” (bold added). Before addressing the specific source they cite to support this claim, it is important to consider the broader context of the literature, which is, at best, inconclusive – and at worst, suggestive of a negative impact, as will be demonstrated in the following section. For instance, a 2020 meta-analysis examining a range of functional outcomes of ADHD medications identified two studies on suicidality ( Boland et al., 2020 ). Notably, despite the overall pro-medication narrative of the meta-analysis, the calculated effect of ADHD medications on suicidality was not significant (for an in-depth look at the discrepancies between the pro-medication narrative of this meta-analysis and its actual findings, see my book on ADHD and its first-line medications; Ophir, 2022a ). The sole source cited by the authors to substantiate their claim regarding the protective effects of ADHD medications on suicidality is a study by Shoval et al. (2021) , titled “Evaluation of ADHD Medications, Externalizing Symptoms, and Suicidality in Children” (2021). However, this source actually suggests the opposite. The two main effects reported in that study were: (1) a significant association between suicidality and externalizing symptoms, and (2) a significant association between suicidality and ADHD medications (OR = 1.32; p = 0.01). In other words, rather than suggesting a protective effect, this source clearly indicates that children receiving ADHD medications are at an elevated risk of suicidality. I admit that I can understand the confusion regarding this source, as Shoval et al. also reported an interaction effect, which they interpreted as suggesting that “in children with substantial externalizing symptoms, ADHD medication use may be associated with less suicidality.” However, this interpretation does not negate the negative main effect of the medications on suicidality, and it is likely inaccurate. This interpretation hinges on the observation that the association between externalizing symptoms and suicidality remained significant only among children who did not receive ADHD medications ( OR = 1.42 for non-medicated children compared with OR = 1.15 for medicated children). Yet, this finding may actually highlight the dominant negative effect of the medications. When ADHD medications were included in the risk calculation, their negative impact may have been so substantial that it overshadowed the contribution of externalizing symptoms. In other words, the reduced significance of externalizing symptoms in medicated children could simply reflect the stronger role of the medications in influencing suicidality risk. Evidence linking ADHD medications to suicide The study by Shoval et al. is, of course, not the only one to find a connection between ADHD medications and an increased risk of suicidality. For example, a large-scale study conducted in the Netherlands found that adults aged 18–40 who started using methylphenidate were twice as likely to attempt suicide compared to a control group with similar demographics ( Bruno et al., 2022 ). Similar findings have been reported in younger populations. A study using a large UK database found that the relative risk of dying by suicide was nearly twice as high among adolescents and young adults using stimulant medications compared to their peers who did not use these drugs ( McCarthy et al., 2009 ). Notably, the risk associated with the medications, according to this UK study, was particularly high among young children (11–14) – a group similar in age to the very young children (7–12) examined in the study by Shanhovsky et al. While the absolute risk of death by suicide in this age group was very low (as suicide deaths are extremely rare at such young ages), the relative differences within this low-risk group were striking. In the UK study, young children who used stimulant medications were nearly 162 times more likely to die by suicide compared to those who did not ( McCarthy et al., 2009 ). This troubling figure may stem from the relatively common occurrence of suicidal thoughts triggered by these medications. Indeed, even small-scale experimental studies have reported suicidal ideation or behaviors as adverse outcomes of these treatments (e.g., Arnold et al., 2010 ; Hoare et al., 2005 ), suggesting that such distressing reactions may be more prevalent than commonly perceived. In my own research, more than 3% of participants reported experiencing suicidal thoughts triggered by these drugs ( Ophir, 2022b ). While this figure might overestimate the actual prevalence due to the observational nature of my study, I would caution against dismissing this outcome as merely 'very rare'. A recent update to the medication leaflet for Concerta XL (a brand of methylphenidate) by the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) explicitly warns that “up to 1 in 100 people” might think about or feel like killing themselves (MHRA, 2024). “Up to 1 in 100 people” may seem like a relatively small percentage, but considering the widespread use of these medications ( Chai et al., 2012 ; McCabe et al., 2023 ; Qato et al., 2018 ; Visser et al., 2016 ), this figure translates to an alarming number of children worldwide. Evidence linking ADHD medications to depression Moreover, ADHD medications, such as methylphenidate, may also contribute to suicidal ideation through their well-documented depression-related effects ( Currie et al., 2014 ; Konrad-Bindl et al., 2016 ; Storebø et al., 2018 ). Commonly reported side effects of methylphenidate include hyperactivity (ironically, the very symptom it is meant to treat and the one that is said to increase the risk of suicide), restlessness, crying, emotional lability, loss of joy, and depression ( Konrad-Bindl et al., 2016 ). In line with these reports, a large Cochrane review found that nearly one in three users of methylphenidate experienced emotional lability, while 4% suffered from depression ( Storebø et al., 2018 ). These findings highlight the potential for ADHD medications to trigger Major Depressive Disorder, which may include “recurrent suicidal ideation without a specific plan, or a suicide attempt or a specific plan for committing suicide” (APA, 2022). A closer look at the study's results Having addressed the critical methodological gaps in the study by Shahnovsky et al., it is equally important to examine its actual findings and evaluate the extent to which they support the broader narrative linking ADHD symptoms to suicide risk. Below is a summary of the findings reported in the study: Children's self-reported hyperactivity was significantly related to lifetime suicide attempts. Children's self-reported inattention was not significantly related to lifetime suicide attempts. Children's self-reported inattention and hyperactivity were not significantly related to suicide behaviors (though the authors note they approached significance). Parental reports of both hyperactivity and inattention were not significantly related to lifetime suicide attempts. Parental reports of both hyperactivity and inattention were not significantly related to suicide behaviors. As this summary shows, ADHD symptoms were largely unrelated to suicide risk. The only significant finding, which linked hyperactivity (but not inattention) to past suicide attempts (but not suicidal behaviors), was based solely on children’s self-reported responses. This raises a critical question: how can children as young as 7 years old – or even those at the upper end of the age range, 12 years old – reliably evaluate their own levels of hyperactivity or inattention, let alone compare these to standardized clinical thresholds? Supporting this reliability concern, the study's concordance test between parent and child reports revealed a “very low level of agreement… suggesting almost no concordance between the children's and parents' reports on ADHD symptoms.” This striking disparity underscores the potential distortion in children’s self-perceptions and further calls into question the reliability of the sole significant result. Furthermore, even if the authors' entire set of hypotheses had been statistically confirmed, it is important to recognize that basic symptom measures of hyperactivity and inattention (without the use of broader, standardized assessments) provide an incomplete and potentially misleading picture. This concern is particularly relevant in the case of hyperactivity, which can arise from a wide range of neurodevelopmental, emotional, or situational factors unrelated to ADHD itself. Taken together, these predominantly non-significant findings, which rely on unreliable self-reports of very young children, challenge the narrative presented in the study’s abstract (see the opening of this article) and cast serious doubt on the validity of hyperactivity as a critical risk factor for suicidality. In my view, the pattern of results suggests that other variables, likely unrelated to ADHD symptoms as discussed throughout this article, may offer a far more robust and accurate explanation for understanding suicide risk in children. Conclusions and discussion Altogether, the actual findings of the study, combined with its omission of critical variables known to increase suicide risk (i.e., comorbid disorders and ADHD medications) and its misinterpretation of previous research (which has essentially documented a troubling link between ADHD medications and suicide risk) ( Shoval et al., 2021 ), undermine the validity of its conclusions. These methodological and interpretative gaps not only limit the study's utility but also risk perpetuating harmful misconceptions. Clinicians might act on its conclusions and prescribe ADHD medications to suppress the supposedly dangerous hyperactivity – medications that were not accounted for in the study and that are well-documented to increase depression and suicide risk (as shown throughout the current article). To avoid such potential harm, and to advance the field of suicidology in children, future research would benefit from a more nuanced and comprehensive approach. Assessments that include both comorbid conditions and negative effects of medications are essential to disentangle the complex relationship between ADHD, its treatments, and suicidality. In my view, addressing these gaps could bring us closer to identifying precise risk factors and developing safe interventions. This recommendation is particularly critical given the growing recognition of the limited validity − and potential risks − of the biomedical framing of ADHD ( Ophir, 2024 ). Several lines of evidence question the assumption that ADHD reflects a chronic biomedical disease ( Ophir, 2022c ), and multiple analyses have raised serious concerns about the appropriateness and safety of its first-line pharmacological treatments (Ophir, in press; Storebø et al., 2018 ). Moreover, patterns of scientific reporting around ADHD have sometimes been shaped by manipulations and selective interpretations, leading to public misconceptions (Ophir &amp; Shir-Raz, 2020). These distortions add further concern regarding medication risks that are not adequately conveyed to the public (Ophir &amp; Shir-Raz, 2022). Against this broader backdrop, the study by Shahnovsky et al. represents an important opportunity for reflection and constructive dialogue. Despite the critical observations raised throughout this article, please allow me to conclude by commending the authors once again for addressing such a crucial and challenging topic. Their lifelong dedication to advancing suicide prevention is deeply appreciated, and it is in this spirit that I offer these reflections. I hope that this discussion fosters a constructive dialogue that contributes to refining research practices and deepening our understanding of the tragic phenomenon of childhood suicide. Supplementary Material Editorial article to open critique: context for the current barriers This supplementary material provides background on the editorial barriers encountered when attempting to publish the current article in the journal where the original study by Shahnovsky et al. (2024) appeared – the European Journal of Investigation in Health, Psychology and Education (EJIHPE). Upon the publication of the study by Shahnovsky et al., which identified hyperactivity as a significant predictor of suicide attempts, I contacted EJIHPE to request an exception to the journal’s policy that prohibits commentaries and letters critiquing published articles. I argued that such a policy contradicts fundamental scientific norms and impedes open academic discourse. Additionally, I outlined several key concerns – detailed in the main article – regarding the study by Shahnovsky et al. Following this exchange, I was granted permission and explicitly encouraged to submit a formal commentary. However, despite this initial approval, my submission was later rejected on the grounds that the journal does not accept commentaries. The rejection was accompanied by two justifications: That the study’s objective was “ not to establish causal relationships between hyperactivity and suicidal behaviors ” but rather “ to examine the association between the facets of ADHD (hyperactivity and inattention) and suicidal behavior and attempts in children. ” That the study’s limitations section had already addressed most of my concerns. Believing this rationale to be flawed, I appealed the decision, clearly demonstrating that the limitations section did not sufficiently address the key methodological issues I raised and that it even contained certain misrepresentations (as can be seen in the main article). Despite this, my appeal was denied without further justification. Given these circumstances, I felt it was my ethical duty to publish my commentary elsewhere to ensure an open scholarly discussion of the study’s findings and their implications.
