Background Nurses are pivotal in tackling malnutrition by implementing innovative and engaging health education strategies. Unhealthy dietary patterns are a major global health problem that can negatively impact child mortality and morbidity. Interactive methods, such as puppet shows and flashcards, have emerged as effective tools to promote nutrition awareness among children. Aim This study explores the role of nursing in promoting children’s nutritional awareness by assessing the effectiveness of interactive educational programs, specifically puppet shows and flashcards, compared to traditional lecture-based programs. Methods A quasi-experimental study with a pre-post design was applied across four maternity and child health (MCH) centres from May to August 2023. Children were divided into three groups: a puppet show group ( n = 31), a flashcard group ( n = 30), and a control group ( n = 30) that received the program through traditional methods. Children’s nutritional awareness was collected through nutritional knowledge and attitude questionnaires, and the children’s perspectives on educational experiences questionnaire using focus interviews. Then, the three educational programs were designed and implemented; the questionnaires were collected again after implementing the programs. Results The study reports a significant improvement in children’s nutritional knowledge and attitudes ( p = 0.000) after implementing the educational programs. Puppet shows achieved the highest mean in nutritional knowledge (12.74 ± 1.63) and positive attitude (52.16 ± 5.279). Conclusions Puppet shows are superior to traditional lectures and flashcards for promoting nutritional health among school-aged children when delivered by nurses. This underscores the need to train nurses in healthcare centres on interactive and visual teaching strategies to enhance children’s health education and disease prevention efforts. Evolving from unidirectional to interactive methods, effective programs now adopt participatory approaches that actively engage communities in designing appropriate interventions to improve dietary literacy. These programs help people build practical skills like meal planning while considering local food availability and socioeconomic barriers, acknowledging that true dietary literacy extends beyond factual knowledge to include the ability to navigate real-world challenges in food selection and preparation [ 1 ]. Health professionals play a vital role in advancing health promotion initiatives [ 1 ]. Their educational competence significantly influences health outcomes, demanding a re-orientation from merely reactive clinical care to proactive and interactive health education [ 1 , 3 ]. Nurses can implement this transition through holistic clinical, personal, and family-centred assessments that evaluate health needs, readiness for change, and individual perspectives. This approach proves especially meaningful when working with children and families, where health education becomes as valued, by parents, as traditional clinical care [ 3 ]. The global nutrition crisis continues to exact a devastating toll, with malnutrition afflicting nearly 104 million children and accounting for 33% of pediatric mortality worldwide [ 4 ]. These striking statistics underscore the critical role of early childhood nutritional education. The literature has consistently emphasised the value of optimal nutrition during early childhood, which significantly impacts both physical growth and cognitive development while concurrently serving as a protective factor against long-term metabolic and cardiovascular risks [ 5 – 7 ]. Yet despite these well-documented associations, significant gaps persist in health literacy among children, especially in developing countries. Compounding this challenge is the absence of dedicated health education personnel capable of implementing practical health promotion interventions [ 8 ]. The World Health Organization’s (WHO) Health-Promoting School framework addresses this gap by integrating health promotion into daily school activities [ 8 ]. Within this framework, nurses, as frontline practitioners with trusted community relationships, are uniquely positioned to translate nutritional knowledge into practice [ 9 – 12 ]. Their clinical expertise enables them to do more than disseminate nutritional information. They can drive tangible behavioural shifts in children’s dietary choices, coach teachers as nutritional allies, and ultimately help reduce childhood illness and mortality risks [ 10 – 12 ]. Literature review Childhood nutritional imbalances, whether deficiencies in essential nutrients or excesses of calories, saturated fats, and added sugars, can significantly impair growth and neurodevelopment and drive epidemics of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia. These effects stem from how early childhood dietary quality and quantity critically influence growth trajectories, developmental milestones, and long-term health status, prompting scrutiny of modern dietary patterns [ 13 , 14 ]. Despite nutrition education efforts, children’s dietary patterns continue to reflect problematic trends, including increased consumption of processed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages [ 15 , 16 ], exacerbated by the pervasive availability and marketing of hyper-palatable, unhealthy foods near schools [ 16 ]. Also, breakfast skipping remains common and is consistently associated with lower academic achievement in school-aged children [ 17 ]. Addressing these challenges requires collaborative, community-based interventions engaging parents, educators, and health professionals to reshape children’s dietary patterns [ 16 , 17 ]. Egyptian children face a triple burden of malnutrition, with 21% stunting and 8% wasting among under-five-year-olds, while 15–25% present with overweight/obesity across all paediatric age groups. This is compounded by prevalent paediatric anaemia, indicating concurrent micronutrient deficiencies [ 18 ]. In addition, patterns of dietary diversity were identified, particularly inadequate consumption of animal-source proteins (eggs, meat, and fish) and fruits/vegetables according to WHO recommendations, reflecting systemic imbalances in childhood nutrition [ 19 ]. The literature suggests that integrating classroom nutrition education with locally sourced school meals and school-organised community farming activities through a Farm-to-School Program (F2SP) could enhance current efforts to combat childhood malnutrition [ 20 ]. Nurse-led, school-based interventions could play a pivotal role in facilitating such a program, leveraging their clinical expertise to deliver nutrition education, foster community engagement, and ultimately improve dietary literacy at scale. These approaches appear particularly relevant in Egypt, where substantial gaps in nutrition education and family awareness have been identified, with socioeconomic disparities significantly predicting low nutritional literacy [ 21 ]. Egypt’s national school feeding program, developed with international partners, provides nutritious in-school snacks, take-home rations, and electronic food vouchers for vulnerable families. These initiatives have demonstrated measurable improvements in children’s cognitive performance and academic achievement [ 22 ]. However, persistent income inequality continues to drive reliance on calorie-dense, nutrient-poor diets, exacerbating the dual burden of malnutrition among children [ 23 ]. Early childhood constitutes a critical developmental window for establishing lifelong dietary patterns. Nutrition education interventions targeting kindergarten and elementary school students are particularly effective, as this formative period presents optimal opportunities to cultivate sustainable healthy eating behaviours and food preferences [ 4 ]. Educational programs promoting physical activity and healthy nutrition have proven effective in enhancing young people’s awareness, encouraging them to lead healthier lives [ 24 ]. School garden-based programs, for example, improve children’s understanding of diet and nutrition while positively shaping their attitudes and willingness to embrace healthy food choices [ 25 ]. Nutritionists emphasise that such programs should adopt a holistic approach, moving beyond knowledge acquisition to practical application, equipping school-aged children with lifelong skills for making healthy dietary choices [ 26 ]. This approach proves most effective when extended to mothers as well, creating family-wide impacts on nutritional status and well-being [ 27 ]. Ultimately, the successful implementation of such initiatives requires a coordinated approach, engaging children, schools, families, healthcare providers, and communities to sustainably address childhood malnutrition [ 16 , 17 , 21 , 25 ]. Behavioural shifts may be particularly achievable through interactive pedagogical methods like puppet shows and flashcards. Research has highlighted the positive impact of these methods in engaging children and sustaining their attention during educational interventions [ 15 , 28 – 33 ], with potential improvements in dietary behaviours, especially among children with selective eating patterns [ 33 ]. This review delves into the efficacy of interactive educational programs employing puppet shows and flashcards in promoting children’s nutrition knowledge. Effectiveness of puppet shows in nutrition education Puppets serve as versatile, low-cost educational tools that effectively engage learners through interactive dialogue while creating inclusive environments. These dynamic figures stimulate imagination, encourage collaboration, and help reshape learners’ perspectives, making them particularly valuable for nurses and educators in health promotion contexts [ 34 – 36 ]. Research demonstrates that educational puppet shows substantially enhance children’s nutritional knowledge and promote healthy behaviours. These educational interventions prove to improve children’s ability to identify balanced diets, exercise benefits, and healthier beverage choices. The acquired knowledge cultivated a great appreciation for healthy eating and exercise, with students not only embracing these concepts but also applying them practically, as evidenced when they challenged a juice-serving event, advocating for water as taught in the program [ 34 ]. Effectiveness of flashcards in nutrition education Flashcards serve as an engaging educational tool that enhances information literacy [ 28 , 32 , 37 ] by capturing students’ attention and stimulating active participation [ 37 ]. Research demonstrates their effectiveness in boosting learning motivation, retention, problem-solving skills, memorizing, and creativity, while their visually appealing design makes learning enjoyable while also nurturing confidence and communication abilities [ 38 , 39 ]. Flashcards-based nutritional education enhances children’s knowledge and awareness of healthy eating habits [ 40 , 41 ]. Comparison with traditional lecture-based programs While lecture-based programs convey foundational knowledge, their passive nature often fails to engage young children effectively. In contrast, interactive methods like puppet shows and flashcards actively involve learners, resulting in superior knowledge retention and behavioural changes. These approaches combine entertainment with education, addressing the limitations of conventional teaching while better fostering long-term healthy habits [ 42 , 43 ]. Aim of the study This study assessed the effectiveness of interactive educational programs like puppet shows and flashcards as health education tools in improving children’s nutritional awareness compared to lecture-based educational programs. Research hypotheses H1: Interactive pedagogical tools - specifically flashcards and puppet shows - yield superior nutritional literacy outcomes in children relative to traditional methods. Methods Study design This study utilised a quasi-experimental study with a pre-post design to examine and compare three instructional designs. Study settings To ensure diversity and representation, the study was conducted in four Maternity and Child Health (MCH) centres in four different districts affiliated with Ismailia city. These MCH centres provide daily primary health care and educational services for patients. All four centres serve populations with similar socioeconomic profiles, ensuring comparable baseline characteristics across study sites. Site selection prioritised centres with a reliable census of children, cooperative management and clinical staff, and accessibility. All four centres had sufficient spaces to carry out the educational programs, and the research team provided educational materials. Participants A minimum sample size of 72 children of school age was required to detect an effect size of 0.4 with a power (1-β = 0.85) of 85% at a significance probability level of p &lt; 0.05 and a partial eta squared of 0.14. Assuming a non-response rate of 15%, the total sample size needed is 81. As per the sample size calculations, each group (A1, A2, A3) should have a minimum of 27 participants, as illustrated in Table 1 . The sample size was determined using G*Power software version 3.1.9.6 [ 44 , 45 ] as illustrated in Fig. 1 where f is the effect size, α = 0.05, β = 0.2, Power = 1- β = 0.80. To ensure the homogeneity of the study participants, the studied children were Egyptians, aged between 6 and 12 years. Participants were recruited from within the same city to decrease the diversity of the geographical boundaries. The economic factors were controlled by recruiting a sample from public MCH centres affiliated with the Egyptian Ministry of Health rather than from private settings. Assuming that participants are subjected to similar Egyptian values, norms, and beliefs from a defined city, the cultural confounding variables were also controlled while the sample was being recruited. Firstly, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied before selecting participants in each MCH centre. The exclusion criteria included children with genetic conditions affecting their mental abilities, children with cognitive or learning disabilities, children with hearing or visual impairments, and children diagnosed with Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder, Attention-Deficit Disorder (ADHD/ADD), Autistic spectrum disorder (ASD), or sensory impairment. The inclusion criteria included children aged 6–12 who agreed to participate in the study, and their legal guardian/parent provided informed consent. Secondly, in each MCH centre, the researchers listed the possible participants, wrote their names, and assigned a number to each participant. Then, the researchers selected children through simple random techniques and divided them into three groups: traditional, flashcards, and puppet show groups. Subsequently, the researchers implemented the three educational programs. This procedure was employed in the same manner in all MCH centres. Measures Instrument I. Children’s demographic questionnaire The researchers developed this questionnaire to collect socio-demographic information, including age, sex, and birth order. Instrument II. Children’s nutritional knowledge questionnaire The researchers developed this questionnaire based on [ 46 – 48 ] to assess children’s knowledge of healthy nutrition. The researchers assessed children’s basic nutritional knowledge, examining their understanding of food group classification, nutrient composition, and healthy dietary habits. The questionnaire evaluated recognition of the five major food groups, the protein content of legumes, the importance of vitamins and minerals, and recommended daily water consumption. Additionally, children’s ability to distinguish grains (e.g., rice, oats) from legumes, identify dairy products (e.g., cheese), and recognize the fiber, vitamin, and mineral content of vegetables (e.g., lettuce) and nuts was assessed. The questionnaire also addressed common misconceptions, such as the perceived necessity of high-sugar foods for energy. It consisted of 15 items with choices of “true”, “false”, or “I do not know.” The correct answer was assigned one point, while the incorrect ones or “I do not know” received zero points. The maximum score attainable was 15, and the minimum was zero. The instrument exhibited a good reliability value of 0.744. Instrument III. Children’s nutritional attitude questionnaire This questionnaire was developed by Moitra et al.,2021 and translated into Arabic by the researchers to assess school-aged children’s attitudes toward healthy eating patterns. It comprised 20 items categorised into four domains: perceived susceptibility and severity (5 items), perceived benefits (6 items), perceived barriers (6 items), and readiness to change and self-efficacy (3 items). It was a four-point Likert scale, with a range of 3 for strongly agree and 0 for strongly disagree, except for the perceived barriers domain, which was a reversed item. The highest possible score was 60, and the lowest was zero. The Cronbach alpha value for the original scale was 0.892 [ 49 ]. Instrument IV. Children’s perspectives on the educational program questionnaire This questionnaire was composed of an open-ended question to collect children’s feedback on their perception of the engaging and enlightening aspects of the applied educational program. The researchers asked this question to each participant across the three educational groups: traditional, flashcards, and puppet show groups. The researchers recorded the children’s responses in their own words and presented them as qualitative data in the results section. Instruments’ validity and reliability To ensure the robust validity of the instruments, a panel of five bilingual experts (two from the nursing administration department, two from the pediatric nursing department, and one from the adult health nursing department) evaluated the instruments for content and face validity. They assessed relevance, clarity, comprehensiveness, simplicity, question length, order, bias, and redundancy. Items scoring below a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 0.80 were revised or removed, resulting in final versions unanimously approved by the panel. The instruments demonstrated strong reliability with high internal consistency coefficients. The nutritional knowledge questionnaire displayed a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.744, while the children’s nutritional attitude questionnaire yielded 0.821. The Nutritional Knowledge Questionnaire (NKQ) was developed by the researchers. The development of assessment items was informed by an extensive literature review, focus-group discussions, and key theoretical components from [ 46 – 48 ]. To establish validity, the panel of expert evaluators examined the instruments for face and content validity, while quantitative measures, including content validity indices, item difficulty parameters, and discrimination indices, were computed. The instruments demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with internal consistency reliability exceeding acceptable thresholds (Cronbach’s α &gt; 0.7). The Children’s Nutritional Attitude Questionnaire (CNAQ) was cross-referenced with the Child Knowledge and Health Behaviors Questionnaire (CKHBQ) [ 49 ] to validate thematic consistency (e.g., preferences for sugary foods and vegetable consumption). Discrepancies in item phrasing or scaling were documented and justified (e.g., cultural adaptations for local dietary practices). CNAQ’s Cronbach’s α was 0.821, aligning with CKHBQ α = 0.832 for knowledge, 0.892 for attitude, and 0.768 for dietary practice. Ethics approval and consent to participate The study followed the principles provided in the Helsinki Declaration. Before beginning this research, the Ethics Committee of Suez Canal University’s Faculty of Nursing authorised the research protocol (approval No. 218). Data coding processes protected the participants’ data. As all the participants in this study are under 16, their parents and legal guardians received comprehensive information about the study’s purpose and objectives, emphasising the voluntary nature of participation and the right to withdraw at any point. Before participating in the study, the participants’ parents or legal guardians carefully read and discussed the consent statement with the researchers and provided informed consent by signing a consent form. Field of work Data collection took place between May and August 2023. The researchers explained the study’s purpose to MCH centre managers to secure their permission and cooperation. Subsequently, the researchers described the study’s purpose to children, their parents, and legal guardians, and informed consent was obtained. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were assigned a numbered ID and randomly allocated to three groups (A1, A2, A3), to include 7–8 children in each educational session. In the pre-test phase, researchers conducted 5–10-minute interviews with each child using the questionnaires to assess baseline knowledge and attitudes toward healthy nutrition. For all three groups, the educational program was implemented in the presence of the children, their parents, and legal guardians. The program covered the definition and components of the healthy eating pyramid, the importance of healthy nutrition, the significance of each food group, illustrations of good eating habits, and tailored examples of good food choices for children. Group A1 (control group—traditional education) received a single 20–25-minute lecture with group discussion. The session was delivered by a researcher in the health education room within each MCH centre. Each group (comprising 7–8 children) and their mothers attended the educational session passively to provide support and ensure the children’s cooperation. Group A1 was considered the control group. Group A2 (flashcards education) was divided into groups of 7–8 children, and each group received a 20–25-minute session of the flashcards educational program. Researchers used 40 pictorial flashcards (Fig. 2 ) featuring 20 unhealthy foods to be avoided and 20 healthy foods to be consumed. In the paediatric clinic waiting area, the researchers approached the children and their accompanying mothers, showing them the flashcards and delivering concise information regarding each food type in simple Arabic. Parents passively attended the flashcards educational session but did not interrupt the flow of the educational process. Group A3 (puppet show education) participated in a 20-25-minute scripted puppet show in a wooden theatre designed by the researchers (Fig. 3 ). The show features four characters (puppets): a child, a mother, a nurse, and a doctor. The played scenario was unfolding in an outpatient clinic. This narrative focused on a situation where a mother sought medical advice for her child due to problems related to unhealthy nutritional habits. During the puppet show, the nurse puppet gave the child and mother puppets health education, delivering the required nutritional information in a simplified Arabic language. Two researchers performed the puppet show, and photos were taken for documentation with parental consent. The theatre was constructed in the garden of each of the MCH centres to provide a pleasurable environment for the children to enjoy the puppet show. Parents were present within reach of their children in the garden during the show. Each group comprised 7–8 children who attended a single show together. After the educational sessions, each child with his/her accompanying parent was allocated time for a question-and-answer (Q&amp;A) session about nutrition. Then, the researchers provided comprehensive responses to all participants and their guardians in all groups (A1, A2, and A3) in order to provide the correct answer for each question. The researchers conducted individual interviews to evaluate children’s knowledge and attitudes. During the interviews, the post-test questionnaires were completed, and open-ended questions about the program experience were asked to gauge children’s perceptions of the educational program’s engaging and educational aspects. The researchers recorded all responses for qualitative analysis. Data analysis Researchers used IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 26 for data analysis. The socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants were presented using frequencies and percentages. The children’s nutritional knowledge and attitudes were summarised through means and standard deviations. Statistical analysis included applying the Student’s t-test, with a significance level set at 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval. Multivariate regression analysis was used to predict nutritional awareness promotion. The MANOVA test was also used to enhance the interpretation of the findings. Results Quantitative data analysis Table 2 presents an overview of the demographic characteristics of the study participants. In the traditional group, 40% of participants were between 10 and 12 years old, 53.3% were male, and 30% were the second child in their families. In the flashcards group, 53.3% fell within the age range of 8 to 10 years, 56.7% were male, and 43.3% were second-born children. In the puppet show group, 54.8% were aged between 6 and 8 years, 54.8% were female, and 41.9% were second-born children. Finally, no statistical relations exist between children’s characteristics and educational strategies. Table 3 illustrates a significant improvement in the mean scores of children’s knowledge when comparing the pre-test and post-test assessments. In the post-test group, the mean scores for children’s knowledge showed a substantial increase compared to their pre-test scores, with the puppet show educational program group achieving the highest mean score (12.74 ± 1.63). The differences in scores between the pre-test and post-test were statistically significant for all three groups ( P &lt; 0.05). Table 4 highlights a significant increase in the mean scores of children’s attitudes when examining the pre-test and post-test assessments. In the post-test group, the mean scores for children’s attitudes exhibited a considerable surge compared to their respective pre-test scores, with the puppet show educational program group achieving the highest mean score (52.16 ± 5.279). The differences in scores between the pre-test and post-test were statistically significant for all three groups ( P &lt; 0.05). The multivariate regression analysis in Table 5 demonstrated that traditional, flashcards, and puppet show educational programs (β = 0.421, 0.954, 0.642, respectively) were significant independent positive predictors for improving nutritional awareness promotion. Qualitative data analysis By extracting insights from children’s feedback on the engaging and enlightening aspects of the educational program across the three educational program groups, Thematic analysis of the qualitative data was performed [ 50 ]. Puppet show group: engagement through storytelling and visual appeal Children in the puppet show group consistently emphasised the entertainment value of this method, describing it as “fun”, “exciting”, and “like watching a play”. A dominant theme was the character-driven storytelling narrative approach to learning, where stories acted out by puppets helped them internalise nutrition concepts effectively. Many children recalled specific puppet characters representing fruits, vegetables, and unhealthy foods, suggesting that personification made the lessons more relatable. The visual and performative elements were frequently reported as key factors in capturing children’s attention. The puppets’ movements, voices, and colours enhanced information retention, with children stating that these multisensory features helped knowledge “stick in their minds”. Puppet show group: emotional connection and retention Beyond engagement, children expressed an emotional connection to the puppet narratives, often describing feelings of empathy when a puppet faced the consequences of poor eating habits. This emotional engagement appears to reinforce short-term retention, as children could recount storylines after the show. Another emerging theme was children liking the interactive approach as they had the opportunity to “talk to the puppets”, which heightened participation and made learning feel like a dynamic dialogue rather than a passive reception. This suggests puppet shows succeed not merely as a visual tool but as an experiential and participatory method that enhances both enjoyment and comprehension. Flashcards group: structured learning and visual memorisation In the flashcards group, children highlighted the clarity and organisation of information as major strengths. The use of colour-coded categories (e.g., green for vegetables and red for unhealthy snacks) helped them quickly classify foods, reinforcing a key theme of systematic learning. Unlike the puppet show’s narrative approach, flashcards relied on repetition and visual reinforcement, which children described as helpful for “remembering facts fast”. Flashcards group: cognitive skill development A significant theme in this group was the development of analytical skills, as children reported improved ability to “compare foods” and “choose healthier options”. The flashcard presentation approach allowed children to process information somewhat independently, unlike the more guided puppet show experience. However, some children perceived this method as overly academic, noting that it felt “more like schoolwork” than play. This theme suggests that while flashcards are effective for factual learning, this method lacked the emotional and imaginative engagement of the puppet shows. This distinction underscores a trade-off between structured memorisation and experiential enjoyment in educational methods. Control group (traditional): knowledge clarification through discussion Children in the traditional instruction group frequently mentioned discussions as the most valuable aspect of their learning experience. A recurring theme was valuing the opportunity to ask questions, which helped correct misconceptions (e.g., “Is juice always healthy?“). Unlike the other groups, where learning was more visual or narrative-based, this method emphasised verbal exchange and critical thinking. Some children appreciated sharing personal experiences, such as family eating habits, indicating that the traditional format allowed for personalised learning in a way that scripted puppet shows or flashcards did not. Control group: limited engagement but deeper conceptual Understanding While children in this group reported lower enjoyment compared to the other methods, they often demonstrated a more nuanced understanding of nutrition, likely due to the reflective nature of discussions. A key theme was conceptual clarification, as they engaged in debates (e.g., “Why is sugar bad in large amounts?“) that required deeper reasoning. However, some feedback indicated that without visual aids or interactive elements, the lessons felt “boring” or “hard to remember,” highlighting a potential drawback of relying solely on verbal instruction for young learners. When comparing the three groups, a clear theme emerges: the more interactive and visually stimulating the method is, the higher the immediate engagement. Puppet shows excelled in emotional and imaginative involvement, flashcards in factual retention and categorisation, and traditional instruction in critical thinking through dialogue. However, each method had trade-offs—while puppet shows were the most enjoyable, some children struggled to recall specific facts without the repetition offered by flashcards. Conversely, traditional discussions fostered deeper understanding but lacked the motivational appeal of more dynamic approaches. The findings suggest that a blended approach might optimise both engagement and learning outcomes. For instance, a multimodal strategy combining puppet shows’ storytelling with flashcard’s structured review while allocating time for reflective discussion could reinforce emotional connection, factual retention, and comprehensive understanding. Discussion The literature consistently demonstrates that well-structured nutrition education programs can effectively mitigate childhood malnutrition, including both undernutrition and obesity, with lasting benefits for population health [ 10 – 12 , 51 ]. Given the profound influence of early lifestyle choices on long-term health outcomes, improving the health behaviours of school-aged children becomes imperative [ 52 ]. This well-documented relationship between childhood dietary patterns and non-communicable diseases [ 53 ] highlights the essential role of nurses, as skilled healthcare professionals, in children’s health promotion [ 52 ]. In this context, targeted nutrition interventions emerge as vital tools for guiding children toward healthier lifestyles [ 54 ]. Our findings reveal significant post-intervention improvement in nutritional knowledge across all study groups, underscoring how structured educational programs that encourage productive discussion and active participation can effectively enhance children’s learning. These findings corroborate existing evidence showing that engaging children in interactive nutrition education substantially enhances their nutrition knowledge acquisition [ 34 , 42 , 43 , 53 ]. Notably, all three intervention modalities enhanced children’s understanding of dietary principles, suggesting that expert guidance during school years can effectively shape nutritional attitudes during this developmentally critical period. This supports existing evidence that nurse-led nutrition education initiatives yield measurable benefits in promoting nutritional health [ 10 – 12 , 21 ]. Our study evaluates three pedagogical methods for nutrition education, identifying puppet shows as the most effective approach for school-aged children. In addition to enhancing knowledge retention, this method created an engaging and comfortable learning environment. These findings align with recent research by Putri et al. (2024), which reported significant improvements in children’s oral health knowledge following puppet-based counselling, despite poor baseline scores [ 35 ]. Similarly, another study highlighted how puppet shows stimulated critical thinking about nutrition, as evidenced by children applying learned concepts to real-world scenarios [ 34 ]. Further, Julianti et al. (2025) examined combining traditional lectures with interactive puppet shows and found significantly improved health knowledge and behaviours among primary school students [ 55 ]. While puppet shows prove highly effective for neurotypical children, their efficacy may vary for learners with special needs. Wahyuni (2019) observed that flashcards outperformed puppet shows in vocabulary retention among neurodiverse children (e.g. ASD, ADHD), although both methods effectively improved English vocabulary learning for children with special needs [ 56 ]. This discrepancy is likely due to the consistent visual format of flashcards, which minimises sensory overload, whereas puppets as dynamic figures may overwhelm some learners or distract them from the core content. This aligns with findings showing that augmented reality flashcards displaying 3D food models successfully educated children with intellectual disabilities about obesity and enhanced their understanding of the importance of healthy eating habits for their growth and development [ 40 ]. The present study shows an observable increase in the post-test score for nutritional knowledge among school-aged children participating in the flashcard-based educational program. This aligns with the literature, demonstrating flashcard’s efficacy across diverse learning contexts, including nutrition education [ 40 , 41 , 57 , 58 ] and language acquisition [ 38 , 59 ]. Notably, flashcards are particularly effective in enhancing knowledge among children with learning disabilities [ 40 , 56 ], attributable to their ability to enhance memory and capture and sustain attention [ 38 , 57 , 59 ]. Beyond these cognitive benefits, flashcards are also highly engaging learning tools that combine educational effectiveness with entertainment value [ 59 ]. Collectively, these findings indicate that flashcards - whether traditional or technologically enhanced - offer a versatile and effective approach to children’s education across various domains, from academic subjects to health literacy. The results demonstrate a marked positive shift in children’s attitudes toward healthy eating following the intervention in all three groups, highlighting how interactive and participatory learning methods can effectively foster favourable behavioural predisposition. By engaging children through storytelling, visual aids, and/or dialogue, the programs not only conveyed knowledge but also encouraged intrinsic motivation and enthusiasm for nutritious food choices. In this context, the study identifies two facilitators of attitude change: the central role of nurse educators and the quality of educator-child communication throughout the program. These interpersonal dynamics appear to promote more mindful food choices. This attitude shift is a potential precursor to sustained behavioural change [ 33 , 53 ]. While these findings emphasise child-centred approaches, the parental engagement dimension warrants careful consideration. Although our study limited parental involvement to providing comfort in clinical settings, Chan et al. (2022) note broader theoretical benefits of caregiver participation in dietary interventions. These benefits are often unrealised in practice due to implementation barriers [ 25 ]. This discrepancy suggests that future research should investigate structured, active parental engagement strategies to clarify their value and impact on nutritional outcomes. The puppet shows achieved superior results in modifying children’s nutritional attitudes compared to other educational methods. By creating a uniquely engaging learning environment, puppet shows enhance attention and content receptivity through their inherent entertainment value. Extending the empirical evidence, prior studies have consistently demonstrated similar outcomes; Aminimanesh et al. (2019) documented puppetry’s significant impact on behavioural modification during early childhood [ 60 ], while Kröger and Nupponen (2019) emphasised puppets’ effectiveness in promoting positive behaviours and classroom management by allowing children to explore new situational perspectives [ 36 ]. Further supporting evidence comes from Murdani and Nanda (2021), who reported increased fruit and vegetable consumption following puppet-based nutrition education [ 61 ]. Both flashcards and traditional methods significantly improved children’s nutritional attitudes, though their impacts varied based on baseline inclinations. While the flashcards group showed more favourable pre-test attitudes toward healthy eating, the traditional method demonstrated stronger relative improvement, particularly among children with initially less healthy attitudes. This advantage likely stems from incorporating interactive discussions into traditional lectures, which fostered deeper understanding through dialogue and personal reflection, enabling children to critically reassess their dietary choices. These observations are supported by the established pedagogical research, which reports that interactive teaching models promote active engagement and precipitate lasting attitude shifts, unlike traditional models with passive information delivery which has minimal impact on attitudes [ 62 , 63 ]. Flashcards, however, provide distinct advantages through their inherently interactive nature. Their visually structured designs promote active cognitive engagement through recall and analysis, while sustaining children’s attention and reducing frustration. These qualities enhance both learning and positive attitudes [ 57 ]. In addition, the entertaining and motivational effects of flashcards have been shown to have an educational impact on children’s attitudes. For instance, Sugar 50 flashcard game successfully improved children’s nutritional knowledge and positively influenced their attitudes toward reducing sugar consumption [ 41 ]. Afra et al. (2021) similarly found that flashcard interventions improved elementary students’ nutritional knowledge and attitudes [ 58 ], with multiple other studies confirming flashcard’s value in health behaviour modification [ 40 , 41 , 57 ]. The results conclusively support our hypothesis (H1), demonstrating that interactive pedagogical tools, particularly puppet shows, produce significantly greater improvements in nutritional literacy outcomes compared to traditional didactic methods. While puppet shows emerged as the most effective for holistic attitude and knowledge improvement, each method demonstrated unique strengths, suggesting their complementary use could optimise nutritional education. This study underscores the transformative potential of child-centred interactive approaches in shaping lifelong healthy eating behaviours, with critical implications for nurse-led interventions in healthcare, school, and community settings. Limitations Several limitations were considered when interpreting the findings of this study. Firstly, the sample comprised school-aged children in Egypt, which implies that the results may be influenced by national culture. To validate the study’s outcomes, conducting similar research involving children from different countries and cultural backgrounds is advisable. Secondly, the sample included school-aged children in the presence of their caregivers, yet the caregiver’s involvement needed to be fully assessed in this study. Therefore, it is recommended that caregivers be involved in future research to gain insights into their health habits and facilitate healthier nutrition choices for their children. Lastly, data collection relied on structured interviews, which may introduce potential source bias. Implications to practice Researchers propose the following recommendations to build a healthier community and enhance the quality of children’s nutritional education. Nursing Training Programs: To enhance nursing competence, MCH centers should establish training programs using innovative and engaging teaching techniques, including puppet shows, flashcards, and interactive small group teaching with discussions. Adequately equipped teaching rooms with materials for puppet shows and flashcards will facilitate the effectiveness of these methods. Nutrition Programs and Community Workshops: MCH centers and hospital administrators should implement nutrition programs and community workshops to promote health, raise awareness, and educate children and caregivers on healthier attitudes and behaviors. Efforts to make educational resources, such as puppet shows and flashcards, accessible to children and caregivers are essential, ensuring widespread availability in healthcare centers, schools, and community settings. Tailored Educational Programs for Caregivers: Caregivers play a crucial role in shaping children’s dietary habits, and targeted interventions can empower them with knowledge and skills to foster healthier daily nutrition practices. Therefore, tailored educational programs for caregivers are recommended. Future research To inform and support evidence-based practices in paediatric nutrition education: Further investigation is warranted to evaluate the sustained efficacy of these educational programs in not only improving children’s nutritional knowledge and attitudes but also translating these gains into long-term behavioral and health outcomes. Studies employing mixed-methods designs would provide valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms driving changes in children’s nutritional knowledge and attitudes, offering a more nuanced understanding of how these programs influence dietary perspectives and decision-making. Future studies should consider expanding this research to include larger, more diverse populations across varied sociocultural and environmental contexts. Replicating these interventions in different demographic settings would further validate their applicability and effectiveness, ensuring broader relevance to global child health initiatives and enhancing the generalizability of findings. Conclusion This study provides robust empirical evidence that interactive pedagogical tools—particularly puppet shows—significantly enhance nutritional literacy outcomes among school-aged children compared to traditional methods. Qualitative findings reveal puppet shows’ unique ability to combine emotional engagement with knowledge retention through narrative-based learning, while flashcards excel in structured knowledge acquisition. Traditional methods, though less engaging, but when incorporating interactive discussions, showed particular effectiveness for children with initially poorer nutritional attitudes. These results have important implications for nursing practice and health education, suggesting that multimodal, interactive approaches should replace passive instruction to sustain behavioural outcomes. Future research should explore longitudinal effects of implementing these methods in diverse educational settings. Originality/value This study makes three key contributions to paediatric health education literature. First, it provides comparative effectiveness data for three distinct pedagogical approaches using rigorous quasi-experimental methodology. Second, it demonstrates how puppet shows—an understudied intervention—can achieve superior outcomes by combining entertainment with education through narrative engagement, filling a gap in existing nutrition health promotion literature. Third, our data uniquely captures both quantitative outcomes and children’s qualitative experiences, revealing why certain methods work better for specific learning objectives. The findings challenge prevailing assumptions about traditional health education by empirically validating interactive tools’ superiority. For practitioners, we offer evidence-based guidance on selecting developmentally appropriate educational strategies, particularly for nurses delivering nutrition programs in healthcare contexts. The study highlights the effect of utilising puppet shows, flashcards, and modified interactive traditional method on enhancing children’s nutrition knowledge and shifting their attitudes to be healthier.
