To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Background Intertrochanteric fractures are common in the elderly, with PFNA being the primary treatment. The shorter PFNA-II is frequently used in Asian populations, but its failure risk factors remain unclear. This study aims to explore these factors to guide clinical treatment. Methods A retrospective study was conducted on 693 intertrochanteric fracture patients treated with PFNA-II. Patients were divided into two groups based on implant failure. Reduction quality, continuity of the medial femoral cortex, nail length, and tip apex distance (TAD) were analyzed to determine risk factors. Results A total of 466 patients were enrolled, with an average age of 83 years old. There were 24 patients with implant failure. There was no significant difference in demographic index between the two groups. After propensity score matching and multivariate logistic regression analysis, there were statistically significant differences in reduction quality and continuity of the medial femoral cortex between the two groups. Poor reduction quality significantly increased the risk of implant failure by 22.2 times, while discontinuity of the medial femoral cortex increased this risk by 12.1 times. Conclusion Poor reduction quality and medial femoral cortex discontinuity are key risk factors for implant failure in intertrochanteric fractures treated with short PFNA-II, increasing failure risk by 22.2 and 12.1 times respectively. Anatomic reduction and medial support reconstruction are crucial for successful outcomes. They are characterized by high rates of disability and mortality, with a one-year mortality rate as high as 36% [ 3 ]. Surgical treatment of intertrochanteric fractures includes intramedullary nail fixation and extramedullary fixation with a sliding hip screw. Intramedullary nailing, which is favored for its central fixation, reduced postoperative pain, and quicker recovery of mobility, has become the preferred method [ 4 , 5 ]. Emil et al. reported that the rate of intramedullary nailing for intertrochanteric fractures has been increasing internationally [ 6 ]. Currently, the proximal femoral nail anti-rotation (PFNA) has become the primary implant for treating intertrochanteric fractures [ 7 ]. However, with the widespread use of intramedullary nails, the failure rate can be as high as 13.3–20.5% [ 8 , 9 ]. Salvage procedures are challenging and expose a population to further complex surgery [ 10 ]. Given these serious consequences, identifying risk factors associated with implant failure is crucial. Kraus et al. reported that a tip-apex-distance (TAD) greater than 30 mm was the primary risk factor [ 11 ]. Turgut et al. demonstrated that inadequate correction of coxa vara deformity significantly increases the risk of intramedullary nail fixation failure even with a small TAD [ 12 ]. Additionally, Hao et al. identified poor reduction quality and loss of posteromedial support as predictors of implant failure in reverse oblique and transverse intertrochanteric fractures treated with PFNA [ 13 ]. Despite these studies, consensus on the specific risk factors for PFNA failure remains elusive. Moreover, the shorter version of PFNA (PFNA-II) with length no more than 240 mm is frequently utilized in Asian populations due to anatomical considerations [ 14 ]. This implant features a distinctive single helical blade with an integrated locking mechanism, which provides superior antirotation properties and enhanced stability compared to alternative fixation devices [ 15 ]. Despite its widespread use, there remains a paucity of literature specifically examining risk factors associated with fixation failures of these short PFNA-II implants. Hence, in this study, a group of intertrochanteric fracture stabilized with short PFNA-II has been chosen for explored the risk factors of the implant failure. It may predict which patients might suffer a complication and guide surgeons in preventing implant failure in patients with intertrochanteric fractures treated with PFNA-II. Methods Subjects Retrospectively analyzed 693 patients with intertrochanteric fractures underwent surgery in our trauma center, including 239 males and 454 females (male: female = 1:1.9), with the mean age of 76 years (from 20 to 103) from Dec 2015 to Oct 2023. Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients diagnosed by X-ray or Computed Tomography (CT) with intertrochanteric fracture (AO/OTA 31 A1 and A2). (2) Patients older than 55. (3) Stabilized with short PFNA-II (nail length no more than 240 mm). Exclusion criteria: (1) Stabilized with plate or external fixator. (2) Combined with femoral neck fracture, subtrochanteric fracture or femoral shaft fractures. (3) patients were not treated with PFNA-II, or PFNA-II length was over 240 mm. (4) Pathological fractures, metabolic bone diseases. (5) Death during follow-up or incomplete follow-up. (6) Patients who are undergoing chemotherapy or radiation, receiving corticosteroids or growth factors for therapy. The research flow chart is shown in Fig. 1 . Operation procedure Following anesthesia (primarily nerve block anesthesia to minimize cardiopulmonary impact), patients were positioned supine on a fluoroscopic surgical traction table. Initial closed reduction was performed via traction, with abduction and external rotation to relieve the fracture site. Subsequently, while maintaining traction, the affected limb was simultaneously adducted and internally rotated until the foot exhibited slight adduction and internal rotation. The reduction quality was checked by fluoroscopy. Once satisfactory reduction was achieved, the surgical site was disinfected and draped. In cases of inadequate reduction, instrument-assisted reduction through a small incision was performed. The PFNA-II device (DePuy Synthes, West Chester, PA, USA) was then implanted into the femur following the manufacturer’s surgical protocol. After intramedullary nail fixation, the deep fascia and skin were sutured without drainage placement. Data collection and postoperative treatment All participant data, including age, gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), surgical site, nail length, and hospital stays were meticulously documented in the case report forms. The preoperative evaluation was based on the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA)score, and bone quality was evaluated by the Singh index of the contralateral hip [ 16 ]. The fracture type was described by AO classification [ 17 ]. All patients were mobilized with weight-bearing walking under the protection of a walker on the second day after surgery. The first mobilization was guided by experienced nurses who provided instructions on sitting up, standing, and walking with both feet. Patients were discharged once they could walk independently with a walker. Before discharge, standardized anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of both hips were obtained in the radiology department to evaluate the final reduction quality and implant position (TAD). The reduction quality was based on the standard proposed by Baumgaertner et al. [ 18 ]. The caput-collum-diaphysis angle (CCD angle) was normal or slightly varus (CCD&lt;5°) on anteroposterior radiographs, or the angle at the fracture site on lateral radiography was not more than 20°. The displacement between fracture fragments was no more than 4 mm. For measuring the angle at the fracture site on lateral radiographs, we determined it by calculating the angle between the central axis of the proximal fragment (from the center of the femoral head to the midpoint of the fracture line) and the central axis of the distal fragment (extending along the femoral shaft center to the fracture line). Reduction quality was classified as good when both criteria were satisfied, acceptable when one criterion was met, and poor when neither criterion was fulfilled. All patients used walkers for 3–4 weeks after discharge and wore compression stockings before mobilization to prevent lower limb venous thrombosis, along with taking rivaroxaban orally for 30 days. During bed rest, patients performed ankle pump exercises under the supervision of family members and extended care service personnel. Sutures were typically removed after 2 weeks. A follow-up evaluation, which included a clinical and radiographic assessment, was performed at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12 and 24 months. Clinically, nonunion was characterized by false motion or persistent pain at the fracture site during weight bearing after 6 months [ 19 , 20 ]. Radiographs of the operated hip were made in standardized anteroposterior and lateral planes at each follow-up visit, and any complications requiring revision surgery as well as the degree of fracture healing were recorded. Complications requiring revision surgery include helical blade cut-out, fracture nonunion, implant breakage, or implant-related femoral fracture without high-energy trauma, indicating implant failure [ 21 , 22 ]. If there was an absence of cortical bridging on at least three cortices within the same timeframe, fractures were radiographically judged to be not healed [ 23 , 24 ]. All mentioned image data were extracted from the imaging browsing system of the trauma center. The image data were analyzed by two experienced attending surgeons, and the data was adopted when they agreed with each other. Discrepancies were resolved by the chief surgeon. Assignment definitions For risk factor analysis, age, BMI, Singh index, reduction quality, ASA index, TAD and medial femoral cortex continuity were classified and assigned values (Table 1 ). Statistical analysis Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows software (version 22.0, SPSS, Chicago, USA) and R software (version 3.6.3). All quantitative data were subjected to normality testing using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using the independent t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were less than 5. Analysis of risk factors was evaluated by logistic regression using the input method, and odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were also obtained. P &lt; 0.05 was defined as significant in all tests. Results There were 466 patients met the inclusion criteria with 178 males (38.2%) and 288 females (61.8%). Average age of those patients was 83 years (range from 52 to 103), and the mean follow-up time was 12.3 months (range from 8 to 19). There were 225 patients with the left hip fracture and 241 for right. Implant failure occurred in 24 patients (5.2%). According to implant failure, the patients were divided into implant failure group and non-failure group. The general conditions of the two groups are shown in Table 2 . There was no significant difference in demographic data between two groups, so the baseline characteristics were the same. Various types of internal fixation failure were observed in this study. Cut-out occurred in 12 patients (3 of which were accompanied by nonunion), 5 patients experienced implant-related femoral fracture without high-energy trauma (2 of which were accompanied by nonunion), and 7 patients developed isolated fracture nonunion. Additionally, through analysis of postoperative X-rays, we identified three typical types of fixation effects: cortical support type (Fig. 2 ), helical blade support type (Fig. 3 ), and nail support type (Fig. 4 ). Due to the substantial difference between the implant failure group and the non-failure group (1:18), propensity score matching was used to match the sample sizes of the implant failure group and the non-failure group at a 1:4 ratio. The matching variables were gender, age, height, and weight. After matching, the implant failure group and the matched non-failure group were compared. In the implant failure group, there were 8 cases of A1 type intertrochanteric fractures and 16 cases of A2 type, while 2 cases had a nail length of 170 mm, 12 cases had a nail length of 200 mm, and 10 cases had a nail length of 240 mm. The difference in fracture type and nail length between the two matched groups was not statistically significant ( p &gt; 0.05). The implant failure rate was 12.6% in patients with good and acceptable reduction quality and 64.7% in patients with poor reduction quality. The difference between the two matched groups was statistically significant ( p &lt; 0.05). Patients with complete medial femoral cortex continuity had an implant failure rate of 6.9%, whereas patients with incomplete medial femoral cortex continuity had an implant failure rate of 39.6%. The difference between the two groups was statistically significant ( p &lt; 0.05). The TAD was 21.6 (15.2–23.2) mm in the failure group and 23.3 (18.5–27.7) mm in the matched non-failure group. The difference in TAD between the two matched groups was statistically significant ( p &lt; 0.05). In terms of hospital stay, the failure group had a stay of 9 (7-12.5) days, and the matched non-failure group had a stay of 9 (8–12) days. The difference in hospital stay between the two matched groups was not statistically significant. The perioperative data of the two matched groups are shown in Table 3 . To further identify truly statistically significant risk factors, we conducted a logistic regression analysis of multiple factors. The analysis results indicated that poor reduction quality and discontinuity of the medial femoral cortex were independent risk factors for implant failure ( p &lt; 0.05). The study revealed that patients with poor reduction quality had a 22.2-fold higher incidence of implant failure compared to those with good or acceptable reduction. Additionally, patients with discontinuous medial femoral cortex exhibited a 12.1-fold higher rate of implant failure than those with continuous medial femoral cortex (Table 4 ). Discussion PFNA-II has been the primary method for the treatment of intertrochanteric fractures due to its advantages, such as shorter operative time and less bleeding [ 25 ]. AO/OTA 31-A1 and A2 fractures accounted for 80–94.7% of all intertrochanteric fractures [ 26 ]. The fracture line distribution and implant failure mechanisms of A1 and A2 fractures differ from those of reverse intertrochanteric fractures (A3) [ 27 ]. Many studies have not analyzed these two types of intertrochanteric fractures separately [ 28 ], which may lead to different results. For patients with A1 and A2 fractures, PFNA-II with a nail length no more than 240 mm is generally recommended [ 29 ]. Therefore, A1 and A2 intertrochanteric fractures stabilized with short PFNA-II (nail length no more than 240 mm) are the most common clinical cases. Determining the risk factors of implant failure is important for guiding the treatment of intertrochanteric fractures. In this study, there was a statistically significant difference in reduction quality between the failure group and the non-failure group, with a higher incidence of poor reduction in the failure group compared to the non-failure group. This may indicate that reduction quality is directly related to implant failure. Morvan A et al. retrospectively analyzed 228 patients aged over 75 years operated by Gamma 3 and Dynamic hip screw (DHS) [ 30 ]. The cut-out rate was 0.89% in cases with good reduction and 12.12% in cases with poor reduction. To minimize mechanical complications, great attention should be paid to fracture reduction and screw positioning. The same results were found in De Brujin’s study [ 31 ]. In our study, reduction quality was a risk factor for implant failure in intertrochanteric fractures treated with short PFNA-II. The implant failure rate was 64.7% in cases with poor reduction and 12.6% in cases with good and acceptable reduction. In multivariate analysis, the risk of implant failure in cases with poor reduction was 22.2 times higher than that in cases with good and acceptable reduction (OR = 22.2, 95%CI 5.44–122, p &lt;0.001). Furthermore, there was a statistically significant difference in the continuity of the medial femoral cortex between the failure group and the non-failure group, with a higher incidence of discontinuous medial femoral cortex in the failure group (79.2%) compared to the non-failure group (30.2%). Posterior medial support is considered an important factor affecting the stability of intertrochanteric fractures, which has been confirmed in both basic and clinical studies. Do et al. report that fixation strength was approximately 5 times greater in cases with a small defect of the femoral medial cortex than in those with a large defect [ 32 ]. Nie et al. confirmed by biomechanics that medial reconstruction is more important than lateral wall reconstruction [ 33 ]. Similar findings have been reported in clinical research. Mariani et al. analyzed 20 patients with nonunion of intertrochanteric fractures and found that all of them were unstable fractures and the reason for implant failure was related to posterior medial comminution [ 34 ]. In our study, the continuity of the medial femoral cortex was a risk factor for implant failure in intertrochanteric fractures treated with short PFNA-II. The implant failure rate was 39.6% in cases with discontinuous medial femoral cortex and 6.9% in cases with continuous intact medial femoral cortex. In multivariate analysis, the risk of implant failure in cases with discontinuous medial femoral cortex was 12.1 times higher than that in cases with good continuity (OR = 12.1, 95%CI 3.56–56.6, p &lt; 0.001). Even if the continuity of the femoral medial cortex is restored, implant failure such as cut-out may still occur during weight bearing. In a previous study, we proposed the concepts of secondary stability and cortical or implant support [ 35 ]. In this study, we also observed this phenomenon (Figs. 1 , 2 and 3 ). If the medial femoral shaft cortex or helical blade forms a support to the medial cortex of the head-neck fragment, the fracture heals (Figs. 1 and 2 ). Otherwise, the fracture will result in loss of reduction, and secondary stabilization may result in implant support (Fig. 3 ). According to our team’s earlier research in recent years, most of the anterior cortex in intertrochanteric fractures is a simple fracture, so the anterior medial cortex needs to provide stable support [ 36 ]. In patients with intertrochanteric fractures, osteoporosis is severe, and the strength of the anterior medial cortex is limited. In Ender’s classification, fractures with varus deformity and retrocurvature of the proximal fragment impacted into the trochanteric spongiosa, leaving a cavity after reduction with a danger of secondary varus, were defined as impacted fractures [ 37 ]. In 2013, Gotfried et al.also reported a similar phenomenon and defined it as negative and neutral support [ 38 ]. Since then, there have been many relevant reports, but the reasons for the failure of neutral support have not been explained [ 39 , 40 ]. Based on our radiological observations, we propose a theoretical hypothesis: after reduction of the head-neck fragment, a “triangular void structure” may form in the compressed area of cancellous bone within the medullary cavity. If the medial femoral cortex fails to provide stable mechanical support during weight bearing, this structure may lead to varus displacement of the head-neck fragment. The association between this “triangular void structure” and postoperative implant failure requires further biomechanical studies for verification. To improve the continuity of the medial cortex and reduction quality, we suggest several key technical points during surgery. Appropriate over-traction should be applied intraoperatively, with traction released during helical blade insertion and local compression applied, aiming to create a larger neck-shaft angle (abduction position reduction). For patients with wider medullary cavities, the greater trochanter entry point should be positioned as close as possible to the piriform fossa, reducing the distance between the main nail and the medial femoral wall, thereby increasing the opportunity for intramedullary nail support. Additionally, the helical blade should be fixed in the middle to lower portion of the femoral neck, both enhancing support to the femoral head and reducing the distance between the blade and the inferior cortex of the femoral neck, strengthening the blade’s supporting effect on the head-neck fragment. Through the comprehensive application of these technical details, the formation of the “triangular void structure” may be reduced, the stability of the medial cortex improved, thereby lowering the risk of implant failure. There are some limitations to this study. First, it was a retrospective study, and the data that were not initially collected could not be evaluated. Second, increasing the sample size of patients with implant failure could further enhance the statistical power of our analysis. Third, potential risk factors such as smoking, diabetes, and wound infection were not assessed, as this study primarily focused on potential risk factors that could be influenced by the surgeon. Fourth, this study had a long follow-up period of up to 8 years, and the included patients were operated on by five different surgeons, albeit all senior surgeons with annual surgical volumes exceeding 500 procedures. Although the surgical technique variations were minimal, surgical bias may still exist. Fifth, our hypothesis that the “triangular void structure” formed in the medullary canal after fracture reduction contributes to implant failure requires further validation through subsequent biomechanical testing and clinical research. Conclusion Poor reduction quality and discontinuity of the medial femoral cortex represent critical risk factors for implant failure in the treatment of intertrochanteric fractures (OA/OTA 31-A1 and A2) with short PFNA-II. Our research findings demonstrate that poor reduction quality dramatically increases the risk of implant failure by 22.2-fold, while discontinuity of the medial femoral cortex elevates this risk by 12.1-fold. Consequently, achieving anatomical reduction and reconstructing medial femoral support are paramount for minimizing implant failure rates. Additionally, we hypothesize that a “triangular void structure” formed between the nail and medial cortex may contribute to fixation failure even in cases with seemingly anatomic reduction. We strongly recommend that surgeons prioritize meticulous attention to reduction quality and ensuring continuity of the medial femoral cortex during the operative procedure to enhance both the immediate success rate and long-term clinical outcomes of PFNA-II fixation in these challenging fractures.
