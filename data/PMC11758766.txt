ABSTRACT Introduction Depression and anxiety are common in persons with cystic fibrosis (PwCF). Genetic polymorphisms in CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 are well‐established predictors of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) treatment failure yet have not been studied specifically in PwCF. The purpose of this study was to determine the rate of SSRI failure in PwCF and to identify factors that predict treatment failure. Methods A retrospective cohort study was conducted of PwCF prescribed an SSRI for depression or anxiety. Potential predictors of SSRI failure were compared between PwCF for SSRI treatment success and failure. When CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 pharmacogenetic (PGx) test results were available, SSRI selection was compared to appropriateness per Clinical PGx Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guideline. PGx results were not available at the time of prescribing. Results The study included 184 PwCF and 45% experienced SSRI treatment failure. Demographics, concomitant drug‐drug interactions, concomitant antidepressant medications, liver disease, and pulmonary function tests were not different between success and failure groups. Only 44 PwCF had PGx results and of these, only nine had actionable genotypes and prescribed an affected SSRI. This cohort had a failure rate of 78% (7 of 9) which was significantly higher compared to 40% (14 of 35) and 45% (83 of 184) in our PGx cohort and total cohort, respectively ( p = 0.04; p = 0.04). Conclusion SSRI failure rate in PwCF is high and consistent with rates of the general population. Greater depression severity and number of SSRIs trialed were seen in the failure group. Pre‐emptive PGx testing may improve SSRI success rates in PwCF. Introduction Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) published 27 evidence‐based guidelines for pharmacogenetics (PGx) that are available to guide dosing [ 1 ]. Since the FDA approval of the first cystic fibrosis (CF) transmembrane conductance regulatorCFTR modulator, ivacaftor, in 2012; treatment of CF has begun to focus on individual CFTR mutations [ 2 ]. To date there are four CFTR modulators available for use in the United States. The most appropriate modulator is determined by the patient's CFTR genotype and CFTR genetic testing is now recommended by by the CF Foundation (CFF) in all patients who are screen postive and have a sweat choloride value ≥ 60 mmol/L [ 3 ]. In additon to CFTR modulator therapy, persons with CF (PwCF) are exposed to many medications that CPIC guidelines, specifically antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) [ 4 , 5 ]. Mental health conditions such as anxiety and depression are common in persons with chronic illness, including PwCF [ 6 , 7 ]. Depression and anxiety have been estimated to affect up to 33% of adults and up to 25% of pediatric PwCF [ 6 , 8 ]. Depressive symptoms in PwCF have been associated with worse health‐related quality of life, even after controlling for lung function [ 9 ]. Genetic polymorphisms in CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 have been well‐established as predictors of treatment failure with SSRIs in the general population yet have not been studied specifically in PwCF [ 10 ]. Clinical pharmacology in PwCF differ from the general population due to pharmacokinetic variations and CF liver disease [ 11 ], but the impact of PGx polymorphisms in this population is unknown. The aim of this study was to determine the rate of SSRI failure in PwCF and to identify factors that predict treatment failure and determine the utility of improved SSRI prescribing in PwCF. 2. Materials and Methods Institutional Review Board approval was obtained to conduct a retrospective cohort study of adult PwCF treated within Indiana University Health System between January 1, 2015 and September 30, 2023. The IRB determined this study was exempt and did not require informed consent (IRB protocol number 16903). Subjects were included if they had a diagnosis of CF, identified by the International Classification of Diseases 10 code E.84, CF clinic patient list and prescription for an SSRI (citalopram, escitalopram, sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, or fluvoxamine) within the study timeframe. Subjects were excluded from analysis if there was no documented follow‐up after SSRI initiation or if they were prescribed the SSRI for an indication other than depression or anxiety, patients with co‐existing anxiety and depression diagnosis were included. To evaluate our primary outcome of SSRI treatment failure, we defined treatment failure by the occurrence of one or more of the following three criteria within 1 year following initiation of the SSRI: (1) electronic medical record (EMR) documentation of SSRI discontinuation due to inefficacy or adverse drug reaction (ADR), (2) a change or addition of antidepressant agent (not limited to SSRIs), or (3) SSRI dose adjustment outside of the initial titration period of 3 months. To identify factors that predict SSRI failure, demographic data, presence of concomitant drug‐drug interactions (DDI) defined as moderate or strong inhibitors or inducers of CYP2C19 or CYP2D6 [ 4 ], pre‐SSRI and post‐SSRI PHQ‐9 scores, the presence of liver disease (defined as liver function tests ≥ 3 times the upper limit of normal or a physician documented diagnosis of liver disease), and lung function measured as percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 min (ppFEV1) were compared between PwCF with SSRI failure and success. A secondary analysis was conducted with PGx test results when available. Implementation of PGx testing began at our institution in 2020 [ 12 ], therefore not available at the time of prescribing SSRI therapy. PGx testing was done using an analytically validated multiple polymerase chain reaction‐ (PCR‐) based genotyping panel using the Taqman OpenArray platform (ThermoFisher; Waltham, MA) CYP2D6 (rs16947(*2); rs1135840(*2); rs35742686(*3); rs3892097(*4); rs5030655(*6); rs5030867(*7); rs5030656(*9); rs1065852(*10); rs28371706(*17); rs59421388(*29); rs28371725(*41)) and CYP2C19 (rs4244285(*2); rs4986893(*3); rs28399504(*4); rs72552267(*6); rs41291556(*8); rs6413438(*10); rs12248560(*17)) genotype and phenotype were utilized to determine if the phenotype was actionable for the initial SSRI prescribed [ 12 ]. Actionable is defined as requires avoidance or dose adjustment to SSRI prescription based on Clinical PGx Implementation Consortium (CPIC) recommendations (Supporting Information S1: Table) [ 11 ]. SSRI failure rates were compared between (1) actionable PGx results with matching drug (PwCF had an actionable phenotypes in either CYP2D6 or CYP2C19 and an initial SSRI that required dose adjustment or avoidance based on phenotype) versus (2) non‐actionable or unknown phenotype (PwCF who either had no actionable phenotype, had an actionable phenotype, but prescribed a drug that did not need adjustment based on individual phenotype, or unknown PGx results). Statistical analyses were performed using Minitab Statistical Software (State College, Pennsylvania). Categorical variables were analyzed by chi‐square test. Continuous variables were analyzed using independent T ‐test if normally distributed or by Mann−Whitney U test if not normally distributed. 3. Results 3.1. Predictors of SSRI Failure We identified 184 PwCF at our institution that received an SSRI meeting the inclusion criteria. Failure of SSRI therapy occurred in 45% of this cohort. Baseline demographics were not significantly different between SSRI treatment success and failure groups. The presence of concomitant antidepressant or anxiolytic medications at the time of SSRI initiation was 45.8% in the failure group and 34.7% success group, but this was not significant ( p = 0.13). The presence of concomitant DDIs, liver disease, or ppFEV1 did not differ significantly between failure and success groups (Table 1 ). ADR were common, with 82 (45%) PwCF experiencing one or more ADRs. The most common ADRs were hyponatremia, gastrointestinal upset, insomnia, and psychological reactions. Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) scores were available for 52 PwCF (failure [ n = 23], success [ n = 29]). The change in PHQ‐9 score before and after SSRI initiation did not differ significantly between failure (−1.9 ± 6.3) and success (−2.7 ± 5.1) groups ( p = 0.6). However, PHQ‐9 scores were significantly higher in the failure group both before (12 ± 9 vs. 6 ± 11.5; p = 0.04) and after (8 ± 11 vs. 3 ± 7; p &lt; 0.01) SSRI initiation, compared to PHQ‐9 scores in the success group. 3.2. SSRI Prescribed The median (range) of SSRIs prescribed was 1 (1−3) SSRI in the treatment failure group and 1 (1−2) SSRI in the treatment success group ( p = 0.004). The most prescribed initial SSRI in our cohort was sertraline (36%) and no PwCFs were initially prescribed fluvoxamine (0%). The initial agent prescribed did not differ significantly between failure and success groups (Table 2 ). 3.3. PGx Results Of the 184 PwCF in our study cohort, PGx data for CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 was available for 44 PwCF. In this PGx cohort, the most prescribed SSRI was sertraline (50%), followed by escitalopram (22.7%), citalopram (9.1%), fluoxetine (9.1%), and paroxetine (6.8%). Of the 44 PwCF taking an SSRI, 20 patients (45.5%) discontinued SSRI for failure; 70% of failure was reported as an ADR. Gastrointestinal upset, sleep disturbance, and psychological reactions were the most common ADRs reported in this PGx cohort. The distribution of CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 metabolizer phenotypes are shown in (Figure 1 ). Sixty percent of PwCF did not have an actionable phenotype in either CYP2D6 (intermediate or normal metabolizer) and CYP2C19 phenotype (intermediate or normal metabolizer). However, 14 PwCF (30%) had an actionable genotype. Six had an actionable CYP2D6 phenotype (poor or ultrarapid metabolizer), 11 PwCFs had an actionable CYP2C19 phenotype (poor, rapid, or ultrarapid metabolizer), and three PwCF had both actionable CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 genotypes. Of these 14 PwCFs with actionable phenotypes that would require dose adjustments, only nine were prescribed an initial SSRI that corresponded to their PGx actionability and would have required dose adjustments based on individual phenotype. In this cohort, treatment failure occurred in seven of the nine patients (78%) compared to 14 of 35 (40%) with either nonactionable genotypes or patients that had actionable genotypes but were prescribed a drug that did not need adjustment based on their specific genotype ( p = 0.04) (Figure 2A ). In a further comparison, we compared the failures in this cohort with actionable genotypes to our total cohort many of which had unknown genotypes. The failure rate of 78% (7 of 9) in the PGx actionable group compared to 45% (83 of 184) in our total cohort ( p = 0.04) (Figure 2B ). 4. Discussion We report the incidence of SSRI treatment failure in a cohort of PwCF receiving treatment for depression or anxiety. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the incidence and potential predictors of SSRI treatment failure in PwCF. Specifically, our results highlight three key findings: (1) SSRI treatment failure is common in PwCF, (2) severity of depression and prior treatments may result in higher SSRI failure rates, and (3) SSRI treatment failure was significantly higher in a cohort of PwCF with actionable genotypes and prescribed a therapy not recommended for their genotype. In our cohort we report 45% of PwCF experienced SSRI treatment failure with initial SSRI prescribed and PwCF received up to three SSRIs before finding an effective SSRI. This is consistent with studies in the general population, which have reported SSRI failure rates of 35–52% [ 13 , 14 ]. Rates of ADRs were high in our cohort, with 45% of PwCF experiencing at least one ADR. In a study of 125 South Asian subjects with moderate to severe depression treated with escitalopram, sertraline, fluoxetine or paroxetine, ADRs were experienced by 26.4% of subjects and included drowsiness, psychological disturbances, and GI upset most [ 8 ]. This markedly lower rate of ADRs compared to our population may be explained by the exclusion of those with comorbid chronic medical conditions as well as comorbid psychiatric disorders. The STAR*D trial, which included those with comorbid medical and psychiatric conditions, reported ADR rates similar to our cohort of 49.9% 12 weeks after citalopram initiation [ 9 ]. In our cohort, ADRs lead to discontinuation in 11.4% of PwCF and contributed to 25.3% of treatment failures. This is consistent with meta‐analyses that report early discontinuation due to ADR in 5‐12% of subjects, and comparable to the 8.6% reported in the STAR*D trial [ 15 ]. The severity of depression and prior treatments may result in higher SSRI failure. Depression severity, measured by PHQ‐9 scores, was significantly higher in the treatment failure group both before and after SSRI initiation, compared to PHQ‐9 scores in the treatment success group. Additionally, the presence of concomitant antidepressant or anxiolytic medications at the time of SSRI initiation trended toward a higher percentage in the treatment failure group (45.8%) compared to the treatment successful group (34.7%), although it did not meet statistical significance. This is consistent with studies of SSRI response in the general population, including the STAR*D trial, which reports significantly greater severity of depression, higher rate of comorbid psychological conditions, and longer duration of depressive episode associated with lower remission rates with citalopram [ 15 ]. The use of PGx data to guide initial SSRI selection in PwCF may improve rates of treatment success for depression and anxiety. Although we have PGx results for some patients, these results were not available at the time of prescribing, therefore based on our data we are unable to confirm that having PGx available at the time of prescribing would have been beneficial. Based on the significant difference in SSRI failure rates in those with actionable PGx results compared to those with either unknown or unactionable PGx results, this supports the benefit of having PGx available to avoid treatment failure may suggest the utility of PGx data to improve success rates with other SSRIs that are dependent on CYP450 metabolism. The benefit of PGx to guide SSRI therapy has been shown in several large clinicals trials and have prompted guidelines [ 10 , 16 , 17 ]. Although SSRIs are typically not thought of as central to CF therapy, in a cohort of 576 PwCF 22% of PwCF were prescribed a SSRI and could have potential benefited from PGx to guide therapy [ 18 ]. Recent evidence has suggested that treating mental health concerns whether primary or resulting as a side‐effect of CFTR modulator therapies [ 7 , 19 ], deserve just as much consideration as other aspects of CF care. For patients with CF, pulmonologists may be the first to address mental health conditions before referrals to psychiatry. When comparing treatment of major depressive disorder by psychiatrist and primary care providers, when all patients had PGx testing, depression treatment outcomes were significantly better in patients treated by primary care providers compared to those treated by psychiatrists [ 20 ]. This suggests that use of PGx by pulmonologist and CF teams could improve mental health outcomes. This study was not without limits. The retrospective nature of the study may have impacted the availability of collected data, as only 52 PwCF had PHQ‐9 scores recorded in their medical record both before and after SSRI initiation and PGx results were only available for 44 PwCF. It is possible that the ADRs attributed to SSRIs in our study could have been caused by other medication. For our study, determination of ADRs and inefficacy were based on EMR clinician documentation and our ability to determine relatedness of ARDs to SSRIs due to the retrospective study design. Additionally, our institution did not implement preemptive PGx testing until 2020, and since we evaluated PwCF initiated on SSRIs from 2015 to 2023, data were not available before initiating SSRI treatment. Furthermore, this secondary analysis, our study was not powered to evaluate PGx efficacy. Despite these limitations, this is the first study to our knowledge evaluating the incidence of SSRI failure and its predictors, including the effect of PGx on SSRI response, in PwCF. The results of this study suggest a high SSRI failure rate in PwCF. Availability of pre‐emptive PGx testing could improve initial SSRI selection leading to improved treatment success in PwCF and should be considered by the CF care team.
