This study aims to describe the differences in diet and weight status of the adult urban Brazilian population by commuting time to work (CTW). Cross-sectional study with data from 69,713 adult workers (20–59 years) from the 2017–2018 Household Budget Survey. The mean consumption of 19 food groups was estimated under four CTW categories for a subsample of 16,967 adults from urban areas (adults from rural areas were excluded). Linear models estimated the predicted mean consumption and 95% confidence interval (CI) by CTW level, adjusted for potential confounding factors. In contrast, logistic models were used to estimate the association between overweight and CTW. The statistical software SAS OnDemand for Academics was used and the analyses considered the sampling weights and the complex sampling plan. Approximately 25% of adults commuted for up to 5 min, 47% for 6–30 min, 17% for 31 min to 1 h, and 10% for more than 1 h. Individuals with CTW &gt; 1 h had lower fruit and higher bean consumption than those with CTW &lt; 5 min. The adjusted analysis showed that fruit consumption remained lower among those with CTW &gt; 1 h. There were no significant differences in the consumption of other foods, and no association was found between overweight and CTW. Urban adults with longer CTW had lower fruit consumption, highlighting the need for policies to improve urban mobility and access to healthy foods. Overweight, one of the possible consequences of such dietary risks, contributes globally to mortality and loss of quality of life 1 . Brazilian national data from the last two Household Budget Surveys (HBS) conducted in 2008–2009 and 2017–2018 showed a significant decline in the consumption of fruits, beans, and dairy products and higher consumption of sandwiches and pizzas across all age groups and income brackets 2 . Recent national data from the Vigitel study (Surveillance of Risk and Protection Factors for Chronic Diseases by Telephone Survey) reiterate the trend of reduced bean consumption and low prevalence of recommended consumption of fruits and vegetables from 2006 to 2023, concomitant with the significant increase in the prevalence of overweight and obesity, reaching 24.3% among adults in 2023 3 . Active adults have reported not having enough time to perform all these important daily tasks, such as eating, exercising, housework, leisure activities, and sleeping 4 – 6 . This event is called “time scarcity” and has already been recognized as a health inequality precisely because many neglected activities are important for human health, which include planning, purchasing, preparing, and consuming food 7 . The time spent preparing food among American adults, for example, fell by approximately 35% between 1965 and 2007, simultaneously with the higher consumption of ultra-processed products and the prevalence of overweight in the country 8 . Among the work elements that impact this collective backdrop of time scarcity, commuting time to work (CTW) is highly relevant since it has been increasing in recent decades in several cities globally 4 Since the 1990s, commuting times of over one hour have increased in Brazil’s metropolitan regions, while remaining relatively stable in non-metropolitan areas 9 . Most Brazilian studies examining the rise in CTW, along with its causes and consequences, have focused on urban areas, particularly in capital cities and metropolitan regions 9 – 11 . Findings indicate that unbridled and unplanned urban expansion has facilitated the concentration of low-income populations in peripheral areas far from the urban core, as well as the formation of heavy traffic through high individual motorization rates and insufficient public transportation systems 10 , 11 . Given the substantial worsening of this issue in urban centers, investigating its potential health implications for urban populations has become increasingly relevant. An American study investigated the relationship between the parents’ mean CTW and eating routines. It found that those with a CTW greater than 30 min were likelier to have less access to fresh foods and felt little control over their children’s food choices 6 . An Australian cohort revealed that fruit and vegetable consumption was slightly lower among workers with higher CTW levels 4 . We did not find a study in the current literature that evaluated the relationship between diet and weight status by CTW in a representative sample of the Brazilian population. Thus, the present study aimed to describe the differences in diet and weight status by CTW levels in the adult Brazilian population living in urban areas. The hypothesis is that urban adults with longer CTW likely have less available time for food-related activities (such as meal planning, purchasing and preparation) and consequently have lower consumption of healthy foods, higher consumption of unhealthy foods, and higher likelihood of being overweight compared to urban adults with shorter CTW. Methods This cross-sectional study employed a secondary database with public and unrestricted access from the Household Budget Survey (HBS 2017–2018) developed by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). The HBS mainly aims to collect information about the Brazilian population’s financial situation and living conditions, covering the country’s five macro-regions. Study population The 2017–2018 HBS sampling plan was conducted by clusters divided into two stages: census tracts and private households. The first stage consisted of stratifying the census tracts (2010 Demographic Census) by the mean income of the heads of households, which were selected by sampling with probability proportional to the number of households in each tract. In the second stage, households were selected by simple random sampling, ensuring geographic and temporal representativeness of the Brazilian population. The collection occurred from July 11, 2017, to July 9, 2018. The HBS sample included 57,920 households and 178,431 participating individuals throughout the national territory 12 . Of these, 45.6% had information regarding CTW, and 85.6% were adults (20–59 years old). Thus, the present analysis totals 69,713 active working adults (39% of the initial HBS sample). This sample was used in Table 1 to analyze the frequency of CTW categories by sociodemographic characteristics (Fig. 1 ). In this study, a low concentration of longer commuting times (above 30 min) was observed among individuals from rural areas (Table 1 ). In general, commuting times differ significantly between urban and rural areas, being a more prevalent and growing issue in urban centers 9 – 11 . Considering this, 14,943 adults from rural areas were excluded from the analysis. A subsample of the general HBS sample was selected to assess individual food consumption, known as the National Food Survey (NFS). This subsample encompassed 34.7% of the households in the total HBS sample, totaling 20,112 households and 46,164 participating individuals 13 . In total, 16,967 (37% of the NFS subsample) were adults living in urban areas with information on CTW. This sample was considered for other analyses related to food consumption and weight status according to the CTW categories (Fig. 1 ). Data collection and analysis Food consumption assessment (NFS 2017–2018) The dietary assessment included individuals at least 10 and older living in the households subsampled for the NFS. Two non-consecutive days of 24-h recalls were adopted, in which participants recorded their food and beverages consumed the day before the interview. Interviewers followed the Multiple Pass Method (MPM) to minimize recall bias 12 , 14 . Food consumption information included time, food preparation, number of household measures, additional items, place of consumption, and meal type consumed. The food and beverage database contained 1,832 items from the list of foods reported in the 2008–2009 HBS. This database was updated by incorporating items reported in a representative Brazilian study with adolescents and items from the GloboDiet database, a platform for the standardized collection of dietary data, which was adapted for Brazil by a group of researchers from various universities 13 . Records with energy intake below 300 kcal, containing fewer than five items or with periods exceeding three hours without any consumption, were deemed incomplete and excluded (682 records). Additionally, records with energy intake above 10,000 kcal, in which reported quantities were highly improbable, were also excluded (24 records) 13 . For this analysis, we considered the first day of the 24-h recall (24hR) of adults living in urban areas who comprised the NFS subsample. The foods mentioned were categorized into 19 food groups that are diet quality markers. These groups were previously identified in studies on the diet of the Brazilian population, as follows: rice and other cereals; beans and leguminous plants; fruits; fruit juices; vegetables and vegetable-based preparations; dairy products; beef and pork; chicken, poultry, and eggs; fish and seafood; roots and tubers; soft drinks and sweetened beverages; sausages, processed meats, and canned foods; cakes, baked sweets, and sweet biscuits; oils and fats; snacks, pizzas and crackers; coffee; alcoholic beverages; flours and pasta; and bread. We recorded the number of household measures for each food item, and the corresponding amount in grams was estimated from the “Table of measurements referred to food consumed in Brazil” 13 . Weight status assessment (NFS 2017–2018) The NFS contained two questions that asked participants to self-report their weight and height 13 . Weight should be reported in kilograms (kg) and height in centimeters (cm), always consisting of whole numbers. At the end of the collection, we recorded weights between 20 and 150 kg and heights between 115 and 204 cm. The body mass index (BMI) was estimated using the formula: weight (kg)/height (m) 2 . Individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m 2 were classified as overweight using the cutoff points recommended by the World Health Organization 15 . Commuting time to work Commuting time to work (CTW) was collected through the individual work and income questionnaire. Individuals aged 10 years or older who had contributed to the household budget during the 12 months prior to the interview were evaluated in this specific HBS questionnaire 12 . The participant could report multiple jobs but should initially respond about his “main job”. The question “How long does it usually take to commute to this job?” was asked within the “main job” section, with five response options: up to 5 min; 6–30 min; more than 30 min to 1 h; more than 1–2 h; more than 2 h 12 . A sixth “no declaration” was available within the database, intended for inadequate or missing completion, representing approximately 1% of the total completions and not included in the present analysis. In the present study, CTW was initially analyzed under the five categories provided by IBGE (≤ 5 min; 6–30 min; 31 min to 1 h; &gt; 1 h to 2 h; &gt; 2 h). The last two CTW categories (&gt; 1 h to 2 h and over 2 h) were grouped as “Above 1 h” in the subsequent analyses regarding food consumption and weight status. This resulted in four levels of CTW (≤ 5 min; 6–30 min; 31 min to 1 h; &gt; 1 h). Sociodemographic characteristics We used information from the HBS related to residents’ demographic and socioeconomic data, besides household characteristics, which included variables such as sex (male and female), age group (20–29 years, 30–39 years, 40–49 years, and 50–59 years), self-reported race (white, mixed-race, black, and others), and schooling years (none, early childhood education, elementary school, high school, and higher education) to determine the schooling level. The variable “Federative Unit” was employed to identify the respondent’s Brazilian state of residence and, subsequently, categorized per the five macro-regions of the country (North, Northeast, Midwest, South, and Southeast). Also, the household’s situation was classified per its location, categorized as urban or rural. Information on the financial income of all residents in the household was divided by the total number of residents in the household, resulting in the per capita household income, later categorized into four minimum wage bands (&lt; 1 minimum wage, 1–2 minimum wages, 2–3 minimum wages, &gt; 3 minimum wages) considering the minimum wage in force at the time (R$ 954.00 or US$ 297.50 on January 15, 2018). Statistical analysis The relative frequency and respective 95% confidence intervals of five CTW categories were estimated per sociodemographic variables (age group, sex, race, ​​residence area, region, per capita household income in minimum wages, and schooling years) for the 69,713 individuals. The objective of the analysis presented in Table 1 was to examine the percentage distribution of the exposure variable (CTW) according to sociodemographic characteristics, in order to understand the behavior of this variable in the Brazilian population. For Tables 2 and 3 , the analysis was conducted to compare the mean food consumption across CTW categories. In Table 4 , the analysis aimed to compare the odds of overweight status across the different CTW categories. The per capita means and 95% confidence intervals of the consumption of foods classified in the 19 food groups were estimated per four CTW categories with and without stratification for the five macro-regions. We adopted a multiple linear regression model to estimate the predicted means of the consumption of the same foods per the CTW categories adjusted for potential confounding factors: sex (female and male), age (continuous), race (white, mixed-race, black, and others), macro-region (North, Northeast, Southeast, South, and Midwest), income (&lt; 1 minimum wage, 1–2 minimum wages, 2–3 minimum wages, &gt; 3 minimum wages) and schooling (none, early childhood education, elementary education, high school, and higher education). These variables were selected based on the literature, considering their relationship with the outcome variables (diet 16 , 17 and Brazilian population weight 18 , 19 ) and the exposure variable (CTW) as indicated by the results presented in Table 1 of the current study. Additionally, the variables were assessed for collinearity to ensure the robustness of the model through the VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) collinearity test. The VIF values ranged from 1.0 to 1.4, indicating low correlation. Linear regression was applied to each food group, with the mean daily weight of the foods in each group as the dependent variable and the CTW categories as the primary independent variable. Statistical significance analysis was conducted by comparing confidence intervals (95%CI), in which the non-overlap of 95%CI had a statistically significant difference (p &lt; 0.05). The association between overweight (dependent variable) and CTW categories (independent variable) was calculated by estimating the odds ratio (OR) using a logistic regression model stratified by sex (female and male) and adjusted for age (continuous), race (white, mixed-race, black, and others), macro-region (North, Northeast, Southeast, South, and Midwest), income (&lt; 1 minimum wage, 1–2 minimum wages, 2–3 minimum wages, &gt; 3 minimum wages), and schooling (none, early childhood education, elementary education, high school, and higher education). This analysis was stratified by variable “sex” due to significant differences in the prevalence of overweight between Brazilian men and women 18 , 19 . The CTW of “up to 5 min” was considered the reference category. All the statistical analyses considered the sampling weights and the complex sampling plan and were performed using the software Statistical Analysis System—SAS OnDemand for Academics version 3.81 ( https://support.sas.com/en/software/ondemand-for-academics-support.html ). Graphs were generated using the package “ggplot2” on R software version 4.2.3, with IDE RStudio version 2023.6.1.524. Ethical aspects This study used secondary databases from the 2017–2018 Household Budget Survey, which are publicly and unrestrictedly accessible. The microdata are available on the institution’s website ( https://www.ibge.gov.br/estatisticas/sociais/saude/24786-pesquisa-de-orcamentosfamiliares2.html?=&amp;t=microdados ). Therefore, the work does not require assessment by the Research Ethics Committee of the educational institution. There is also no conflict of interest to be declared. Results Most of the 69,713 adults surveyed were male (54%), lived in urban areas (87%), had completed high school (44%), and earned up to 1 minimum wage (36%). Approximately 25% of adults commuted to work in up to 5 min, 47% from 6 to 30 min, 17% from 31 min to 1 h, 8% from 1 to 2 h, and 2% in more than 2 h. Approximately 23% of urban residents commuted in up to 5 min, while this frequency almost doubled in rural areas (41%). Individuals in the Southeast region had higher frequencies of high CTW, in which 13,6% commuted to work for more than 1 h, unlike the other regions, in which 10%, 8%, 7%, and 5% of CTW higher than 1 h were observed in the Midwest, North, Northeast, and South, respectively (Table 1 ). A lower mean per capita consumption of fruits and a higher mean per capita consumption of beans was observed among urban adults with a CTW of more than 1 h compared to those with a CTW of up to 5 min. The mean fruit intake was approximately 40% lower among individuals who commuted to work in more than 1 h than those who commuted in up to 5 min (53 g vs. 84 g). In contrast, the mean consumption of beans among adults with CTW above 1 h was approximately 15% higher than that among individuals with up to 5 min of CTW (196 g vs. 171 g). We observed that the mean intake of flour and pasta among adults with CTW from 31 min to 1 h was 30% higher than the mean in CTW of up to 5 min (65 g vs. 50 g). No statistically significant differences were found in the mean consumption of foods from the other food groups (Table 2 ). There was a statistically significant difference in the mean consumption of fruits, meats, and tubers per the CTW categories in some regions of the country (Fig. 2 ). The mean consumption of tubers in the North was approximately three times higher among those who commuted in up to 5 min than those who commuted in more than 1 h (17 g and 4 g). Adults living in the Northeast with a CTW of 6 to 30 min had a mean meat consumption approximately 40% higher than those who commuted in more than 1 h (65 g vs. 47 g). Moreover, a 30 to 40% lower mean fruit consumption was observed among adults in the other three CTW categories compared to CTW of up to 5 min in the Southeast (47 g vs. 69 g), South (60 g vs. 104 g) and Midwest (54 g vs. 92 g) (Fig. 2 ). No statistically significant differences were found in the mean consumption of foods from the other food groups in the stratified analysis (Supplementary Table 1 ). Only the fruits group remained when the food consumption means were adjusted for potential confounding factors, showing statistically significant differences per the CTW categories. Individuals with CTW of more than 1 h had a 30% lower mean consumption than the consumption observed among those with CTW of up to 5 min (56 g vs. 79 g) (Table 3 ). No statistically significant association between overweight and CTW was observed in the crude and adjusted analyses for the total sample and by sex (Table 4 ). Discussion Most of the Brazilian adult population commuted within 30 min to work, and there was a low frequency of CTW over 2 h compared to the other categories. However, regional disparities were identified in this distribution, both for the area of residence and the macro-regions of the country. The urban area, for example, had a higher mean CTW than the rural area, while the Southeast had a higher mean CTW than the country’s other regions. Regarding the differences observed in food consumption by CTW category, a significantly lower crude and adjusted mean intake of fruits was observed in the highest CTW strata. Longer commuting times in urban centers have already been observed in other countries with intense urban growth, such as China and the United States 9 , 20 . In this backdrop, the Southeast has cities with high urbanization and spatial segregation rates, such as São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro 21 . The CTW is a component of spatial segregation observed in urban centers since the most vulnerable portion of the population generally lives in peripheral areas far from their workplaces and has access to substandard transportation systems 9 , 20 . Further studies are needed to assess other regions’ urbanization and urban mobility conditions to understand the specific factors involved in high CTW. Researchers have already studied the mobility conditions of Brazilian metropolitan regions and municipalities. However, data such as connectivity between roads and public vs. private transport use ratio were absent in several municipalities, mainly in the North and Northeast 22 , 23 . The lower fruit consumption observed in higher CTW categories was similarly identified by Oostenbach et al. 4 , who found fewer fruit servings consumed as the sample’s weekly CTW increased by 1 h. The time scarcity associated with this aspect of work may be related to the need to minimize time and effort in selecting, purchasing, preparing, and consuming healthy foods 4 . In their study, a serving was considered one medium piece of fruit or two small pieces, but it was not established how much a serving would be in grams 4 . Therefore, further research on the subject that measured the differences in food consumption more precisely, such as in grams, would be valuable to compare it with the present study. Compared with the up to 5-min CTW, we observed a lower mean fruit consumption among adults in the other CTW categories in the Southeast, South, and Midwest. Furthermore, this was the only food group with statistically significant differences after adjusting for possible confounders. A similar result was observed in a US study in which parents with high CTW reported difficulties accessing fresh fruits and vegetables for their children 6 . Despite not using individual CTW and not studying fruit consumption directly, White et al. findings (2019) shed light on potential difficulties in accessing healthy foods, such as reaching a market and poor local transportation infrastructure. In this sense, it is crucial to consider the spatial distribution of food supply in the city to make the purchase and transportation of these foods viable amid the city’s hustle and bustle 6 , 24 . The study by Oostenbach et al. 4 had a method closer to ours since it used individual CTW and self-reported food consumption of fruits and vegetables. The consumption of meals outside the home was higher when the CTW was also higher, while the consumption of fruits was slightly lower, which corroborates the results of the present study. In this context, the declining healthy eating markers are possibly associated with the lack of time to access and prepare healthy foods. Fruits are highly perishable foods that require more frequent purchases and greater attention to storage time, which can be impaired with high CTW. Nonetheless, some of the most commonly consumed fruits in Brazil are quick and easy to handle anywhere, even without utensils, such as bananas, apples, tangerines, and grapes 13 . However, Brazilian cities in general do not provide favorable environments for the purchase and consumption of healthy foods on streets, sidewalks, stations, and public transportation. On the contrary, in addition to the high availability and widespread advertising of ultra-processed foods, the vast majority of businesses in these urban spaces predominantly sell a variety of packaged products that are high in sugar, sodium, and additives 25 , 26 . Thus, access to fruit consumption is not only neglected but also significantly hindered. The consumption of snacks, soft drinks, and sweets was notably high. This supports findings from other studies on Brazilian dietary patterns, which highlight a high intake of ultra-processed foods, such as pizzas, soft drinks, biscuits, and sausages 27 . No significant differences were found across CTW categories regarding these groups, considered markers of an unhealthy diet. Similarly, an Australian study with 769 adults found no association between long CTW and higher consumption of snacks and soft drinks 24 . These findings show the need for further research on the Brazilian population’s diet according to CTW, considering alternative food classifications and deeper analyses of CTW’s links to time scarcity. Other studies have also observed the lack of association between overweight and CTW 5 , 28 . Notably, obesity is a multifactorial disease, with usually slow progression and treatment 29 , 30 . Therefore, collecting and analyzing CTW at one point does not encompass such gradual changes in weight status. Furthermore, we should underscore that commuting is divided into passive (by car, bus, or other motorized vehicles) or active (by walking, cycling, or other physical activity). Passive commuting is associated with higher stress levels 31 and lower physical activity levels 5 , which could affect long-term weight status. Most commuting to work in Brazil is passive 9 . From this perspective, the present study has some limitations. Since it is a cross-sectional analysis, it is impossible to observe a causal relationship between CTW and nutritional status. Furthermore, the lack of information on the type of commuting (active or passive) limited a better understanding of the participants’ reality since active commuting could contribute to greater weight adequacy than passive commuting 5 , 28 . Another limitation would be possible information biases in self-reporting food consumption, weight and height. However, nationally representative studies of the Brazilian population tend to show a high agreement rate between measured and self-reported weight and height 32 . The present study has potential, such as using a national sample representative of the Brazilian population, questionnaires, and standardized data collection strategies to reduce information biases, such as using the multiple-pass method to collect dietary data 12 , 13 . The results of this study revealed for the first time that fruit consumption, a healthy diet marker, was lower in Brazilian adults living in urban areas who took longer to commute to work than those with less commuting time. In this setting, policies are crucial to reduce CTW among the population and increase access to healthy food through investments in urban mobility and fairer, more egalitarian, and sustainable urbanization. In the short term, encouraging the increase in easy and affordable access points to healthy food in cities, including in places with access to public transportation, such as bus terminals, train stations, ferries, and subway stations, would be a simple and effective alternative to increase the Brazilian population’s fruit consumption.
