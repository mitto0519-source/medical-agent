Abstract Abstract Objectives This study assessed changes in the prevalence of malnutrition and obesity in children from North Korean (NK) refugee families resettling in South Korea (SK). Design A longitudinal cohort study. Setting This study, conducted between 2017 and 2023, recruited participants through snowball sampling using local Hana Centres, alternative schools, religious organisations, civic groups, and other community-based networks. Participants Of the 441 children initially recruited, 406 participated in the baseline survey after excluding 35 with missing height or weight data. Among them, 187 (46.1%) completed a follow-up survey conducted at least 1 year later. Primary outcome measures The prevalences of stunting, underweight, wasting and obesity were defined according to the 2017 Korean National Growth Chart: stunting as height-for-age below the third percentile, underweight as weight-for-age below the fifth percentile, wasting as weight-for-height below the fifth percentile for ages 0–23 months or body mass index (BMI)-for-age below the fifth percentile for ages 2–18 years and obesity as BMI-for-age at or above the 95th percentile for ages 2–18 years. Results The prevalence of stunting was 7.5% at baseline and 5.4% at follow-up, while those of underweight and wasting were 6.4% vs 5.4% and 11.2% vs 5.9%, respectively; however, none of these changes were statistically significant. In contrast, the prevalence of obesity increased significantly from 11.1% at baseline to 18.8% at follow-up (p=0.045). Conclusion Despite residing in SK for an extended period, children from NK refugee families continue to experience malnutrition, while the prevalence of obesity is rising significantly. STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY This study is notable for its longitudinal design, in which a cohort of children from North Korean (NK) refugee families was repeatedly surveyed to assess changes in nutritional status following resettlement in South Korea. The use of snowball sampling limits the generalisability of the findings to the broader population of children from NK refugee families. Key factors that may influence children’s nutritional status, such as household economic conditions and parental education levels, were not assessed. The follow-up loss rate was high (53.9%), potentially influenced by the concurrent COVID-19 pandemic. This may have led to the underestimation of the results, as children with greater health issues or socio-economic disadvantages were more likely to be excluded. Introduction As of the end of December 2023, 34 078 North Korean (NK) refugees had entered South Korea (SK). 1 While the number exceeded 2000 annually during the early 2010s, it declined sharply to 63 in 2021 and 67 in 2022, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, subsequently increasing to 196 in 2023. 1 The total number included 5116 children under the age of 19, accounting for 15.0% of all NK refugees. However, accurate numbers on children growing up in NK refugee families in SK are not available as a result of their diverse backgrounds. For instance, some children were born in NK and defected to SK, others were born in China and then resettled in SK and still others were born and raised in SK. 2 Considering these circumstances, the actual number might be higher than the statistics provided by the Ministry of Unification. 3 Research on NK refugee children has increased with the growing number of NK refugees, focusing mainly on parenting, 4 adaptation to SK society 5 and mental health 6 ; however, little emphasis has been given to their nutrition and health. 7 Studies of the nutritional status of NK refugee children can be categorised into three distinct periods 8 : immediately after defection from NK, immediately after entering SK and after resettling in SK. Research on subjects immediately after defecting from NK is limited due to difficulties contacting the subjects, 9 while the study of subjects immediately after entering SK has mainly used health check-up data from Hanawon, which is an education and resettlement support institute for NK refugees operated by the SK government. 10 11 Previous research on the children from NK refugee family resettled in SK has been conducted through alternative schools, religious facilities and civic organisations, many of which have reported high rates of severe malnutrition. 3 11 12 Additionally, recent studies have drawn attention to the double burden of malnutrition and obesity. 13 14 However, most of these studies were cross-sectional in design, making it difficult to assess changes in nutritional status over time. 11 12 Therefore, the present study conducted a follow-up survey at least 1 year after the baseline assessment to evaluate changes in the prevalence of malnutrition and obesity among children from NK refugee families following their resettlement in SK. Materials and methods Subjects This study was conducted between 2017 and 2023 and investigated children under the age of 13 who had at least one parent who was an NK refugee. We recruited participants using snowball sampling through local Hana Centres, alternative schools, religious organisations, civic groups and other community-based networks. Hana Centres are public institutions that support the adaptation and stable resettlement of NK refugees in local communities. Prior to participation, we explained to the parents or legal guardians of the children and obtained informed consent. Further details regarding study procedures for children from NK refugee families have been described in a previous publication. 15 A total of 441 children were recruited. Of these, 35 participants were excluded due to missing data on height or weight. As a result, 406 children participated in the baseline survey. Among them, 187 completed a follow-up survey conducted at least 1 year after the baseline survey, resulting in a follow-up participation rate of 46.1%. For the present study, analyses were conducted on the subset of 187 children who completed both the baseline and follow-up surveys. Measures Through a structured questionnaire, we acquired the participants’ gender, date of birth, country of birth and parents’ nationalities. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg while the subjects were dressed in light clothes using the CAS HE-58 scale (CAS, Gyeonggi-do, Korea), and height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm in stocking feet using the mobile InKids height metre (InLab S50, InBody, Seoul, Korea). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m 2 ). Malnutrition and obesity The Korea Centre for Disease Control and Prevention and Korea Paediatrics Society developed the 2017 Korean National Growth Chart for Children and Adolescents (KNGC). 16 We used the 2017 KNGC to assess the prevalence of malnutrition and obesity among the subjects. The 2017 KNGC defined stunting as height-for-age below the 3rd percentile for age, wasting as weight-for-height below the 5th percentile for ages 0–23 months and BMI-for-age below the 5th percentile for ages 2–18 years, underweight as weight-for-age below the 5th percentile for age and obesity as BMI-for-age equal to or above the 95th percentile for ages 2–18 years. Statistical analysis All data were analysed using SPSS Statistics ver. 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as means and SD. Statistical differences in the prevalences of stunting, underweight, wasting and obesity between the baseline and follow-up surveys were evaluated using the McNemar test, with effect sizes quantified using Cohen’s g. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results General characteristics of subjects Table 1 summarises the general characteristics of subjects. The subjects were 53.5% male. The country of birth was SK in 58.7%, China in 34.8% and NK in 6.5%. The mother’s nationality was NK in 96.8%, SK in 1.6% and Chinese in 1.6%, while the father’s nationality was Chinese in 46.5%, SK in 29.2% and NK in 24.3%. Changes In physical status between the baseline and follow-up surveys Table 2 shows changes in physical status between the baseline and follow-up surveys. The mean follow-up period was 2.5±1.1 years. Height, weight, BMI and percentile of weight for age increased significantly in the follow-up survey compared with the baseline survey. However, the percentile of height for age, percentile of weight for age and percentile of BMI for age did not show significant differences. Changes in the prevalence of malnutrition and obesity between baseline and follow-up surveys Table 3 shows changes in the prevalence of malnutrition and obesity between the baseline and follow-up surveys. The prevalence of stunting was 7.5% at baseline and 5.4% at follow-up (p=0.581, Cohen’s g=0.021), and underweight prevalence was 6.4% and 5.4%, respectively (p=0.727, Cohen’s g=0.011); neither showed a statistically significant difference. Wasting prevalence decreased from 11.2% to 5.9% (p=0.064, Cohen’s g=0.054), which was also not statistically significant. In contrast, the prevalence of obesity showed a significant increase, rising from 11.1% at baseline to 18.8% at follow-up (p=0.045, Cohen’s g=0.086). Discussion This study evaluated changes in nutritional status among children aged under 13 years with at least one parent being a NK refugee. A total of 187 children participated in both the baseline and follow-up surveys conducted at least 1 year afterwards. The baseline and follow-up surveys of the nutritional status of children from NK refugee families found no significant differences in the prevalence of stunting (7.5% vs 5.4%), underweight (6.4% vs 5.4%) and wasting (11.2% vs 5.9%). Studies of NK refugee children shortly after they entered SK reported significantly higher rates of stunting (up to 30%) and underweight (10–30%). 10 17 Subsequent studies aimed to assess improvements in the nutritional status of NK refugee children after resettlement in SK by comparing them with their SK counterparts. 12 , 15 One study compared 103 NK adolescent refugees with SK adolescents, reporting that the former were significantly shorter and lighter. 12 Another study compared 527 children from NK refugee families who had resettled in SK for over 4 years with 1609 SK children, 14 revealing that although the nutritional status improved, compared with immediately after resettlement, children from NK refugee families still suffered higher rates of malnutrition than their SK counterparts. Furthermore, most studies of the nutritional status of children from NK refugee families had cross-sectional designs, 11 12 15 18 with few studies investigating changes over a prolonged period. For instance, one study conducted a follow-up survey 2 years after the baseline survey, 13 showing significant improvements in both the prevalence of stunting and underweight, results that differed from our findings. However, that study had only 70 participants, and children born in China or SK were not included. Children’s health, growth and nutritional status are influenced by maternal health and nutritional status during pregnancy and childbirth, 19 as well as by other factors such as parental socio-economic status. 20 Many NK female refugees suffer from severe stress and malnutrition during pregnancy and childbirth before entering SK, and even after resettlement in SK, they often face significant socio-economic difficulties while raising their children. 21 Therefore, the nutritional status of children from NK refugee families may not necessarily improve solely with a longer duration of resettlement in SK. In our study, the obesity rate among children from NK refugee families increased significantly from 11.1% to 18.8% between the baseline and follow-up surveys (p=0.045). Previous studies conducted immediately after entering SK reported few cases of obesity among NK refugee children, 10 11 17 with some recent studies highlighting the growing prevalence of obesity. 14 15 This increase in obesity rates among children from NK refugee families reflects a common phenomenon seen in socio-economically disadvantaged groups in high-income countries, such as the USA, 22 Australia, 23 Spain 24 and UK. 25 Many NK refugee parents lack knowledge about healthy eating habits, and because they spend a significant portion of their day at work, their children tend to eat fast food more often than nutritious meals. 26 In addition, since many studies have already confirmed that stress is a major cause of obesity, 27 28 an additional factor that needs to be considered is the social and psychological stress that children from NK refugee families are continuously subjected to during pregnancy, childbirth and growth, as well as during defection from NK and resettlement in SK. These findings underscore the need for timely educational efforts and comprehensive intervention strategies to mitigate the rising prevalence of obesity in this vulnerable population. Our study has several limitations. First, the use of snowball sampling limits the generalisability of the findings to the broader population of children from NK refugee families. Second, we did not collect data on factors that may significantly influence children’s nutritional status, such as household economic conditions and parental education levels. Third, the follow-up loss rate was 53.9%, which may have been affected by the concurrent COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, the prevalence of nutritional issues may have been underestimated, as children with greater health problems or socio-economic disadvantages were more likely to be excluded. Nevertheless, unlike most previous studies with cross-sectional designs, the strength of the present study lies in its longitudinal approach, assessing changes in nutritional status over time among children from NK refugee families following resettlement in SK. In conclusion, despite residing in SK for an extended period, children from NK refugee families continue to experience malnutrition, while the prevalence of obesity is rising significantly. These findings highlight the need for tailored public health strategies that address both undernutrition and emerging obesity in this vulnerable population. Future research should explore the underlying causes and risk factors to inform more effective, targeted interventions.
