Abstract The present study describes the data sets produced in Warsaw, Poland with the aim of developing tools and methods for the implementation of human-centred and data-driven solutions to the enhancement of sustainable mobility transition. This study focuses on school commutes and alternatives to private cars for children drop off and pick up from primary schools. The dataset enables the complex analysis of interactions between determinants of transport mode choice, revealed choices, and air quality impact. We draw on four data collection methods, namely, (i) air quality and noise sensors’ measurements, (ii) in-person observations of transport behaviours, (iii) travel diaries, and (iv) social surveys. Moreover, all trip data from travel diaries are complemented with the calculated attributes of alternative travel modes. The data produced in the project can be also combined with publicly available information on air quality, public transport schedules, and traffic flows. The present data sets help to open new venues for interdisciplinary analyses of sustainable mobility transition effectiveness and efficiency. Background &amp; Summary Sustainable transitions can be implemented with various policies which, to be most efficient, should differ between regions and sectors 1 . Commutes to school often increase road congestion and air pollution, [e.g. 2 ]. The present study deals with sustainable mobility transition, with the focus on school commutes of children who attend three public primary schools in Warsaw, Poland. Most highly cited studies on urban transport behaviour rely on single data sources, such as aggregate data on car and public transport usage 3 – 10 or discrete choice experiments 11 – 13 . This also applies to studies on the transport behaviour of parents, where opinion surveys and questionnaires are popular 14 . Research based on individual revealed preferences referring to data from travel diaries 15 – 17 remains relatively scarce because of expensive data collection. A growing body of literature examines traffic related emissions and their impact on air quality 18 – 20 , also at schools 21 . However, transport behaviours related to school commutes are rarely studied in the region of Central and Eastern Europe. Our study reduces this gap. The present study adopted an unique interdisciplinary approach to the collection of original data. In the first stage, we selected three primary schools in different areas of the city representing different local communities. Next, we employed four data collection methods used in environmental and social sciences, namely sensors’ measurements, in-person observations of transport behaviours, and social surveys as well as travel diaries. We developed a network of low-cost air quality sensors monitoring ambient nitrogen dioxide (NO 2 ), ozone (O 3 ), and particulate matter (PM) concentrations (PM 1 , PM 2.5 , and PM 10 – PM with a diameter ≤1 μ m, ≤2.5 μ m, ≤10 μ m, respectively), accompanied with the mid-cost weather stations, noise monitoring sensors, and passive samplers for external validation of NO 2 measurements. Moreover, we conducted in-person observations in the three schools’ neighbourhoods of parental transport behaviours during the child’s delivery to and pick-up from school. In addition, we conducted social surveys on self-reported individual characteristics and collected travel diaries of parents whose children attended the three primary schools. For all trips reported in travel diaries, we calculated the attributes of alternative travel modes (e.g. walking, car, public transport) using public data on transport schedules and road network. Figure 1 presents the timeline of all the data collection. The original data collection during the project served two purposes. Firstly, the sensors’ monitoring was used for the evaluation of air pollution in specific schools’ neighbourhoods, citizen science activities at schools as well as external validation of the low-cost sensors’ data quality. Secondly, the original survey and in-person observations data were accompanied by the public information in order to explain individual transport decisions, their impact on aggregated traffic flows in greater Warsaw and in turn pollution emissions, to be included in newly-developed environmental model of air quality in greater Warsaw 22 based on the existing models 23 , 24 . Moreover, travel diaries combined with survey data and public transport schedules were used for travel mode choice modelling 25 , 26 . Microscopic environmental analyses can use the data from the sensors’ network. There has been a growing interest in utilizing low-cost sensors for air quality monitoring due to their affordability and ease of deployment. These sensors offer the advantage of real-time data collection at a fraction of the cost of traditional monitoring stations, making them particularly attractive for applications requiring high spatial resolution, such as residential solid fuel burning 27 , 28 or community-based initiatives 29 , 30 . However, these sensors often suffer from accuracy and reliability issues, limiting their widespread adoption for regulatory purposes 31 – 33 . Here, we incorporated the use of NO 2 passive samplers to evaluate the performance of NO 2 sensors. We provide insights into the performance of Airly sensor kits in measuring O 3 concentrations in urban settings. Additionally, we developed a multi-step quality control system specifically designed to flag erroneous measurements from the sensor data, particularly for PM 2.5 and O 3 , followed by remote calibration. The approach does not require colocation with reference air quality instruments (i.e., instruments with higher accuracy). This offers a more flexible and scalable method for evaluating the data quality in scenarios where access to reference instruments is limited. Hassani et al . 34 also proposed a multistep data assurance scheme; however, that approach relies on the spatial correlation between the measurements from the sensor and a nearby reference station. This method is not effective in situations where no reference instrument is present in the vicinity. Additionally, the spatial correlation method is more relevant to PM 2.5 sensors and may not be directly applicable to NO 2 measurements, where different factors influence sensor performance. Results from qualitative analysis 27 were crucial in designing the social survey. A software system able to collect and calculate data needed for travel mode choice modelling based on actual availability of public transport 35 was proposed 36 , and methods quantifying the estimated difficulty of finding a parking place 37 were developed. Luckner et al. 38 showed that over 56% of travel to schools can be covered by public transport in less than 15 minutes and that the benefits of using a car on longer journeys to work are limited. The obtained dataset enables analysis of actual and individual transport behaviour of school-age children’s parents. Travel diary data allows the analyses of transport mode choice controlling for individual attitudes and socioeconomic features. Additionally, the dataset supports the investigation of the relationship between transport choices and air quality, especially with regards to characteristics unavailable in previous data, such as individual attitudes or detailed characteristics of available mobility choices. Methods The present study provides the datasets showing air quality and traffic behaviors observed for three communities of public primary schools in Warsaw. The ethical considerations related to data collection concerned mainly the survey data, but also decisions on the selection of participating schools. We decided to cooperate with free public primary schools funded by the state. Transparent communication of project’s goals and the collection of formal consents from institutions and individuals took place. Respective forms where prepared by the research team, written consents for pupils participation in the project as well as the parental decisions whether their children’s image could be used for project’s purposes were collected. School and city authorities were informed on the developments in the project in general as well as sensors’ network deployment and field observations on a regular basis. As for the social survey, the research design was planned in cooperation with the University of Warsaw (UW) Data Protection Inspector following detailed instructions of this body (available on-line at https://www.uw.edu.pl/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/informacje-na-temat-zbierania-i-przetwarzania-danych-osobowych_2021.pdf ) and designed according to the guidelines of the Ethics Committee at the UW. Because planned survey used anonymous data that would be published after anonymization for research purposes, a separate opinion of the Committee was not required, in line with the information published on the Committee’s website ( https://www.uw.edu.pl/uniwersytet/wladze-i-administracja/komisje-i-zespoly/ ). The same exemption from the Committee’s opinion is made for in person observations of individuals in public places, where no interaction nor intervention from researchers is present. The data has been suitably anonymized such that the ethics committee has waived consent and allowed for the open publication of the data. In addition, the Ethics Committee at the Warsaw School of Economics (SGH) was involved in the preparation of the contract with the survey agency, which resulted in the internal agreement and signing of contract no. CRU-4458/2021 of June 26., 2021. All applicable ethical regulations have been met. The survey data were collected according to the above mentioned contract by ARC Rynek i Opinia (ARC), one of the leading Polish marketing research company handling certificates confirming surveying quality control and data protection standards issued by Polish Organization of Opinion and Market Research Companies, while the questionnaires were authored by the research team. The contractor has been selected through a legally required public procurement process. The project team acquired full rights to publish the data, according to the contract. The school authorities and city council approved the survey and agreed to collaborate with the research team. All respondents were informed of the purposes of the surveys, read and signed the informed consent for data collection. The database was anonymized using the k-anonymity technique and Disclosure Risk Assessment. To achieve the established threshold (k = 5) some variables were removed. Detailed information about the transformations performed is provided in the following paragraphs. No privacy issues have been observed to conflict with the public release of the underlying processed data. The schools participating in the study were chosen in cooperation with the city council, school principals, and the research team based on the following criteria. Firstly, only primary schools were eligible because of the age of pupils in primary education (7-15 in Poland), which makes children in grades 1 to 3 more likely to commute to schools with parents responsible for making decisions on the commute route and travel mode. Secondly, we aimed for the diversity of school neighbourhoods in terms of public transport availability, road network, parking, and housing infrastructures. Thirdly, due to formal research collaboration with the City of Warsaw authorities, only schools approved by the council were considered for collaboration. Out of ten schools willing to participate in the project and pre-selected by the city council, three were selected by the research team. The data sets described in the present paper were collected and developed as a part of the Co-Designing Inclusive Mobility (CoMobility) research project granted funding for conducting analyses of air quality and transport behaviours in three school communities in Warsaw, Poland. The selection process described above have not aimed to produce a random sample of schools or students. Instead, the three schools were chosen to represent various school characteristics. The primary school no. 34 (Kruczkowskiego street) is located in the central Warsaw area with high population density and is well-accessible with public transport (urban rail and bus) connections. The primary school no. 377 (Trocka street) is, likewise, placed in the north-eastern Warsaw suburb of high apartment blocks and in the direct proximity of public transport (underground and bus) interchange. The primary school no. 218 (Kajki street) stands out in a certain contrast, being situated in a south-eastern Warsaw suburb of low-density housing and with relatively limited public transport due to its concentration around main roads only. The schools also span a range in the Warsaw school ranking based on the final exam obligatory for all pupils graduating from primary school: no. 218 was 38th, no. 34 was 108th, and no. 377 was 175th out of 189 schools covered in the ranking. For all three schools, data collection took place for the period of two school years (2021/22 and 2022/23). We deployed a sensor network at each school, conducted in-situ observations of delivery to and pick-up from school travel behaviours, as well as surveyed pupils’ parents at each of the project schools, collecting among other information on the methods of children’s commute to school, the reasons for choosing a particular mode of transport, parents’ opinions related to transportation, and about all trips made by a parent in one working day. COVID-19 started in Poland in March 2020, and the initial policy of general lockdowns lasting for the majority of 2020, were replaced with more flexible and localized measures targeted against the virus’ spread. In particular, school principals were allowed to make individual decisions for each school, based on the ongoing teachers’ and students’ testing against COVID-19 infections. Hence, the national lockdowns have not affected our study. However, in 2021 there were numerous cases of self-isolation and on-line teaching in selected classes of the school. Therefore, the field observations we attempted to conduct in 2021 were not included in the released data, because they were affected by COVID-19 whereas the two rounds of school field observations included in present study took place in the time, when no COVID-19 interference occurred. As for the remaining datasets using on-line surveying and deploying sensors and diffusive samplers, they were not affected by COVID-19. However, the transport behaviors might have been affected by the COVID-19 repercussions and our data can be used for the analysis of the impact of COVID-19 on Warsaw citizen’s travel behaviors, if combined with the travel diaries data collected by the City of Warsaw most recently in 2015 ( https://transport.um.warszawa.pl/-/wyniki-wbr-2015 ). We detail the methods used in the data collection of all these data sets below. Sensor network To understand and address environmental factors affecting school surroundings, the CoMobility project employed a combination of student engagement and an expert data collection approach, particularly focusing on air quality and noise levels. The process involved installing noise and air quality sensors with the support and supervision of sensor manufacturers/providers and collaborative efforts with students to monitor and analyze the collected data. For air quality assessment, each school hosted two Airly low-cost air quality sensor kits (LCSs) — Airly-GSM-1, sp. z o.o., Poland, resulting in total six sensor kits placed as shown in Fig. 2 . These sensor kits, strategically positioned near road traffic and in a quieter area of the school’s yard, measured air temperature, relative humidity (RH), ambient NO 2 , O 3 , PM 1 , PM 2.5 , and PM 10 using different sensors on the kit’s board ( https://airly.org/en/EN_AIRLY_PRODUCTCARD_SENSOR_GEN2_2022.pdf ). The real-time data, accessible at the hourly resolution, were collected from 14-Oct-2021 at 12:00:00 to 10-Oct-2023 at 13:00:00. However, there are some periods with data unavailability due to random events such as power failure. The Airly LCS is equipped with the Plantower PMS5003 sensor for measuring air quality pollutants. Additionally, it incorporates a DHT22 temperature/RH sensor, enabling the measurement of temperature and RH. The Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) enabled communication between sensors and databases. The sensor data were first transferred to the Airly database for initial proprietary data processing; from there, data were pushed to the Climate and Environmental Research Institute (NILU) Sensor Data Platform for in-depth analysis. The manufacturer asserts a high accuracy level, with a declared accuracy of ±1 μg m −3 for PM in three fractions (PM 1 , PM 2.5 , and PM 10 ) and ±1 ppb for the NO 2 and O 3 components. While the original measurement intervals are 5 minutes, NILU received data in hourly resolution from the Airly database, and accordingly, this analysis records data in hourly resolution. Due to challenges in the quality of NO 2 sensor data, attributed to inherent limitations of LCSs, calibration issues, drift in readings over time, the influence of meteorological parameters such as temperature and RH, and cross-sensitivity to other ambient pollutants such as O 3 39 , 40 , schools’ pupils actively participated under the supervision of the qualified technician in additional monitoring campaigns by installing diffusive samplers measuring NO 2 in and around the school premises (Fig. 2 ). Specifically, we used diffusive samplers (model DIF100-20WA) to check and correct bias in NO 2 sensor data. Diffusive NO 2 samplers, also known as passive NO 2 samplers, are commonly employed for their simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and efficacy in prolonged air quality monitoring 41 . These samplers function based on the principle of diffusion, including a compact container containing a chemical absorbent that selectively captures NO 2 from the air. As air flows through the sampler, NO 2 molecules diffuse into the device and react with the absorbent. The concentration of NO 2 in the absorbent shows the average ambient NO 2 concentration in the air surrounding the sampler. Acting as a benchmark, the samplers’ readings were compared with the NO 2 sensors’ measurements to identify discrepancies. They also were used as a backup in case of issues with the sensors. Additionally, each school featured a noise sensor kit (SVANTEK, SV307) strategically positioned at the front, exposed to the road traffic, and in proximity to one of the air quality sensors (Fig. 2 ). The SV307 noise monitoring sensors (Class 1 sound level meter) were also accessorized with the SVANTEK SP276 weather station (GILL MaxiMet GMX600; https://gillinstruments.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/1957-010-Maximet-gmx600-Iss-10.pdf ), to measure the wind speed and direction, ambient temperature, RH, atmospheric pressure, and precipitation. All weather measurement parameters were transmitted from the SP276 to the SV307 monitoring station at a sampling rate of 1 Hz. The data collected by the SVANTEK kit were transmitted to the SvanNET server. From this server, the data were downloaded and processed to meet the specific requirements of the CoMobility project. Air Quality sensor kits - Airly The table associated with the data collected by the air quality sensor kits (Airly) offers a set of information, including “Date”, “id”, “timestamp_from_epoch”, “timestamp_to_epoch”, “component”, “value”, “longitude”, “latitude”, and “altitude” columns. The “id” column represents the unique identifier for each sensor, while “timestamp_from_epoch” and “timestamp_to_epoch” indicate the Unix timestamp epochs (in seconds) marking the start and end of each hourly measurement. The “Date” column represents the “timestamp_from_epoch” converted to a human-readable date. The geographical coordinates of the sensor, including longitude and latitude, along with altitude during the measurement campaigns, are provided in the corresponding columns. The “component” column details the type of component measured by the sensor kit, including “Atmospheric Pressure”, “Humidity”, “NO 2 ”, “O 3 ”, “PM 1 ”, “PM 2.5 ”, “PM 10 ”, and “Temperature”. The value column also contains the recorded measurements for each component captured by the sensors. While the original measurements (raw measurements received form the Aily platform) are retained, each measurement is associated with quality control flags, denoted as “QC1”, “QC2”, “QC3”, and “QC4”. Furthermore, three more variables containing corrected data for the main measured pollutants (NO 2 , PM 2.5 , and O 3 ) are provided in 42 . The correction approach and the rationale behind the correction are detailed in the Technical Validation section. NO 2 diffusive samplers In total, data was collected over nine periods, each spanning nearly one month, from October 2022 to August 2023, using diffusive samplers (Table 1 ). At the end of each measurement period, samplers were retrieved and dispatched to the laboratory of 4sfera Innova, S.L. in Spain for additional analysis, in accordance with the stipulations of ISO/IEC 17025. The samplers measured the average concentration of NO 2 near schools during each monthly period. The associated set for diffusive samplers’ data includes the following columns: “Site”: Representing the location where the sample was taken, “Sample_Number”: A unique identifier for each measurement, “Date_Start”, “Date_End”, “Duration”: Indicating the beginning, end, and duration (in hours) of the period when the sampler measured NO 2 concentrations, “Concentration”: Representing the average concentration of NO 2 (μg m −1 ) during the specified period, and “Latitude” and “Longitude”: Providing the coordinates of each sampling site. Typically, two samples were located at the same site to enhance the reliability of the collected data as released in 43 . Two variables in the data set associated with diffusive samplers’ results represent the quality of the measurements, namely “Uncertainty” and “QC flag”. The Technical Validation section provides more details on the technical validation of passive samplers. Noise sensor kits - SVANTEK SVANTEK noise sensors were set to log the noise-related data at 15-minute intervals, with 1/3 octave bands. The data from these sensors are organized into a set with several variables named: “Id”, “lon”, “lat”, “weighting_method”, “Date”, “L peak ”, “L max ”, “L min ”, “L”, “L eq ”, and “L E ”. Here, “Id”, “lon”, and “lat” indicate the unique identifiers and geographical coordinates of the three noise sensors. The “weighting_method” column denotes the sound frequency weighting method used, which can be A, C, or Z, as published in 44 . The weighting methods are described in detail in the manual ( https://svantek.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/SV-307-4G_Man_EN_v.2.01_2021-03-12.pdf ). The “Date” column records the end of the time window for each measurement. According to the sensor manual, “L peak ” represents the peak sound level of the frequency-weighted signal (LA peak , LC peak , LZ peak ) within the 15-minute interval, expressed in decibels (dB). “L max ” is the highest sound level of the frequency- and fast time-weighted signal (with an exponential time constant of 0.125 seconds) observed within this interval, also in dB. Conversely, “L min ” is the lowest sound level of the frequency and fast time-weighted signal within the same time frame. “L” refers to the instantaneous time and frequency-weighted sound level. “L eq ” (dB) is the Equivalent continuous sound level of the frequency-weighted signal (LA eq , LC eq , LZ eq ), averaged over the 15-minute period. Time weighting does not influence the determination of this time-averaged sound level. Lastly, “L E ” represents the frequency-weighted Sound Exposure Level (SEL) (LA E , LC E , LZ E ). SEL is calculated as L eq + 10 l o g ( T ), where T is the duration of the interval, which is 900 seconds (15 minutes) in this case. Meteorological stations - SVANTEK The data from SVANTEK meteorological stations are organized into sets providing all collected meteorological data in 15-minute time intervals, the same as noise data. “Id” is the unique identifier for each meteorological station, and the “lon” and “lat” columns represent the longitude and latitude of the meteorological station, respectively. The “Date” column records the date and time of each data entry (the right/end side of the 15-minute time interval). “TEMP” shows the measurements of ambient temperature (°C). “PRES” represents atmospheric pressure, measured in hectopascals (hPa; 1 hPa = 1 millibar). “HUMI” is the average ambient RH, while “AVG_WIND” shows the average wind speed over the 15-minute period, measured in meters per second (m s −1 ). “MAX_WIND_DIR” Indicates the direction from which the maximum wind speed was recorded (degrees), while “MAX_WIND_SPD” is the maximum wind speed (m s −1 ) recorded during the measurement interval (15 min). Lastly, “PRECI” shows precipitation occurrence (0 for non-precipitation and 1 for precipitation moment), as published in 45 . Field observations of school traffic While the sensor network was deployed to constantly monitor air quality and weather data around the project schools, the objective of in-situ school observations was to collect empirical data on car traffic generated by parents dropping off (in the morning) or picking up (in the afternoon) the children on a typical school day. Researchers monitored specific car manoeuvre characteristics, i.e., parking, stopping, and idling. The field observations were preceded by careful on-site inspections of the school area and all the possible nearby locations where parents dropped off and/or picked up children from/to the primary school. In addition to that, pupils mapped their typical school routes during project workshops, which was used in the observations’ design, and the principal and teachers were consulted about the locations included in field observations. Parents, unlike school authorities, were not informed a priori about the specific date of the field observations which took place in the public street spaces surrounding three schools participating in the project as shown in Fig. 3 . Surveyors were individually instructed and each of them was assigned to specific observation location (street sections, parking lots etc.) for which they were responsible. Each of them wore an ID disclosing information on the project in order to mitigate any concerns regarding their presence in the school’s neighbourhood. No privacy issues were violated and no personal sensitive information was collected. All the individual records on car traffic manoeuvres were stored and processed in an anonymized manner. School traffic observations were conducted thrice throughout the project, with the last survey providing the most reliable data on typical travel behaviours. The first two rounds of observations were affected by external events and thus cannot be treated as representative of average school drop-off and pick-up car traffic. The pilot observations were conducted in October 2021. Unfortunately, they were affected by disruptions in school schedules due to the COVID-19 outbreak in school communities. Regular school drop-off and pick-up patterns were significantly distorted as teaching was moved on a day-to-day basis to on-line platforms. Hence, the pilot data was excluded from the present data set. Second, the spring observations’ phase took place on May 9-11. 2022 (Monday–Wednesday) and overlapped with the ‘Cycling May’ campaign co-organized by the schools and the City of Warsaw, which featured incentives encouraging schoolchildren to travel by bicycles or (e-)scooters. We treat it as a benchmark corresponding to the maximum potential of active travel modes in school commutes. As such, the May 2022 observations are included in the present data set. Finally, the final phase of data collected on October 11-12., 2022 (Tuesday and Wednesday) can be deemed most representative of the school-traffic picture on a typical school day. Hence, the results of the October 2022 phase should represent the ‘full-scope’, typical school traffic patterns, while the May 2022 phase should reflect the ‘lower-bound’ of school traffic volumes, being influenced by the ‘Cycling May’ campaign and shifting mode choices towards the active ones. Field observations took place over at least two working days (Wednesday to Thursday) per each phase. Fair weather conditions were present all the time, i.e. sunny weather with no precipitation, and temperatures of ca. 10 to 20 Celsius degrees. Observations covered time intervals throughout the day when majority of classes either began or ended, thus capturing the morning and afternoon periods of ‘peak’ school traffic. This implies that traffic counts were registered between 7:15–8:15 a.m. (drop-offs) and 12:15–3:00 p.m. (pick-ups). An exception was the day of October 11., 2022 when the morning traffic count occurred between 7:30–9:00 a.m. (so as to observe late drop-offs after 8 a.m.). A group of trained surveyors (approx. 3 to 5 persons per school) conducted traffic observations around the schools, so that all the individual car manoeuvres that involved dropping-off or picking-up children from/to the above schools could be recorded. Observation areas around the perimeter of each school aimed to cover all the (potentially relevant) street sections and parking lay-bys where parents of school children stop their cars on a regular basis, according to the pilot observations. The observation areas were designated before the proper stage of traffic observations, based on initial field reconnaissance and feedback from school authorities. Each time a school-related car manoeuvre was identified (i.e. parents stopping and walking their children in or out of the school, or just dropping them off/picking them up in the school proximity), it was registered with the following characteristics in the observation card (as shown in 46 ): Time instance of school traffic manoeuvre, i.e. registered arrival time of car vehicle that dropped off (or picked up) the schoolchild(ren) from/to the nearby school. Arrival times were eventually grouped into 15-minute intervals. Stopping (or parking) duration, in seconds. Stopping (or parking) location within the street cross-section. The following categories were identified: in a designated parking lay-by; kerbside (e.g. on the sidewalk); on-street, i.e. stopping within an active traffic lane. Information on whether car engine was switched on, i.e. idling (or else – switched off) whilst stationary. Car vehicle size (silhouette). The following categories were identified: small vehicle (e.g. compact, hatchback); medium vehicle (e.g. saloon, coupe); large vehicle (e.g. sportswagon/estate, SUV). Computer-assisted social surveys The social surveys aim to enrich the understanding of air quality and observed urban mobility patterns by exploring characteristics of mobility choices made by parents of the school-age children in the project schools. Specifically, it provides the opportunity to investigate the role of individual characteristics, attitudes and preferences on top of external factors, in transport decisions and their impact on air quality in three project schools. The questionnaires were meticulously designed to reflect the research objectives of the project. The survey was planned in two waves as a repeated cross-section. The first wave was to be performed early in the collaboration with schools (from March 24. to May 2., 2022), while the second wave after one full school year of project activities took place at the schools, actively engaging their communities (from November 14. to December 16., 2022). The survey consisted of three major parts: 1) questions about socio-economic characteristics of parents, household information and general beliefs related to transport; 2) questions directly related to each child’s school commutes; and 3) a detailed travel diary from one selected weekday conducted only in first wave of data collection. The social surveys data sets include information collected from parts 1) and 2) of the interview for each respondent participating in the survey, whereas part 3) was used to generate a separate mode choice data set, which will be described in the following section on travel mode choice data. ARC, one of the leading Polish research companies, carried out the study. To recruit a sample for the study, ARC’s project manager contacted the school principals, the teachers of all classes in each school, and the representatives of the Parents’ Council Boards, to send the parents of all children studying at the school an invitation to participate in the survey. This was done mainly through the e-journal system used at each school, and teachers provided additional information during regular school meetings with parents (during which SGH research team members where available upon request). All parents received an invitation to participate in the survey through the e-journal, which is the official channel of communication between parents and school authorities. Thanks to this approach, every parent had the opportunity to take part in the survey. Throughout the data collection in social surveys, the ARC project manager was in regular contact with research team and school representatives. Gamification techniques were used to encourage parents to participate in the survey. Parents, teachers, and pupils were informed that the two classes with the highest response rate in each school, will earn prizes in a form of interesting trips or gifts, to encourage completion of the survey. A donation of PLN 5,000 (about USD 1,112) to Parents’ Council Board in all schools in both waves was granted after the survey data collection. No parents (nor their children) were individually remunerated for the interviews. All surveys were conducted using the Computer-Assisted Web Interview (CAWI) technique. The questionnaire was prepared in Polish. The average time to complete the questionnaire in the first wave was 27 minutes. Data cleaning A wide database containing over a thousand of variables underwent a significant transformation to enhance its efficiency and usability. The first step was the separation of the database into three thematic tables with respect to the questions regarding (i) parent and household; (ii) children; and (iii) trips. The first two tables were used for the preparations of the present data sets, while the latter was moved to a separate analysis. All three tables have maintained unique identifiers to allow relinking of the data. The second step was the reduction of redundant variables. This was achieved by pivoting the database from a wide format, where a similar variable in the loop formed the next column, to a long format, where information from duplicate variables became additional rows in one variable. The number of observations in the first table was still the same and corresponding with the number of respondents. In the second table, the number of observations corresponded to the number of children about whom parents commented in the survey, while in the last table, the number of observations corresponded to the number of individual stages of the journeys made by all respondents. The last step of data cleaning included changing multi-answer questions from categorical format variables into more commonly used dichotomous ones, translating answers to English in open-ended questions, and translating variable and value labels to English. The questionnaire and dictionaries are available in 47 . Disclosure risk assessment The final stage in the data preparation for the release was the Disclosure Risk Assessment (DRA) relying on the k-anonymity technique. This technique constitutes a rigorous approach to evaluating the potential risks associated with the re-identification of individuals in a dataset before its release. The k-anonymity model itself, introduced by Sweeney in 2002 48 , ensures that each record in a dataset is indistinguishable from at least k-1 other records based on indirect identifiers. An indirect identifier is defined as a single variable that does not by itself identify a specific person, but when combined with other indirect identifiers can be used for re-identification. Examples of such variables include metric variables (e.g. gender, education, place of residence, zipcode). K-anonymity operates by modifying the dataset through processes such as generalization (e.g., changing a continuous variables to an interval ones) and suppression (by removing the entire variable, or locally removing rare values) to ensure that each individual’s data cannot be singled out, thus protecting their anonymity. By applying the k-anonymity model, the DRA seeks to strike a balance between data utility and the privacy of individuals, ensuring that the released data minimizes the risk of re-identification and potential privacy breaches. In addition to evaluating the implementation of the adopted k-anonymity threshold, DRA also counts Individual and Global Disclosure Risk (GDR). The Individual Disclosure Risk is the probability that an individual within a dataset could be correctly re-identified, the Global Risk commonly is calculated as the average of the individual disclosure risk scores 49 . The first step of the DRA included a thorough review of the questionnaire and database to remove any direct identifiers and find all possible indirect identifiers. To achieve the k-anonymity at the threshold of 5 (meaning each record cannot be distinguished from at least 4 others) most of the indirect identifiers were removed, and the remaining variables were transformed into categorical variables by creating intervals or dichotomous variables. All these manipulations are reported in Table 4 . After selecting and aggregating the variables, a DRA analysis was performed. We used sdcMicro (package in R environment) to perform this analysis. This is one of a few open-source tools that can be used to apply Statistical Disclosure Control (SDC). First, the number of observations that violate the k-anonymity principle (at levels 2, 3 and 5) and the GRD were calculated. We used the aggregated indirect identifiers as key variables presented in Table 4 . Despite meeting the k-anonymity threshold of 3 (k = 3), we obtained the GRD in the database from the first wave above 3%, which we adopted as a threshold following the standard from Humanitarian Data Exchange 49 . Hence, we conducted local suppression on selected key variables with individual risk above the threshold of 0.25%. Subsequently, we repeated a DRA analysis, ensuring adherence to the threshold of k = 5. The final results of the assessment are presented in Table 5 . Mode choice data To examine travel mode choices made by parents of the children for their trips recorded in travel diaries, an additional mode choice data set was calculated. One record in this data set corresponds to one trip record in the travel diary data. Each record documents the actual travel mode choice made by a respondent for a trip as well as the attributes of travel modes which could be estimated before the trip and hence considered by a traveller going from the origin of the trip to its destination, given the origin and destination of the real trip and its timing as reported in the travel diary data. The data set provides the features of available modes calculated based on the road network, walking and cycling paths, and public transport schedules. These features include but are not limited to estimated travel duration for each travel mode, the share of the trip that could be made by a specific public transport mean (bus, tram, train, metro), and the walking distances needed to use public transport connection(s), among others. Importantly, the calculation of the available mode choice data relies on the origin and destination points provided by respondents in the travel diary, both expressed as the geographical longitude and latitude of a location. Hence, actual circumstances such as the need to walk to get to a car parking or nearest bus stop are reflected in the mode choice data. However, the location data (longitude and latitude) were removed from the database to ensure respondents’ anonymity. The mode choice data set includes the estimated features of each travel made using one of four travel modes i.e. private car; public transport; bicycle and walking; as well as a mixture of these four modes. The selection of modes was based on the popularity of the travel modes in Warsaw, which was determined based on the survey data. The features of a trip assuming it was made with each of the four examined travel modes were calculated by calling OpenTripPlanner ( https://www.opentripplanner.org ) (OTP) instance, i.e. the instance of open source multimodal trip planning engine. OTP was used to estimate both routes under different transport modalities and determine the best public transport connections. The OTP instance relied on OpenStreetMap ( https://www.openstreetmap.org ) service for spatial data of city infrastructure including, but not limited to streets, pavements, and bicycle paths. Importantly, the OTP instance was configured with public transport schedules for individual days of the period documented in travel diaries. The schedules used by the OTP instance were developed based on the schedules for buses, trams, metro, and trains operated by the Public Transport Authority of the City of Warsaw, and the schedules of other transport companies offering relevant transport services in the city i.e. Mazovian Railways and Warsaw Commuter Railway operating suburban trains of potential use for persons travelling within the city area. The travels beyond the city borders were excluded from the analysis. In the case of car, bicycle and walking, OTP determines one route and provides its features for each origin and destination pair. Hence, one request to OTP was made for each of these modalities in order to obtain the route linking the origin and destination of one trip from the travel diary. The response from OTP included the route and relevant mode choice features, such as estimated travel duration, overall distance, and distance to be walked to use this mode. In the case of public transport (PT), multimodal connections were supported, possibly relying on different modes, e.g. bus and metro, to get from the origin to the destination. For each trip, the starting time was shifted 5 minutes beforehand. Next, the OTP request returned up to ten connections that start no longer than 15 minutes from the shifted starting time. Because many possible PT connections can be obtained for a trip, their features were aggregated to provide a number of relevant PT attributes placed in the mode choice data set. They include attributes such as minimum, average, and maximum number of transfers; minimum, average and maximum travel duration; and other aggregates of the features documenting individual connections available for a trip of interest. As a consequence, the mode choice data set includes multiple transport features answering the issues considered by travellers when planning their connections, such as the time of travel with different means of transport, the need to walk to, between and from relevant PT stops, the number of available PT connections, the ratio of the trip that could be made by tram, and other. The data set generated in the procedure described above consists of 87 features provided for each travel made by parents of children attending three project schools, as discussed in 50 . This goes beyond the number of mode choice attributes typically used in studies on mode choice modelling, e.g. 13 features calculated for trips in the London area in 51 . Compared to other studies considering mode choice data, including data made public with 51 , the data set accompanying this work includes additional attributes aggregating the features of possibly multiple public transport connections suitable for a trip within a 15-minute window. This includes minimum values of individual attributes of public transport connections, but also average and maximum values of these attributes. As an example, not only the duration of the best connection but also the average duration of all relevant connections and the maximum duration of relevant connections are provided in the mode choice data set. Furthermore, walking distances needed to use public transport, but also a car and a bicycle were included. Importantly, as observed in 52 , even many studies on ML-based mode choice modelling do not include Level of Service (LOS) attributes of mode alternatives, while the majority of the remaining ones generate their values from static zonal graphs, i.e. rely on the approach which does not capture highly granular spatial and temporal variability of transport conditions. Unlike in the former approach, in the mode choice data provided with this study, all LOS attributes are calculated considering precise coordinates of trip endpoints and exact trip start dates and times collected from the surveys, as these have a major impact on the values of the LOS attributes of the trip. The following three variables of the mode choice data contain data recorded directly in the travel diaries: “Destination” (aggregated to three categories: work, child’s school, and other); “time”, which is a starting moment of 15-minutes window that includes the travel beginning; and “travelAggregation”. The latter variable describes travel mode reported in the travel diary for a trip and takes one of the following values: only car; only public transport; only walking; only private bicycle; only city bicycle; car and other modes; bicycle and other modes; and other. The rest of the variables were calculated by the WUT research team. They describe the Level of Service, a term used to describe transport conditions based on speed, travel time, flexibility, delay, and safety, for simulated travels by foot, bicycle, car, or public transport. For each mean of transportation, distance (in meters), duration (in seconds), speed (in minutes per second), and a ratio of distance to a distance in a straight line are given. For active transport modes, changes of elevation (in meters) are provided. All motorized trips have separately calculated walking time (in seconds) and distance (in meters) to the vehicle. For the public transport the number of possible routes, stops, and transfers is given. The travel time is split into waiting time and transit time (in vehicle, expressed in seconds). In addition, the travel cost by PT transport is calculated (expressed in PLN). Each PT travel includes information about its duration distribution between bus, tram, train, and subway (in seconds and in shares). Technical Validation Sensor network Air Quality sensor kit - Airly As previously discussed, LCSs require data quality control due to factors such as poor factory calibration, fluctuations in RH, and susceptibility to cross-sensitivity with other pollutants. Although Airly data platform performs some proprietary data preprocessing steps, the measurements for each component from the Airly LCSs underwent a multi-step Quality Control (QC) procedure, developed in this study. A QC flag of one indicates that the measurement successfully passed the applied QC step, whereas a zero flag signifies that it did not. The first QC step assesses whether the measurement falls within an acceptable range (see Table 6 for acceptable ranges for each measured component). In the second step, constant values are evaluated by calculating the moving window variance (with a window size of 24 hours), and if the variance is zero, QC2 is set to zero. The third QC step involves detecting outliers by computing the moving scaled Median Absolute Deviation ( s M A D ) from the moving median (with a window size of 72 hours). If a measurement value is three times greater than the calculated moving s M A D from the moving median, with 1 s M A D = c × m e d i a n ( ∣ V − m e d i a n ( V ) ∣ ) where c = − 1 / ( 2 × e r f c i n v ( 3 / 2 ) ) and V is the vector of measurements in the window, it is identified as an outlier, and the corresponding QC flag is set to zero. The fourth QC step assessed the consistency between measurements from two sensor kits installed near each school. The assumption was that, for each component measured, there would be some level of agreement in the 24-hour averages between the two sensors, given their proximity (a few dozen meters apart). Deviation Percentage ( D P ) was calculated for each component and day by determining the absolute difference of daily averages and dividing it by the mean of the daily averages. For instance, if the daily average of one sensor was A and the other was B , D P was calculated as: ∣ A − B ∣/ M e a n ( A , B ) × 100. Only days with at least 75% (18 hours) of data coverage were considered for D P calculations. A truncated normal distribution was fitted to the DPs for each pair of sensors, and the standard deviation ( s t d ) of the fitted distribution was the threshold of discrepancy. If the D P for a day between a pair of sensors exceeded the estimated std for that pair, the QC4 flag for that day was set to zero. However, recognizing that D P s for days with low values of the components could be exceptionally high, an additional condition was introduced. The daily average measurements by the sensors should not have differed by more than n units for each component. Table 6 shows the accepted thresholds ( n ) for each component. For example, for PM 2.5 , the difference between daily averages measured by the sensors should not have exceeded 5 μg m −3 . Both conditions — D P being less than one s t d for that component and the daily average difference being less than n — had to be met to pass QC step 4. For days with low data coverage or when data from one sensor was unavailable, QC4 was set to zero. Quality controls were not conducted for SVANTEK sensors since they fall into the mid-cost category, and their inherent quality was presumed to be sufficient. LCSs require calibration to improve their data quality 32 . Calibrating or correcting air quality data is a commonly employed technique to improve the precision of readings from air quality sensors. This involves aligning the sensor data with measurements obtained from high-quality reference instruments, typically located at official air quality monitoring sites. These reference instruments are considered more reliable for assessing pollutant concentrations like PM 2.5 , NO 2 , and O 3 . The calibration process typically involves placing LCSs close to the reference instruments for a specific duration, usually spanning from two weeks to a month. Past research has shown that the calibration equations established during this initial colocation period may vary with seasonal fluctuations or changes in ambient environmental conditions 28 , 31 , 53 . Therefore, to uphold data accuracy, it is necessary to regularly conduct colocation campaigns to account for evolving environmental factors. Additionally, in the case of gas sensors, especially those monitoring NO 2 , and O 3 , the aging of sensors becomes a significant consideration, necessitating routine calibration procedures 54 , 55 . To address those limitations, we utilized a remote calibration method, only for PM 2.5 , and O 3 , pollutants recognized for their higher spatial correlation within urban environments 53 , 56 . The remote calibration technique eliminates the need for direct physical colocation with reference instruments. It involves aligning the sensor data with measurements from the closest reference station. However, due to the notable spatial variability observed in NO 2 sensor readings, we did not use such approach for calibrating NO 2 sensor data. In our study, the solution for evaluating and improving NO 2 sensor data was comparison of NO 2 sensors’ data against diffusive samplers. However, this approach could only correct the mean bias of the sensors, only for the periods when data from the diffusive samplers are available. Figure 4a shows the average of LCS NO 2 measurements in comparison to the mean NO 2 concentrations recorded by diffusive samplers during each period. Figure 4b displays the mean bias of LCS NO 2 measurements from diffusive samplers over time. The process of remote calibration included identifying particular times of the day when there was a significant spatial correlation in pollutant measurements throughout the city. We operated under the assumption that during these specific periods, sensor readings would closely align with those obtained from reference instruments. Calibration equations were then formulated based on this assumption. To define these diurnal periods of high correlation, we used data from reference instruments and grouped them into hourly segments. For each group, we computed the station-to-station Pearson Correlation Coefficient. Suppose t A represents the time series of PM 2.5 or O 3 collected at site A, and t B represents the time series of PM 2.5 or O 3 collected at site B. We grouped the t A and t B data using the hour of the day ( h ) as the grouping parameter (0 to 23; 24 groups in total). For each group of data, we calculated the Pearson Correlation Coefficient r as: 2 r = ∑ i = 1 n ( X i h − X h ¯ ) ( Y i h − Y h ¯ ) ∑ i = 1 n ( X i h − X h ¯ ) 2 ∑ i = 1 n ( Y i h − Y h ¯ ) 2 . Here: n is the number of data points in the hour of the day group h , X h and Y h are the data vectors in the hour of the day group h , X i h and Y i h are the individual data points in X h and Y h , respectively, and X h ¯ and Y h ¯ are the means of vectors X h and Y h , respectively. Hours demonstrating substantial correlation were chosen for the calibration. For PM 2.5 the time from 09:00 to 14:59 was chosen due to its highest spatial correlation throughout the city. During these specific hours, sensor and reference measurements were used for remote data calibration. The results of this calibration, involving curve fitting, are displayed in Fig. 5 , panels a, b, and c. The calibration process used the mean of measurements from the two sensors. High ambient RH is a factor that can influence the performance of PMS5003 LCSs that operate based on the optical particle counting principle 28 , 57 . High RH levels (above 70%) lead to an overestimation of PM 2.5 58 . However, RH was excluded from the final calibration models due to its statistically insignificant coefficient, indicated by a p -value for the t-statistic ≥ 0.05. For the sensor data in proximity to Kruczkowskiego (IDs 419 and 420), the calibration was carried out using data from the traffic reference station pl0140a (see Fig. 2 for the location of reference stations). However, sensors near Kajki and Trocka were calibrated using measurements from the reference background station pl0717a (data from station pl0143a, near Trocka, were not incorporated into the calibration process due to missing data from 2021). Similar to the data from PM 2.5 sensors, the O 3 recordings underwent correction and calibration against reference measurements obtained from nearby monitoring stations. In this instance, the calibration process for O 3 involved utilizing the mean of measurements from all available reference monitoring stations (reference network mean), which included background stations pl0739a, pl0141a, pl0787a, and pl0143a (refer to Fig. 2 ). The time frame with the highest spatial correlation in measured O 3 levels was identified to be between 08:00 and 14:59. The outcomes of calibrating the sensor measurements (using the mean of the two O 3 sensors near each school), including the Coefficient of Determination ( R 2 ) and Root Mean Square Error ( R M S E ) from the linear model fitting, are presented in Fig. 6 . It is worth noting that NO 2 , a known cross-interference gas, can influence the results of O 3 sensors. The results in Fig. 6 show that elevated NO 2 concentrations (indicated by the color of the scatter dots) lead to a greater deviation of the sensor O 3 measurements from the reference network mean. However, the application of a linear model significantly enhances the quality of the data. Panel d in Fig. 6 demonstrates how employing the calibration equation to the measurements of each O 3 sensor has improved the quality of the data, making the diurnal pattern of O 3 post-calibration more consistent with the network mean. In a review, Kang et al . 59 analyzed 80 studies that assessed the performance of PM 2.5 , NO 2 , and O 3 LCSs in outdoor environments. They reported median r 2 values of approximately 0.72 (Q1 = 0.53, Q3 = 0.85) for PM 2.5 , 0.57 (Q1 = 0.24, Q3 = 0.84) for NO 2 , and 0.78 (Q1 = 0.51, Q3 = 0.85) for O 3 . These r 2 values include correlations ( r ) that were converted to r 2 for consistency, making it unclear if they are calculated based on raw measurements or if post calibration values are also included. Hassani et al . 28 also evaluated the performance of Airly LCSs in measuring the mean daily PM 2.5 against the gravimetric PM reference method — Kleinfiltergerät LVS3 and MVS6 — over a 4-week period. The results showed a sensor-to-reference correlation of 0.86 for raw PM 2.5 measurements at a daily resolution, with a mean bias (MB) and RMSE of 9.45 to 11.65 μg m −3 , respectively. Vogt et al . 58 assessed the performance of three Airly PM sensor systems by comparing them to the FIDAS optical reference-equivalent instrument at the Kirkeveien air quality station in Oslo, Norway, from August 28. to October 19., 2020. After applying sensor-specific multi-linear regression models to the measured PM 2.5 values, the RMSE ranged from 4.39 to 10.39 μg m −3 . In our study, the RMSE following remote calibration for PM 2.5 was between 2.88 and 3.42 μg m −3 (before calibration: Pearson correlation coefficient: 0.85 - 0.93 and RMSE between 3.41 and 4.49 μg m −3 ). Similarly, Hofman et al . 60 evaluated the performance of three Airly PM sensor systems against a reference-grade instrument (FIDAS 200, Palas) on the roof of a background regulatory air quality monitoring site in Antwerp, Belgium, over a series of 14-day colocation periods. After applying local re-calibration factors, the quality of fit for PM 2.5 was R 2 = 0.79–0.92, with RMSEs between 3.69 and 3.93 μg m −3 . It should be noted that we calibrated the average measurements from the two sensors installed near each school, following a four-step data quality control process. Therefore, the calibration was not based on the raw signal output. Hofman et al . 60 also co-located three Airly NO 2 sensors at the same site in Antwerp where they reported MBs of -33.44, 123.30, and -33.44 μg m −3 , indicating a significant variation in the accuracy of the sensors. For comparison, in our sturdy, the MB of the sensor from the diffusive samplers was between -5 and 15 μg m −3 . It should be noted, however, that our comparison was based solely on the monthly mean measurements of the sensors, and these were compared against diffusive samplers rather than a reference instrument. Similar to this study, Forsmalm 61 also observed a seasonal dependency of the bias when evaluating Airly sensor kits for measuring NO 2 in Gothenburg, Sweden, against diffusive samplers. This seasonal variation in bias can be partly attributed to the effects of temperature on the performance of electrochemical sensors. In our study, the performance of the Ailry O 3 LCSs was assessed before and after applying remote calibration. Before calibration, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the sensor measurements and the reference was between 0.912 and 0.914, and the RMSE ranged from 13.3 to 15.54 μg m −3 . After remote calibration, the RMSE improved to a range of 11.5 to 14.51 μg m −3 . This suggests that the calibration might not have been necessary in this case, as the sensors’ performance was already quite strong before calibration. However, similar to PM 2.5 , we calibrated the average measurements from two sensors near each school using a four-step data quality control process, rather than the raw signal output. Field observations of school traffic Technical validation of field traffic observations was carried out from a couple of perspectives. Firstly, survey design and its outcomes were verified based on pilot observations in autumn 2021. As a result, individual observation sites were extended to capture maximum possible locations where drop-offs and pick-ups take place. This is all the more relevant as school stopping and parking manoeuvres tend to be spatially distributed across adjacent streets and parking lots. Moreover, pilot surveys were crucial in defining observation periods, i.e. times when a major share of drop-offs and pick-ups occur. This is especially relevant in the afternoon when parental arrivals are more temporally dispersed. After the data was collected, it was analyzed. In 29 cases some information was missing, so it was not possible to include them in the further analysis. After clearing the dataset, the distribution of stopping durations was analyzed (cf. Fig. 7 ). It has turned out that for all three schools, the distributions were similar (cf. Fig. 7 ), but there were also individual outliers. Some stops were very short (only a few seconds), while others were quite long (at least 1 hour). The variation in the stopping duration reveals significant differences between the schools, representing three types of city area: the city centre in a relatively busy traffic location; residential parts of the city surrounded by street sections with low volumes and traffic calming measures, and with relatively high availability of parking spaces; and the suburban area of Warsaw, along a busy street section with limited availability of parking spaces. We also analyzed distributions of the number of cars stopping in different time periods; the number of cars with engines turned on; the car sizes; and stopping location. During this analysis, other than the identified missing data and outliers, we did not identify any significant anomalies or counterintuitive results. It was therefore concluded that the cleaned dataset could be used for further analysis. Moreover, as part of technical validation, we also aimed to compare our outcomes with relevant literature findings. It should be noted that state-of-the-art literature contains very limited comparable resources, with just a few studies on parental driving of children from/to primary schools on the national level 62 gathered information on children’s deliveries to four primary schools (in two distinct Polish cities: 3 schools in Łódź + 1 school in Bielsko-Biała). Estimates of car traffic volumes are reported for the whole morning period and feature major variations between each individual school (76, 138, 168 and 207 vehicles). Likewise, car modal share in children’s deliveries varies between ca. 20% to 60%. Moreover, traffic observations were collected during a single day only. Results obtained in our data collection range from 13% to 33% (cf. Table 7 ), which is line with relatively more developed public transport in Warsaw as compared to other towns and cities in the country. In addition, 63 provide traffic observations’ data from the Warsaw pilot project on ‘school streets’ where school traffic volumes are reported in the morning peak hour (7:15–8:15 am) for three primary schools in Warsaw, and they are equal to: 26, 45, and 80 [veh/h]. Unfortunately, it cannot be determined whether these figures are based on a single- or multi-day observation(s). Another study 64 reports the car modal share in children’s deliveries to school. This investigation is based on self-reported behaviours of a representative sample of adult Polish respondents. Findings show that on average, 31% of school children are driven by cars to their schools. This figure varies between larger cities (24%) and rural areas (41%). Computer-assisted social surveys The technical validation and quality control comprised of three stages. The first stage of quality control was done before the survey was carried by thorough pre-testing of the questionnaire. A pilot study was conducted before main study in February 2022 by ARC on the sample of 50 respondents. Additional questions were added to the base part of the questionnaire, regarding general conclusions and reflections of respondents on the experience of interview. The database from this pilot study was carefully analyzed, and minor adjustments were made to the wording and technical functionality of the questionnaire, so to enhance the quality of the responses. The second stage of validation took place during and soon after completion of the first wave of the survey and it involved checking completeness and quality of responses to all questions. Below we present the final numbers of interviews completed by their status (see Table 8 ). The interview status “screen-out” denotes those interviews that did not pass specific criteria (having a child and attending a given school). The status “manually interrupted” refers to the interviews only partially completed by the respondent (he or she abandoned the completion or did not return to it before the end of the fieldwork). Due to the adopted motivation system for respondents (gamification), effective interviews have been reviewed by the research company for duplicates. Three groups of duplicates were discovered, based on the same email address and same IP address of a respondent: duplicates where one record is a response of a woman and another of a man. We consider such response as a couple using the same computer and email address, therefore those are not considered as duplicates. duplicates where both records belong to a woman but of different declared age (year-month of birth). We do not consider those as duplicates. duplicates where all records belong to a person of the same sex and year-month of birth. Such cases were considered duplicates. Of those duplicates, we leave only one record that is considered to be taken more seriously by the respondent, i.e. either there is a longer response time, more answers are provided in the travel diary, more answers in open questions, more answers with “Definitely” in Likert-like questions, etc. The third stage of validation was checking the quality of data. We checked the time of filling the survey, and reliability of answers in database such as series of similar answers and answers contradicting each other (cf. Table 8 ). There are no records which were classified as outliers due to a too short response time. We used the Interquartile Range Rule for classifying observations as outliers, i.e. we would consider a record as an outlier if the response time was lower than Q1 - [1.5 * IQR] (cf. Table 8 ). The distribution of response times is right-skewed, so the outliers lay rather in the right tail with extremely large response times. Longer interviews were not considered problematic, because of more loops into which the respondent could fell (e.g. she has a larger number of children and completes a long series of questions for each of them) or the probability that she took a break while completing the survey and returned to completing it later. The last part of quality assessment was control of the answers as reported in the survey. We identified some records with a series of the same answers in the “Likert-scale” questions (e.g. all “Definitely yes” answers in grid question), which were flagged using variable “flag_series”; and records with answers that contradict each other, which ware flagged using variable “flag_contradict”. These observations remained in the database and the flags can be used for individual decisions on their exclusion. Mode choice data Mode choice data were verified by spatial and statistical analysis. The decision to remove all trips outside of the City of Warsaw borders resulted from the fact that it was impossible to calculate the Level of Service attributes for trips outside the city borders due to missing schedules from multiple public transport operators present in neighbouring areas. Next, all columns with a standard deviation equal to zero were removed as unimportant in further analysis. Duration was analyzed as an original feature to check the estimated Level of Services. The duration distribution for various means of transport is presented in Fig. 8 . The plot (limited to 90 minutes) shows that a tail is the longest for travel by foot and the smallest using a car. Additionally, speed was calculated as the ratio between distance and duration (or the average of the ratio values for public transport) to compare its distribution. The obtained results are feasible. In particular, the walking speed is less than 5 km/h. The average car speed is about 20 km/h, an expected result in the city. The mean for public transport is about 10 km/h because walking time is an important part of such travel, especially on short distances. Finally, the correlations were calculated between various Levels of Service. Figure 9 shows several of them. The results confirm expected correlations between distance and duration for various means of transport. Usage Notes The data sets can be used in various subsets in order to examine specific areas of our research interest. Firstly, the localized sensors’ data can be merged with the data on air quality (AQ) in Warsaw area as modeled and released by 22 , which can be used as a way of detailed validation of low-sensors’ data with the state-of-the arts environmental modeling techniques. Secondly, survey data on idling and maneuvering as well as on the prevalence of car school commutes can be combined with the respective data observed in the field for external validation of the two alternative sources, for methodological developments, and to validate the effects of self-reported parental driving with real-world behaviour. These two sets are characterized with separate limitations, and combining them allows to use the more reliable data source on the prevalence of idling (surveys) and maneuvering (field observations). These in turn, can be further combined with the AQ data to understand the impact of congestion, idling, and maneuvering on NO 2 on a fine local scale. Thirdly, by merging the data from travel mode choice and social surveys sets, we are able to examine the role of various aspects in the transport decisions, such as household composition, pro-social or pro-ecological attitudes, etc. The data from travel diaries can be used to check 15-minute city model assumptions in existing urban infrastructure. The survey data can serve as a base for a model that calculates connections between the actual locations visited by the respondents and parameters of travel from home to school and work. The same data can be used to check how mobility choices affect daily travel to school in the context of a 15-minute city and physical activity (PA). Another research question worth studying using the described datasets, is the analysis of the benefits of public transport on PA and distance, and other factors limiting PA. Field observations of car traffic volumes (associated with children’s delivery or pick-up) can serve as the basis for updating the official Warsaw transport model and developing a specific school-traffic generation algorithm, combined with the demand simulation (assignment) model. The updated transport model is the tool for simulation analyses of the CoMobility projects to assess the school-traffic impacts upon road network performance (e.g. traffic loads, journey parameters) and for the evaluation of the influence of various intervention scenarios (e.g. traffic calming or street closure, removal of school-traffic). More detailed instructions on the data usage follow. The survey data (COMOB-P) consists of sets of databases for every wave. Every set consists of a table with answers regarding parents and a table with answers regarding children’s travel to school. Tables are linked by the respondent’s identification number. All questionnaire variables are named according to the name inside square brackets located next to each question in the questionnaire. The content of the question is described in the variable labels (for SAV format) and codebook (for CSV format), as published in 47 . The mode choice data (COMOB-M) consists of the Level of Services calculated for simulated travels routed between the reported origin and destination (travelling time, distance, and other). Database are linked by the respondent’s identification number to data from first wave (COMOB-P). Therefore mode choice data could be linked with the opinions, beliefs of respondent or children’s travel mode to school. The data were used to create a model that predicts transport behaviour understood as a type of used mean of transport. The prediction has been done using the level of service for various means of transportation (COMOB-M), socioeconomic data from surveys (COMOB-P), information about pollution (COMOB-A) and weather conditions (COMOB-W). The analysis of the features’ importance has shown that features from all datasets had a significant influence on the model. No data values in air quality and noise relevant data sets are shown by -9999. The efforts were made to select the localizations so that a variety of schools and neighborhoods were captured. However, the data are not representative for the entire city. A separate, comparable, and unreleased social survey was conducted on the representative sample of Warsaw residents (COMOB-WAW) using the same questionnaire in order to provide the benchmark for the school-specific samples. Researchers willing to access the data can contact the CoMobility research team and an access to COMOB-WAW data could be granted for scientific purposes upon signing a respective non-disclosure agreement.
