To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Abstract Background Children and adolescents’ physical inactivity is a global problem. Financial incentives are increasingly utilised to support children’s participation in sport, with programs introduced in multiple jurisdictions globally, however few programs have comprehensive evaluations. This study used a Delphi methodology to develop evaluation recommendations for an existing population-wide financial incentive program based in South Australia. Methods A three-round Delphi study was conducted from October 2022 to April 2023. A diverse range of stakeholders including end-users, government representatives, academics/researchers, and Non-Government Organisation representatives participated in the Delphi panel. Round 1 focussed on the perceived importance of various evaluation components, including reach, process, and effectiveness. Round 2 focussed on identifying evaluation approaches and tools, while Round 3 determined methodological priorities and timeframes. A consensus level of ≥ 70% was set a priori (rating 6 or 7 on 7-point scale). Round 3 results were used to formulate evaluation recommendations. Results Sixty-seven stakeholders agreed to participate, with an average response rate of 76% (87%; 73% and 67% for each round, respectively). All evaluation components gained consensus in Round 1. In Round 2, consensus was gained on 14 evaluation measures, including measuring the cost of sport, awareness of the program amongst eligible families, and reasons for non-participation, with evaluation methods generated for each measure. Round 3 results determined participants’ preferred methods of evaluation and informed the development of evaluation recommendations. Recommended evaluation methods included mixed method approaches for provider and user process evaluations and measuring program awareness using an epidemiological survey. Conclusion This Delphi study sought the views and opinions from diverse stakeholders to create evaluation recommendations for a population-wide sports voucher program. Results suggested that a comprehensive evaluation strategy is needed to understand the reach, effectiveness, and impact of the program. Additionally, there was a consensus on reallocating program funds for this evaluation. Background The burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is a global public health problem and contributed to 74% of all deaths globally in 2019 [ 1 ]. Children and adolescents are increasingly at risk of developing NCDs, with increases in rates of metabolic risk factors [ 2 , 3 ] and mental health disorders [ 4 , 5 ]. Physical activity plays an integral role in the primary prevention of NCDs [ 6 ] and is vital to children and adolescents’ physical and mental health, wellbeing, and learning [ 7 , 8 ]. Childhood and adolescence are pivotal periods for intervention, as positive physical activity behaviours established in childhood are often carried through into adult life [ 9 ]. Given that 81% of children and adolescents globally (aged 11–17 years old) fail to achieve the recommended physical activity levels (≥ 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity per day) [ 10 ], effective interventions are essential. Sports participation can offer a valuable contribution to children and adolescent’s accumulation of moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity [ 11 ]. Sports participation also appears to provide important benefits above regular physical activity, including improved social and psychological outcomes [ 12 , 13 ], higher levels of academic achievement [ 14 , 15 ] and reduced likelihood of illicit substance use [ 16 ]. Children and adolescents’ sport participation is suboptimal globally, with approximately half (47–53%) of 5–17-year-olds participating in sport [ 17 ]. This means many young people are missing out on the benefits offered by regular sport and physical activity participation. Population-based sports policy interventions have an important role in addressing this gap [ 18 – 20 ]. The costs associated with organised sports can present a significant barrier to participation [ 21 , 22 ]. To help address the issue of cost, population-based government-administered financial incentives to encourage children’s participation in organised sports are being increasingly adopted. These programs have been introduced in Australia [ 23 – 29 ], Canada [ 30 ], Luxembourg [ 31 ], Germany [ 32 ], Iceland [ 33 , 34 ] and the Czech Republic [ 35 ], with varied designs and implementations between jurisdictions. In particular, there is significant investment in these programs in Australia, with every state and territory having introduced at least one financial incentive program [ 23 – 29 ], with an estimated investment of over $470 M AUD [ 36 ]. Whilst there is significant government investment in financial incentive programs in Australia [ 36 ], there is limited publicly available information on the evaluation of these programs, with one program (Active Kids, New South Wales) having a rigorous, peer-reviewed evaluation [ 37 , 38 ]. Evaluation of public health programs can help to understand whether programs are being adopted and implemented as intended, are reaching their intended recipients, and achieving their intended outcomes [ 39 ]. Rigorous program evaluations also contribute to developing evidence-based public health policy [ 40 ]. However, determining the most suitable evaluation strategy is complex, with at least 71 different frameworks for evaluating physical activity and dietary change programs available [ 40 ]. The RE-AIM framework [ 41 ] is the most commonly used evaluation framework for physical activity interventions in the academic literature and recommends evaluating program reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation and program maintenance [ 42 ]. However, even in well-resourced settings, applying all 34 criteria across the five RE-AIM dimensions can be prohibitive [ 43 ]. Subsequently, the RE-AIM authors have recommended that the framework can be applied pragmatically, with justification for the selection of dimensions used [ 41 , 43 ]. This allows for a more adaptable framework, but also presents challenges for evaluators, in determining what to measure, which methods are most appropriate and how to incorporate participatory research methods into program evaluations [ 40 , 43 ]. Informed by the RE-AIM framework and existing evaluations of financial incentives for children’s sports and physical activity participation, this study aimed to undertake a rigorous stakeholder consultation process to develop evaluation recommendations for a population-based financial incentive for children’s sports participation – the South Australian (SA) Sports Voucher program. We aimed to answer several research questions: What are stakeholders’ opinions on the most important evaluation measures for the SA Sports Voucher Program? What are the most suitable measures of program reach? How to evaluate how well the program is operating for those using and delivering it (process measures)? What are the indicators of program effectiveness? The Delphi approach is suitable for our study as it allows for systematic, iterative consultation with a diverse panel of stakeholders, facilitating the consensus-based evaluation recommendations. Method Ethics This study was approved by the University of South Australia Human Research Ethics Committee (protocol no. 204705). All participants gave informed consent to participate as described in the methods. Study design This study used a Delphi methodology to develop evaluation recommendations for a population-based financial incentive to promote children’s sports participation. Delphi studies involve the development of consensus between anonymous stakeholders through multiple rounds of inquiry [ 44 , 45 ]. This Delphi study used online surveys developed in the Qualtrics survey platform (Qualtrics software, Version XM, Qualtrics, Provo, Utah, USA) and complies with the recommendations for the Conducting and REporting of DElphi Studies (CREDES) [ 45 ]. Context This study focusses on the SA Sports Voucher program, which was introduced by the Government of South Australia in 2015, with the stated aim “to increase the number of children playing organised sport or participating in dance or learn to swim activities by reducing cost as a barrier” [ 25 ]. The program offers users a subsidy of $100 for membership or registration fees for one eligible activity per year, and is universally available to SA children from the first year of elementary school (age approximately 5 years) through to Year 9 (age approximately 15 years) [ 25 ]. In 2022, 85,444 vouchers were claimed to the value of $8,506,187 [ 45 ]. The SA Sports Voucher program administrators have conducted ad-hoc process evaluations and recently partnered with academics to analyse program uptake [ 46 ]. Comprehensive evaluation of whether the program is achieving its aim has not yet been undertaken. This study was initiated by the Government of South Australia, in partnership with researchers, to address this knowledge gap. Panel selection and recruitment Panellists representing a range of stakeholder groups were identified and invited to participate in the Delphi study. These included academics, based nationally and internationally, with expertise in areas relevant to Sports Vouchers (such as Health economists, children’s physical activity and sport researchers; identified through relevant peer review literature); representatives from Australian States/Territories administering financial incentives for children’s sport (identified through the Cross-Jurisdictional Voucher Scheme Network); representatives from other SA Government agencies with relevant interest (Wellbeing SA, Education, Treasury; identified through the industry partner’s professional networks); parents/guardians of SA Sports Voucher users (identified through the Sports Voucher user database); providers of eligible services in SA (e.g., state organisations and sports club administrators from different regions and diverse socioeconomic areas; identified through Office of Recreation, Sport and Racing Sports Voucher database); and representatives from organisations supporting children from culturally diverse and/or disadvantaged backgrounds (identified through the industry partner’s professional networks). To ensure adequate representation from each stakeholder group, we aimed to recruit 5–10 participants from each group [ 47 ]. Panellists were invited to participate by email which contained information on the Delphi study, explaining its purpose, time commitment, the voluntary nature of the Delphi study, and the anticipated outcomes [ 44 ], and sought their consent to participate. They were considered participants if they responded to the email agreeing to participate. Delphi survey The Delphi study consisted of three rounds of survey conducted between October 2022 and April 2023. The Round 1 survey was informed by a scoping review of evaluations of financial incentives for children’s participation in sport and physical activity [ 48 ]. As the scoping review identified that the evaluation approaches used were largely focused on reach and effectiveness, the RE-AIM framework [ 41 , 43 ] was also used to inform the Round 1 survey. The RE-AIM framework has been selected to underpin this research for several key reasons. Firstly, it provides a comprehensive framework for program evaluation [ 41 , 43 , 49 ]. Secondly, its pragmatic application is conducive to incorporating stakeholders’ opinions and priorities, as the domains selected for the evaluation can be informed by stakeholder preferences. The Round 2 and Round 3 surveys were developed from the Delphi panel responses and the panel were given a report summarising the results from the previous round. The survey was conducted online using the Qualtrics survey platform (Qualtrics software, Version XM, Qualtrics, Provo, UT), with each round being open for 3 weeks with reminder emails sent at 7 and 14 days. The methods are summarised in Fig. 1 . The beginning of each survey described the aim of the SA Sports Voucher program and definitions of common terms and processes relevant to the program and evaluation (e.g., User, Provider, Sport, Reach evaluation, the claiming process). The participants completed the same survey, presented in lay language, for each round, ensuring clarity for and accessibility for all participants. Round 1 The aim of the Round 1 Delphi survey was to understand the importance of potential evaluation components for the SA Sports Voucher, encompassing broad evaluation approaches such as reach and effectiveness, and to generate possible evaluation measures, identifying potential data sources for measurement. The survey had 17 items (Supplementary file 1) informed by the RE-AIM framework and a scoping review on financial incentives for children’s sport and physical activity [ 41 , 43 , 48 ]. It collected demographic information (location and gender) and professional information (stakeholder group and job role, if relevant). Participants were invited to rate their level of agreement on the importance of different defined evaluation components (reach, process, and effectiveness) on a 7-point Likert scale: (1) “Strongly disagree” (2) “Disagree” (3) “Somewhat disagree” (4) “Neutral/unsure” (5) “Somewhat agree” (6) “Agree” (7) “Strongly agree”. Participants were invited to share their thoughts and ideas about suitable measures and/or methods of evaluation and the barriers, facilitators, and other considerations important to each evaluation component in open-ended items. The definition of consensus varies between Delphi studies [ 50 ]. In our study, consensus was defined a priori as ≥ 70% of respondents rating an item as agree (6) or strongly agree (7). Round 1 was piloted with 4 representatives of the Delphi panel (1 academic, 1 government representative and 2 parents/guardians). Minor changes to the wording of the survey were made in response to pilot feedback. Round 2 The aim of the Round 2 Delphi survey (Supplementary file 2) was to seek participants’ views on the importance of the evaluation measures proposed by participants in the in the open-ended questions in the Round 1 survey and to generate evaluation methods. Participants were provided with a report summarising the findings from Round 1 (Supplementary file 3). Evaluation measures generated from Round 1 responses included additional measures of program reach, reasons for non-participation, measures of process evaluation for program users and providers, measures of participation, and other measures of program effectiveness. Participants were required to rate the importance of the measure or their satisfaction with the measure on a 7-point Likert scale, from “not at all important” (1) to “very important” (7), or “not at all satisfied” (1) to “very satisfied” (7). Participants were invited to suggest suitable way(s) to collect data and for any other comments. The Round 2 Delphi results were separated into four categories: Evaluation measures that reached consensus (≥ 70% agreement). Evaluation measures that approached consensus (60–69% agreement). Evaluation measures lacking consensus that were modified for re-review based on participant comments. Evaluation measures that neither reached nor approach consensus (≤ 59% agreement). Round 3 The aim of the Round 3 Delphi (Supplementary file 4) was to seek participants’ views on the different methods of evaluation (e.g., survey, focus groups, interviews, mixed methods), put forward by participants in Round 2, for the evaluation measures that reached consensus in Round 2. Participants were provided with a report on the Round 2 results (Supplementary file 5) summarising the measures that reached consensus, the measures that approached consensus, and those that did not reach consensus and therefore would not be recommended for the Sports Voucher evaluation. The report also summarised two measures that did not reach consensus that had been modified in response to comments from the Round 2 survey, which the participants would be asked to review. For each evaluation measure, participants were asked to indicate their preferred evaluation methods using 5 multiple choice options (survey, interviews, focus groups, a combination of survey and focus group/interview, or don’t mind). Participants were also asked to rate the importance of/satisfaction with these evaluation methods on a 7-point Likert scale. Each question provided participants with a brief explanation of the possible evaluation methods. Finally, participants were required to indicate their views on how regularly each method of evaluation should be used (2–4 times per year, annually, every 2–4 years, 5 + years) and when the overall evaluation approach should be reviewed (open-ended). Data analysis and management Overall survey response rate was calculated as the percentage of participants that completed the survey from all participants contacted. Demographic information was reported descriptively (categorical data described as percentages). The level of agreement for the Likert items was expressed as percentages and whether they met consensus. For the Round 3 results, percentages were used to determine the preferred methods of evaluation from the multiple-choice items. When a mixed methods approach was favoured, the strength of agreement with Likert items were used to prioritise the methods. For example, if surveys garnered higher agreement than qualitative measures, they were deemed a higher priority. A consensus threshold of ≥ 70% was applied to the remaining Likert items, in alignment with the criteria used in Rounds 1 and 2. Median proportions were used to determine when the overall evaluation approach should be reviewed. Lastly, to ensure the rigour of proposed evaluation, a subgroup analysis of academics’ responses was conducted. Qualitative analysis included categorisation of data by pattern or theme [ 51 ], with the analysis completed by the first author (CS) then cross-checked by at least one other member of the research team (CM, RV, MC, SL, BF). Results Delphi proper In total, 67 stakeholders agreed to participate in the Delphi study and were invited to contribute to all three rounds (Fig. 1 ). Fifty-eight of the 67 participants responded to Round 1 (response rate 87%) and provided information on their characteristics (Table 1 ). Most participants were from Australia ( n = 54, 93.2%, Table 1 ). This predominance is due to the SA Sports Voucher program’s more substantial stakeholder presence within Australia. Participants from outside Australia ( n = 4, 6.8%) were from Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Singapore. Eleven participants identified themselves as representing multiple stakeholder groups. Parents/guardians of Sports Voucher users made up the largest group with 17 participants. Amongst them, eight were directly recruited as parent/guardian stakeholders, and the other nine, although representing different stakeholder categories, also identified as parents of Sports Voucher users. Round 1 The Round 1 Delphi survey focussed on discerning which potential evaluation components stakeholders considered important and to identify possible evaluation measures. Participants’ ratings of agreement/disagreement with different potential evaluation components are shown in Table 2 . Most of the participants agreed that it was important to evaluate the program reach (89% agreement), reasons for non-use of the program (89% agreement), to collect process measures from program users (83% agreement) and providers (91% agreement) and evaluate the impact of the program on users’ participation in sport and physical activity (98% agreement). All items exceeded the a priori threshold for consensus. Participants proposed measures they deemed valuable for each component of the Sports Voucher program evaluation. When assessing program reach, frequently suggested themes included measures of cultural and linguistically diverse status (CALD), socioeconomic status, household size and school sector. Reasons for not using the voucher included awareness of the program, accessibility factors like language and digital literacy, and the availability of suitable providers. Themes for user and provider process evaluation included acceptability and accessibility of the program, and a broad exploration of beliefs and attitudes around the program. Measures of program effectiveness were explored in two categories: participation-related metrics, and other effectiveness measures. Under participation measures, themes included determining whether the subsidy influenced the user’s decision to participate and the child’s engagement in the subsidised activity. Additional proposed effectiveness measures included mental health, social wellbeing, and quality of life, as we as economic impacts. Notably, the extra costs associated with sport emerged as a recurrent theme across several evaluation components, such as reach and both user and provider process evaluation. Examples of Delphi participant comments are presented in Table 3 . Participants provided comments on barriers, facilitators, and other considerations for the Sports Voucher evaluation. Common themes (Table 4 ) included cost, time, accessibility of evaluation, and the need (and difficulty) of recruiting a representative sample. Round 2 The Round 2 Delphi survey sought participants’ views on the importance of different evaluation measures put forward by participants in Round 1. Forty-nine participants responded (73% response rate). Eleven items reached consensus (≥ 70% agreement), and four items approached consensus (60–69% agreement) (Table 5 ). Results are presented in Table 5 , with green denoting consensus, orange denoting items that approached consensus or progressed to Round 3, and red denoting items that did not reach consensus. Items reaching consensus included measuring awareness of the Sports Voucher program; the cost of sport; process evaluation with providers; a budget allocation for the evaluation; the user’s attendance at subsidised activities; and whether the Sports Voucher enabled participation. Several Delphi participants highlighted the importance of representativeness in the data collection, emphasising socio-economic status and culturally and linguistically diverse status. Delphi participants reviewed and commented on the phrasing of the existing sociodemographic questions used during registration. Of the 31 participants that provided comments, recurrent themes emerged. These encompassed the addition of a ‘prefer not to say’ option ( n = 4), aligning the phrasing of items with how they are collected in reference datasets ( n = 3) and proposing an alternative measure of CALD status ( n = 10). None of the additional items pertaining to the characteristics of Sports Voucher users reach consensus (as shown in Table 5 ). Due to the volume of comments about the current question assessing CALD status (presently framed as “is your child from a culturally and linguistically diverse background?” y/n), the ‘main language spoken in the child’s home’ progressed to Round 3 to seek participant views on using it as an alternative measure. All other pre-existing measures of reach will be retained in user registration, including the use of postcode as an indicator of socioeconomic status. Round 3 The Round 3 Delphi determined methodological priorities and timeframes for the measures that reached consensus in the previous rounds. Forty-five participants responded (67% response rate). For most evaluation measures, a mixed method approach was preferred, except participation measures (i.e., user attendance and voucher enabled participation) where survey were the preferred method (Table 6 ). Where a mixed methods evaluation was preferred, Likert items were used to prioritise the evaluation method (Table 6 ). This facilitated the creation of an adaptable evaluation framework that is modifiable to different budget allocations. Table 6 summarises the Round 3 results with green denoting a recommended evaluation method, yellow denoting a recommended but lower priority evaluation method and red denoting a method not recommended by the Delphi panel. Participants rated their satisfaction with changing the question asked on registration, “Is your child from a culturally and linguistically diverse background? (yes/no)” to “What is the main language spoken in the child’s home (select box that applies – 5 most commonly spoken languages in SA)? or prefer not to say”. Consensus was reached for the proposed change. Participants also rated their satisfaction with measuring awareness amongst eligible users using a state-based epidemiological survey (the Population Health Survey Module System (PHSMS) [ 52 ]), with a consensus reached with the academic/researcher participants but not all participants. Academic/researcher results reported when consensus differed from overall results. Most evaluation methods were recommended to be used annually (range 50–88%), except for focus groups/interviews with users (60%) and non-users (48%), which were recommended to be used every 2–4 years. The median time to review the evaluation approach was 2.5 years. The final evaluation recommendations were summarised and linked with RE-AIM framework (Fig. 2 ), with measures relevant to the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption and Implementations dimension supported by the Delphi panel, with the Maintenance dimension being excluded in Round 2. Discussion This study used a Delphi process to gather perspectives and recommendations from a range of stakeholders, culminating in an evaluation strategy for the SA Sports Voucher program, a population-wide financial incentive for children’s sports participation. The participation of stakeholders, ranging from local experts to international authorities, ensured the evaluation strategy was both pragmatic and a significant contribution to this emerging research domain. Results revealed that stakeholders saw value in evaluating a wide range of program impacts, including program reach, reasons for non-use of the program, user and provider experiences, program adoption and implementation by providers, and users’ participation in the incentivised activities. This has resulted in the development of comprehensive evaluation recommendations, anchored in the RE-AIM framework. The recommendations included maintaining routine measures, leveraging existing population-based data collection systems, and the introduction of additional evaluation measures to fill knowledge gaps. Strengths and limitations To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first application of a Delphi study to achieve consensus on an evaluation strategy. Typically, Delphi studies tend to recruit a narrower group of panellists with specific expertise in the area of interest (such as specialist clinicians) [ 45 , 53 ]. A strength of this study was the diverse stakeholder participation in the panel representing a broad range of both local, national, and international perspectives relevant to this population-wide program. Participation by end users in research planning is recognised as best practice in implementation research, a noted strength of this approach being the involvement of end users in identifying research priorities [ 54 ]. The Delphi process used in this study facilitated the development of a pragmatic evaluation recommendations for the SA Sports Voucher program, that are modifiable to different budget allocations. The involvement of government representatives enhanced this process, as research-policy partnerships in evaluation are important for generating relevant and practical evidence [ 55 ]. The collaboration between government representatives and academics has resulted in a highly implementable output that will strengthen the rigour of program evaluation. The Delphi process proved to be an effective method for stakeholder collaboration and contributed academic rigour to government decision making. The three-round structure of addressing evaluation components, measures and finally, methods could be transferrable to devising evaluation recommendations for other public health programs. The three Delphi rounds had an average response rate of 76%, which is consistent with the overall response rate of Delphi studies [ 56 ]. Whilst best efforts were made to recruit a diverse panel representing many stakeholder groups, we acknowledge that society is deeply diverse, and it was not possible to represent all aspects of this diversity. Participation in this Delphi study required proficiency in written English, which meant that children and people who could not read/write in English could not contribute. Furthermore, the Round 3 Delphi sought the participants’ views on rigorous evaluation methods. Whilst all participants’ responses were valuable, not all stakeholders had the knowledge to advise on this. Therefore, a subgroup analysis of researchers’ responses was conducted. Implications and future directions The Delphi methodology employed in this study not only paved the way for a robust evaluation strategy for the SA Sports Voucher program but also yielded unforeseen positive outcomes. Conducted through a research-policy partnership, the study convened a diverse set of stakeholders. As an unanticipated byproduct of the Delphi discussions, various government departments converged on an inter-departmental agreement. This newfound collaboration will facilitate ongoing data collection and data sharing from an existing epidemiological survey managed by Wellbeing SA, thereby bolstering the evaluation capabilities of the Sports Voucher program, overseen by the Office for Recreation, Sport, and Racing. Similarly, the Delphi process provided insight and facilitated information sharing from other Australian jurisdictions regarding their evaluations. This subsequently informed the development of survey items that will allow for cross-jurisdictional comparison of program awareness. This Delphi process has established evaluation recommendations for a program in operation since 2015. Whilst it is recommended that evaluation be considered from program inception [ 57 , 58 ], this is often not sufficiently practiced. Some jurisdictions mandate that government programs are evaluated [ 59 ], including external evaluations for programs exceeding a pre-determined level of investment. However, in other jurisdictions, it is at the discretion of program administrators. Although opinions diverge on budgetary portions dedicated to evaluations – ranging from 1% [ 59 ] to beyond 10% [ 60 ], a hesitancy persists in rerouting money from program delivery to evaluation. This study found that a comprehensive evaluation is highly value by diverse stakeholder groups, even if it means reallocating funds from service delivery to evaluation. This study highlighted that equity in program accessibility is a priority to stakeholders. Whilst universal programs offer simplicity of administration and may reduce the stigma associated with accessing a financial support program (compared to means-tested programs), there can be inequity in who accesses the program. Lower socioeconomic status children and families are significantly less likely to access financial incentives for children’s sports and physical activity [ 30 , 46 , 61 ]. Those at the highest risk of non-communicable diseases face barriers to accessing community-based programs, due to language, cultural, personal and other systemic barriers [ 62 ]. Including measures of program reach into low socioeconomic status families should be considered for all similar public health programs. Further to this, future research into strategies that support underrepresented groups to access financial incentive programs is warranted. The cost of sport resonated strongly across stakeholder groups. Foley et al. examined annual sport costs in the Australian state of New South Wales, identifying that a $100 sport voucher supported just 20% of annual sports expenditure [ 38 ]. Financial constraints often deter participation from low-income families [ 21 , 22 ], so understanding the broader economic landscape is pivotal. In Australia, AusPlay provides nationwide data on sports registration and membership costs [ 63 ]. However, ancillary costs, like transport, equipment, and uniforms, are not well understood. Undertaking a program of work to understand the broader financial costs of participating in sport, including how to measure and address these costs, would provide valuable insight into one of the major barriers to children and young people’s sport participation. Conclusion This study underscores the power of inclusive stakeholder engagement in shaping comprehensive evaluation strategies for public health programs. The Delphi process facilitated insights from a broad range of stakeholders, leading to evaluation recommendations for a population-wide children’s sports voucher program. The findings underscore the need to allocate program funds to evaluation, and highlighted areas of priority for future research, particularly in understanding barriers to program access and the comprehensive costs of sports participation. As governments and organisations worldwide seek to optimise their programs, this study serves as a testament to the importance of collaborative, evidence-based decision-making.
