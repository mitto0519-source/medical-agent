ABSTRACT Background: Psychophysiological dysregulations and negative alterations in cognitions and mood characterize post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex PTSD (C-PTSD), contributing to an increased risk of disorder persistence and chronic health problems. However, understanding the differences in physiological stress reactivity and negative cognitive-emotional patterns between adolescents with PTSD and C-PTSD remains a notable research gap. Objective: This study examined group differences in autonomic nervous system (ANS) reactivity during the sharing of a short trauma narrative, including resting and recovery phases, and compared subjective experiences of stress, shame, and guilt among adolescents with PTSD, C-PTSD, and trauma-exposed controls. Methods: In a repeated-measures design, 52 adolescents (14–18 years) with PTSD ( n = 17), C-PTSD ( n = 18), and a control group ( n = 17) were assessed for heart rate, heart rate variability, and subjective experiences of stress, shame, and guilt during a standardized trauma interview, as well as during baseline and recovery phases. Results: Linear mixed-effects models revealed a significant interaction between group and time point ( F = 4.134, p &lt; .001). The C-PTSD group exhibited a significantly higher heart rate in the recovery phase compared to the PTSD ( p = .010) and control groups ( p = .036), alongside significantly higher subjective stress, guilt, and shame experiences. Main effects of group were identified for perceived stress ( F = 7.543, p = .002), guilt ( F = 21.779, p &lt; .001), and shame ( F = 19.309, p &lt; .001), with the C-PTSD group exhibiting higher levels compared to PTSD and control groups across all conditions. Conclusions: Prolonged stress responses and elevated experiences of shame and guilt in adolescents with C-PTSD align with the diagnostic criteria of affective dysregulation and negative self-concept. Objective stress measures during trauma interviews may support the diagnosis of C-PTSD. Findings highlight the importance of phase-based trauma therapies that target emotional dysregulation, shame, and guilt. KEYWORDS: Post traumatic stress, heart rate, autonomic dysregulation, shame, trauma narrative HIGHLIGHTS This was the first study examining autonomic nervous system responses to short trauma narratives and the following recovery phase in adolescents with C-PTSD compared to PTSD and trauma-exposed youth without a (C-)PTSD diagnosis. Heart rate recovery following the trauma interview appears to be a promising candidate biomarker for C-PTSD, as the C-PTSD group showed significantly slower heart rate recovery compared to both the PTSD and trauma-exposed control groups. Compared to the PTSD and control groups, individuals with C-PTSD further exhibited significantly higher levels of guilt and shame at baseline, across all narrative phases and during recovery. Introduction Around two-thirds of children and adolescents experience a traumatic event before reaching adulthood (Landolt et al., 2013 ; Lewis et al., 2019 ; McLaughlin et al., 2013 ). A substantial proportion of individuals develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or complex PTSD (C-PTSD), with the highest incidence observed after interpersonal trauma (Alisic et al., 2014 ; Daniunaite et al., 2021 ; Hiller et al., 2016 ; Redican et al., 2022 ). Untreated, both disorders are rather persistent, disabling, and associated with increased psychiatric comorbidity alongside compromised physical health (Hiller et al., 2016 ; Ho et al., 2021 ; Kazlauskas et al., 2024 ; Lewis et al., 2019 ; Lofthouse et al., 2024 ; Morina et al., 2014 ; Redican et al., 2022 ). According to the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11; World Health Organization, 2022 ), PTSD involves core symptoms of re-experiencing the trauma, avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, and changes in arousal and reactivity. C-PTSD requires all diagnostic criteria of PTSD along with additional disturbances in self-organization, including affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and disturbances in relationships (Cloitre et al., 2013 ; Maercker et al., 2013 ; World Health Organization, 2022 ). Although trauma, by definition, precedes (C-)PTSD, not all children and adolescents exposed to trauma develop either of these disorders, highlighting the importance of investigating the differences between those with and without (C-)PTSD (Hiller et al., 2016 ). Physiological disruptions, including alterations in the autonomic nervous system (ANS), could be key differentiating factors, as they contribute to impaired stress responses to trauma-related stimuli and elevate the risk of chronic health complications (Dayan et al., 2016 ; Grasso &amp; Simons, 2012 ; Siciliano et al., 2022 ; Speer et al., 2019 ). Building on the allostatic load model (McEwen, 1998 ), physiological dysregulation in trauma-exposed individuals results from wear and tear on stress systems due to continuous activation by intense or prolonged stress. This may result in either a hyperreactive profile, characterized by initial activation of stress systems with delayed recovery (i.e. prolonged response), or a hyporesponsive profile, marked by a blunted response to stressors (McEwen, 1998 ). The parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) is involved in stress responses, particularly through the vagal regulation of heart rate (HR) (Porges, 2007 ; Zantvoord et al., 2024 ). Extensive literature documents autonomic irregularities in adults with PTSD, including meta-analyses showing heightened sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity and diminished PNS activity across different tasks (e.g. resting, startle response, standardized, and idiographic trauma cue paradigms; Pole, 2007 ; Schneider &amp; Schwerdtfeger, 2020 ). Considering meta-analytic findings, Schneider and Schwerdtfeger ( 2020 ) reported that adults with PTSD, compared to healthy controls, exhibit significantly reduced parasympathetic activity at rest, as reflected by lower root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), low-frequency heart rate variability (LF-HRV), and high-frequency HRV (HF-HRV), as well as reduced normalized NN intervals (SDNN), a higher LF/HF ratio indicating sympathetic dominance, and elevated heart rate (HR) both at rest and during stress exposure. Similarly, another meta-analysis demonstrated significantly reduced HF-HRV and RMSSD at rest, but no significant differences in LF-HRV, among individuals with PTSD compared to healthy controls, suggesting diminished parasympathetic cardiac control and underscoring the potential diagnostic and therapeutic relevance of specific HRV indices (Ge et al., 2020 ). However, the findings from adult samples cannot be generalized to youth, given the substantial development of the ANS during childhood and adolescence (Koenig, 2020 ), as well as differences in developmental experiences, social contexts, and environmental factors (Harteveld et al., 2021 ; Siciliano et al., 2022 ). Yet, research on the ANS in children and adolescents with PTSD is limited and produces inconsistent findings, with results differing from those observed in adult samples (Campbell et al., 2019 ; Grasso &amp; Simons, 2012 ; Siciliano et al., 2022 ). A recent meta-analysis focusing on children and adolescents found that during stress tasks only increased overall ANS reactivity (e.g. HR, HRV, LF-HRV) was weakly associated with post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) in children and adolescents, while isolated PNS (e.g. respiratory sinus arrhythmia [RSA], HF-HRV) or SNS measures (e.g. pre-ejection period [PEP], skin conductance response [SCR]) showed no significant associations, whereas during rest only SNS activity showed a significant relationship with PTSS (Siciliano et al., 2022 ). In contrast, a study investigating specific physiological responses to threat-related images in youth with PTSD, compared to healthy controls, reported no significant group differences in HR responses, but slightly elevated acoustic startle reflexes and sustained SCRs, indicating increased sympathetic arousal under stress (Grasso &amp; Simons, 2012 ). However, when examining HR responses to trauma-related stimuli or narratives, several studies indicate that trauma-exposed youth exhibit elevated HR during active engagement, such as trauma narration, with this reactivity being associated with PTSD symptom severity (Bailey et al., 2023 ; Haag et al., 2019 ; Saltzman et al., 2005 ; Scheeringa et al., 2004 ), whereas passive exposure does not appear to result in significant HR differences (Jones-Alexander et al., 2005 ; Kirsch et al., 2015 ). Empirical evidence further suggests that greater trauma exposure is associated with increased SNS reactivity during trauma narratives in children and adolescents (Grasser et al., 2022 ). While findings by Siciliano et al. ( 2022 ) indicate a limited role of PNS activity at rest in youth with PTSD, a separate meta-analysis examining RSA as a marker of PNS activity across both adult and youth samples found a small but significant association with PTSD at rest, with stronger effects observed in adults than in younger populations (Campbell et al., 2019 ). Supporting this, studies on HRV have demonstrated greater RSA withdrawal during trauma recall in preschool-aged children with PTSD compared to trauma-exposed controls (Gray et al., 2018 ). In the context of trauma treatment, HR typically increases in early exposure phases of trauma-focused therapies due to sympathetic activation, accompanied by reduced HRV, and followed by gradual HR reduction across sessions, reflecting habituation and diminished threat perception (Wangelin &amp; Tuerk, 2015 ). Although autonomic habituation during trauma-focused therapy is more consistently documented in adults (Raz &amp; Lahad, 2022 ; Sack et al., 2008 ), emerging youth data show similar trends, as a recent single-case study with adolescents reported session-level HR habituation alongside symptom improvements (Rocca et al., 2025 ), and a randomized controlled feasibility trial reported decreasing RSA during trauma exposure and increasing RSA stability over time (Shenk et al., 2022 ), both predictive of better outcomes. Further evidence from a larger study on TF-CBT and EMDR indicates that youth with (partial) PTSD showed higher overall HR during both neutral and trauma imagery, and decreased RSA during trauma imagery, with treatment-related changes limited to increased RSA in responders when applying a more stringent response criterion, while HR and PEP remained unchanged (Zantvoord et al., 2024 ). When predicting treatment outcomes in adults, it has been shown that stronger initial HR reactivity and higher baseline HRV predict better outcomes, whereas blunted responses are associated with poorer treatment response and increased dropout rates (Carpenter et al., 2024 ; Soder et al., 2019 ). Overall, understanding the physiological basis of C-PTSD remains a significant research gap. Biological frameworks provide essential insights into the understanding of (C-)PTSD, by highlighting heightened physiological arousal resulting from both top-down processes, primarily driven by fear and anxiety and involving cortical regulation (Freed &amp; D’Andrea, 2015 ), and bottom-up processes, underlying subconscious defensive responses and originating in the cerebellum, brainstem, and midbrain regions, including the reticular activating system, the innate alarm system (e.g. amygdala, medial prefrontal cortex, parahippocampus, visual cortex), the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine system, the dorsolateral periaqueductal grey, and the superior colliculus (Lanius et al., 2017 ; Liddell et al., 2005 ; McCall et al., 2024 ; Naegeli et al., 2018 ). Complementing these accounts, cognitive models (Ehlers &amp; Clark, 2000 ) and the emotional processing theory (Foa et al., 2006 ; Foa &amp; Kozak, 1986 ) highlight the role of trauma-related negative appraisals, such as shame and guilt. The emotional processing theory (Foa &amp; Kozak, 1986 ) posits that the reduction of pathological fear, as observed in PTSD, requires both the activation of the fear memory and the integration of corrective cognitive and emotional information that contradicts the dysfunctional components of the fear structure, thereby enabling the formation of a new, adaptive memory representation. Consistently, the cognitive model of Ehlers and Clark ( 2000 ) attributes the maintenance of PTSD to maladaptive interpretations of the trauma and its aftermath, in which dysfunctional appraisals (e.g. guilt, shame) and disturbances in autobiographical memory contribute to a persistent perception of current threat. Based on this model, effective treatment should target three core processes: the elaboration and contextual integration of the trauma memory, the modification of dysfunctional appraisals, and the reduction of maladaptive cognitive and behavioural strategies that interfere with adaptive processing and maintain symptomatology (Ehlers &amp; Clark, 2000 ). In line with these theoretical models, evidence suggests that these distorted self-appraisals are key predictors of PTSD symptom development, mediating the relationship between time or treatment and PTSD symptom reduction (Brown et al., 2019 ; Bub &amp; Lommen, 2017 ; Cunningham et al., 2018 ; Seah et al., 2023 ). Correspondingly, reductions in negative self-beliefs and improvements in trauma memory integration have been linked to symptom reduction (Kangaslampi &amp; Peltonen, 2020 ; McLean et al., 2015 ). Although prevalent in PTSD, these self-appraisals are more central to the C-PTSD framework. However, studies examining their differences across adolescents with PTSD, C-PTSD, and trauma-exposed adolescents without a (C-)PTSD diagnosis are missing. 1.1. Current study To summarize, evidence regarding biomarkers in youth with PTSD is both limited and inconclusive, and there is a significant research gap concerning psychophysiological markers in youth with C-PTSD, despite their importance for understanding underlying psychophysiological processes, improving diagnostic accuracy, and tailoring therapeutic interventions. Moreover, research on group differences in stress, shame, and guilt among adolescents with PTSD, C-PTSD, and controls is lacking. Thus, the present study aimed to examine differences in ANS (re)activity, measured by HR and heart rate variability (HRV), in trauma-exposed youth with C-PTSD, PTSD, and those without a (C-)PTSD diagnosis across trauma narrative, resting, and recovery phases. Additionally, differences in subjective stress, shame, and guilt were explored. We expected to observe differences in HR and HRV both between diagnostic groups and across the resting, narrative, and recovery phases within these groups. 2. Method 2.1. Sample Participants aged between 14 and 18 years were recruited at the Department of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry at the Medical University of Vienna in Austria. Both inpatients and outpatients with a history of traumatic events were included. The most recent traumatic event had to have occurred at least 6 months prior. In total, we included 56 adolescents with a history of trauma in this study, however, 4 (all female) patients refused to participate in the study at the beginning. The final sample included 52 participants (38 female, 14 male), comprising 18 individuals diagnosed with C-PTSD, 17 diagnosed with PTSD, and 17 control participants who had experienced a significant traumatic event but were diagnosed with an affective symptoms. The characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1 . There were no significant differences between the groups regarding age ( F = 2.196, p = .122) and gender ( χ² = 5.890, p = .053), but significant for biological sex ( χ² = 7.800, p = .020). Sensitivity analysis for biological sex revealed no significant differences for main effects (see Supplement 3). 2.2. Procedures The protocol for this study (no. 1573/2019) was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of the Medical University of Vienna. Participation was voluntary and not rewarded, and written informed consent was obtained from both the participants and their legal guardians. After inclusion, participants were invited to a single-session experiment. Based on the previous medical record and a confirmatory assessment using clinical interviews, participants were assigned to the respective groups: (1) meeting the ICD-11 criteria for C-PTSD diagnosis, (2) meeting the ICD-11 criteria for PTSD diagnosis, or (3) showing affective symptoms and having experienced potentially traumatic events. Exposure to a (C-)PTSD criterion A traumatic event was assessed through an interview-based administration of the German version of the Life Event Checklist – Child Version for DSM-IV. The procedure is outlined in Figure 1 . Participants were guided to a waiting room to complete questionnaires about general mental health. Afterwards, a mobile ECG measuring continuously the autonomic nervous system was attached. The measurements started with a 5-min baseline resting-state assessment, followed by a 10-min trauma narrative, and concluded with a 5-min recovery phase in a resting state. Between the baseline and trauma narrative, participants briefly completed questionnaires, while a 30-min waiting period between the trauma narrative and recovery phases allowed for physiological and psychological recovery, during which participants rested and completed additional questionnaires. Visual analogue scales (VAS) assessing perceived stress, shame, and guilt were administered at four time points. For the trauma narratives, participants were instructed using the standardized prompt from the CAPS-CA-5 (Pynoos et al., 2015 ) to report, in as much detail as possible, the event they considered the most distressing, for 10 min. If the spontaneous account did not last the full 10 min, open-ended questions were used to extend the description, focusing on the experience, thoughts, and emotions. The ANS measurements during the narrative were split into two parts to capture distinct responses at both the initiation and progression of the narrative. Throughout the ECG measurements, participants remained seated. 2.3. Measures Questionnaires: The German Version of the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) by Cohen et al. ( 1983 ) was utilized to assess perceived stress over the past month. Participants rated all items on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = always). The PSS-10 was administered on-site prior to the start of the trauma narrative. Further, we used the German translation of the Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI-II; Kühner et al., 2007 ) to assess depressive symptoms over the past two weeks through 21 items. Each item offered four response options on a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3, with 3 indicating the strongest symptom severity. Clinical Interview: After screening the patients for traumatic events and reviewing their medical histories, they were further assessed using the German version of the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 in Children and Adolescents (CAPS-CA-5; Pynoos et al., 2015 ). The CAPS-CA-5 is a structured interview designed to evaluate PTSD in children aged 7 and older, similar to the widely used adult version of the CAPS-5. It assesses DSM-5 criteria through 30 items that have been adapted for age-appropriateness, including image-based response options. Responses are rated on a scale from 0 (no problem) to 4 (extreme or frequent problem). According to the CAPS-CA-5 scoring rule, symptoms or impairments are considered present if rated with a severity of 2 or higher. Supplement 1 describes our framework for an ICD-11-adapted algorithm. Visual Analogue Scales: To indicate momentary perceived stress, shame, and guilt, participants rated single-item VAS at four time points: before the trauma narrative, immediately after it, 30 and 60 min post-narrative. The VAS were printed on paper and distributed to participants at each of the four time points. Participants were asked to rate how stressed, ashamed, and guilty they felt at that moment. Responses for each were recorded by marking a 100 mm line with a pen, where 0 indicated ‘not at all’ and 100 indicated ‘extremely.’ Across the VAS measures, 13 stress ratings were missing (6.4% of 204; Control = 4, PTSD = 1, C-PTSD = 8), 12 guilt ratings were missing (5.9%; Control = 4, C-PTSD = 8), and 12 shame ratings were missing (5.9%; Control = 4, C-PTSD = 8). Electrophysiological Measures : HR served as an indicator of stress-related autonomic nervous system activity. Additionally, the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) served as a measure of HRV, representing a short-term parasympathetic component of HRV. Cardiovascular data were continuously recorded over the experiment using the movisens EcgMove 4 sensor (movisens GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany), with a sampling rate of 1024 Hz. The sensor, attached to a chest belt with two integrated electrodes, was positioned beneath the sternum. The ECG signals were converted into time-series HR (beats per minute) and RMSSD (milliseconds) using the DataAnalyzer® software (movisens GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany). HR and HRV were analyzed in continuous 5-min intervals. Due to technical issues, one HR value was missing in the C-PTSD group (0.5% of all HR time points), and 34 HRV values were missing – nine in the C-PTSD group, 16 in the PTSD group, and nine in the traumatic-event-with-affective-symptoms group – representing 16.7% of all HRV time points. 2.4. Analyses All analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.3 (R Core Team, 2021 ), utilizing the packages ‘lme4’ (Bates et al., 2015 ) and ‘ggplot2’ (Wickham, 2016 ) for analyses and plotting, respectively. Linear mixed-effects models were used to analyze physiological (HR, HRV) and subjective (stress, shame, guilt) outcomes across the experiment. For each outcome, we specified a model with a random intercept for participant and fixed effects of Group (PTSD, C-PTSD, control) and Time (four levels: baseline, Narrative 1, Narrative 2, recovery for HR/HRV; baseline, immediately post-narrative, + 30 min, + 60 min for subjective ratings). Because mixed models incorporate all available observations and remain valid under the missing-at-random assumption, they inherently accommodate the few missing data points without the need for additional imputation. Significant effects were evaluated via an ANOVA-style table of fixed factors (α = .05). Power-analysis details are provided in Supplement 2. 3. Results 3.1. Heart rate A significant group × time point interaction was observed ( F = 4.134, p &lt; .001). The trauma interview increased HR across all three conditions compared to baseline (see Figure 2 A). During the recovery period, HR returned to baseline in the PTSD and control groups but remained elevated in the C-PTSD group. This group difference at recovery was significant (PTSD vs. C-PTSD: p = .010, control vs. C-PTSD: p = .036). 3.2. Heart rate variability There was a significant main effect of time ( F = 3.571, p = .016), indicating that HRV varied across the four measurement points. However, neither the main effect of group ( F = 1.112, p = .339) nor the Time × Group interaction ( F = 1.412, p = .216) reached significance. Although the PTSD group appears to show a relatively sharper HRV increase after the trauma narrative ( Figure 2 B), that difference did not survive statistical testing. Approximately 16.7% of all HRV time points were missing (due to signal artefacts or participant non-response), which reduced power for detecting group-specific changes. Overall, the main effect of Time was driven primarily by increased HRV during the recovery period, rather than by differential group trajectories. 3.3. Perceived stress A significant main effect of group was found ( F = 7.543, p = .002), indicating that the C-PTSD group had higher overall levels of perceived stress than the PTSD or control groups (see Figure 3 A). The trauma narrative did not significantly alter perceived stress levels in any group. 3.4. Perceived guilt and shame There was a significant main effect of group ( F = 21.779, p &lt; .001), with the C-PTSD group reporting significantly higher levels of perceived guilt at baseline, during the trauma narrative, and during recovery (see Figure 3 B). A similar pattern emerged for perceived shame (see Figure 3 C). Engaging in the narrative increased perceived shame levels in all three groups (main effect of time: F = 2.692, p = .477), with overall higher levels in the C-PTSD group compared to the other two groups (main effect of group: F = 19.309, p &lt; .001). 4. Discussion Given the lack of studies comparing the psychophysiological characteristics of adolescents with C-PTSD and PTSD, this study aimed to deepen the understanding of ANS responses to trauma narratives and recovery in these groups, as well as in trauma-exposed youth without a (C-)PTSD diagnosis. While all groups showed heightened ANS reactivity during trauma recall, as evidenced by an increased HR, adolescents with C-PTSD were characterized by sustaining high HR rates during recovery time compared to both the PTSD and trauma-exposed control groups. Additionally, compared to the other two groups, adolescents with C-PTSD reported significantly higher levels of perceived stress, guilt, and shame across the baseline, narrative, and recovery phases. The observed increase in HR during the recovery phase among adolescents with C-PTSD may reflect greater difficulties in down-regulating arousal following trauma recall, consistent with the affective dysregulation criterion characteristic of C-PTSD (Cloitre et al., 2013 ; Maercker et al., 2013 ). Narratives of interpersonal traumatic events have been shown to trigger hyperactivation of brain systems commonly implicated in PTSD among adolescents, including the insula, hippocampus, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC), and thalamus (Malejko et al., 2020 ). Neural hyperresponsivity in these brain networks has been specifically linked to PTSD symptoms of flashbacks, intrusive re-experiencing, and hyperarousal, which frequently co-occur with autonomic markers of arousal, including increased HR (Malejko et al., 2020 ). Building on this, our findings may reflect an ANS correlate of these processes, with prolonged HR during the recovery phase suggesting persistent activation within dysregulated salience and fear-related networks, which are associated with heightened threat sensitivity beyond the immediate stressor, increased interoceptive awareness, and limited top-down regulatory control (Freed &amp; D’Andrea, 2015 ; Lanius et al., 2017 ; Naegeli et al., 2018 ). Together, these mechanisms could contribute to impaired cognitive regulation of salience, a failure to habituate to trauma cues, and sustained defensive responding, that, in turn interfere with autonomic recovery by disrupting arousal modulation and hindering the transition from hyperarousal to homeostasis, particularly in individuals with C-PTSD. The absence of baseline HR differences among the PTSD, C-PTSD, and control groups in this study aligns with previous research reporting no significant associations between baseline HR or other ANS indices and post-traumatic stress symptoms in trauma-exposed youth (Haag et al., 2019 ; Jones-Alexander et al., 2005 ; Kirsch et al., 2015 ; MacMillan et al., 2009 ; Saltzman et al., 2005 ). Regarding ANS reactivity, the absence of HR differences between the C-PTSD and PTSD groups during the trauma narrative aligns with most findings from the only comparable study on adolescents with C-PTSD identified to date (Iffland et al., 2023 ). Our findings also correspond to studies reporting no HR or SNS reactivity differences between youth with PTSD and controls during exposure to trauma-related stimuli (Jones-Alexander et al., 2005 ; Kirsch et al., 2015 ; Scheeringa et al., 2004 ), and no group × condition interactions for trauma- or threat-related versus neutral stimuli (Grasso &amp; Simons, 2012 ; Zantvoord et al., 2024 ). In contrast, studies showing group differences, such as increased HR reactivity in trauma-exposed adolescents (Chen &amp; Ghazali, 2022 ; Saltzman et al., 2005 ) or reduced HR reactivity in youth with child abuse histories (Iffland et al., 2020 ), typically include healthy control groups without trauma exposure, suggesting that these differences may reflect trauma exposure rather than (C-)PTSD diagnosis (Kirsch et al., 2015 ; Saltzman et al., 2005 ; Scheeringa et al., 2004 ). The absence of group differences during trauma narration may also partly be attributable to the control group’s trauma exposure and the presence of affective symptoms, which could contribute to subclinical post-traumatic symptoms and similar patterns of physiological arousal (Radell et al., 2020 ). Although our findings did not reveal significant differences in HR reactivity to trauma narratives between the groups, individuals with C-PTSD reported significantly higher levels of subjective stress compared to the other groups. This discordance aligns with findings that adult PTSD subgroups report situational stress without corresponding physiological responses (Pineles et al., 2013 ). However, instead of corresponding to physiological reactivity, the heightened perceived stress in adolescents with C-PTSD may have manifested as reduced physiological habituation, as observed in the recovery phase of the current study. Results suggest a more pervasive sense of threat in youth with C-PTSD, aligning with the nature of traumatic events most strongly associated with this diagnosis, specifically prolonged or repeated exposures to childhood trauma (Corrigan et al., 2011 ; Kemp et al., 2017 ; Sigrist et al., 2021 ). Contrary to our hypotheses, our analyses revealed no significant group differences in PNS reactivity or recovery, as reflected by HRV. However, this aligns with previous research showing no association between post-traumatic stress symptoms or history of child abuse and PNS reactivity (Cohen et al., 2020 ; Grasso &amp; Simons, 2012 ; Kirsch et al., 2015 ; Scheeringa et al., 2004 ; Shenk et al., 2012 , 2014 ), as well as with meta-analytic findings (Siciliano et al., 2022 ) and research indicating that PNS measures are more strongly linked to post-traumatic stress symptoms in adults (Campbell et al., 2019 ; Koenig, 2020 ; Sigrist et al., 2021 ). Still, the absence of HRV differences between the C-PTSD group and the other groups is surprising, given the disorder’s defining feature of self-regulation impairments and the theoretical (Porges, 2007 ) and empirical evidence highlighting the role of the PNS in these processes (Kim et al., 2018 ). Beyond the developmental explanation outlined above, accounting for less pronounced HRV alterations, additional factors warrant consideration. First, physiological dysregulation in youth with C-PTSD might manifest as fluctuating PNS responses, which could hinder the identification of HRV differences compared to the other groups. Idiographic trauma narratives, rather than standardized cues, may also have introduced variability, thereby masking group differences. Additionally, potential heterogeneity within the diagnostic groups (e.g. trauma severity, symptom profiles, duration of [C-]PTSD, time since trauma, or comorbidities) and homogeneity across groups (i.e. all having a history of interpersonal trauma) could have minimized detectable differences, given that trauma exposure itself may affect autonomic functioning (Kirsch et al., 2015 ). Smaller effects of group differences may not have been detectable due to the sample size. However, HRV may also not fully capture the complexity of ANS dysregulation in C-PTSD, and other physiological markers may be more sensitive to disruptions in self-regulation. Given the absence of group differences in HR reactivity, it is noteworthy that previous studies suggest that sympathetically dominated measures (e.g. PEP), may be more sensitive in distinguishing youth with PTSD from controls (Cohen et al., 2020 ; Schuurmans et al., 2021 ), whereas HR reflects the combined influence of both the SNS and PNS. While our findings provide preliminary evidence that HR recovery may serve as a significant biomarker distinguishing youth with C-PTSD from those with PTSD or no (C-)PTSD diagnosis, the absence of reactivity differences underscores the need to explore additional physiological markers. Our findings of elevated shame and guilt in adolescents with C-PTSD, relative to those with PTSD and the control group, align with the diagnostic criteria of negative self-concept in C-PTSD (Cloitre et al., 2013 ). Prolonged and exaggerated experiences of shame and guilt may heighten the emotional aversiveness of trauma memories, intensify intrusive experiences in both frequency and severity, and promote ruminative introspection on negative self-attributes (Fine et al., 2023 ; Øktedalen et al., 2015 ). Persistent self-critical cognitions may further sustain a sense of ongoing threat (Fine et al., 2023 ), a mechanism central to the maintenance of (C-)PTSD, as conceptualized in Ehlers and Clark’s ( 2000 ) cognitive model. Furthermore, it can be concluded that the mechanisms of shame and guilt contribute to the reduced physiological habituation to trauma recall observed in adolescents with C-PTSD in the present study. 4.1. Clinical and research implications The findings from the present study contribute to a deeper understanding of the psychophysiological distinctions between youth with C-PTSD and those with PTSD or no (C-)PTSD diagnosis, with significant clinical implications. First, the results provide preliminary evidence supporting the integration of objective, non-intrusive biological measures into psychological assessments of (C-)PTSD, thereby enhancing diagnostic specificity, informing individualized treatment planning, and providing objective markers for monitoring treatment progress. Such a multi-method assessment approach could combine a validated trauma interview with guided trauma narratives and continuous HR monitoring via wearable sensors, embedded in a structured procedure comprising standardized baseline, trauma recall, and recovery phases of fixed duration. The collection of normative data could help to establish threshold values or characteristic response patterns. The findings also underscore the importance of phase-based trauma-focused treatments for adolescents with C-PTSD, such as Dialectical Behaviour Therapy for PTSD (Steil et al., 2011 ) or trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (Cohen &amp; Mannarino, 2015 ), which specifically target impairments in emotional dysregulation and negative cognitions, including shame and guilt (Karatzias et al., 2019 ; Prillinger et al., 2024 ). Targeting emotional dysregulation and negative self-concepts either prior to or in conjunction with exposure therapy may enhance the ability of youth with C-PTSD to tolerate stress, reduce avoidance behaviours, engage more effectively in trauma processing, and ultimately improve treatment outcomes. Finally, the diminished ANS recovery observed in adolescents with C-PTSD emphasizes the need to closely monitor the long-term cardiovascular health of this population, given the potential risks associated with chronic ANS dysregulation (Siciliano et al., 2022 ). 4.2. Limitations State and trait factors influencing stress-regulatory systems, such as genetic predispositions, hormonal influences (e.g. menstrual status, contraceptive use), environmental conditions (e.g. caregiving environment), time of day, and health-related behaviours (e.g. physical activity, BMI, smoking, substance use, caffeine intake, and sleep) (Sigrist et al., 2021 ), were not accounted for in this study and therefore pose limitations. Similarly, trauma-related moderators, including time since last trauma exposure, age at first trauma, number and severity of traumatic events, symptom burden, trauma diversity, and (C-)PTSD duration, were uncontrolled, despite their potential effects on ANS functioning (Kirsch et al., 2015 ; Siciliano et al., 2022 ). As a result, the distinct HR recovery observed in the C-PTSD group may reflect PTSD severity or trauma exposure rather than C-PTSD-specific psychopathology. The potential range of psychiatric comorbidities and impairment severity was not assessed, despite participants receiving either inpatient or outpatient treatment, nor were dissociative symptoms controlled for, which could contribute to heterogeneous ANS profiles in (C-)PTSD. Furthermore, anticipatory anxiety related to the single-session format, along with distress elicited by trauma-related questionnaires, may have affected physiological baseline measurements, potentially attenuating observable physiological differences between baseline and the narrative phase. The predominance of female participants and the use of psychopharmacological treatment across the sample, further constrain the findings’ generalizability. Despite these limitations, this study is one of the first to present evidence of unique psychophysiological profiles in adolescents with C-PTSD, as compared to those with PTSD or a trauma history but no (C-)PTSD diagnosis. Strengths of the study include the comprehensive assessment of ANS functioning across resting, stress-inducing, and recovery phases, the use of trauma narratives as stress-inducing tasks resembling diagnostic situations, the distinction between individuals with PTSD and C-PTSD, balanced subgroup sizes, and the integration of objective ANS measures with subjective evaluations of situational stress, shame, and guilt. Future research should include control groups with trauma exposure but no psychiatric diagnosis. Expanding the physiological measures used in our study could enable a more comprehensive understanding of ANS functioning and its specific differences between C-PTSD and control groups. Additionally, a deeper investigation into the impact of shame and guilt on ANS (re)activity and habituation could provide valuable insights for both the understanding and treatment of C-PTSD in youth. Our study provides no information on whether prolonged HR recovery precedes or follows the onset of C-PTSD. Longitudinal studies examining ANS reactivity and recovery profiles, alongside various C-PTSD symptom clusters such as negative self-concept and emotional dysregulation, could deepen our understanding of the unique and combined contributions of these factors to the onset, persistence, and recovery of C-PTSD. 4.3. Conclusion The present study provides novel insights into the distinct ANS profiles and negative self-appraisals among trauma-exposed youth with C-PTSD, compared to those with PTSD or without a (C-)PTSD diagnosis, offering diagnostic implications and underscoring the importance of phase-based trauma-focused interventions for youth with C-PTSD. Building on this initial evidence, future studies with larger samples could explore ANS (re)activity and recovery as potential biomarkers of ANS dysfunction linked to C-PTSD in youth, and their associations with specific symptom clusters, which may inform treatment interventions.
