Abstract Background Excessive anxiety is a common and hazardous mental disorder, especially in medical education. Positive psychology has proposed an effective path to reduce anxiety among medical students through mindset change. This study tests whether a growth mindset curbs anxiety in Chinese medical students. Methods The online cross-sectional survey was conducted among medical students enrolled in medical schools in Northeast China, yielding 3,114 valid responses (valid response rate = 89.2%). Data were statistically analyzed using STATA 17.0. The questionnaire covered sociodemographic statistics, the Growth Mindset Scale, and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale. Propensity score matching was utilized to investigate the causal relationship between a growth mindset and anxiety, thereby controlling for potential confounding variables. Results The detection rate of anxiety symptoms was 41.1%, and anxiety status significantly differed by scholarship, grades, parenting style, and academic stress. Propensity score matching showed a mean treatment effect of -0.096 in the one-to-one matching sample, and − 0.112 and − 0.126 for K-nearest-neighbor matching and kernel matching, respectively. All were significant at the 1% level. Conclusion Medical students have a high prevalence of anxiety symptoms influenced by scholarship, grades, parenting style, and academic pressure. Furthermore, their growth mindset may help alleviate their anxiety, and it continues to develop as educators emphasize their health status. Fostering a growth mindset among Chinese medical students may promote their mental health, alleviate anxiety, and improve overall well-being. Parents should support their children to overcome anxiety and encourage them to try new things, and Chinese medical students are encouraged to continually adjust their mindset to cope with challenges positively. Educators should foster the cultivation of growth mindset in medical students through multidimensional interventions involving cognitive scaffolding, behavioral reinforcement, and environmental structuring, which consequently enhances their psychological well-being. Background Anxiety disorders increase the risk of depression and substance use disorders as well as of suicidal thoughts and behaviors [ 1 , 2 ]. The World Health Organization’s (WHO) “Comprehensive mental health action plan 2013–2030” highlights the necessity to provide appropriate interventions for people with mental health conditions, including anxiety disorders [ 3 ]. Anxiety, a pervasive condition affecting individuals across various age groups, significantly influences one’s academic, professional, and daily life. In severe cases, it can threaten an individual’s health and livelihood. Data suggest that anxiety disorders are the second most important mental health factor contributing to the global increase in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [ 4 ]. Anxiety is a common mental disorder that refers to an unpleasant and complex emotional state of nervousness, uneasiness, worry, and annoyance that an individual experiences in response to an impending, potentially dangerous, or threatening situation [ 5 ]. It includes excessive rumination, worry, restlessness, apprehension, and fear of future uncertainty based on real or imagined events. Anxiety jeopardizes human health, and its causes are complex and accompanied by serious complications that affect physical and psychological health [ 6 ]. A 2019 survey showed that the global prevalence of anxiety disorders among medical students is as high as 33.8%, which is higher than that of the general population [ 7 ]. Synchronously, one study revealed that the average prevalence of anxiety among medical students in China was 27.22% [ 8 ]. Similar results were found in Western studies, Dyrbye et al. found that the symptoms of anxiety among medical students in the United States were significantly higher than those of the general university student population, with approximately 25% of medical students experiencing symptoms of depression and anxiety [ 9 ]. More seriously, a study conducted by Taneja et al. in September 2017 at the Government Medical College in New Delhi found that 40.1% of the students experienced symptoms of anxiety [ 10 ]. All findings mentioned in the above highlight that anxiety has emerged as a prevalent psychological concern among medical students globally. At the same time, researchers and institutions are actively exploring solutions to address anxiety. Medication and cognitive-behavioral therapy are currently globally recognized as more effective treatments for anxiety [ 11 ]. While pharmacological treatments may provide effective symptom relief through modulation of neurotransmitter activity, they typically fail to address the underlying psychological and behavioral mechanisms that sustain anxiety disorders [ 12 ]. Cognitive-behavioral therapy is an active, problem-focused, and time-efficient treatment approach, which goals is to reduce emotional distress and foster adaptive behaviors in individuals coping with a variety of mental health and adjustment challenges [ 13 ]. This therapy effectively curbs dysfunctional emotions and behaviors by modifying the individual’s maladaptive thinking and behavior patterns, thereby enhancing patient mental health [ 14 ]. In recent years, positive psychology as a form of cognitive behavioral therapy has introduced innovative strategies to alleviate anxiety, such as cultivating growth mindset to alleviate anxiety, depression, and other mental health issues [ 15 , 16 ]. Adolescents’ mindset was found to affect their anxiety status, and adolescents with a growth mindset have relatively low symptoms of anxiety [ 17 ]; a growth mindset has a sustained positive impact on adolescents’ mental health [ 18 ]. Therefore, it is essential to investigate whether growth mindset can curb anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students in the context of Oriental culture. Dweck introduced the concept of implicit theories, categorizing human thinking patterns into two types: growth mindset and fixed mindset [ 19 ]. Someone with a fixed mindset tends to doubt themselves after the negative feedback of frustration, label themselves, and easily give up the current task goal; conversely, someone with a growth mindset tends to believe that their own ability can be changed through efforts, is able to objectively assess their own successes and failures, and tends to seek improved solutions when faced with challenges. The growth mindset is a positive, optimistic, and exploratory cognitive model that emphasizes an individual’s ability to enhance and expand their skills through learning, practice, and effort, and it stresses the importance of hard work and perseverance as well as growth in the process of accepting challenges. Students with a growth mindset value the brain’s plasticity and believe that human abilities and skills are capable of be developed through hard work; thus, they are more resilient in the face of adversity. This theory demonstrates significant relevance in educational contexts, particularly in medical education, where cultivating psychological resilience and academic competence is essential for addressing complex learning challenges and clinical demands. Medical students with a growth mindset believe their abilities can be enhanced through effort and learning, enabling them to adopt adaptive strategies and maintain sustained progress when facing academic and professional difficulties. Although growth mindset has been shown to alleviate anxiety in the context of Western culture [ 20 ], it is necessary to demonstrate it in the context of Chinese culture for differences in Chinese and Western cultural contexts, for example, in terms of cultural values, educational systems, and social support systems. Therefore this study, guided by implicit theory, aims to explore whether the curbing effect of growth mindset on adolescents’ anxiety remains valid in the Chinese cultural context, thereby providing more culturally adaptive theoretical support for mental health education and intervention within the framework of implicit beliefs. Much of the research on the growth mindset has focused on its relationship with students’ academic achievement [ 21 ] and has found that it significantly improves student performance and academic achievement [ 22 , 23 ]. EEG experiments found that individuals holding a growth mindset who made errors in a learning task proactively reflected on and analyzed the error, improved in time, and proactively avoided the error and increased their rate of correctness in the next task [ 24 ]. Moreover, students with a growth mindset demonstrate greater mental toughness in the face of challenging dilemmas [ 25 ]. Studies have shown a moderate negative correlation between growth mindset and anxiety [ 26 ], and larger survey has found that students with a growth mindset generally show more positive emotional states, and this phenomenon is particularly prominent in East Asian countries, including China and Japan [ 27 ]. This mindset helps medical students to better cope with academic stress and challenges, thereby reducing anxiety. A study on the relationship between growth mindset and anxiety status found that a high level of fixed mindset al.so had relatively high symptoms of anxiety, while a growth mindset can help alleviate anxiety [ 28 ]. Furthermore, the growth mindset is an important factor in medical students’ ability to cope with future healthcare career challenges, such as job adaptability and medical errors [ 29 – 31 ]; to some extent, it suppresses anxiety. Current study posits a hypothesis that growth mindset can curb anxiety in Chinese medical students. In China, fewer studies have explored the relationship between growth mindset and anxiety among medical students. Most studies on anxiety have used ordinary least squares (OLS) regression [ 32 ] and, less often, propensity score matching (PSM), which addresses the problem of selective bias and improves the credibility of causal inferences [ 33 ]. In this study, PSM was used to investigate the inhibitory effect of growth mindset on anxiety. The exploratory study of the relationship between growth mindset and anxiety among medical students was affected by demographic characteristics and other confounding factors that were not effectively controlled, which adversely affected the accuracy of the findings. PSM contributes to reducing bias by matching treated and control groups based on observed confounding factors, thereby strengthening the associations between variables and making them more robust [ 34 ]. Current study uses PSM to assess the curbing effect of growth mindset on anxiety in Chinese medical students. Previous studies have demonstrated that anxiety among medical students arises from a range of factors, including gender [ 35 – 37 ], grade [ 38 ], academic performance [ 39 ], only-child status [ 40 ], academic pressure [ 41 ], and parenting styles [ 42 ]. Therefore, it is necessary to include them in the latent variable to assess the curbing effect of growth mindset on medical students’ anxiety under Chinese educational culture. This study helps medical students better understand the meaning of growth mindset and how to apply it to face challenges in their studies and life, which may enhance their psychological quality. It helps medical students to continue learning and growing in their careers, thus promoting their personal development; further, it helps to develop a more professional and empathetic healthcare workforce, thus improving the quality of healthcare services for the whole society. Methods Participants A cross-sectional survey was carried out in Northeast China between November and December 2022. Utilizing the “Questionnaire Star” platform and employing both cluster and stratified sampling methods, undergraduate students from three medical colleges were surveyed. Cluster sampling is a method that involves dividing a population into several groups (such as geographic areas, time periods, or organizational units) and then randomly selecting a number of clusters from these groups as samples [ 43 ]. The subjects of this study consist of a large and geographically dispersed group of medical students. Based on the medical colleges, medical students were classified into different groups, and medical students from three medical colleges were randomly selected as research subjects. This method helps to effectively reduce the dispersion of research subjects and provide a comprehensive understanding of the overall situation of medical students. Stratified sampling is a research method that divides the population into a number of non-overlapping layers (or groups, categories), and then samples from each layer independently [ 44 ]. This method adequately accounts for the structural characteristics of the population, thereby enhancing the sample’s representativeness and precision. In this study, in addition to factors such as the reputation of the medical school, academic discipline, and grade level, the gender ratio of medical students in China was also considered. Participants were selected using stratified sampling from the medical student populations in Harbin, Qiqihar, and Mudanjiang. To protect the privacy of participants, the research process was carried out with rigorous adherence to privacy protection protocols, thereby ensuring the confidentiality and anonymity of all data and personal identifiers. The study adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board of Harbin Medical University (ECHMU). For this survey, a total of 3,114 questionnaires were collected. Following a rigorous data cleaning process, questionnaires with excessively short response times, incomplete responses, and incorrect answers to quality control questions were excluded. This resulted in a final sample of 2,778 valid questionnaires, yielding a valid response rate of 89.2%. Additionally, the researchers engaged with the educational administrators of the institutions under study, secured their consent and collaboration, and provided the survey instructions. Following this, departmental counselors coordinated the voluntary completion of questionnaires by their students. Measures Eight items of demographic information were collected using a self-designed questionnaire, including gender, grade, length of study, The one-child, ranking of academic performance, parenting style, serve as a class cadre, and academic pressure. Ranking of academic performance and academic pressure of students were collected as 1–5 continuous variables. The Ranking of academic performance was categorized as “Top Academic Achievement”, “Above Average Academic Performance”, “Average Academic Performance”, “Below Average Academic Performance” and “Low Academic Rankings”. The academic pressure was categorized as “Tiny”, “Little”, “General”, “Large”, “Major”. Categorize gender as male or female, and classify grade levels as “Freshman”, “Sophomore”, “Junior”, “Senior”, and “Senior 5 and above”. The experience of student leaders is categorized into “student leaders” and “ordinary students”. The length of study is divided into “four-year system”, “ five-year system”, “ ‘5 + 2’ year program”, “ ‘5 + 3’ year program”. Parenting styles have been divided into four categories: “neglecting (less strict requirements and less companionship)”, “tolerant (less strict requirements and more companionship)”, “autocratic (strict requirements and less companionship)”, “authoritative (strict requirements and more companionship)”. The questionnaire began with an instructional section, providing a brief overview of the purpose of our study and emphasizing precautions to be observed during completion. An initial test of the questionnaire was conducted to ensure that the respondents were able to understand it more easily, and the time taken to complete the questionnaire was recorded. Generalized anxiety disorder 7 (GAD-7) Anxiety symptoms were measured by the Generalized Anxiety Scale 7 [ 45 ], which has been adapted cross-culturally and validated in other studies in China [ 46 ]. The scale assesses the severity of students’ anxiety symptoms over a two-week period. Each item is rated from 0 = not at all to 3 = almost every day , with higher scores indicating higher symptoms of anxiety. The scale comprises the following items: (1) Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge. (2) Not being able to stop or control worrying. (3) Worrying too much about different things. (4) Having trouble relaxing. (5) Being so restless that it is hard to sit still. (6) Becoming easily annoyed or irritable. (7) Feeling afraid as if something awful might happen [ 47 ]. Based on the total scores, this study categorizes anxiety symptoms into four levels: minimal anxiety (scores ranging from 0 to 4), mild anxiety (scores ranging from 5 to 9), moderate anxiety (scores ranging from 10 to 14), and severe anxiety (scores ranging from 15 to 21) [ 48 ]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.937. Growth mindset scale (GMS) Growth mindset in medical students was assessed using the 8-item Growth Mindset Scale developed by Dweck [ 19 ]. Scholars in China validated the cross-cultural adaptability of the scale [ 49 ]. Each item is rated from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree , for “growth mindset” items (3, 4, 6, 8) and “fixed mindset” items (1, 2, 5, 7). As an example, “no matter what type of person I am, a drastic change can always be made by me.” Fixed-mindset items within the scale were reverse scored, and the higher the score, the greater the growth mindset. This investigation utilized a cutoff score of “3” on the Growth Mindset Scale to demarcate the median, mode, and mean. Participants who achieved a score of 3 or above were identified as possessing a growth mindset, while those with scores below 3 were categorized as exhibiting a fixed mindset. The sample population comprised 1,397 individuals (50.2%) exhibiting a growth mindset and 1,381 individuals (49.7%) demonstrating a fixed mindset. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of this scale was 0.723 in this study. Date analysis All statistical analyses were conducted utilizing STATA 17.0. A two-tailed probability value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. T-tests were employed to evaluate differences between categorical variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized to examine differences among continuous variables. By simulating randomization of a randomized controlled trial [ 34 ], PSM classifies study participants into experimental and control groups based on the presence or absence of a growth mindset, drawing on recent research on the relationship between growth mindset and anxiety, where PSM was employed to balance covariates, minimize potential confounders, and simulate randomization. Utilizing PSM rather than regression provides greater flexibility, especially while analyzing the effect of growth mindset on anxiety, which is common (the outcome) but less well researched (the risk factor) [ 50 – 52 ]. The propensity score was used to pair each growth-minded medical student (designated as the “treatment group”) with one or more medical students (designated as the “control group”) without a growth mindset; both had nearly identical distributions on all observed covariates. Thus, covariates that may have confounded the relationship between growth mindset and anxiety in medical students would no longer be influential. The PSM procedure is as follows: first, select the covariates; second, estimate the propensity score, which, in this study, was obtained through a Logit model; third, perform PSM; and fourth, compute the average treatment effect (ATT) based on the matching results. The expression for ATT calculation is: and denote the outcome variables; denotes the medical student’s anxiety symptom score in case i in the presence of a growth mindset, and denotes the medical student’s anxiety symptom score in absence of a growth mindset; takes the value of 1 and vice versa. denotes the propensity score value, which is the conditional probability that medical student i has a growth mindset, controlling for individual characteristics and family background. For the result of PSM to be more robust and reliable, in addition to the one-to-one matching method for model estimation, the estimation was also conducted at the same time through the nearest-neighbor matching and kernel matching methods, and the model’s estimation quality was tested for balance of matching. Results Characteristics of respondents Among the 2,778 participating medical students, 1,020 (36.7%) were male and 1,758 (63.3%) were female. Participants had the highest percentage of five-year students (75.1%). The predominant parenting style exhibited by parents towards their children was tolerant (66.6%). Table 2 shows the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics. Differences of participants’ characteristics for growth mindset and anxiety T-tests and ANOVA were employed to compare the differences in individual characteristics and learning characteristics in terms of thinking styles among medical students. Table 2 shows a significant difference in the way of thinking of medical students in terms of different grades ( p &lt; 0.001), length of study ( p &lt; 0.001), ranking of academic performance ( p &lt; 0.001), and academic pressure ( p &lt; 0.001), as well as whether or not they were only children ( p &lt; 0.001) or served as class cadre ( p &lt; 0.001). Freshmen displayed higher growth mindset scores relative to both juniors and seniors. Medical students in the “5 + 3” year program yielded higher growth mindset scores than those in the five-year program. Students who achieved top academic standing demonstrated superior scores in medical generative thinking compared to their fellow students. Additionally, medical students under low academic pressure achieved higher growth mindset scores than those in general, large, and major medical programs. After analyzing medical students’ anxiety status using the same methodology, in the statistical results, we found significant differences in terms of scholarships ( p &lt; 0.001), different grades ( p &lt; 0.001), parenting styles ( p &lt; 0.001), and academic pressure ( p &lt; 0.001). Junior medical students demonstrated greater symptoms of anxiety than both freshmen and sophomores. Students in the “5 + 3” year program reported significantly higher anxiety symptoms than those in the “5 + 2” year program. Medical students experiencing major academic pressure exhibited higher symptoms of anxiety compared to their peers. Furthermore, medical students from families characterized by a tolerant parenting style exhibited lower anxiety scores compared to peers from families with neglectful or autocratic parenting styles. Thereafter, an estimation of the propensity score via a Logit model was conducted at a significance level of 10%. In this estimation model, the dependent variable was a dummy variable for whether individual medical students had a growth mindset or not, and the covariates were the factors affecting the students’ growth mindset. The results of this Logit model regression, as well as the marginal effect of each covariate on the growth mindset, are shown in Table 3 . Grade, academic pressure were the main factors influencing medical students’ growth mindsets. The data show that the OLS estimation results are meaningful, with an R² of 9.3%; the coefficient for the correlation between growth mindset and medical students’ anxiety is -0.117 and is significant at the 1% level, indicating a strong correlation between growth mindset and medical students’ anxiety. The result is consistent with the reality of perception, and the medical students’ growth mindset promotes their active thinking and healthy growth and suppresses their anxiety symptom. However, since the growth mindset is an individual’s internal idea, simple OLS behavior does not correct selective bias, and the results of model fitting are not accurate; thus, the results of OLS regression analysis are only for comparative reference. The regression results of the Logit model passed the Roc test with an R2 of 0.0176, which indicates that the model fit is better, and the covariates have a relatively strong explanatory power for whether an individual has a growth mindset or not. The regression coefficients of the Logit model show that scholarship, grade, and academic stress have a significant effect on growth mindset among medical students. Particularly, the pseudo-R-squared value (0.022) indicated that the model fits well. Moreover, the results revealed an association between confounding variables and growth mindset. For example, student leaders (coefficient=-0.253, p = 0.001) and those with higher academic performance rankings (coefficient=-0.141, p &lt; 0.001) were more likely to possess a growth mindset. Discussion The results indicate that the anxiety among Chinese medical students is also influenced by factors such as scholarship, grade level, family upbringing style, and academic stress, and different individual characteristics and environmental factors affect their anxiety state to different degrees. This study explored the impact of medical university students’ growth mindset on anxiety, finding that the growth mindset had an arresting effect on anxiety; this is consistent with previous findings [ 17 ]. Higher detection rate of anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students The results showed that the detection rate of anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students was 41.1%—slightly higher than the 33.8% global prevalence of anxiety among medical students (95% confidence interval = 29.2–38.7%) [ 7 ].The anxiety symptom among the student population is higher than that of the general population, with as many as 20% of students reaching an extremely high symptom of anxiety. The pressure of looking for a job, difficulty of rationally arranging time, large gap between expectation and reality, and pressure of schooling are all important factors affecting students’ anxiety symptom [ 53 ]. Medical students, tasked with saving lives, face a more demanding education that involves exposure to a wide range of diseases. As a result, the prevalence of anxiety among medical students may exceed that of the general student population [ 7 ]. Chinese medical students are more likely to have a high professional calling and are therefore highly susceptible to anxiety when failing to achieve their personal goals [ 54 ]; the increasing academic burden due to the various medical coursesand the reduction in sleep time and exposure to deceased patients are also recognized as possible causes of anxiety in medical students [ 55 , 56 ]. This study identified significant differences in the symptoms of anxiety among medical students in relation to scholarship status, grade level, parenting style, and academic pressure. Chinese medical students with scholarships, higher grade levels, higher academic pressure, and autocratic parenting styles have higher symptoms of anxiety Medical students who were awarded scholarships exhibited higher symptoms of anxiety compared to their counterparts who did not receive scholarships, aligning with the findings of prior research [ 57 ]. Due to the special nature of its occupation, the medical profession usually requires students’ performance to be stricter [ 58 ], and those who get scholarships under such strict requirements may have higher requirements for themselves and higher psychological pressure, pay more attention to academic performance and other concerns, and be more prone to anxiety [ 7 ]. If not regulated in time, this may have a greater impact on their mental health status, which is not conducive to the long-term development of their future careers and personal and healthy growth. The results showed differences in the severity of anxiety symptoms among medical students of different grades, with students in middle and upper grades having higher symptoms of anxiety. The reason may be that medical students in higher grades endure heavier study tasks and increasing academic burdens; moreover, most medical students in higher grades are at the internship stage, and internship and study tasks sometimes conflict, leading to the emergence of anxiety. This is consistent with previous findings by which higher-grade medical students had more severe anxiety symptoms compared to lower-grade medical students [ 59 ]. This is inconsistent with other findings pointing to the different teaching methods in China and abroad [ 60 ]. Further, one study showed that the symptom of anxiety symptoms increased by grade level among Chinese college students [ 61 ]. Academic pressure may also have a negative impact on health and lead to anxiety symptoms [ 62 ]. A longitudinal study also showed that medical students experienced increasing academic stress and anxiety as they progressed through the grades, which may be due to the increasingly complex course content and the increasing demands on medical students’ professional competence [ 63 ]. Parenting style significantly influences anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students, with those exposed to autocratic or neglectful parenting styles demonstrating a higher prevalence of anxiety symptoms compared to those from tolerant parenting backgrounds. Autocratic parenting, characterized by excessive control, may intensify academic and psychological pressure among medical students, potentially contributing to heightened anxiety. In contrast, neglectful parenting, which fails to provide adequate emotional support and attention, may foster feelings of insecurity and loneliness, further exacerbating the risk of anxiety. These findings are consistent with prior research indicating that varying parenting styles significantly influence the mental health of adolescents [ 64 ]. For instance, Morales et al. [ 65 ]reported that individuals raised under tolerant parenting exhibited lower anxiety symptoms, a result that aligns with the findings of the present study. Similarly, Gallagher et al. [ 66 ] observed that parenting styles marked by severe, punitive, or inconsistent disciplinary measures were associated with elevated anxiety symptoms in children, further supporting the association between parenting styles and anxiety symptoms. The implications of these findings suggest that parents should adopt balanced and supportive parenting strategies to promote their children’s psychological well-being and academic growth. Rather than relying on excessive control or neglect, parents should provide guidance while allowing space for self-development, thereby fostering resilience and reducing the risk of anxiety. Negative correlation between growth mindset and anxiety in Chinese medical students Figure 1 demonstrates a significant shift in the distribution of propensity scores between the treatment and control groups before and after PSM. Prior to matching, the propensity score distributions were more dispersed and dissimilar between the two groups; however, after matching, the distributions became more concentrated and closely aligned. This matching process effectively minimized the differences in propensity score distributions, enhancing the comparability of the treatment and control groups and strengthening the reliability of the findings regarding the effect of growth mindset on anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , the standardized percentage bias for the four key covariates—parenting style, scholarship status, academic performance, and academic stress level—was significantly below the 10% threshold[ 34 ], demonstrating that the PSM approach employed in this study effectively balanced the distribution of covariates between the treatment and control groups. This balance not only enhances the precision of the study design but also substantially mitigates confounding effects arising from selection bias, thereby strengthening the reliability of causal inferences[ 67 ]. As shown in Figs. 1 and 2 , before and after propensity score matching, the distribution of propensity scores and the balance of covariates in the two groups have undergone significant changes. After matching, the differences in propensity scores and covariates between the two groups have significantly decreased, which provides strong support for the reliability of the research conclusion. In this study, a counterfactual framework was employed to simulate changes in anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students with and without a growth mindset, aiming to explore the relationship between growth mindset and anxiety. The analysis revealed that, using PSM to estimate the average treatment effect, a growth mindset was associated with a reduction in anxiety symptoms ranging from − 12.6% to -9.6% across three matching methods (Table 6 ). This suggests a significant decrease in anxiety among Chinese medical students with a growth mindset compared to those without it. Furthermore, a statistically significant difference in anxiety symptoms was observed between students possessing a growth mindset and those lacking it, after controlling for covariates included in the study. These findings indicate a significant association between growth mindset and anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students. Table 6 indicates that across various matching approaches, the average treatment effect of a growth mindset on anxiety symptoms remains negative and statistically significant at the 1% level. This finding implies that a growth mindset is capable of substantially decreasing anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students, thereby reinforcing its role as an inhibitor of anxiety. The growth mindset has proved to be a negative influence factor of anxiety [ 68 – 71 ]. Medical students with a fixed mindset show negative feelings and emotions when they face difficulties and setbacks, whereas medical students with a growth mindset more often keep a positive mindset to face these challenges and constantly adjust themselves to cope with difficulties and setbacks, with a lower symptom of anxiety and depression. A fixed mindset restricts medical students’ innovation and flexibility, making it difficult for them to get rid of traditional, fixed ways of thinking and inhibiting the generation of new ideas and solutions. It hinders their comprehensive understanding of problems, causing them to easily overlook important details and struggle to effectively solve problems. It also limits their growth potential, making it difficult for them to adapt to the rapidly changing environment and leading to missed opportunities for development. A growth mindset helps individuals better adapt to the environment, and those with a growth mindset are better able to adjust their mindset to rise to the occasion and overcome setbacks. In the context of Chinese culture, Chinese medical students with a growth mindset are more likely to accept challenges and failures, learn from them, and continue to learn and grow. This positive attitude and approach helps them to better manage study stress and reduce anxiety. They believe that their intelligence, talent, personality, and ability are developed through effort, practice, and educational development, and they establish a positive mindset to alleviate academic pressure, reduce academic burden, and solve academic dilemmas. Therefore, compared to fixed-mindset medical students, they have a lower anxiety condition and better adjust their mental state and reduce their self-burden. Growth-mindset medical students show lower anxiety and higher academic resilience to achieve when facing challenging academic problems. Therefore, the growth mindset, serves as a protective factor in the context of Chinese culture, enabling Chinese medical students to adapt and cope with the highly competitive and high-pressure environment in medical education. The growth mindset is negatively correlated with psychological distress, and it facilitates the satisfaction of learners’ psychological needs in their learning process [ 26 ]. Implications Theoretical implications Situated within China’s distinctive sociocultural context, this study employs a positive psychology framework to investigate innovative pathways for anxiety reduction among medical students, contributing to the advancement of evidence-based interventions rooted in growth mindset theory.Academically, it addresses critical gaps in understanding causality relationship between mindset development and mental health outcomes in medical education, while also offering a solid theoretical foundation for positive psychological interventions. This study provides both conceptual and empirical insights essential for developing tailored mental health support systems in Chinese medical education. The findings offer significant implications for addressing college mental health challenges, particularly within the context of medical professional training in non-Western settings. Practical implications This study underscores the significant impact of a growth mindset on reducing anxiety symptoms among Chinese medical students, offering actionable insights for educational practice and policy development. The findings highlight the following practical implications: The demonstrated link between growth mindset and reduced anxiety provides a strong rationale for integrating mindset-focused interventions into Chinese medical curricula. By fostering a growth mindset, educational institutions equip students with the psychological tools needed to manage stress and thrive in high-pressure academic and clinical environments. Secondly, the study’s results emphasize the need for policymakers to prioritize the mental health of medical students within medical education frameworks. This includes allocating resources for psychological support services, promoting mental health literacy among Chinese medical students, and creating environments that foster adaptive coping strategies and resilience-building for Chinese medical students. Finally, this study emphasizes the role of a growth mindset in reducing anxiety among Chinese medical students in the context of Chinese culture. A growth mindset enables them to confront challenges with greater proactivity, further to achieving happiness career development goals. Limitations and future research This study utilizes a cross-sectional design combined with PSM to mitigate selection bias and control for known confounding factors. Although PSM effectively balances observed confounders, it has inherent limitations, particularly its inability to address endogeneity issues arising from unobserved variables that may still influence outcomes. Future research could explore advanced matching techniques, such as interval matching or blocking, to better account for latent biases and further strengthen the robustness of the findings. A further limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which prevents the establishment of temporal relationships between variables. Although the findings offer valuable insights into the association between growth mindset and anxiety, causal inferences cannot be definitively drawn. To address this limitation, future studies should consider employing longitudinal designs or randomized controlled trials (RCTs). These approaches would overcome the constraints of cross-sectional data, provide stronger evidence for causal relationships, and clarify the directionality of the relationship between growth mindset and anxiety. Finally, the study sample was limited to medical students from three medical schools in Northeast China, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other regions or countries. Future research should expand the geographical scope and include participants from diverse regions to improve the representativeness and applicability of the results. Additionally, further studies could investigate the prevalence and distribution of anxiety across populations with varying ages, genders, occupations, and socio-economic backgrounds. Such research would provide a more comprehensive understanding of anxiety’s diverse manifestations and inform the development of targeted interventions and resource allocation strategies, ultimately contributing to improved public mental health outcomes. Conclusion This study found a high prevalence of anxiety symptoms in Chinese medical students. Significant differences in anxiety among medical students were observed with respect to scholarship status, grade level, academic pressure, and family parenting style. In the context of Chinese culture, a growth mindset helps Chinese medical students better cope with learning stress and challenges through positive attitudes and approaches, thus reducing anxiety. Our results indicate that Chinese medical students’ current anxiety symptom is high, and more attention should be paid to Chinese medical students’ mental health status. Considering these psychological characteristics, sense, protective measures for their mental health should be tailored according to the Chinese medical students’ psychological characteristics. Medical school administrators may consider instructing Chinese medical students on proper learning concepts and mindsets through classroom instruction, lectures, and seminars. Counseling centers may also be established to provide counseling services to help Chinese medical students cope with anxiety. Parents work toward creating a good family atmosphere where Chinese medical students feel loved and supported, developing self-discipline and autonomy, encouraging active exploration and practice, and improving their coping skills. In the context of Chinese culture, Chinese medical students focus on their personal growth and progress, addressing difficulties and challenges with a positive mindset and constantly improving themselves.
