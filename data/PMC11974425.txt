Abstract Aims This study aimed to explore the brain activity characteristics of individuals with Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) during mobile gameplay, focusing on neural responses to positive and negative game events. The findings may enhance our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying IGD. Methods Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was employed to measure hemodynamic responses (HbO/HbR) in the prefrontal cortex of both IGD participants and recreational gaming users (RGU), during solo and multiplayer mobile gameplay. Results In solo mode, IGD participants exhibited stronger activation in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dLPFC), frontopolar area (FPA), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) in response to positive events compared to RGU. Negative events led to reduced activation in the FPA among IGD participants. In multiplayer mode, IGD participants displayed lower activation in the dLPFC and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vLPFC), although overall brain response trends to positive and negative events were similar between IGD and RGU. Conclusions This study suggests that individuals with IGD exhibit heightened sensitivity to rewards and diminished sensitivity to losses, along with potential impairments in the executive control network. These results contribute to a better understanding of the neural mechanisms of IGD and offer insights for developing targeted interventions aimed at addressing abnormal reward and loss processing. Introduction Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) is defined as a persistent or recurrent pattern of internet gaming behavior lasting at least 12 months, resulting in clinically significant impairment or distress ( American Psychiatric Association, 2013 ). In 2019, the World Health Organization (WHO) has also included Gaming Disorder as a disorder due to addictive behaviors in the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases ( World Health Organization, 2019 ). Therefore, researchers have focused on understanding the behavioral patterns and neural characteristics of IGD to improve early diagnosis and treatment. The Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model ( Brand, Young, Laier, Wölfling, &amp; Potenza, 2016 ) suggests that internet-use disorders emerge from the interaction of predisposing factors, affective and cognitive responses to situational triggers, and executive functioning. Building on this, Brand (2022) emphasized that the imbalance between positive (reward-based) and negative (punishment-based) reinforcement, coupled with impaired self-control—including inhibitory control and other executive functions—plays a pivotal role in the development and maintenance of addiction. Neurobiologically, the mesolimbic system is central to this process ( Berridge &amp; Robinson, 2016 ; Ma et al., 2024 ). The dorsal and ventral striatum are heavily implicated in reward processing and habit formation, while the prefrontal cortex (PFC) is responsible for regulating impulsivity, emotional responses, and decision-making ( Brand et al., 2019 ; Koob &amp; Volkow, 2016 ; Volkow, Michaelides, &amp; Baler, 2019 ). The interplay between these brain regions creates the neural foundation for IGD, where heightened reward sensitivity reinforces gaming behaviors and diminished loss sensitivity perpetuates them, even in the face of negative consequences ( Dong &amp; Potenza, 2014 ). Supporting neuroimaging studies have found that individuals with IGD show greater activation in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dLPFC) and posterior cingulate cortex in response to gaming-related cues compared to recreational gaming users (RGU) ( Zhang et al., 2021 ). This heightened activation aligns with their high expectations or difficulties in craving regulation ( Dong, Li, Wang, &amp; Potenza, 2017 ; Wu et al., 2020 ). Furthermore, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) targeting the right dLPFC has been shown to improve craving regulation in men with IGD ( Wu et al., 2020 ). Conversely, reduced sensitivity to losses may sustain IGD, with diminished activation in the superior frontal gyrus observed in response to losses ( Dong et al., 2017 ), as well as decreased activation in the posterior cingulate cortex in problematic internet users ( Dong, Hu, &amp; Lin, 2013 ; He et al., 2017 ). These patterns suggest impaired loss processing, which may contribute to the persistence of gaming despite negative consequences ( Paulus, Ohmann, Von Gontard, &amp; Popow, 2018 ; Tian et al., 2018 ). Notably, discussing how individuals with IGD process reward and loss during actual gameplay may provide new insights into its neural mechanisms. Cho, Nah, Park, and Han (2022) were the first to examine brain responses in IGD during specific game events, finding that IGD individuals showed significantly stronger left dLPFC responses during positive game events, but reduced orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) responses after negative game events compared to healthy controls. Zhang et al. (2023) found that IGD participants had lower left dLPFC activity after positive events and significantly lower left middle frontal gyrus activity after negative events compared to RGU. Additionally, studies have shown increased dLPFC and frontopolar area (FPA) activation during game events ( Li, Zhang, Long, Gong, &amp; Lei, 2018 ; Long, Zhang, Wang, &amp; Lei, 2024 ). However, brain responses in IGD during gameplay are inconsistent, likely due to varying definitions of game events and types of games studied ( Cho et al., 2022 ; Li et al., 2018 ; Long et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ). The focus and aims of current study Previous studies have indicated that, compared to healthy controls or RGU, individuals with IGD exhibited abnormal sensitivity to reward and loss ( He et al., 2017 ; Wang et al., 2021 ; Zhang et al., 2021 ; Zhou, Wang, Zheng, Wang, &amp; Dong, 2021 ), including during gameplay ( Cho et al., 2022 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ). However, there is limited research on how IGD individuals process reward and loss in mobile gameplay, despite the significant market share and multi-billion revenue of mobile games (SensorTower, 2024) . Furthermore, mobile games are unrestricted by time and space, allowing players to instantly engage regardless of their location or time ( Tanuar et al., 2018 ), which may increase the risk of addiction and deeper immersion into the gaming world ( Hew et al., 2024 ). Considering the potential for motion artifacts during actual gameplay and the advantages of Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) in uncovering real-time neural mechanisms of addiction, as demonstrated by Carollo, Cataldo, Fong, Corazza, and Esposito (2022) and Dempsey et al. (2015) , this study utilized fNIRS in the experimental process. fNIRS is a relatively new neuroimaging technology that non-invasively measures brain activity by detecting changes in cerebral blood oxygenation, specifically oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR). Compared to functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), fNIRS provides higher temporal resolution and greater tolerance to motion artifacts ( Pinti et al., 2020 ). However, the main limitation of fNIRS lies in its inability to probe deeper subcortical brain regions (e.g., the striatum and other deep structures), as it is primarily suited for detecting brain activity in areas close to the cortical surface, typically within a few millimeters beneath the cortex. The PFC regions play crucial roles in reward and loss processing ( Bartra, McGuire, &amp; Kable, 2013 ), particularly in individuals with IGD ( Wang et al., 2021 ; Yan, Li, Yu, &amp; Zhao, 2021 ; Zhang, Hu, Wang, Wang, &amp; Dong, 2020 ), including the processing of monetary gains and losses ( Dong et al., 2013 , 2017 ) and responses to addiction-related reward cues ( Cho et al., 2022 ; Zhang et al., 2021 ; Zhou et al., 2019 ). However, it is important to note that game-related reward stimuli differ from monetary rewards, even though they may elicit similar brain responses across different tasks. Therefore, this study focuses exclusively on game-related reward stimuli in actual gameplay, with an emphasis on the PFC, including the dLPFC, FPA, ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vLPFC) and OFC. Additionally, to effectively mitigate the otherwise confounding effects of frequent internet gaming, this study selected RGU as the control group. Therefore, hypothesis of this study proposed that during positive game events in mobile gameplay, the IGD group would be expected to show heightened HbO/HbR responses in the PFC region, indicating increased sensitivity to reward. Conversely, during negative game events, the IGD group would be expected to exhibit reduced HbO/HbR responses in the PFC region, indicating decreased sensitivity to loss. In addition, due to the variety of game modes in Multiplayer Online Battle Arena games, the multiplayer game mode is the most commonly played and has frequently been used to investigate the impact of game events on players' brain activity during actual gameplay. For example, studies by Cho et al. (2022) and Long et al. (2024) utilized the real multiplayer mode in League of Legends , while Zhang et al. (2023) also employed the multiplayer game mode. Building on this foundation, this study aims to investigate the multiplayer gameplay in mobile games to further contribute to the growing body of research in this field. However, different game modes vary in gameplay processes, and factors such as social interaction, cooperation, and opponent types may influence participants' cognitive activities during positive and negative game events. Long et al. (2024) suggested that PFC activity during gameplay is affected by inherent properties of the game itself. Therefore, the brain activity characteristics of IGD participants in the solo game mode also deserve attention, which may help isolate potential confounding factors and allow for a clearer understanding of PFC activity during specific game events in actual gameplay. In summary, this study aimed to examine HbO/HbR responses in the PFC region of IGD and RGU participants during positive and negative game events in the solo and multiplayer game modes of actual mobile gameplay. This was done separately in experiment 1 and experiment 2, to better understand the neural activity related to reward and loss in the PFC region of IGD participants during different actual mobile gameplay. Method Participants In experiment 1, three participants from the RGU group and two from the IGD group were excluded from the analysis due to low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) fNIRS signals. Ultimately, experiment 1 included 33 IGD players (21 females) and 31 RGU players (18 females). In experiment 2, two IGD participants and four RGU participants with low SNR. Experiment 2 included a total of 40 RGU players (20 females) and 45 IGD players (21 females), 27 participants of IGD and 27 participants of RGU of whom overlapped with experiment 1. G*Power 3.1.9.2 was used to calculate the desired sample size prior to conducting the research. The desired sample size was 34, assuming a moderate effect size ( α = 0.05, power = 0.8, effect size = 0.25). This study initially screened participants through online advertisements of social media, such as WeChat , QQ , targeting college students from Tianjin, China. The Simplified Chinese Version of the Ten-Item Internet Gaming Disorder Test (IGDT-10) ( Yang &amp; Zhang, 2023 ) and face-to-face paper-based interviews based on IGD criteria from DSM-5 were employed to identify participants with IGD and RGU. The IGDT-10 has demonstrated good cross-cultural consistency and was suitable for assessing IGD ( Chiu, Pan, &amp; Lin, 2018 ; Király et al., 2019 ; Männikkö, Ruotsalainen, Tolvanen, &amp; Kääriäinen, 2019 ). The interviews were conducted by the experimenters, who had received prior training. In previous studies, a combination of assessment tools (e.g., IGDT-10) and interviews was commonly used for IGD screening, with a cutoff score of 5 ( Chiu et al., 2018 ; Petry et al., 2014 ). In this study, the Cronbach's α for the IGDT-10 was 0.86. Participants with scores ≥5 on tool and paper-based interviews were classified into the IGD group, while those with scores &lt;5 on both were classified into the RGU group. All participants received compensation of 75 Chinese RMB upon completion of the experiment. Participants' daily gaming usage time was a screening criterion. Only those who played games for at least 3–4 h daily or a minimum of 28 h weekly were included in the study (specific questions included: “How many hours per day did you spend gaming in the past week?” and “What was your total gaming time in the past week?”), referencing the average gaming usage time of individuals with IGD in previous studies ( Cho et al., 2022 ; Wang, Tian, Zheng, Li, &amp; Liu, 2020 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ; Zhou et al., 2021 ). Restrictions were imposed on participants' alcohol and smoking behaviors (abstaining from alcohol one day prior to the experiment and refraining from smoking three hours before the experiment), and individuals with any mental or physical disorders were excluded. Only university students were recruited to exclude education as a confounding factor. Additionally, since the experimental materials involved the Honor of Kings , the question “Is Honor of Kings your main game?” served as a primary screening criterion. Task design Experiment 1 : In solo gameplay rewards and losses stimuli are clearer and are not influenced by social, cooperative, and other game-related factors. This allows for a better exploration of the sensitivity to rewards and losses in IGD individuals during real game events. Therefore, this study aimed, through experiment 1, to focus on the HbO/HbR responses in the PFC region during positive and negative game events to understand the neural mechanisms underlying the sensitivity to rewards and losses in IGD individuals in solo gameplay. Experiment 2 : Given recent studies exploring the brain activity of individuals with IGD or regular gamers during specific game events in multiplayer PC games ( Cho et al., 2022 ; Huang et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2018 ; Long et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ). This study aimed, through experiment 2, to focus on HbO/HbR responses of IGD individuals during game events in multiplayer mobile gameplay. This will provide a deeper understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying reward and loss sensitivity in IGD individuals during real gaming experiences. Game selection and procedure Building on previous research findings of our team members (see Yang and Zhang (2023) for details), this study selected the popular mobile Multiplayer Online Battle Arena game Honor of Kings in China. Choosing this game ensures widespread familiarity and helps avoid other confounding factors related to game type ( Na et al., 2017 ). The game mode of experiment 1 is 1v1 mode (as illustrated in Fig. 1c ) and experiment 2 employed a randomized 5v5 mode (as illustrated in Fig. 1d ). Participants all used their own accounts for gameplay. To simulate a genuine gaming experience, no restrictions were imposed on the gaming process. Players were free to select characters according to their gaming habits and commence the game. In line with the study by Li et al. (2018) , which underscored the need for the accumulation of game events to ensure result stability in analyses based on actual gaming processes, preliminary work found that a single round of 1v1 gaming struggled to guarantee this stability. Therefore, in the formal experiment, the complete experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 1a , while the actual experimental process is illustrated in Fig. 1b . During the fNIRS scanning, each participant played two rounds in solo or multiplayer modes. In the solo mode, there was no significant difference in the duration of individual games between the IGD and RGU groups ( M IGD = 449.29 s, SD IGD = 90.68 s, M RGU = 412.06 s, SD RGU = 86.43 s, t = 1.68, p = 0.098, Cohen's d = 0.42). Similarly, in the multiplayer mode, no significant differences in game duration were found between the IGD and RGU groups ( M IGD = 947.03 s, SD IGD = 211.13 s, M RGU = 936.59 s, SD RGU = 193.65 s, t = 0.24, p = 0.814, Cohen's d = 0.05). In China, mainstream mobile phone devices mainly fall into two categories: IOS and Android systems. Due to the non-shared nature of game accounts between these two platforms, this study prepared two devices for players to choose from: the iPhone 11 (75.7 mm* 150.9 mm) and Vivo X60 (75.01 mm* 159.63 mm), for iOS and Android respectively. Data acquisition This study employed near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) brain imaging equipment manufactured by Shimadzu Corporation (LABNIRS/16, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). The sampling rate was set at 22.22 Hz, used for collecting HbO and HbR data. A 3 × 9 array of multi-channel probes with an inter-probe distance of 3 cm was utilized, comprising 14 laser sources and 13 detectors, forming a total of 42 channels, covering the prefrontal cortex (PFC), as shown in Fig. 1e . The optode positions were referenced to the international 10–20 EEG system, using Fpz as the reference point. The spatial positions for all channels were obtained using a 3D digitizer (Vpen) and converted to standard MNI (Montreal Neurological Institute) coordinates (see Table S1 ). Data analysis Figure 2 showed the whole analytical pipeline of this study. The event-related approach ( Li et al., 2018 ) was utilized to analyze the concentration of HbO/HbR associated with specific game events. Time markers were assigned to game events. Positive events were when the player's hero killed an opponent without dying, and negative events were when the player's hero was killed. Only game segments with no other events within 5 s before and 20 s after the marked point were included for analysis. Second, raw data underwent a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) test. Channels with CV Channel &gt; 25% were rejected to reduce physical artifacts ( Piper et al., 2014 ). Participants with over 20% bad channels were excluded, leading to the removal of three RGU and two IGD participants. Channels 9/17/26/34/35/42 were excluded from further analysis due to poor SNR. Third, the Homer2 toolbox in Matlab (version 2014b) was used to preprocess fNIRS data ( Huppert, Diamond, Franceschini, &amp; Boas, 2009 ). At the individual level, General Linear Models (GLMs) were used to compute β values for task-related responses under different conditions. Standardized time series were averaged to create representative time series for positive and negative game events. Baseline HbO/HbR responses were measured from −5 to −3 s before event onset, and event-related responses were measured from 4 to 6 s after onset. At the group level, statistical and regression analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.3.2). Nearest neighbor interpolation corrected data instead of direct deletion of large CV channel segments. A mixed ANOVA (2 game events × 2 groups) was conducted ( Bao, 2023 ). False discovery rate (FDR) and Bonferroni corrections were applied for multiple comparisons and post-hoc tests, respectively. Regression analysis compared HbO/HbR responses with IGDT-10 scores across the full sample. In addition, Shapiro-Wilk tests performed to assess normality for each channel under different conditions. While some channels showed slight deviations from normality ( p &lt; 0.05), previous research suggests that ANOVA is robust to minor violations of normality ( Blanca et al., 2017 ). Therefore, we proceeded with ANOVA for our main analysis. Ethics The research procedure adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Normal University (2023122703). All participants were provided with information about the research, signed informed consent forms. Discussion This study used fNIRS to measure HbO/HbR responses in the PFC of IGD and RGU participants during real positive and negative game events, aiming to investigate reward and loss processing in IGD individuals during mobile gameplay. Results showed that in solo mode, the IGD group had higher HbO responses in the dLPFC, OFC, and FPA regions during positive game events and lower HbO responses in the FPA region during negative game events compared to the RGU group, indicating altered neural responses to gaming rewards and losses in IGD individuals. However, these differences were not observed in multiplayer mode. In multiplayer mode, the IGD group also showed lower HbR responses in the dLPFC and vLPFC regions during both event types compared to the RGU group. Additionally, HbO responses were significantly higher during positive events across both game modes, with a more widespread distribution in the PFC during multiplayer mode. Reward/loss processing in IGD individuals during mobile solo gameplay In solo game mode, the IGD group showed higher HbO responses in the dLPFC, FPA, and OFC regions during positive game events, suggesting higher reward sensitivity due to increased anticipation or craving for positive outcomes. This is supported by the significantly higher craving scores in the IGD group, consistent with findings from Cho et al. (2022) , who reported higher dLPFC responses in IGD individuals during positive events. Other studies also highlight increased craving and overactivation in these brain regions in IGD individuals ( Dong &amp; Lin, 2013 ; Locke &amp; Braver, 2008 ; Wang et al., 2021 ), similar to patterns seen in substance addiction ( Goldstein et al., 2011 ; Kufahl et al., 2008 ; Yang et al., 2022 ). During negative game events, the IGD group showed lower HbO responses in the FPA region compared to the RGU group. This aligns with findings from Dong et al. (2017) , where IGD individuals exhibited reduced activation in the superior frontal gyrus during loss events. These results suggest that reduced sensitivity to losses is a core feature of IGD ( He et al., 2017 ; Wang et al., 2020 ; Yao et al., 2020 ; Zhang et al., 2020 ), similar to what is observed in gambling and alcohol disorders ( Fauth-Bühler &amp; Mann, 2017 ). Additionally, the reduced activation in this region suggests that individuals with IGD may be immersed in the game rather than focusing on losses or negative emotions, even in a “spectator” state after being killed. Previous research indicates that immersion, flow experiences, and sustained attention are associated with reduced activation in the FPA ( Ganesan et al., 2022 ). Reduced PFC responses in IGD individuals during mobile multiplayer gamplay In multiplayer game mode, this study found lower HbR responses in the dLPFC and vLPFC regions of the IGD group compared to the RGU group. Impairments in the dLPFC and vLPFC have been consistently identified as key features of behavioral addictions, including IGD ( Burrows, Kay-Lambkin, Pursey, Skinner, &amp; Dayas, 2018 ; Chun et al., 2020 ; Lin, Lin, Lin, Yen, &amp; Ko, 2020 ; Weiss, Forkus, Contractor, &amp; Schick, 2018 ; Zhang et al., 2021 ). This finding may be because multiplayer games are more challenging and involve complex social interactions, requiring players to adapt to changing game dynamics and manage both cooperative and competitive behaviors. For individuals with IGD, such impairments could hinder their ability to effectively process and respond to in-game events, leading to difficulties in regulating impulsive actions or emotional responses during gameplay. Previous research has widely confirmed that these regions are part of the executive control network, which plays a crucial role in top-down cognitive control, including the regulation of thoughts, emotions, and behaviors ( Menon &amp; D’Esposito, 2022 ; Etkin, Büchel, &amp; Gross, 2015 ). Although HbR responses may sometimes be unstable, further regression analysis in both solo and multiplayer modes revealed a significant positive correlation between HbO/HbR responses in the dLPFC and vLPFC and IGDT-10 scores. This indicates that activation in these regions is associated with the severity of IGD, confirming that impairments in the dLPFC and vLPFC are primary manifestations of IGD. Consistent with this, Zhou et al. (2021) also emphasized the relationship between the executive control network and IGD severity, particularly in terms of gaming time. Enhanced PFC responses to positive game events and the impact of game mode This study resulted that in both solo and multiplayer game modes, positive game events elicited stronger HbO responses compared to negative events. This aligns with previous research indicating that the pursuit of victory and the enjoyment derived from gaming are major motivations for players ( Bäcklund, Elbe, Gavelin, Sörman, &amp; Ljungberg, 2022 ). Positive events trigger a greater reward experience, leading to significant PFC activation in both groups ( Etzel, Cole, Zacks, Kay, &amp; Braver, 2016 ; Locke &amp; Braver, 2008 ; Wang et al., 2021 ). Moreover, the study found that HbO responses in the PFC were more widespread in multiplayer mode, likely because multiplayer games not only introduce greater challenges but also incorporate social elements, making the experience of satisfaction more complex and enriching the emotional and cognitive stimuli involved. This is consistent with findings on the correlation between PFC activity, task difficulty, and subjective enjoyment ( Alameda, Sanabria, &amp; Ciria, 2022 ; de Sampaio Barros et al., 2018 ; Fishburn, Norr, Medvedev, &amp; Vaidya, 2014 ; Lucas, Urieta, Balada, Blanco, &amp; Aluja, 2020 ; Pereira, Castro, Villafaina, CarvalhoSantos, &amp; Fuentes-García, 2020 ). Yu et al. (2023) also observed higher HbO responses in the PFC during high-difficulty games, supporting this view. Additionally, both IGD and RGU groups exhibited similar HbO/HbR response patterns during multiplayer mode. One possible explanation, as Clark and Zack (2023) suggested, is that the variability in game rewards may imbue non-pharmacological rewards with addictive potential comparable to drugs. For individuals with IGD, matching game difficulty to their skill level enhances immersion, which is a key factor in the development of IGD ( Larche &amp; Dixon, 2021 ; Yilmaz &amp; Tunca, 2024 ). Similarly, in multiplayer modes, RGUs may also experience strong immersion, leading to cognitive activity related to reward and loss processing similar to that of individuals with IGD. This aligns with Clark and Zack's (2023) perspective on the addictive potential of variable rewards. Moreover, our supplementary data (as shown in the supplementary files ) indicate that gamers generally prefer multiplayer games over solo ones, finding them more challenging and enjoyable (see Table S9 for details). However, it is important to note that since participants in the two experiments were not entirely the same, this study did not directly compare the effects of different game modes on PFC responses to various game events in IGD individuals. As such, the conclusions drawn here remain speculative, and further research is needed to explore this issue more thoroughly. Limitations and future directions A significant limitation of this study was the inability to compare HbO/HbR responses in the PFC between the IGD and RGU groups across game modes (solo vs. multiplayer). Future research should balance the number of game events to further determine the impact of game attributes on individuals with IGD. Additionally, previous research has consistently shown gender differences in IGD ( Stevens, Dorstyn, Delfabbro, &amp; King, 2021 ), with a higher prevalence among males, who may also exhibit different reward/loss response patterns ( Zhang et al., 2020 ). However, this study had a higher proportion of female participants, potentially limiting the generalizability of the results in terms of gender representation. This study restricted participants' gaming to at least 3–4 h daily or a minimum of 28 h weekly. Some participants' gaming usage time fell below the time spent for disordered gaming proposed by Pontes, Schivinski, Kannen, and Montag (2022) . However, there currently lacks a standardized criterion for selecting participants based on gaming duration in experiments. Future research should adopt a more consistent approach to screen participants with IGD. Moreover, this study did not assess participants' craving levels after the gaming process, so the conclusion that IGD individuals have difficulty regulating cravings requires further validation. Conclusion In conclusion, this study examined HbO/HbR responses in the PFC during positive and negative game events among IGD and RGU individuals in mobile gaming. IGD participants demonstrated heightened reward processing in the dLPFC, FPA, and OFC regions during solo mode, alongside reduced sensitivity to losses in the FPA. In multiplayer mode, they exhibited impairments in the executive control network (dLPFC and vLPFC), despite similar overall brain response patterns to game events in both groups. These findings highlight the complex neural dynamics of IGD and gaming behaviors, pointing to the need for further research to fully understand their implications. Clinically, altered reward and loss sensitivity may serve as potential criteria for the assessment and intervention of IGD.
