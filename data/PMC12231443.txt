Abstract Background and Objectives Internet gaming disorder (IGD) is a growing public health concern, especially among adolescents. However, most IGD studies are cross-sectional, leaving its developmental course poorly understood. This study examined IGD trajectories, their predictors, and their long-term mental health outcomes among Chinese adolescents. Methods A cohort of 16,833 adolescent gamers (mean age = 13.40, 46.56% girls) was recruited from 76 middle schools in Zigong City through cluster sampling. Assessments were conducted in November 2020 (T1), November 2021 (T2), and November 2022 (T3). IGD was measured using the Internet Gaming Disorder Scale–Short Form (IGDS9-SF). Demographic, gaming, environmental, and psychological factors (e.g., depression, anxiety, sleep problems, suicidal ideation, conduct problems, hyperactivity, resilience, mental wellbeing, prosocial behavior) were also assessed. Growth mixture modeling (GMM) identified IGD trajectories, and logistic regression evaluated predictors and mental health outcomes at T3. Results GMM identified four IGD trajectories: High-Risk Decreasing (4.5%), Moderate-Risk Stable (19.5%), Moderate-Risk Increasing (3.0%), and Low-Risk Stable (73.0%). Age, gaming time, anxiety, sleep problems, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, and low prosocial behavior predicted worsening IGD symptoms. At T3, the Moderate-Risk Increasing group had significantly higher risks for adverse mental health outcomes compared to the Low-Risk Stable group, whereas the High-Risk Decreasing group showed no significant or minimal differences in most domains from the Low-Risk Stable group after adjusting for baseline characteristics. Discussion and Conclusions These findings underscore the heterogeneous nature of IGD development and highlight the need for long-term monitoring and tailored interventions to mitigate adverse mental health outcomes in at-risk adolescents. Introduction Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) is a growing global public health concern among adolescents. Meta-analyses indicate a prevalence of IGD ranging from 1.96% to 3.3%, with variations largely attributable to differences in sample characteristics, assessment tools, and diagnostic criteria emphasizing functional impairment ( Kim et al., 2022 ; Stevens, Dorstyn, Delfabbro, &amp; King, 2021 ). IGD is consistently linked to negative outcomes such as psychological distress, physical health issues, and functional impairments, highlighting the urgent need for early identification and targeted interventions ( Hosozawa et al., 2022 ; Ko, Lin, Lin, &amp; Yen, 2020 ; Lemmens, Valkenburg, &amp; Peter, 2011 ; P. Peng et al., 2025 ; Pu Peng, Chen, Ren, He, et al., 2025 ; van den Eijnden, Koning, Doornwaard, van Gurp, &amp; Ter Bogt, 2018 ). Research indicates that IGD arises from intricate interactions between individual vulnerabilities and environmental influences ( Gao, Wang, &amp; Dong, 2022 ; Zhuang et al., 2023 ). Key risk factors encompass biological elements (e.g., sex, developmental stage), adverse environmental exposures (e.g., bullying, family dysfunction), excessive gaming, pre-existing mental health conditions (e.g., depression, anxiety, hyperactivity/inattention), and individual traits (e.g., resilience, mental well-being). The Interaction of Person–Affect–Cognition–Execution (I-PACE) model categorizes these as “Personal factors” which contribute to susceptibility to addictive behaviors ( Brand et al., 2019 ; Brand, Young, Laier, Wölfling, &amp; Potenza, 2016 ). For instance, neurodevelopmental immaturity (younger age) and psychological conditions (e.g., hyperactivity) may increase sensitivity to gaming's rewarding effects, whereas environmental stressors (e.g., peer conflicts) trigger negative affect (e.g., depression, anxiety), prompting compensatory gaming. Over time, cognitive biases toward gaming rewards and impaired self-regulation (e.g., extended gaming time, decreased resilience) further reinforce maladaptive gaming patterns, perpetuating dependency. Despite growing attention, the developmental course of IGD remains inadequately understood. Previous research primarily employs cross-sectional or short-term variable-centered approaches (e.g., regression analyses), focusing on risk factors and thus failing to capture the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of IGD progression. According to the I-PACE model ( Brand et al., 2016 , 2019 ), adolescents differ substantially in their susceptibility to addictive behaviors due to varying levels of personal vulnerabilities. These differences naturally yield distinct IGD trajectories, ranging from escalating risk to spontaneous remission. Person-centered trajectory analysis represents an advanced methodological alternative, enabling identification of subgroups with unique IGD developmental patterns (e.g., stable low-risk, increasing risk, decreasing risk). Such approaches have successfully captured the heterogeneous nature of internet-related behavioral addictions ( Geng et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Yoo, 2024 ; Zhou, Zhen, &amp; Wu, 2018 ), demonstrating that addictive behaviors do not follow uniform trajectories. Subgroups exhibiting worsening or persistently high-risk patterns have been associated with poorer mental health outcomes ( Geng et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2023 ). Adolescence, characterized by heightened susceptibility to peer influence, risk-taking behaviors, and regulatory challenges, is a critical period for investigating IGD trajectories. Understanding distinct IGD trajectories aligns with the developmental psychopathology framework, which posits that maladaptive behaviors such as IGD result from dynamic interactions between individuals and their environments over time ( Cicchetti &amp; Rogosch, 2002 ; Eme, 2017 ). A longitudinal, person-centered approach thus offers a nuanced understanding of IGD's variability and clarifies specific risk and protective factors ( Nagin, Jones, &amp; Elmer, 2024 ). This perspective identifies adolescents at elevated risk and informs targeted interventions aimed at mitigating long-term psychological harm, including depression, anxiety, and suicidality. Given IGD's relatively recent recognition and limited longitudinal evidence ( Petry et al., 2014 ), clarifying its natural course is critical for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies. However, existing longitudinal research on IGD trajectories remains scarce and inconsistent ( Coyne et al., 2020 ; King, Delfabbro, &amp; Griffiths, 2013 ; Qiao, Chen, Zhu, &amp; Shi, 2024 ). For example, a six-year study among 385 adolescents (mean age = 15.01 years) identified a high-risk subgroup whose IGD symptoms increased over time ( Coyne et al., 2020 ). Conversely, a recent study among Chinese young adults (mean age = 18.63 years) indicated a nature recovery in IGD severity over two years ( Qiao et al., 2024 ). Small-to-moderate sample sizes ( N = 177–5,787) in previous studies may contribute to these contradictory findings by limiting statistical power. Given the relatively low prevalence of IGD, large-scale longitudinal studies are necessary to enhance reliability, generalizability, and to comprehensively explore trajectory heterogeneity. In response to these gaps, this three-wave longitudinal study aims to: (1) explore the developmental trajectories of IGD among Chinese adolescent gamers through person-centered methods; (2) determine predictors of these trajectories based on individual (e.g., sex, age, gaming time, substance use, mental health conditions, resilience) and environmental factors (e.g., family structure, peer relationships, parental education), informed by the I-PACE model ( Brand et al., 2016 , 2019 ) or past empirical research on IGD ( Gao et al., 2022 ; Zhuang et al., 2023 ); and (3) investigate how these distinct IGD trajectories relate to adverse psychological health outcomes. Methods Study procedure and participants This three-wave, school-based longitudinal study was conducted in Zigong City, located in southwestern China. Detailed information on baseline sampling, data collection, and quality control has been published elsewhere ( Z. Chen et al., 2023 ; Pu Peng et al., 2023 ; Pu Peng, Chen, Ren, Liu, et al., 2025 ; Pu Peng, Jin, et al., 2025 ). In November 2020 (T1), cluster sampling was used to recruit 63,205 middle and high school students (grades 7–12) from 76 schools. Of these, 13,361 seventh-grade and 6,776 tenth-grade students were invited to complete follow-up surveys in November 2021 (T2) and November 2022 (T3), respectively. Students in other grades were excluded from follow-up due to impending graduation. The one-year interval between each assessment was guided by three considerations: alignment with DSM-5 diagnostic criteria and the assessment of the Internet Gaming Disorder Scale - Short Form (IGDS9-SF), both of which assess symptoms in the past year; logistical feasibility in a large-scale school-based study ( N &gt; 20,000) while minimizing participant burden; and a focus on long-term developmental trajectories rather than short-term fluctuations. Measurements Internet gaming disorder and gaming usage at T1, T2, and T3 IGD was assessed at all three time points using the IGDS9-SF, comprising nine DSM-5-based symptoms scored from 9 to 45, with higher scores indicating greater severity ( Pontes &amp; Griffiths, 2015 ). A cutoff point of 32 was employed to screen for IGD ( Qin et al., 2020 ). Additionally, participants reported their average gaming time on weekdays and weekends over the past year. Gaming time was calculated as weekday gaming time × 5 + weekend gaming time × 2. Gamers were defined as those who reported gaming experience within the last year. Demographics and environmental factors at T1 Demographic data were collected via a self-administered questionnaire, encompassing individual factors such as gender, age, residence, and grade level and family-related variables. Family structure details encompassed whether participants were only children, left-behind children (a child who remains in their rural hometown while one or both parents migrate to urban areas for work), from single-parent or blended families, and their parental education levels as a proxy for socioeconomic index. Information on tobacco use (both traditional and e-cigarettes) and alcohol consumption was also included. Peer victimization was measured using the Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS), which comprises 16 items covering physical, verbal, social, and property-related bullying ( Mynard &amp; Joseph, 2000 ). Peer relationships were further assessed via the peer problems subscale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), where scores above 6 indicated significant peer relationship difficulties ( Du, Kou, &amp; Coghill, 2008 ; Goodman, 1997 ). Mental health problems at T1 and T3 Mental health measures included internalizing problems (depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation), externalizing problems (conduct, hyperactivity/inattention), sleep disturbances, low psychological resilience, low prosocial behavior, and overall mental well-being. Depression was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; cutoff ≥10) (Y.-L. Zhang et al., 2013 ), anxiety via the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 (GAD-7; cutoff ≥10) ( Plummer, Manea, Trepel, &amp; McMillan, 2016 ), conduct and hyperactivity problems using SDQ subscales (cutoffs: conduct ≥6, hyperactivity ≥7) ( Du et al., 2008 ; Goodman, 1997 ), sleep quality with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; cutoff &gt;5) ( Tsai et al., 2005 ), mental well-being via the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS; cutoff ≤42) ( Dong et al., 2016 ), resilience using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10; cutoff &lt;25) (Z. J. Ye et al., 2017 ), and prosocial behavior using the SDQ prosocial subscale (cutoff ≤4). Suicidal ideation was assessed at both T1 and T3 using the question, “Within the last month, have you seriously thought about suicide?”, which was adapted from the Composite International Diagnostic Interview and have been utilized in numerous large-scale epidemiological studies ( Borges et al., 2010 ; Cheng et al., 2024 ). Statistical analysis Because this study aimed to determine the trajectory of IGD, analyses were restricted to gamers ( N = 16,833 of 20,137). Descriptive statistics were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Attrition analysis utilized Student's t -tests and chi-square tests to compare baseline characteristics of completers and non-completers, with Cohen's d (&lt;0.3) and Cramer's V (&lt;0.1) indicating negligible differences. Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) was employed to identify distinct IGD trajectories over time using a linear growth model. Missing data were handled via Full Information Maximum Likelihood, ensuring the inclusion of all available data ( N = 16,833). Following established guidelines ( Nylund, Asparouhov, &amp; Muthén, 2007 ; Nylund-Gibson &amp; Choi, 2018 ; Weller, Bowen, &amp; Faubert, 2020 ), models with one to five trajectory classes were evaluated based on the following criteria: lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample-Size Adjusted BIC (SABIC), and its elbow point, significant Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Tests (BLRT; p &lt; 0.05), and clinical interpretability. Entropy was considered as a secondary indicator of classification accuracy rather than a primary criterion. Because IGD is relatively rare, no minimum subgroup size was set; small but clinically meaningful trajectories were therefore included. To test the robustness of our findings, we also performed a sensitivity analysis, using data from only who participated in the follow-up ( n = 14,214). Upon determining the optimal trajectory model, baseline characteristics across trajectory classes were compared using one-way ANOVA and chi-square tests. Effect sizes (η 2 for ANOVA, Cramer's V for chi-square) and Holm-corrected pairwise comparisons controlled for multiple comparisons. Multinomial logistic regression then identified predictors of trajectory membership, including only variables meeting clinical relevance criteria ( η 2 ≥ 0.01 or V ≥ 0.10). Model fit indices (McFadden's R 2 , Nagelkerke's R 2 , Cox &amp; Snell's R 2 ), adjusted odds ratios (AORs), and likelihood ratio tests were reported. Finally, associations between IGD trajectories and T3 mental health outcomes (depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, suicidal ideation, conduct problems, hyperactivity, low resilience, low mental well-being, and low prosocial behavior) were examined via chi-square tests and multivariable logistic regression, controlling for baseline factors. All descriptive analyses, group comparisons, and regression models were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0). GMM was performed using Mplus (version 8.3). All statistical tests were two-tailed with p &lt; 0.05 indicating significance. Ethics The study procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Zigong Mental Health Center (Approval No. 2020-8-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and for those under 18, consent was also secured from their parents. Results Sample characteristics Of the 20,137 students initially enrolled from grades 7 and 10, 16,833 identified as gamers and were thus included in the subsequent analyses. Follow-up participation remained high, with 12,570 students at T2 and 12,320 at T3. Overall, 14,214 (84.4%) participants completed at least one follow-up. Attrition analysis revealed statistically significant yet mostly negligible differences between the 14,214 retained participants and the 2,619 lost to follow-up ( Table S1 ). Those lost to follow-up were younger, predominantly seventh graders, less likely to be left-behind or urban residents, and had mothers with higher education levels. They also reported higher levels of peer bullying, peer relationship problems, suicidal ideation, depression, anxiety, and conduct problems. Gaming-related variables showed no significant differences. Most effect sizes were negligible, except for age (Cohen's d = 0.42) and grade (Cramer's V = 0.165), indicating that the retained sample remained reasonably representative. At baseline, participants' mean age was 13.44 years, and 46.56% were female ( Table 1 ). Most participants lived in rural areas (64.03%), were not only children (77.41%), and roughly one-third were left-behind children. Parental education was typically below high school. Substance use was relatively low (smoking: 7.81%, alcohol consumption: 16.88%), and IGD prevalence was 3.1%, with average daily gaming of 1.91 h. Mental health issues were common, including suicidal ideation (19.88%), depression (23.51%), anxiety (14.03%), sleep disturbances (30.62%), conduct problems and hyperactivity (each ∼10%), impaired resilience (64.51%), low mental well-being (41.32%), and low prosocial behavior (9.20%). Trajectory of the IGD over two years Model fit indices for one to five trajectory classes are shown in Table 2 , with trajectories and fit criteria visualized in Figs S1 and S2 . A four-class model (High-Risk Decreasing [4.5%], Moderate-Risk Stable [19.5%], Moderate-Risk Increasing [3.0%], and Low-Risk Stable [73.0%]) emerged as optimal, based on statistical criteria and clinical interpretability ( Fig. 1 ). Statistically, the four-class model exhibited lower AIC, BIC, and SABIC values compared to the three-class model and showed a significant improvement on the bootstrap likelihood ratio test. The entropy was acceptable at 0.78, indicating good classification accuracy. While the five-class model showed even lower AIC, BIC, and SABIC values ( Table 2 ), the additional patterns it identified (e.g., a low-moderate stable versus a moderate with slight growth trajectory) closely resembled the moderate-stable group from the four-class solution, lacking substantial clinical distinction or more prominent changes (see Fig. S1 for details on the 1-5 class solutions). This suggested potential overfitting rather than genuinely more informative or clinically significant patterns. Therefore, our model selection prioritized a balance of statistical fit with clinical interpretability and parsimony, leading to the four-class solution. This solution effectively captured meaningful heterogeneity, encompassing distinct increasing, decreasing, and stable developmental courses. We limited our exploration to five classes, as including more classes could further reduce clinical interpretability. The five-class model's tendency towards over-differentiation without enhancing distinct clinical utility affirmed our choice of the four-class model. Clinically, the four-class solution offered meaningful distinctions: the Moderate-Risk Increasing group (3.0%), which starts at a moderate baseline (T1 = 24.25) but escalates sharply (T3 = 31.30), may require focused early intervention to prevent progression to severe IGD. The High-Risk Decreasing group (4.5%) shows severe baseline symptoms (T1 = 31.35) that decline significantly (T3 = 13.71), illustrating a natural recovery course. The Moderate-Risk Stable group (19.5%), with moderately elevated symptoms that remain stable or rise slightly (T1 = 17.46; T3 = 19.84), may benefit from lower-intensity preventive measures rather than urgent intervention. Finally, the Low-Risk Stable group (73.0%) displays consistently minimal IGD symptoms (T1 = 14.05; T3 = 11.23) and likely does not require intervention. Sensitivity analysis among participants completing follow-ups ( n = 14,214) yielded consistent results ( Table S2 and Figs S3 and S4 ). Predictors of the IGD trajectory Significant baseline differences across trajectories were observed for demographic, environmental, gaming, and mental health factors ( Table 3 ). The High-Risk Decreasing group exhibited the highest severity across all indicators, followed sequentially by Moderate-Risk Increasing, Moderate-Risk Stable, and Low-Risk Stable groups. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed distinct risk profiles. Multinomial logistic regression identified several predictors for higher-risk trajectories compared to the Low-Risk Stable group ( Table 4 ). To ensure clinical relevance, only variables exceeding established effect size thresholds ( η 2 ≥ 0.01 or V ≥ 0.10) were included: sex, gaming time, smoking, MPVS, SDQ subscales, PHQ-9, GAD-7, PSQI, WEMWBS, CD-RISC10, and suicidal ideation. IGDS9-SF scores were not included in the regression model to avoid circulation analysis. Boys were significantly more likely than girls to belong to all higher-risk trajectories. Smoking significantly increased odds for Moderate-Risk Stable and High-Risk Decreasing groups. Greater daily gaming usage and peer victimization (MPVS scores) predicted membership in all higher-risk trajectories, strongest in the High-Risk Decreasing group. Mental health issues, including anxiety (GAD-7), sleep disturbance (PSQI), depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), hyperactivity, and conduct problems (SDQ), significantly predicted Moderate-Risk Increasing and High-Risk Decreasing trajectories, with suicidal ideation uniquely predicting the High-Risk Decreasing group. Higher prosocial behavior was protective against all elevated risk trajectories. Peer problems, resilience, and well-being were not consistently significant. Model fit indices were modest (McFadden's R 2 = 0.105, Nagelkerke's R 2 = 0.192, Cox &amp; Snell's R 2 = 0.153), appropriate given the aim was to identify associated factors rather than predictive accuracy. The Likelihood Ratio Test confirmed these predictors significantly improved model fit ( χ 2 = 2796.152, df = 42, p &lt; 0.001). An explanatory analysis comparing the Moderate-Risk Stable group and Moderate-Risk Increasing group In an exploratory post-hoc analysis, we compared the Moderate-Risk Stable ( n = 3,276) and Moderate-Risk Increasing ( n = 514) groups to identify factors distinguishing these clinically relevant trajectories. Although both groups started with subclinical moderate IGD severity, the Moderate-Risk Increasing group exhibited worsening severity over time. At baseline, this group was significantly younger, spent more time gaming, and reported higher levels of smoking, peer victimization, peer relationship problems, suicidal ideation, depressive symptoms, anxiety, sleep disturbances, conduct problems, and hyperactivity/inattention. Additionally, they showed lower prosocial behavior, mental well-being, and resilience. No significant differences were observed in other individual or environmental factors. Logistic regression indicated ( Table 5 ) that younger age, increased gaming time, conduct problems, hyperactivity, anxiety, and sleep disturbances predicted membership in the Moderate-Risk Increasing group, while higher prosocial behavior was protective. Model fit indices were moderate (Nagelkerke's R 2 = 0.118, Cox &amp; Snell's R 2 = 0.065), with good fit according to the Hosmer-Lemeshow test ( χ 2 = 9.816, df = 8, p = 0.278). An explanatory analysis comparing the High-Risk Decreasing group and Moderate-Risk Increasing group We further compared the High-Risk Decreasing group and the Moderate-Risk Increasing group as an explanatory analysis. The logistic regression model revealed several independent predictors (see Table 6 for full model results). Specifically, older age, smoking experience, longer average daily gaming time, and higher scores on peer bullying victimization were significantly associated with an increased likelihood of being in the High-Risk Decreasing group compared to the Moderate-Risk Increasing group. Other baseline factors, including the initially observed differences in mental health problem severity, did not emerge as significant independent predictors in this multivariate model distinguishing these two specific groups. Model fit indices were moderate (Nagelkerke's R 2 = 0.064, Cox &amp; Snell's R 2 = 0.047), with good fit according to the Hosmer-Lemeshow test ( χ 2 = 10.152, df = 8, p = 0.255). Association of IGD trajectory with mental health outcomes Significant differences in mental health outcomes at T3 emerged across IGD trajectories ( Table 7 ). The Moderate-Risk Increasing group consistently exhibited the highest risks, followed by Moderate-Risk Stable and High-Risk Decreasing groups, with the Low-Risk Stable group showing the lowest risks. Table 8 illustrates the associations between IGD trajectories and long-term mental health outcomes. Compared to the Low-Risk Stable trajectory, the Moderate-Risk Increasing group consistently exhibited the highest risks across all measured outcomes after adjusting for covariates. Specifically, participants in the Moderate-Risk Increasing trajectory demonstrated significantly elevated odds for depression (AOR = 14.65, 95% CI: 11.65–18.44), anxiety (AOR = 14.80, 95% CI: 11.57–18.93), sleep problems (AOR = 9.30, 95% CI: 7.42–11.66), conduct problems (AOR = 7.59, 95% CI: 5.92–9.75), hyperactivity/inattention (AOR = 9.13, 95% CI: 7.17–11.62), suicidal ideation (AOR = 6.02, 95% CI: 4.74–7.63), low resilience (AOR = 6.78, 95% CI: 5.14–8.94), low mental well-being (AOR = 6.79, 95% CI: 5.43–8.50), and low prosocial behavior (AOR = 3.42, 95% CI: 2.64–4.44). The Moderate-Risk Stable group also showed significantly increased odds across all outcomes, albeit lower than the Moderate-Risk Increasing group. The High-Risk Decreasing group had slightly elevated odds of anxiety (AOR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.05–2.08), sleep problems (AOR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.02–1.62), and hyperactivity/inattention (AOR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.16–2.20). Other outcomes, including depression, conduct problems, suicidal ideation, resilience, mental well-being, and prosocial behavior, were not significantly associated with the High-Risk Decreasing trajectory in adjusted models. Discussion This study examined the developmental trajectories of IGD, their predictive factors, and associated long-term mental health outcomes in Chinese adolescents. Four distinct trajectories emerged over the two-year follow-up: High-Risk Decreasing (4.5%), Moderate-Risk Stable (19.5%), Moderate-Risk Increasing (3.0%), and Low-Risk Stable (73.0%). These trajectories were differentially predicted by individual and environmental factors, with younger age, longer gaming time, anxiety, sleep problems, conduct issues, hyperactivity/inattention, and low prosocial behavior distinguishing the Moderate-Risk Increasing group from the Moderate-Risk Stable group. Notably, the Moderate-Risk Increasing trajectory was associated with the poorest mental health outcomes, whereas the High-Risk Decreasing group showed minimal differences from the Low-Risk Stable trajectory after baseline adjustment, suggesting potential natural recovery. The trajectory of IGD symptoms over two years This study is among the first to examine IGD trajectories specifically within a Chinese adolescent population. The identified trajectories partially align with prior research ( Qiao et al., 2024 ), while uniquely identifying a Moderate-Risk Stable group. The observed trajectories differ from those reported for other behavioral addictions, such as internet or smartphone addiction, where stable high-risk or increasing patterns are more common ( Geng et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Yoo, 2024 ; Zhou et al., 2018 ). For example, Li et al. found stable addiction patterns across risk levels among university students with smartphone addiction ( Li et al., 2022 ). These discrepancies highlight the distinct developmental course of IGD, reinforcing the need for disorder-specific assessment in research and clinical practice. Future comparative studies across behavioral addictions within the same cohort could elucidate disorder-specific mechanisms. Additionally, our results support previous evidence suggesting low temporal stability of IGD symptoms in adolescent community samples. The marked decline in severity among initially high-risk adolescents implies that IGD symptoms may often be transient, potentially reflecting temporary maladaptive coping, developmental stressors, or experimental phases rather than chronic pathology during adolescence. These results align with previous work ( King et al., 2013 ; Qiao et al., 2024 ) and reinforce reports of relatively low IGD persistence rates, typically ranging between 20% and 40% ( Jeong et al., 2021 ; Liu et al., 2021 ; Rothmund, Klimmt, &amp; Gollwitzer, 2018 ; Scharkow, Festl, &amp; Quandt, 2014 ). This phenomenon of fluctuating symptom severity and potentially low temporal stability is not unique to IGD when considering community-based samples. For instance, research on alcohol use disorder has indicated lower temporal stability in community samples compared to clinical samples ( Culverhouse et al., 2005 ). Similarly, the stability of problem gambling and its associated symptoms has also been reported to be low in general population studies ( Sleczka &amp; Romild, 2021 ). This context is important, as it underscores that the natural course of addictive behaviors can be highly variable, particularly during adolescence and in non-clinical populations. A critical implication of this low temporal stability is the challenge and importance of identifying adolescents who are likely to follow a more chronic disorder course versus those whose symptoms will naturally remit. While our High-Risk Decreasing group exemplifies natural recovery, trajectories like the Moderate-Risk Increasing group, which showed escalating symptoms and the poorest long-term mental health outcomes, may represent a pathway towards more entrenched problems requiring targeted intervention. Understanding the factors that differentiate these pathways is paramount for effective prevention and treatment efforts. However, most existing studies, including ours, rely on community samples and screening tools rather than clinical diagnoses. Future research should prioritize clinical cohorts and diagnostic evaluations to clarify IGD's stability, remission patterns, and predictors of chronicity, ultimately refining early identification and intervention strategies. Influencing factors of IGD developmental trajectories Aligned with previous studies (W. Zhang et al., 2022 ; Zhuang et al., 2023 ), our results suggested that individual vulnerabilities and negative psychological factors—rather than environmental influences or positive psychological resources—drive the progression of IGD. In particular, younger age, prolonged gaming time, anxiety, sleep disturbances, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, and low prosocial behavior independently distinguished adolescents with worsening IGD trajectories from those with stable symptoms. These factors aligned well with prior research on the risk factors of IGD as well as the I-PACE model, collectively reflecting an interplay of neurodevelopmental, affective, and behavioral mechanisms that perpetuate IGD. Notably, many of these factors are modifiable, suggesting avenues for targeted interventions. Younger adolescents exhibited heightened vulnerability to IGD progression, likely due to neurodevelopmental immaturity in prefrontal regions governing motivation, impulsivity, and addiction ( Chambers, Taylor, &amp; Potenza, 2003 ). Prolonged gaming time emerged as a critical behavioral driver, fostering immersion—a state of intense focus that diminishes awareness of real-world consequences. Such immersion disrupts circadian rhythms, erodes self-regulatory capacity, and reinforces addictive patterns through dopamine-driven reward sensitization, creating a self-perpetuating cycle ( King &amp; Delfabbro, 2018 ; Marmet et al., 2023 ). Internalizing issues, such as anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation, contribute to IGD through maladaptive coping behaviors, where gaming becomes a mechanism to alleviate emotional distress temporarily. However, this compensatory use exacerbates emotional instability, as gaming-induced real-life responsibilities intensifies, creating a vicious cycle ( Brand et al., 2016 , 2019 ). Sleep disturbances can impair emotional regulation and cognitive function, exacerbating gaming behaviors due to increased susceptibility to impulsivity and diminished self-control ( Wei et al., 2025 ). Externalizing problems, such as conduct problems and hyperactivity/inattention is associated with reduced behavioral regulation and greater impulsivity, heightening the likelihood of IGD. Hyperactivity/inattention traits increase vulnerability by seeking immediate stimulation and rewards provided by gaming ( Thorell, Burén, Ström Wiman, Sandberg, &amp; Nutley, 2022 ). It further compounds risk by impairing sustained focus on non-gaming activities, increasing reliance on gaming for stimulation ( Thorell et al., 2022 ). Finally, low prosocial behavior, reflecting weaker social connections, could increase reliance on virtual interactions and online gaming as substitutes for real-world relationships ( Caplan, 2003 ). One particularly noteworthy observation is that adolescents in the High-Risk Decreasing group reported more baseline risk factors than the Moderate-Risk Increasing group, yet experienced diminishing IGD severity over time. This pattern echoes findings from Chinese young adults ( Qiao et al., 2024 ), suggesting that the mere presence of risk factors does not dictate an inevitable escalation of IGD symptoms. This finding might reflect the nature recovery course of IGD. Additionally, unmeasured individual traits—such as genetic, neurobiological, or personality factors—may help explain why some adolescents recover more rapidly, reinforcing the importance of personalized approaches to IGD prevention and intervention. Association between IGD trajectories and psychological outcomes Differential associations between IGD trajectories and subsequent mental health outcomes were evident. Adolescents in the Moderate-Risk Increasing trajectory exhibited significantly heightened risks for multiple mental health issues, aligning with previous findings ( Coyne et al., 2020 ). Regular monitoring and early interventions targeting adolescents with increasing IGD symptoms are therefore essential. By contrast, the High-Risk Decreasing group exhibited improvements in mental health outcomes, despite presenting initially heightened risk. These results suggest that reducing IGD severity may alleviate or at least partially attenuate associated psychological symptoms. Indeed, prior clinical trials have demonstrated that interventions for IGD, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, can concurrently lower depression and anxiety ( Y. Chen, Lu, Wang, &amp; Gao, 2023 ), although results have not always been consistent ( Lindenberg, Kindt, &amp; Szász-Janocha, 2022 ). Further large-scale trials are needed to better understand the broader effects of IGD interventions on mental health and its mechanism. The implications of the present study The findings of this study carry important clinical and academic implications. Clinically, the identification of distinct IGD trajectories highlights the need for personalized, rather than uniform, prevention and intervention strategies. The Moderate-Risk Increasing trajectory is particularly critical, as adolescents in this group—despite not having the highest initial symptom severity—experienced the most severe long-term mental health consequences. This underscores the importance of monitoring symptom progression to facilitate early intervention. Conversely, the High-Risk Decreasing trajectory suggests that some adolescents may recover naturally, supporting a stepped-care approach that avoids over-pathologization. These findings have direct implications for clinical practice. Assessment should move beyond static, cross-sectional evaluations and incorporate longitudinal monitoring to identify an adolescent's specific trajectory—whether escalation from moderate symptoms or remission from high-risk states. Screening efforts should thus capture not only symptom severity but also patterns of change and associated risk factors (e.g., gaming duration, anxiety, sleep problems) to detect those at risk of worsening trajectories. Intervention strategies should be tailored accordingly: adolescents on an escalating path may require intensive support targeting modifiable risk factors (e.g., excessive gaming, co-occurring mental health symptoms), while those showing natural recovery may benefit from reinforcement of protective factors or low-intensity monitoring. However, further research is needed to validate these approaches. Academically, this research contributes to a more nuanced, developmental psychopathology understanding of IGD, challenging monolithic views by demonstrating heterogeneous pathways. The low temporal stability of symptoms observed in our community sample raises critical questions about IGD's natural course—a particularly salient issue given its relatively short history of classification as a mental disorder ( Petry et al., 2014 ). Transient symptom patterns that frequently remit may not reach clinical significance, underscoring the need to distinguish between short-term maladaptive gaming behaviors and persistent, pathological cases. A further longitudinal investigation in clinical sample is essential. Strength and limitations This study's strengths include its large sample size, comprehensive assessment of multiple predictors, and advanced person-centered analytical approach. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the annual assessment interval may not capture shorter-term fluctuations in IGD severity. Transient shifts, episodic patterns, or acute stressor effects—potentially critical to understanding dynamic symptom changes—might be overlooked. The three-wave design primarily captured linear trajectories, potentially overlooking non-linear developmental patterns (e.g., quadratic changes linked to adolescent cognitive control, executive functioning, or emotion regulation abilities). Future studies with more frequent and extended assessments could elucidate these nuances. Second, although the sample size is substantial, it is limited to Chinese adolescents from a single city, potentially restricting the generalizability of the findings to other populations. All participants were students, excluding adolescents who dropped out of school, who may be at higher risk for IGD. In addition, this study examined IGD symptom trajectories in a general community sample of adolescent gamers, where most (73.0%) followed a Low-Risk Stable path and baseline IGD prevalence was low (3.1%). Thus, our findings primarily reflect symptom trajectories across a broad spectrum of gaming behaviors rather than clinical disorder progression. While this approach was chosen to understand varying levels of gaming behavior in a community setting, future studies focusing specifically on clinically diagnosed or high-risk populations are necessary to delineate disorder progression with greater precision in those specific groups. Third, while self-reported gaming time and the IGDS9-SF are standard measures, perceived gaming duration may not accurately reflect actual usage ( Jin, Kittaneh, Sidhu, &amp; Lechner, 2022 ). Additionally, this study did not assess game genres or gaming motivations, both of which may differentially influence IGD trajectories. For example, a recent longitudinal study suggests that First-Person Shooter games and Massively Multiplayer Online Role-Playing games are associated with a higher risk of IGD (X. Ye, Fong, &amp; Yip, 2025 ). Another large-scale study found that certain genres, such as adventure and sports games, exhibited a stronger association with IGD compared to music, card, or simulation games ( Oka, Kubo, Murakami, &amp; Kobayashi, 2024 ). Moreover, gaming motivations may play a critical role ( Larrieu, Fombouchet, Billieux, &amp; Decamps, 2023 ), as longitudinal research has shown that motivations related to escapism, coping, and competence predict IGD development ( Cudo, Kopiś-Posiej, &amp; Griffiths, 2023 ). Future research should incorporate objective usage tracking, genre-specific analyses, and motivational assessments to refine our understanding of the diverse pathways leading to IGD. Attrition-related differences, though minimal in effect size, may slightly underrepresent several subgroups, such as younger adolescents, 7th graders, and those with marginally elevated mental health risks. However, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings, and the negligible effect sizes suggest such attrition likely had limited impact on overall conclusions. Finally, our reliance on the DSM-5-based IGDS9-SF warrants consideration, as the DSM-5 criteria face critiques regarding diagnostic validity and differentiation from high engagement ( Castro-Calvo et al., 2021 ; Deleuze et al., 2017 ). Alternative frameworks, such as ICD-11, offer different perspectives by emphasizing functional impairment for “Gaming Disorder” and including the distinct category of “Hazardous Gaming”—a pattern of gaming that significantly increases the risk of harmful health consequences. Our DSM-5 based IGDS9-SF does not directly assess or differentiate these specific ICD-11 constructs. Future research could benefit from employing or comparing multiple diagnostic frameworks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of problematic gaming. Furthermore, our GMM analysis relied on a global IGD symptom score. While this approach is common for parsimoniously mapping overall symptom trajectories, it overlooks potential heterogeneity in individual symptom pathways. Future work using multi-latent class analysis or focusing on evolving symptom profiles could provide more granular insights into IGD development. Conclusion This study identified distinct IGD trajectories among Chinese adolescents, emphasizing that adolescents following a Moderate-Risk Increasing trajectory represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup at significant risk for adverse mental health outcomes. The findings underscore the necessity for ongoing monitoring and tailored interventions, highlighting modifiable factors such as gaming duration, anxiety, sleep issues, impaired prosocial behavior, conduct problems, and hyperactivity/inattention as critical targets. Addressing these factors may mitigate IGD severity and enhance adolescents' overall psychological well-being.
