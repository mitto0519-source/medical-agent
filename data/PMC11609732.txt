Abstract Aim The COVID‐19 pandemic has had negative physical and psychological impacts worldwide. However, there has been a lack of real‐world evidence concerning the predictors of severe psychological distress (SPD) among the general population in Japan during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The aim of this study was to examine predictors of SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Methods We investigated the predictors of new‐onset SPD in the general Japanese population using data from a large‐scale internet‐based cohort study. Results We included 16 489 study participants (age range = 16–81, mean age = 52.7, percentage of male = 50%) in the analysis. Over the course of 1 year from baseline, the estimated proportion of participants who experienced SPD was 5.2% with inverse probability weighting. The predictors of SPD included younger age, being never married, being unemployed, having a higher education background, scoring higher on the Fear of Coronavirus‐19 Scale, experiencing more adverse childhood experiences, reporting poorer subjective health status, and COVID‐19 with oxygen therapy. Our internet‐based survey of the Japanese population may have selection bias, limiting the generalizability to other countries and cultures. Conclusion This study revealed that being afflicted with COVID‐19 requiring oxygen therapy is the most significant predictor of SPD. In addition, we found that vulnerability to social isolation, such as never being unmarried, anxiety toward COVID‐19, and susceptibility to stress, are predictors of the emergence of SPD. Therefore, the implementation of online support systems and ensuring access to accurate information may protect against SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic in Japan. Results from a pre‐pandemic prior study that analyzed a sample of 201 116 participants from the 2016 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions in Japan indicated a prevalence of SPD (a score of ≥13 on the K6) of 3.9% in the Japanese general population. 32 Under the COVID‐19 pandemic, the higher incidence rate of SPD in the Japanese general population has been suggested to be linked to the psychosocial changes brought about by the pandemic, potentially impacting long‐term psychological distress. We also found that the need for oxygen therapy during COVID‐19 treatment is a predictive factor for the subsequent development of SPD. In accordance with the COVID‐19 treatment guidelines, if a patient's respiratory function is compromised, hospitalization and oxygen therapy are necessary. 33 Therefore, patients receiving oxygen therapy during treatment are considered to have a higher severity of illness. 33 Individuals with severe cases of COVID‐19 are more likely to exhibit long‐COVID symptoms, including psychological symptoms, and particularly anxiety and depression, with a higher incidence in the long phase compared to physical symptoms as reported. 34 According to recent meta‐analyses, it has been discovered that being in a more severe condition during acute infection is associated with a higher risk of long‐term sequelae of COVID‐19, including conditions such as depression and PTSD. 35 We found that subjective health status assessment can also predict the risk of SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. It has been reported that as subjective physical health worsens, there is an increased likelihood of heightened anxiety and concerns about contracting COVID‐19, and as psychological distress increases, there is a greater likelihood of downplaying or denying the pandemic. 36 This suggests that psychological care for patients with respiratory infections requiring oxygen administration or with worsening subjective physical health may be an effective means of preventing subsequent SPD. Interestingly, our research findings reveal that the greater the adversity childhood experiences, the higher the risk of experiencing SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Childhood adversity can encompass various adverse childhood experiences, such as abuse, neglect, household dysfunction, or traumatic events during childhood. 37 These experiences can have profound and lasting effects on an individual's mental and emotional well‐being, shaping their coping mechanisms and vulnerability to stressors in adulthood. According to the sensitization hypothesis, individuals who have previously been exposed to traumatic events might react more severely to additional stressors due to heightened sensitivity to stress or depleted coping capacities. 38 , 39 This suggests that the psychological scars of childhood adversity can remain dormant and resurface in response to important life stressors, such as a severe respiratory infection like COVID‐19. A meta‐analysis that was based on 68 studies indicated that previous exposure to a traumatic event was related to an elevated risk for PTSD following a later trauma. 40 A longitudinal study revealed that traumatized individuals with PTSD were at a higher risk for PTSD than were individuals with no history of trauma exposure. 41 For this reason, support focusing on past traumatic experiences, including ACEs, may be necessary during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Furthermore, we have revealed that participants at higher risk of social isolation, such as those who are never married, are more prone to experiencing SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. It has been reported from previous research that being unmarried is a risk factor for social isolation‐related depression. 42 Findings from another study have emphasized the need to be mindful of the potential negative impacts of long‐term social isolation on conditions such as depression and anxiety. 43 Reduced social interactions and loneliness are well‐known risk factors for psychopathology 44 and might increase individuals' vulnerability, particularly under conditions of exposure to stressors. We also found that individuals who have never been married are at a higher risk of developing SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The association between marital status and post‐COVID‐19 depression highlights the potential influence of social support, emotional stability, and coping mechanisms in the face of important life stressors. 45 Marriage often provides a source of emotional support, companionship, and a sense of belonging, which can play a protective role against mental health challenges. Married individuals may have access to a support system that can offer encouragement and understanding during difficult times, including the recovery period after a severe respiratory infection such as COVID‐19. This a social support network can play a crucial role in reducing feelings of isolation and anxiety. A previous study suggests that promising interventions may include cognitive modification for subjective social isolation and interventions with mixed strategies and supported socialization for objective social isolation. 46 From our perspective, based on previous research, it appears that online interventions could be beneficial for psychological distress related to social isolation caused by the COVID‐19 pandemic lockdown, regardless of age group. 47 , 48 In addition, we found that higher scores on the Fear of Coronavirus‐19 Scale (FCV‐19S) 21 were predictor of SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Higher scores on the FCV‐19S suggest a heightened fear of the virus, which can manifest in various forms of psychological distress, such as anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder. 21 This finding underscores the necessity of prioritizing mental health services and preventative measures alongside ongoing physical health efforts in managing the effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic. The FCV‐19S score has been reported to be associated with intolerance of uncertainty and depression. 49 Intolerance of uncertainty refers to an inability to tolerate negative uncertain situations, leading to an overestimation of threats and a predisposition to the development of psychopathological symptoms. Intolerance of uncertainty has been identified as a predictor of anxiety and depression, particularly among young individuals. 50 Additionally, attention must also be paid to the “infodemic” caused by the proliferation of uncertain and false information. 51 , 52 Especially with information overload through social media, it has been reported that emotional reactions toward COVID‐19 are triggered, increasing the risk of depression. 53 Furthermore, it is understood that the longer the general population is exposed to COVID‐19‐related news, the higher the likelihood of developing symptoms of depression. 54 Therefore, maintaining psychological distance from excessive information about the pandemic and scrutinizing the accuracy of information may have the potential to reduce negative emotional responses, including fear of COVID‐19. On the other hand, it has been reported that eliminating uncertainty from the fear of COVID‐19 will contribute to reducing depression, anxiety, and stress and increasing positivity. 55 Therefore, we considered that the dissemination of accurate information about the pandemic would be crucial for the prevention of anxiety and depression. Surprisingly, our study revealed an intriguing finding that the general population with higher education levels was more prone to developing SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic in Japan. A previous study reported that a higher educational level seemed to have a protective effect against anxiety and depression 56 , 57 ; however, this may not be generalizable under the outbreak of emerging infectious diseases. Under the COVID‐19 pandemic, it has been reported that better‐educated individuals experience higher levels of subjective psychological distress during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which is consistent with our findings. 58 People with higher education may be more prone to distress, possibly due to their heightened self‐awareness of health issues. 59 The disruptions caused by the pandemic, such as job losses or financial insecurity, may have a more substantial toll on their mental well‐being, leading to an increased risk of depression. Additionally, societal expectations and pressure on individuals with higher education levels may also play a role. However, further research is needed to establish a causal relationship between education level and SPD during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Interestingly, being unemployed was a protective factor for SPD. It is possible that the pressure of being forced to work during a pandemic of emerging infectious disease may have affected SPD. In a previous study, workers reported increased stress due to changes in the working environment caused by the COVID‐19 pandemic. 60 Increased occupational stress during a COVID‐19 epidemic is of particular concern among healthcare workers. 61 On the other hand, contact with COVID‐19 patients has been reported to cause psychological distress even among nonhealthcare workers. 62 In our study, participants in the unemployment group were able to exclude occupational stressors related to COVID‐19, which may have resulted in a decrease in SPD. However, since social isolation and economic insecurity associated with job loss have been reported to be exacerbating factors for SPD, 63 further research on the relationship between employment status and SPD is needed. 4.1 Limitations Our study had several limitations. First, it was conducted with a primary focus on Japanese residents, making it uncertain whether the findings can be extrapolated to other countries and cultures. Second, our reliance on internet‐based surveys may have introduced selection bias into our data. Third, despite our efforts to account for potential confounding variables, the presence of unidentified or residual confounders could have influenced our study outcomes. Finally, this study investigated the risk of participants without a history of psychiatric disorders developing severe psychological distress after newly contracting COVID‐19. Since psychological distress can be influenced by mental illnesses such as depression and alcohol dependence, participants with a history of these conditions were excluded from the analysis. While this reduces the heterogeneity of the study population, it was considered that selection bias might be introduced as a result. 5 CONCLUSIONS Our study revealed that being afflicted with COVID‐19 requiring oxygen therapy is the most significant predictor of SPD. As a result, the importance of psychological support as one aspect of the COVID‐19 pandemic has come to the forefront. In addition, it has also been found that vulnerability to social isolation, anxiety toward COVID‐19, and susceptibility to stress are predictors of the emergence of SPD. Consequently, the establishment of online support systems and access to accurate information were deemed desirable public health initiatives to address these factors.
