ABSTRACT In recent years, post‐secondary students' mental health has become an important public health concern. However, studies examining protective factors of mental health among students and during challenging times are limited. Guided by the strength‐based Resilience Portfolio Model and following a group of undergraduates ( N = 1004) throughout the 2020/2021 academic year, this study examined multiple domains of resilience internal assets and external resources and simultaneously tested multiple protective mechanisms for student mental health using structural equation modelling. Results provided support for insulating effects: both internal assets such as emotion regulation and external recourses such as social network supportiveness and cultural fit in university (i.e., perceived congruity between students' personal and cultural selves and their university environment) were associated with reducing academic stress which in turn promoted student mental health at the end of the academic year. There was also support for additive effects: greater cultural fit in university was also directly related to better end‐of‐year student mental health. We compared a model in which regression paths were constrained to be equal between female and male students with a model in which regression paths were freely estimated. A non‐significant difference in deviance statistics (52.37, df = 39, p = 0.075) suggested that the model represented female and male students equally well. We also compared a model with equality constraints between students who self‐identified as visible minority and those who did not with a freely estimated model, and found a significant difference in deviance statistics (69.80, df = 39, p = 0.002). Upon further inspection, we found that most of the model represented the two groups equally well, while the additive effect of emotion regulation differed by minority status (Wald test = 7.02, df = 1, p = 0.008). Among students who were not visible minority, emotion regulation negatively predicted T4 psychological distress ( β = −0.10, p = 0.024); the same was not found for visible minority students ( β = 0.05, p = 0.359). 4 Discussion Guided by the strength‐based Resilience Portfolio Model (Grych, Hamby, and Banyard 2015 ), this study extended prior research on protective factors and mechanisms for university students' mental health by simultaneously examining multiple domains of protective resilience assets and resources and testing multiple protective mechanisms in the same model. As suggested by the Resilience Portfolio Model, our results showed that both internal assets and external resources were important for reducing stress and promoting healthy functioning. Moreover, our results showed that different protective factors could be involved in the same or different protective mechanisms at different stages of the resilience process. 4.1 Effects of Protective Assets and Resources on Academic Stress and Mental Health Although additive effects are commonly documented in the literature (Grych, Hamby, and Banyard 2015 ), in our study, the only protective factor showing an additive effect on mental health for our sample as a whole is cultural fit in university. Controlling for start‐of‐the‐year mental health, greater perceived cultural congruity in the university context predicted increased subjective well‐being at the end of the academic year. It is possible that students who saw themselves as belonging in university were less likely to feel alienated culturally and more satisfied with their experience, thus may be more likely to thrive. In previous studies, cultural fit in university was found to be directly associated with academic outcomes and psychological well‐being among Latino and Native American university students (Chee, Shorty, and Robinson Kurpius 2019 ; Gloria et al. 2009 ). Moreover, when campus culture emphasised being part of a community and learning and working together, first‐generation university students perceived academic tasks as less difficult, and they showed similar performance compared with their continuing‐generation peers (Stephens et al. 2012 ). Our findings suggest that greater perceived cultural fit in university may boost student subjective well‐being even in the general student population. When examining different groups, we also found an additive effect of emotion regulation, though only for university students who were not members of visible minority groups. For these students, better emotion regulation predicted decreased psychological distress at the end of the academic year; for visible minority students, emotion regulation was not directly associated with end‐of‐the‐year psychological distress. Our findings suggest that improving students' emotion regulation and increasing their sense of cultural fit in university may promote different aspects of student mental health for different groups of students. We found three of the five domains of the resilience portfolio to have insulating effects on academic stress. Better emotion regulation, more supportive social network, and greater cultural fit in the university predicted less perceived academic stress averaged across the academic year. The association between emotion regulation and academic stress has also been seen in intervention studies. For instance, participating in a brief mindfulness‐based intervention reduced emotion regulation difficulties and in turn resulted in decreased academic stress in a group of trauma‐exposed university students (Cherry and Wilcox 2020 ). In addition, when surrounded by caring and supportive social networks including families, friends and university instructors and fellow classmates, university students may perceive their academic demands to be more manageable and less stressful knowing that support will be there when they need it. However, university students who feel that their own background is incompatible with the university environment may feel uncomfortable in higher education settings and face more academic barriers than those who ‘belong’. Given that higher levels of average academic stress predicted increased psychological distress and decreased subjective well‐being at the end of the academic year, it is important to prevent students from feeling academically overwhelmed. Our findings suggest emotion regulation, social support and cultural congruity as potential targets of preventive interventions for reducing student academic stress. When other protective mechanisms and domains of resilience portfolio were considered in the same model, our results found no evidence to support the buffering effects or the protective effects of extraversion and reflective tendency. This suggests that protective assets and resources considered in our study mainly operate through additive and insulating effects to promote healthy functioning. This is not to reject the buffering effects. Review of literature on suicidality shows that positive attributional style and agency buffer the effect of stress on suicidality (J. Johnson et al. 2011 ). It is possible that other protective factors not included in this study play buffering role for other types of stress or healthy functioning. In addition, extraversion as an indicator of interpersonal strengths was not associated with university students' stress and healthy functioning. However, we believe that interpersonal strengths matter. Other interpersonal strengths such as gratitude or forgiveness that are important for relationship maintenance after initiation may be more closely related to early adversities, stress and healthy functioning (Grych, Hamby, and Banyard 2015 ). Moreover, reflective tendency as an indicator of meaning‐making strengths was not associated with student academic stress or mental health, but was positively associated with adverse life experience. It is possible that students with more adversities in life engage in more meaning‐making, but in both positive and negative forms, such as reflection and rumination. Reflection was found to be more adaptive for mental health, whereas rumination had the opposite effect (Takano and Tanno 2009 ). Exploring potential buffering effects of other assets and resources and examining other indicators of interpersonal and meaning‐making strengths are important directions for future research on the resilience process. Our study is novel in that we utilised longitudinal data to simultaneously test protective mechanisms proposed by the Resilience Portfolio Model. Our study also complemented other empirical studies guided by the Model. For instance, we extended the application of the Model which was first developed for a better understanding of resilience after experiencing violence and trauma (Grych, Hamby, and Banyard 2015 ). Thus, many previous studies examined the Model in victimised or highly disadvantaged samples, with most of the samples situated in the US (e.g., Hamby et al. 2020 ; L. Johnson 2024 ). Based on a large representative sample of university students with relatively low levels of victimization growing up, we gained valuable insights into student healthy functioning during a global pandemic through the lens of the Resilience Portfolio Model. This suggests that the Model may be adopted in a variety of different settings. Moreover, our findings and results from previous empirical research support the Model's notion that different protective factors may function differently depending on types of contexts and/or stress (Grych, Hamby, and Banyard 2015 ). We found that better emotion regulation, higher social network supportiveness and greater cultural fit in university were most salient at reducing student stress and improving their healthy functioning. Emotion regulation and social support have been identified as important protective factors in some previous studies of the Resilience Portfolio Model. However, a study with Spanish adolescents found that greater emotion regulation was associated with lower post‐traumatic growth (Gonzalez‐Mendez, Ramírez‐Santana, and Hamby 2021 ), whereas a study with participants from the Appalachian region of the US found few associations between social support and psychological well‐being and mental health (Hamby, Grych, and Banyard 2018 ). This suggests that it is useful to use the Model as a conceptual framework, but it is also important to consider situation and population specific protective factors. 4.2 Adverse Life Experience and Protective Assets and Resources We found adverse life experience in childhood and adolescence, including history of abuse, negative life events and experience of victimization, to be associated with four of the five domains of the resilience portfolio. According to the Resilience Portfolio Model (Grych, Hamby, and Banyard 2015 ), the resilience process is set in motion by exposure to stress, and exposure to manageable amounts of stress early in life may promote the development of protective resilience assets and resources and inoculate individuals against future stress. However, although we found several significant nonlinear associations between adverse life experience and protective factors, our findings did not support the inoculation effects. In general, protective assets and resources were negatively impacted by exposure to adverse life experience growing up. In addition to linear associations, there were curvilinear associations between adverse life experience growing up and emotion regulation, reflective tendency and social network supportiveness. We plotted the results and found that emotion regulation decreased and reflective tendency increased when adverse life experience increased, and the decrease in emotion regulation and increase in reflective tendency plateaued with more extreme adverse life experience (e.g., greater than 1 standard deviation above the sample mean). Moreover, social network supportiveness deceased to a greater extent with when adverse life experience increased to the extreme. These results were not in line with the inoculation effects, but they were consistent with findings of linear dose‐response relations between stressors and outcomes (Hamby et al. 2021 ). Thus, our findings suggest that, overall, the less adverse life experience students endure, the more protective assets and resources they are likely to possess. 4.3 Limitations and Future Directions Despite the strengths, our study also has several limitations. First, although our study considered all five domains of resilience assets and resources and four protective mechanisms proposed in the Resilience Portfolio Model, we were unable to include coping. Coping is another important component of the Resilience Portfolio Model. However, coping is complex: coping responses vary across individuals, and individuals vary in their coping responses across situations; a particular coping response may be adaptive in one situation but maladaptive in another (Carver and Connor‐Smith 2010 ). It is beyond the scope of this study to include an in‐depth examination of how various coping responses are associated with other components of the Resilience Portfolio Model. Future research may consider examining how different coping strategies are related to stress, protective factors and healthy functioning. In addition, we only selected one indicator for each domain of the resilience portfolio. Some argue that personality is stable over time and not to be considered as strengths. However, a recent meta‐analysis suggests that personality traits, such as neuroticism and extraversion, could change after interventions (Roberts et al. 2017 ). Thus, some personality traits could be considered as modifiable individual characteristics. Nevertheless, there are a wide array of other internal assets and strengths and external resources that may be explored in future studies. Some promising protective factors from prior research (e.g., sense of purpose, psychological endurance) were not included in the study. It would be interesting for future research to test whether our findings hold in the presence of these well‐established protective factors. Moreover, assessment of adverse life experiences included in our study was retrospective and potentially affected by recall bias. From a developmental perspective, a longitudinal design spanning multiple life stages may help better understand how early adversities influence the development of assets and resources and whether different types of life adversities are associated with different assets and resources. Last, our study followed students across an entire academic year during the pandemic when courses were delivered almost entirely online. Although this context was appropriate for testing a model of resilience, generalisability to the post‐pandemic academic experience is unknown. Protective factors or mechanisms may operate differently for other cohorts of students studying in hybrid format or in‐person at different points in time. 4.4 Conclusion Our study is one of the first to simultaneously examine multiple protective assets and resources and protective mechanisms proposed by the Resilience Portfolio Model, and it is the first application of longitudinal data to the Resilience Portfolio Model framework. Taken together, our findings suggest that minimising early life adversities (e.g., abuse, victimization, difficulties in life) facilitates the development of important protective resilience factors, and both internal assets such as emotion regulation and external resources such as social network supportiveness and cultural fit in university help reduce a key stressor in university, academic stress, which in turn promotes university student healthy functioning. At the institutional level, creating an inclusive university community may help reduce student academic stress, lower student psychological distress and improve student subjective well‐being.
