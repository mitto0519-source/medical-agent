Abstract Aim Priorities for public health interventions may vary depending on demographic characteristics. This study investigated sex‐ and age‐specific associations of diabetes‐related metabolic and lifestyle risk factors in middle‐aged and older adults. Methods Using long‐term data from the community‐based cohort of the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study, sex‐ and age‐specific sub‐cohorts were constructed at ages 45, 55 and 65 years. As diabetes‐related risk factors, five metabolic factors (waist circumference [WC], mean arterial pressure, triglycerides [TG], high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol and alanine aminotransferase) and five lifestyle factors (sleep duration, alcohol consumption, cumulative smoking, physical activity and dietary diversity score) were considered. The associations between these risk factors and incident diabetes were estimated for each sex‐ and age‐specific sub‐cohort. Additionally, hierarchical Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression was used to determine the relative importance of individual risk factors in the development of diabetes and to quantify their joint association. Results The incidence of diabetes ranged from 8.8% to 13.4% across the sub‐cohorts. Overall, metabolic risk factors—particularly WC and TG—appeared to play a more prominent role in the development of incident diabetes than lifestyle‐related factors. Among lifestyle factors, alcohol consumption and cumulative smoking showed an association with the development of diabetes only in women. The estimated collective association of the 10 risk factors with incident diabetes ranged from 0.39 to 0.46 in men and from 0.26 to 0.45 in women, with relatively weaker association observed in older women. The ranking of relative importance of individual risk factors varied depending on sex and age sub‐cohorts. Furthermore, no clear evidence of nonlinearity or potential interactions in the association was observed (Figures S5 and S6 ). 4 DISCUSSION This study aimed to construct sub‐cohorts stratified by sex and age using long‐term follow‐up cohort data and to explore potential differences in the influence of risk factors. In addition, this study applied BKMR, which is suitable for analysing multiple correlated exposures by accounting for nonlinearity or potential interactions, to derive results on the collective association and the relative importance of the 10 selected risk factors. The findings suggest that, overall, metabolic risk factors may play a more prominent role in the development of incident diabetes compared to lifestyle‐related factors across the defined sub‐cohorts. Notably, the relative ranking of risk factor contributions appeared to vary by sex, even within comparable age groups, suggesting that sex‐specific factors may influence diabetes risk. Identifying age‐specific risk factors for tailored primary prevention has been highlighted in several epidemiological studies. A cohort study involving 19.5 million Korean adults found a positive association between the number of metabolic syndrome components and the 5‐year risk of developing diabetes, with this relationship appearing to be more pronounced in younger adults (&lt;40 years). 13 Similarly, a large longitudinal study conducted in China, which focused on age at diabetes onset, found that abdominal obesity and unfavourable metabolic profiles were associated with diabetes risk across all age groups, with the most pronounced effect observed among young adults. 14 These patterns were further supported by findings from the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort Study. 15 While the incidence of diabetes generally increases with age, age‐related deterioration of metabolic function may contribute to heterogeneous effects on disease development. Indeed, the mean levels of metabolic risk factors have been shown to vary with age. In addition to physiological changes associated with aging, socioeconomic and behavioural factors may also influence these variations. 15 Moreover, the birth cohort effect—which is inherently tied to age at baseline—may also influence disease risk, potentially reflecting generational differences in sociocultural and environmental exposures. 15 To address possible bias arising from such cohort effects, this study constructed age‐specific sub‐cohorts at multiple time points. Findings from this study suggest that metabolic and lifestyle factors exhibit differing joint associations with diabetes incidence across age groups. In particular, the relatively weaker collective association observed in older women may imply the presence of additional, unmeasured risk factors that contribute to diabetes development in this group. 9 Furthermore, the observed attenuation in the effect sizes of metabolic factors among elderly individuals appeared to vary by sex, which may reflect the distributions of metabolic factors according to both sex and age. Despite this attenuation, the high prevalence of unfavourable metabolic profiles in older populations underscores the continued importance of addressing modifiable metabolic risk factors in diabetes prevention efforts. Although various pathophysiological, psychological and behavioural differences between men and women are well‐established, 1 , 21 the assessment of sex‐specific risk factors for diabetes remains relatively limited. A population‐based cohort study from Central Europe reported that, in addition to BMI, HDL‐C and parental history of diabetes, factors such as SBP, regular smoking and high alcohol consumption (≥40 g/day) were associated with diabetes only in men, whereas uric acid levels and physical inactivity were linked to diabetes risk only in women. 8 A study of Japanese patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease also found a negative association between the AST‐to‐ALT ratio and diabetes, with the significant relationship observed only in men, but not in women. 36 Another study based on machine learning analysis using UK Biobank data identified BMI and GGT as major predictors of diabetes in men, whereas WC and serum uric acid were key predictors in women, in addition to glycaemic measures and family history. 37 Despite the presence of several such studies, the diversity in the range of risk factors considered limits direct comparisons across findings. Ectopic fat accumulation and sex steroid hormones are thought to play a crucial role in the sex‐dimorphic susceptibility to diabetes, particularly in women, who are known to have an increased cardiometabolic risk profile due to oestrogen deficiency and fat redistribution following menopause. 6 , 21 , 38 Indeed, the increase in mean WC in women with age was more pronounced than in men, with notable variability in MAP and TG. Behavioural sex differences in smoking and alcohol consumption were also observed. Furthermore, socioeconomic and psychosocial factors may contribute to sex‐ and age‐related differences in diabetes incidence. Socioeconomic conditions may have varied across generations, and women have generally been exposed to less favourable socioeconomic environments and have been more vulnerable to adverse psychosocial conditions than men. 21 , 39 Even though this study constructed age‐specific cohorts to avoid potential birth cohort effects, differences in educational attainment still emerged by sex and age, with a higher proportion of older women having lower levels of education. Socioeconomic factors may also influence lifestyle‐related risk factors. 40 Consequently, further research is needed to examine diabetes risk factors from a sex‐specific perspective, which could enable more tailored and effective interventions. In line with previous findings, the present study found that the relative contribution of metabolic risk clusters to diabetes incidence appeared to be prominent, while no clear evidence of nonlinearity or interactions among these factors was observed. Among the individual metabolic risk factors, WC and TG emerged as relatively important factors in the development of diabetes, although the strength of their associations varied by sex and age. WC, a proxy for visceral adiposity, has been shown to have a stronger association with diabetes and cardiovascular risk than BMI. 4 , 13 A study conducted in the United States reported that for every 1SD increase in WC, the risk of diabetes increased by 1.64 times in men and 1.80 times in women. 4 The findings of this study underscore the potential importance of effectively managing abdominal obesity, as WC consistently emerged to be a key risk factor across sex and age groups. Although various lipid profile markers—total cholesterol, HDL‐C, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol and TG—have been evaluated in relation to diabetes risk, findings across studies remain inconsistent. Both hypo‐HDL‐cholesterolaemia and hypertriglyceridaemia are recognized components of atherogenic dyslipidaemia associated with insulin resistance. 28 These factors have also been concurrently examined as potential predictors of diabetes risk in studies such as the Framingham Offspring Study 26 and the Chin‐Shan Community Cardiovascular Cohort Study. 41 In this study, TG demonstrated a more robust association with incident diabetes than HDL‐C, supporting previous research. 14 In that study, although sex‐specific risks were not reported, each 1SD increase in TG was associated with a 1.18 to 1.23‐fold higher risk of diabetes, depending on age of onset. Interestingly, the sex difference in average level of WC and TG appeared to diminish with advancing age, which is consistent with previous findings. 28 , 38 Nonetheless, the positive associations of both WC and TG with diabetes risk remained relatively stable across sub‐cohorts, suggesting these factors may exert consistent effects irrespective of sex and age. Meanwhile, the associations of MAP and ALT with incident diabetes appeared to show some heterogeneity. Elevated ALT levels may reflect impaired insulin signalling rather than purely hepatocellular injury. 42 Given the central role of liver in glucose and lipid metabolism, numerous epidemiological studies have explored the relationship between liver enzymes and diabetes risk, but substantial heterogeneity along with potential publication bias have been noted. 23 In addition, as liver enzyme levels can be influenced by various factors—including medications, alcohol consumption, autoimmune, metabolic or hereditary conditions—these factors should be considered collectively when assessing diabetes risk. Although several studies have suggested that elevated blood pressure may predict diabetes onset, 14 , 25 , 26 others have not supported this association. 41 MAP, which represents the average blood pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, has been assessed in a few Chinese cohort studies. 43 , 44 These studies reported potential variations in the MAP and diabetes association according to sex and age. Interestingly, in line with this study, the association was more pronounced in women, while no significant association was observed in either sex after age 60. 43 , 44 Sex steroid hormones influence sex‐specific diabetes susceptibility, with effects that may vary with aging. 21 Age‐related physiological changes, such as reduced insulin secretion and β‐cell sensitivity, may partly explain the diminished impact of metabolic risk factors on diabetes. 15 Nevertheless, the current body of evidence remains limited, indicating a need for further investigation into the role of MAP in diabetes development. Regarding lifestyle risk factors, while alcohol consumption and smoking showed some association with the development of diabetes in women, no consistent or substantial contribution of lifestyle factors to diabetes incidence was observed overall. Sex differences in the association between alcohol consumption and diabetes risk have been reported, 45 potentially due to a higher prevalence of heavy episodic drinking among men and sex‐specific effects of alcohol on glucose metabolism. 6 Additionally, these associations may vary across ethnic groups, 45 warranting further investigation. Several previous studies have also failed to demonstrate a clear association between lifestyle factors and the development of diabetes. 15 , 37 , 41 A multinational cohort study involving populations from Korea, Japan and the United Kingdom also reported that anthropometric measurements, glycaemic traits, liver enzyme levels and age were more strongly associated with diabetes onset than lifestyle factors. 46 Lifestyle factors may interact with one another, and unfavourable lifestyle—poor diet, physical inactivity, smoking, alcohol consumption and insufficient sleep duration—can contribute to obesity and poor metabolism. 7 , 28 , 47 Therefore, lifestyle factors may influence diabetes risk indirectly by worsening metabolic profiles rather than having a direct causal role. 15 Indeed, several significant associations between lifestyle and metabolic risk factors were identified (Table S1 ). Ultimately, given the strong association between obesity, unfavourable metabolic factors and diabetes risk, it seems reasonable to target modifiable lifestyle behaviours as a preventive strategy to mitigate these obesity and metabolic risks. Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting this study. First, the findings were derived from two community‐based cohorts, which enhances internal validity but may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, the study assessed only a limited set of risk factors, and potential measurement errors in metabolic factors could have influenced the results. Additionally, dietary factors and physical activity were assessed only at baseline and may not accurately reflect behaviours at the time of observation, potentially affecting their associations with diabetes risk. Information obtained through questionnaires may be subject to recall bias, and the underreporting of unfavourable health behaviours could have led to an underestimation of their associations with diabetes risk. Finally, although this study considered a wide range of covariates, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors, such as medication use or disease history, cannot be completely ruled out. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights by evaluating sex‐ and age‐specific risk factors for diabetes, revealing differences in the relative importance of these factors across subgroups. These findings suggest that risk factors may need to be prioritized differently based on demographic characteristics, providing useful guidance for targeted first‐line interventions. In addition, advanced statistical methods such as BKMR enabled the exploration of nonlinear relationships and potential interactions among risk factors, allowing the quantification of their collective association with diabetes risk. To minimize cohort effects and better capture age‐related variation, age‐specific cohorts were constructed, enabling a more nuanced assessment of age‐specific diabetes risk. In summary, the findings of this study underscore the importance of considering sex‐ and age‐specific priorities in strategies for diabetes prevention and intervention. For example, among individuals under 50 years of age, central obesity may be more important to target in men, while elevated TG levels may warrant greater attention in women. While obesity and unfavourable metabolic profiles appear to play a greater role in diabetes development, addressing these risks may require a comprehensive approach targeting clusters of unhealthy lifestyle behaviours. Furthermore, exploring age‐specific risk factors beyond traditional ones may be necessary, especially in older women.
