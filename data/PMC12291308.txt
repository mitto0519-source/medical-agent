Correction: Psychosocial support during pregnancy: perceptions and expectations among pregnant teenagers in Ahvaz, Iran-a qualitative content analysis 22 30 8 2025 154 Reproductive Health 10.1186/s12978-025-02134-7 PMC12398985 40886012 Background Teenage pregnancy remains a major global concern, with profound effects on young mothers and their families. Understanding the specific needs and expectations of pregnant adolescents regarding psychosocial support is crucial for developing effective interventions. This study explores how pregnant adolescents perceive and what they expect from psychosocial support during pregnancy. Methods A qualitative study was conducted in Ahvaz, Iran, from 2023 to 2024. Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure maximum diversity and included pregnant adolescents, family members, and healthcare providers. Data were gathered through 30 semi-structured, in-depth interviews and analyzed using conventional qualitative content analysis with MAXQDA 2020 software. To enhance validity, member checking was employed, allowing participants to verify the accuracy of the interpretations. Reliability was ensured by applying a consistent coding framework across all interviews. Results The analysis revealed three major themes: Challenges and Burdens– Participants reported societal stigma, emotional instability, limited family support, autonomy conflicts, and health issues, highlighting a disconnect between societal expectations and their lived realities. Positive Experiences and Adaptations– Despite challenges, many adolescents described resilience, the joy of motherhood, emotional bonding, personal growth, and improved spousal relationships. These experiences underscore the importance of strengths-based psychosocial support. Support and Intervention Needs– Participants emphasized the need for tailored education, active family involvement, and culturally sensitive healthcare to provide holistic care for pregnant adolescents. Conclusions These findings highlight the urgent need for culturally tailored interventions that address the unique developmental and psychosocial needs of adolescent mothers. Effective strategies should include targeted education, accessible counseling, active family engagement, and specialized training for healthcare providers. Implementing a comprehensive, strengths-based support framework is essential to promote the well-being and resilience of this vulnerable population. This study explores the experiences of pregnant teenage girls in Ahvaz, Iran, with a focus on the challenges they face and the support they require. Teenage pregnancy often brings emotional, social, and economic difficulties that can adversely affect both the young mother and her baby’s well-being. Many of the adolescents in this study expressed a pressing need for emotional and social support to help them navigate the pressures of pregnancy. The findings indicate that pregnant teenagers frequently experience anxiety, depression, and limited social support. Despite these challenges, many also reported positive developments, including personal growth and strengthened family relationships. Support from spouses and family members emerged as a critical factor in alleviating stress and enhancing the self-confidence of these young mothers. The study underscores the importance of developing support programs specifically tailored to the unique needs of pregnant adolescents. Providing pre-pregnancy education and access to psychological counseling can significantly enhance their quality of life. The findings also emphasize the need for coordinated efforts among families, communities, and healthcare providers to promote better health outcomes for both the adolescents and their babies, while fostering more positive prospects for their futures. Overall, this research shows that meeting the emotional and social needs of pregnant adolescents is vital for promoting their reproductive health and improving pregnancy outcomes. Pregnancy and parenting during this critical period can disrupt normal developmental processes. The concurrent biological transitions of adolescence and pregnancy may heighten psychological and physical vulnerabilities, increasing the risk of mood disorders. Research indicates that adolescent mothers experience higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress compared to their older counterparts [ 1 , 2 ]. Several studies have identified key challenges faced by pregnant adolescents, including perinatal and parental stress, fear of loss, diminished self-esteem, financial hardship, and inadequate material and emotional support from parents and partners. Stigmatization by healthcare providers further compounds these difficulties [ 3 ]. Adolescent pregnancy also poses broader challenges for families and public care systems, including economic, educational, health, and social costs, and may impede progress toward the Millennium Development Goals. Nevertheless, some studies have noted potential positive outcomes. These include strengthened family relationships, increased happiness, fulfillment of traditional roles, and the development of maturity, responsibility, independence, and a sense of purpose and self-esteem in adolescent mothers [ 2 ]. Globally, adolescent pregnancy remains a significant public health concern. According to the latest WHO statistics, the global adolescent pregnancy rate stands at 10.3% [ 4 ]. The highest number of births among teenage mothers has been recorded in East Asia (95,153) and West Africa (70,423) [ 5 ]. Annually, approximately 15 million adolescents give birth, representing about 11% of all births worldwide [ 6 ]. Furthermore, WHO data from 2019 indicate that adolescents aged 15 to 19 in low- and middle-income countries experience an estimated 21 million pregnancies each year, nearly half of which are unintended. These unintended pregnancies result in approximately 12 million live births annually. However, comprehensive data on childbirth among girls aged 10 to 14 remain scarce [ 7 ]. In Iran, a country with a notably young demographic profile, the adolescent fertility rate has seen a marked increase. Between 2010 and 2011, the age-specific fertility rate among adolescents rose from 25 to 35 per 1,000 individuals. Projections suggest that this trend will continue, with a significant rise in the fertility rate among Iran’s young population anticipated by 2025 [ 2 ]. Early marriage is identified as one of the primary drivers of adolescent pregnancy. Globally, it is estimated that between 2010 and 2030, approximately 130 million early marriages will occur [ 8 ]. By 2021, the total number of individuals in early marriages worldwide had reached an estimated 650 million [ 7 ]. Despite international efforts, the current rate of progress remains insufficient to achieve the Sustainable Development Goal of ending child marriage by 2030 [ 6 ]. In Iran, statistics indicate that around 36% of registered marriages involve individuals under the age of 19 [ 9 ]. Teenage pregnancy and child marriage are particularly pressing issues in regions such as Ahvaz, where cultural and socio-economic factors contribute to their prevalence. Ahvaz, as a microcosm of Iran’s richly diverse and multi-ethnic society, is characterized by significant ethnic and linguistic variation. Nevertheless, the majority of the population adheres to Islam and is deeply committed to its religious principles and teachings. Within this context, motherhood and childbearing are regarded as signs of personal and social fulfillment, and families often encourage early marriage and childbearing. Government policies also support early marriage and fertility as part of broader population growth initiatives. The legal age of marriage in Iran is 13 years for girls and 15 years for boys [ 2 ]. Many young women in such marriages frequently lack the necessary life skills and preparedness for marriage and motherhood, leading to adverse health outcomes for both mothers and their children [ 4 , 5 , 7 ]. Societal pressures and limited access to contraception further exacerbate the situation, as many adolescent girls become pregnant without adequate knowledge or resources to manage their reproductive health [ 7 ]. Accurately understanding adolescent women’s perspectives and experiences of pregnancy and motherhood is essential for designing effective interventions, increasing access, and ensuring active participation in prenatal care [ 10 ]. A critical factor in improving the quality of maternal health services is the provision of care that addresses psychosocial needs. Psychosocial support plays a vital role in reducing maternal vulnerability and stress [ 8 ] and facilitates the transition to motherhood. Research demonstrates that a lack of social support increases the risk of psychological distress during pregnancy and the postpartum period [ 9 , 11 , 12 ]. It is important, however, to distinguish between perceived and received social support. Studies indicate that perceived support—an individual’s belief that support is available if needed—has a greater influence on attitudes and behaviors than the actual support received. Wills and Cohen further reported that, for health-promoting behaviors, perceived support is more significant than actual support [ 13 ]. Supporting this, a study conducted in Iran by Naghizadeh et al. found that 50% of pregnant women lacked adequate social support [ 14 ]. Several studies have explored the experiences and challenges faced by pregnant adolescents in Iran and other countries. Moridi et al. (2019) conducted a qualitative content analysis to examine the perceptions of Iranian married adolescent females toward pregnancy. The study identified the concept of the “paradox of acceptance,” wherein participants expressed a combination of positive emotions, such as hope and satisfaction, alongside negative feelings like fear, regret, and frustration in response to their pregnancies. These mixed emotions reflected their struggle to reconcile social expectations with personal readiness for motherhood [ 15 ]. Similarly, Mohammadi et al. (2016) carried out a phenomenological study to understand the lived experiences of Iranian pregnant teenagers. The researchers introduced the concept of “fast development,” highlighting how teenage mothers undergo rapid, overlapping transitions—including marriage, pregnancy, and child-rearing. These overlapping roles contribute to significant emotional and psychological pressure as adolescents attempt to adapt quickly to adult responsibilities [ 2 ]. To provide a broader regional context, Varmaghani et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence and consequences of adolescent pregnancy in the Eastern Mediterranean Region. The study revealed a significant prevalence of adolescent pregnancy, with associated consequences including increased maternal and neonatal risks, disrupted education, and long-term socio-economic challenges. The authors emphasized the need for culturally tailored reproductive health programs targeting adolescents in the region [ 16 ]. Studies from other countries also corroborate these findings. For example, in sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America, adolescent pregnancy is frequently associated with limited access to reproductive health education, early marriage, and economic hardship, further complicating young mothers’ ability to cope with parenthood [ 17 ] These factors collectively increase the risk of adverse health, educational, and socio-economic outcomes for adolescent mothers and their children. These studies underscore the complex emotional and psychological landscape that pregnant adolescents must navigate, often in the absence of sufficient support or resources. Notably, there is limited research that specifically addresses the perspectives of adolescent mothers within the context of formal marriage. This gap highlights the need for comprehensive studies that can more effectively address the unique challenges faced by pregnant adolescents and inform strategies to improve health care and support for this vulnerable group [ 2 ]. Despite these insights, a significant gap remains in understanding the specific psychosocial support needs of pregnant adolescents in Iran. While existing research has explored the broader experiences and challenges associated with teenage pregnancy, little is known about the particular forms of psychosocial support that can enhance the well-being and health outcomes of these young women. This study seeks to address this gap by examining the specific needs and expectations of pregnant adolescents regarding psychosocial support, drawing on the perspectives of the adolescents themselves, their families, and healthcare providers. The findings are intended to inform the development of more effective and culturally sensitive health promotion programs tailored to this vulnerable population. Materials and methods Study design This study employed a descriptive qualitative design, utilizing conventional content analysis as outlined by Graneheim and Lundman (2004) [ 18 ]. The qualitative phase was conducted as part of a larger mixed-methods research project aimed at developing effective interventions to enhance psychosocial support for adolescent pregnancies in Iran. The qualitative approach was chosen to provide rich, in-depth insights into the social and emotional dimensions of adolescent pregnancy, with a particular focus on the perceptions, lived experiences, and expectations of pregnant adolescents regarding psychosocial support. To ensure a comprehensive understanding of the support landscape, perspectives from adolescents, family members, and healthcare providers were included. Settings, sample and recruitment Participants were recruited between September 2023 and July 2024 from comprehensive health service centers affiliated with Ahvaz University of Medical Sciences. Following approval from the Vice-Chancellor for Research and formal introduction to the health centers, coordination was established with center officials and the maternal health unit. The researcher maintained an active presence in the research setting, identified eligible mothers using the Sib system, and initiated contact to explain the study’s aims and procedures. Purposive sampling was employed to ensure maximum diversity and relevance among participants. Informational brochures were distributed to potential participants to raise awareness about the research objectives. Individuals expressing interest were invited to brief screening interviews, during which the researcher assessed mental and physical health status, proficiency in Persian, and willingness to participate. These interviews also provided an opportunity to explain the study in detail and obtain formal, informed consent. The study adhered to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) definition of adolescence, encompassing individuals aged 13 to 19 years, with those aged 20 and above classified as adults [ 19 ]. Inclusion criteria were carefully defined to focus on a specific demographic of adolescent mothers: participants were required to be primigravida with singleton pregnancies, in good mental and physical health (as indicated by a score below 12 on the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale), literate, and receiving prenatal care at the selected health service centers. Additionally, participants needed to demonstrate the ability to clearly articulate their experiences and expectations regarding psychosocial support during in-depth interviews. Exclusion criteria included high-risk pregnancies (such as those with pre-existing medical conditions) and inability to communicate in Persian. This careful selection process ensured that the sample represented the target population while maintaining the integrity and relevance of the qualitative inquiry. Data collection Qualitative data were collected through 30 semi-structured, in-depth, face-to-face interviews designed to explore participants’ perceptions and expectations regarding psychosocial support during pregnancy. The participant pool included pregnant adolescents, family members, and healthcare providers, ensuring a comprehensive perspective on the phenomenon under study. All interviews were conducted in Persian, in private rooms at the health center, with strict confidentiality maintained between the interviewer and participants. Each interview was audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim to ensure accuracy and to facilitate rigorous analysis. Interviews were conducted by the first author (SY), a PhD candidate in Midwifery at Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences. SY’s formal training in communication skills and counseling methods, acquired during her master’s studies in midwifery counseling, significantly enhanced her ability to conduct sensitive interviews with adolescent participants. She also completed advanced qualitative research workshops and had prior experience conducting interviews in two previous qualitative studies. At the time of this research, she was affiliated with the School of Nursing and Midwifery at Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences. To ensure clarity and relevance, the semi-structured interview guide was pilot tested prior to the main data collection phase. The pilot included three interviews—one with an adolescent, one with a family member, and one with a healthcare provider. These pilot interviews enabled the research team to assess the comprehensibility of the questions, evaluate the flow of the interview, and identify any ambiguities or cultural sensitivities. Based on participant feedback and initial observations, minor revisions were made to improve the phrasing and sequencing of questions. The final version of the interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1. Data from the pilot interviews were excluded from the main analysis. Key questions included: “Can you describe your feelings about the psychosocial support you received (or observed) during pregnancy?”, “What factors influenced those feelings?”, “How would you define or describe psychosocial support during pregnancy?”, “What were your experiences with psychosocial support in this context?”, “What kind of support did you find helpful or lacking?”, “What suggestions do you have for improving psychosocial support for pregnant adolescents?”, and “What could have been done to improve your experience of support?” Probing questions were used throughout the interviews to elicit deeper insights. Each interview lasted between 45 and 60 min. To further enrich the data and capture a broader range of perspectives, 15 key informant interviews were conducted—nine with healthcare providers and six with family members. Key informants are individuals who possess specialized knowledge or experience relevant to the research topic, and their insights can enhance the depth and credibility of qualitative findings [ 9 , 20 , 21 ]. To address potential bias, the interviewer maintained a reflective journal before and after each interview session, bracketing personal views and maintaining a focus on participants’ perspectives. Additionally, transcripts and emerging codes were reviewed by other members of the research team to enhance objectivity and analytic rigor. One participant withdrew from the study due to her husband’s objection, and three participants were interviewed twice to clarify or expand on their responses. Consistent with qualitative research standards, the sample size was not predetermined. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved—that is, when no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews [ 9 , 22 ] This point was confirmed after 30 interviews, through joint review and consensus by all authors. Data analysis The analysis of qualitative data in this study was both systematic and reflective, guided by the conventional content analysis approach described by Graneheim and Lundman (2004). Immediately after each interview, the first author (SY) transcribed the recordings verbatim, immersing herself in the data to ensure a deep and nuanced understanding of participants’ experiences. Using MAXQDA 2020 software, the transcripts were carefully organized, and initial coding commenced. Sentences and phrases that captured meaningful aspects of participants’ accounts were coded inductively, allowing the data to speak for itself rather than being constrained by pre-existing categories. As the analysis progressed, similar codes were clustered together to form subcategories that reflected shared meanings. These subcategories were then compared and, where appropriate, merged into broader categories that captured the essence of the participants’ experiences. Through this iterative process, the research team distilled the data into overarching themes that illuminated the psychosocial realities of adolescent pregnancy. Both the first and second researchers were actively involved in the coding process, and the results were reviewed by additional team members with expertise in qualitative analysis, ensuring the credibility and robustness of the findings. Data analysis followed the conventional content analysis method outlined by Graneheim and Lundman, which involves five key steps: [ 1 ] transcribing each interview immediately after completion; [ 2 ] reading and rereading the transcript to gain an overall understanding; [ 3 ] identifying units of meaning and generating underlying codes; [ 4 ] grouping similar codes into broader categories and subcategories based on conceptual similarities; and [ 5 ] uncovering the underlying content in the data to extract the main theme [ 9 ]. To further enhance the rigor of the analysis, the study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [ 23 ], which provided a structured framework for reporting and evaluating qualitative research. The completed COREQ checklist is available as Supplementary File 2, offering transparency and accountability in the analytic process. Rigor and trustworthiness Establishing the trustworthiness of qualitative research requires careful attention to four key criteria: credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability [ 24 ] In this study, several strategies were employed to meet these standards. To enhance credibility, participants were purposefully selected to maximize diversity in demographic characteristics, ensuring a wide range of perspectives. During the analysis, member checking was conducted by sharing coded interviews with participants and inviting their feedback to determine whether the researchers’ interpretations accurately reflected their intended meanings. This process not only validated the findings but also fostered a sense of collaboration between researchers and participants. Additionally, the opinions of three qualitative research experts were sought to confirm the consistency and accuracy of the coding and thematic structure. Dependability was addressed by maintaining a clear audit trail of all coding decisions and analytic steps. Multiple researchers independently reviewed and coded portions of the data, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus, further strengthening the reliability of the analysis. To support transferability, the study provided rich, detailed descriptions of the context and participant experiences, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings. Selected findings were also presented to several participants, who confirmed that the results resonated with their own experiences. Finally, confirmability was ensured through ongoing reflexivity and external review. The lead researcher maintained a reflective journal to bracket personal assumptions and remain focused on participants’ perspectives. Additionally, qualitative research experts reviewed the analytic process at each stage, verifying the accuracy and relevance of the codes and themes. Ethical considerations Ethical integrity was central to every stage of the research process. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (IR.AJUMS.REC.1402.140). Prior to participation, all individuals were thoroughly informed about the aims, procedures, and potential risks and benefits of the study. Informed consent was obtained not only from the adolescent participants but also from their family members and, when applicable, their husbands, in recognition of the participants’ young age and the cultural context. Confidentiality was carefully protected throughout the study. Each participant was assigned a code (e.g., A, B, C) to anonymize their data during collection and analysis. Audio recordings and transcripts were securely stored on password-protected computers, accessible only to the research team. Participants were assured that their involvement was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. They were also informed that the findings would be submitted for publication, with all identifying information removed to safeguard their privacy. Through these comprehensive measures, the study upheld the highest standards of ethical conduct, ensuring that participants’ rights, dignity, and well-being were respected throughout the research process. Results Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the study participants. In total, 30 individuals participated in the interviews, including 15 pregnant adolescents, 9 healthcare professionals (comprising gynecologists, reproductive health physicians, midwifery PhDs, general healthcare providers, midwifery experts, senior psychologists, and women’s ward nurses), and 6 family members (husbands, mothers, and mothers-in-law). The mean age of the pregnant adolescents was 17.53 years. Most were primigravida and reported their pregnancies as unintended. The majority were carrying a female fetus, expressed a preference for natural vaginal delivery (NVD), and planned to breastfeed. Challenges and Burdens Social and Emotional Challenges Adolescent pregnancy as a realm of inequality and overcoming challenges Both adolescents and healthcare providers widely perceive adolescent pregnancy as an experience marked by profound inequality and adversity. Young mothers often find themselves with limited access to social, educational, and healthcare opportunities compared to older women and must navigate a landscape shaped by systemic neglect and disadvantage. As one healthcare provider reflected, “I think pregnancy in adolescence means a world full of inequalities, where some face more difficult challenges and have fewer opportunities, you have to try to overcome the challenges, while everyone else is going along easily.” (Healthcare provider NO.9). Social stigma and shame Many adolescents described experiencing judgment, shame, and social alienation, particularly within school environments where pregnancy is viewed as inappropriate or embarrassing. One adolescent shared, “School classmates said it’s shameful to get pregnant in school and come back because school is a childish environment. I thought it was shameful in my mind. I would think to myself, don’t they feel embarrassed showing up with a big belly?” (Adolescent NO.9). Another added, “It’s like this here: if you don’t get pregnant soon, they’ll talk about you, but if you do it too early, they shame you too.” (Adolescent NO.3). Emotional instability Pregnant adolescents frequently experience emotional instability, characterized by mood swings, uncertainty about self-identity, and unpredictable emotional reactions. The rapid physical and psychological changes of early pregnancy often leave them feeling overwhelmed and unable to regulate their emotions. As one participant described, “I don’t know how I feel. One hour I’m fine, the next hour I’m bad. One day I’m happy, and suddenly I’m crying. I don’t know what’s happening to me, and I can’t control it.” (Adolescent NO.5). Lack of spousal and family support A recurring theme in adolescents’ narratives is the lack of consistent emotional and practical support from family members and spouses. Many described feeling neglected, misunderstood, or unsupported, especially in extended family households where in-laws may exert control or interfere in personal decisions. For example, one adolescent lamented, “My mother-in-law should take care of me… She doesn’t understand me. Look, since yesterday, she hasn’t called me. I want to feel safe knowing that someone always cares about me.” (Adolescent NO.1). A healthcare provider added, “Families are involved, but sometimes in a negative way. The mother-in-law often interferes in the personal life of the adolescent girl, especially since the couple lives with the husband’s family. Their young age and dependence make it hard for the girl to assert herself.” (Healthcare provider NO.2). Role and autonomy challenges Transition and disruption of life roles The abrupt shift from adolescence to motherhood is often described as disorienting and overwhelming. Many adolescents struggle to reconcile their previous identities as students or carefree teenagers with the new responsibilities of marriage and motherhood. As one participant noted, “Even now, I sometimes think about how I went from being a housewife to a mother, and sometimes it’s much harder for me than I thought.” (Adolescent NO.9). Another shared, “At first, it was very difficult. I had to go to school and also manage being a wife. Sometimes I said to myself, ‘Who am I? What am I doing here?‘” (Adolescent NO.9). Restrictions and lack of autonomy Adolescent mothers often report a significant loss of autonomy, particularly due to controlling behaviors from husbands or in-laws. Many feel unable to make independent decisions or maintain connections with their families of origin. One adolescent explained, “They don’t let me do anything. I haven’t even been allowed to stay at my mom’s house for a single night since I got married. My husband and mother-in-law always say, ‘You’re married now.’” (Adolescent NO.13). Lack of spousal support The emotional distance or physical absence of husbands—due to work, military service, or other obligations—further compounds the vulnerability of adolescent mothers. As one healthcare provider observed, “Sometimes there is no spouse. He’s doing military service or working far away. And when a teenager desperately needs love and support, he’s not there. She feels abandoned.” (Healthcare provider NO.6). Physical health and well-being challenges Physical health challenges Adolescent mothers frequently experience physical symptoms such as fatigue, malnutrition, and general discomfort, often exacerbated by limited resources and lack of knowledge. One adolescent shared, “I didn’t eat properly for five months… I have a constant headache, I feel weak, restless, and my body hurts all the time.” (Adolescent NO.13). Health complications The risk of medical complications is notably higher among adolescent mothers, including premature birth, poor fetal growth, and other pregnancy-related complications. As one healthcare provider explained, “Teenage pregnancy is a type of high-risk pregnancy that faces dangers like premature birth, poor fetal growth, and many complications that can affect both the mother and baby.” (Healthcare provider NO.4). Positive experiences and adaptations Emotional fulfillment Joy and fulfillment of motherhood Despite initial shock and uncertainty, many adolescents spoke of the deep emotional satisfaction and sense of meaning they discovered in motherhood. As one participant shared, “Becoming a mother is a great feeling. At first, it was a shock. I didn’t know what it would feel like. But now that I feel it in my heart, it’s a beautiful emotion I never expected.” (Adolescent NO.5). Establishing an emotional bond with the fetus Participants described a growing emotional connection with their unborn child, particularly through sensory experiences such as feeling fetal movement or hearing the heartbeat. One adolescent reflected, “The baby’s movements are wonderful. Hearing their heartbeat is so good. There’s a being living inside you. They live in your soul. It’s amazing. Even choosing their name is enjoyable and makes you feel closer to them.” (Adolescent NO.11). Personal growth and relationship changes Increased responsibility and maturity Many adolescents recognized significant personal growth, describing how pregnancy motivated them to become more organized and self-reliant. “I’ve become more responsible. I do everything on time. When the baby is born, I’ll be ready. I feel like I’ve grown up a lot through this experience,” (Adolescent NO.11). Improved spousal relationships For some, pregnancy served as a catalyst for strengthening marital bonds and fostering more supportive dynamics. Adolescents and their partners described greater emotional closeness and improved communication. As one adolescent noted, “My husband’s behavior has changed a lot. He talks to me more and helps more. Our relationship has improved significantly.” (Adolescent NO.4) Similarly, one husband shared, “I tried to support my wife during this pregnancy by being more patient, showing more tolerance, and spending quality time with her.” (Husband NO.2). Motherhood and future aspirations Motherhood often inspired adolescents to envision a better future for themselves and their children. “I want my children to grow up and have better lives than I did. I want them to study, follow their dreams, and not face the same struggles I had,” said one adolescent (Adolescent NO.1). Support and intervention needs Educational and counseling interventions Importance of education and counseling There was strong consensus on the necessity of early and ongoing education for adolescent girls, particularly in reproductive health, parenting, and life skills. As one healthcare provider suggested, “It’s better to start our education from pre-marriage classes and pre-pregnancy care. Health centers should educate newly married girls on contraception and early pregnancy prevention. Life skills training is also crucial.” (Healthcare provider NO.1) Another added, “Premarital counseling should be practical and specific for adolescents. The existing workshops are too general. We need to tailor education to their real needs.” (Healthcare provider NO.2) A third emphasized, “I think we should try to teach them the ten life skills.” (Healthcare provider NO.6). Need for flexible education programs Adolescents and providers alike stressed the importance of flexible, accessible educational options, such as online learning and adaptable class schedules, to accommodate the unique challenges faced by pregnant teenagers. As one healthcare provider explained, “Pregnant teens should be supported with special educational programs, online or flexible classes that let them study and get counseling at the same time.” (Healthcare provider NO.9) Another added, “Childbirth preparation classes should be mandatory for pregnant adolescents. This education should start as early as possible.” (Healthcare provider NO.2). Family and partner involvement in education Participants emphasized that educational and counseling interventions should include family members and partners, fostering a more supportive environment. “The solution is education, not just for the girl, but for the whole family and her partner. They need to learn about childbirth and emotional support, too,” noted a healthcare provider. (Health care provider NO 5). Healthcare provider and system support Need for specialized healthcare provider training Healthcare providers recognized the need for adolescent-specific training to improve communication and care. “As a healthcare provider, I need specialized training to better understand the needs of pregnant adolescents. Otherwise, we miss important signals,” explained one provider. (Health care provider NO 4). Value of healthcare provider support Adolescents expressed appreciation for proactive, caring follow-up from healthcare providers, describing such support as emotionally reassuring. “When health services call me, I feel very happy because I know they are taking good care of me,” shared one adolescent (Adolescent NO 10). Discussion This qualitative study explored the perceptions and expectations of pregnant adolescents, their families, and healthcare providers regarding psychosocial support during pregnancy in Ahvaz, Iran. The analysis identified three primary categories: (A) Challenges and Burdens, (B) Positive Experiences and Adaptations, and (C) Support and Intervention Needs. These categories, further divided into specific subcategories, highlight the multifaceted nature of adolescent pregnancy. The study underscored significant social and emotional challenges, conflicts related to roles and autonomy, and physical health burdens experienced by pregnant adolescents, alongside instances of emotional fulfillment, personal growth, and a critical need for comprehensive support interventions. Family and spousal relationships emerged as pivotal factors, either facilitating or impeding support. Adolescents expressed a strong desire for greater emotional support, particularly from their spouses and mothers-in-law, highlighting the importance of security and care. Healthcare providers, recognizing the crucial role of family involvement, advocated for pre-marital, pre-pregnancy, and childbirth education, along with specialized training to deliver adolescent-centered care. Adolescents reported numerous challenges, especially related to social stigma, emotional instability, role disruption, and health complications. These findings support prior literature identifying adolescent pregnancy as a period marked by intensified social inequality and emotional vulnerability [ 25 ]. The subcategory “Adolescent Pregnancy as a Realm of Inequality” parallels global research illustrating the correlation between adolescent pregnancy and social disparities [ 26 ]. Similarly, the theme of “Social Stigma and Shame” aligns with studies demonstrating the detrimental impact of negative social attitudes on the mental health of young mothers. Pregnant adolescents frequently face stigmatization from family, friends, and the community, leading to feelings of shame, embarrassment, and social isolation [ 27 , 28 ]. “Social Stigma and Shame” was particularly salient in school and familial settings. This supports more recent findings by Chandra-Mouli et al. (2021) and others, which show that adolescents frequently encounter moral judgment, especially in conservative societies, reinforcing isolation and depression. In Iran, where early childbearing is sometimes culturally endorsed, the stigma is often tied to disruption of educational trajectories rather than the pregnancy itself [ 29 ]. Shafiian et al. assert that “social attitudes and reflections towards teenage pregnancy are often negative in many countries; however, this negative perception is not universally applicable across all ethnicities and races, as the dominant culture of a given society largely influences it” [ 30 ]. Social norms, which dictate acceptable behaviors within communities, play a critical role in shaping sexual and reproductive health (SRH) outcomes. Norms that discourage contraceptive use and promote early marriage contribute to unmet contraceptive needs among adolescents [ 31 ]. In Iran, particularly in Ahvaz, where pregnancy typically occurs within marriage and early childbearing is culturally supported, the timing of pregnancy, especially within educational settings, becomes a source of shame due to inadequate support. Montazeri et al. highlight insufficient preparation by families and schools for marital responsibilities and limited access to essential information [ 32 ]. Among other challenges, the prevalent “Emotional Instability” reported by adolescents can be interpreted through developmental psychology, which posits that adolescence is a period of heightened emotional vulnerability [ 33 ]. Many studies focus on the role of a supportive family, but our results suggest that family involvement in the lives of pregnant adolescents is complex, yielding both positive and negative outcomes. Parental closeness, school environments, peer relationships and open communication are protective factors that reduce adolescent pregnancy and enhance well-being [ 34 ]. While parental absence, authoritarian parenting, and lack of communication about sexual health, significantly influence adolescent pregnancy risk [ 35 ]. Family empowerment programs can improve quality of life, but conflict and inadequate support may lead to adverse psychological and developmental effects [ 36 , 37 ]. The quality of family involvement-marked by trust, support, and effective communication-matters more than mere presence. Policies should prioritize strengthening positive family engagement, expanding access to evidence-based empowerment programs, and fostering communication and conflict-resolution skills. Comprehensive mental health, as well as intergenerational and social support services are essential, particularly for families with insufficient support [ 38 – 40 ] also the lack of consistent spousal support is a recurring theme in the literature, linked to increased stress and poorer outcomes for both mothers and infants [ 41 ]. The sudden “transition to motherhood,” coupled with hormonal changes, likely exacerbates these vulnerabilities. Furthermore, the reported “Restrictions and Lack of Autonomy” can be understood within the framework of social control theories, highlighting the power dynamics that often limit the agency of young women [ 42 ]. The emotional and physical struggles reported by participants reflect the dual burden of adolescence and pregnancy. The adolescents’ uncertainty about their feelings (“I don’t know how I feel?“) suggests the intense emotional upheaval experienced during this period, which may be exacerbated by limited coping strategies and lack of emotional literacy. Adolescence is a period in which individuals seek to discover their self, values, beliefs, and social roles. Erik Erikson, a renowned psychologist, referred to this stage as “identity versus role confusion” [ 43 ]. Adolescent identity formation is complex, and pregnancy often disrupts it, causing crises due to role changes. Rosado et al. note that early motherhood forces teens into adult roles prematurely, leading to a loss of control and challenges in individuation and separation [ 44 ]. The study by Meneses et al. (2015) emphasizes that young mothers face considerable role conflicts stemming from the early responsibilities of motherhood. The absence of parental guidance and family support worsens these conflicts, making it difficult for teenage mothers to juggle their roles as students, caregivers, and individuals striving to enhance their socioeconomic status [ 45 ]. Our study is also consistent with previous studies that have shown that pregnant adolescents face numerous “Physical Health and Well-being Challenges” that significantly impact their mental health and overall well-being. For example, a survey by Laurenzi et al., (2020) found that teenage mothers are at higher risk for depression and anxiety, compared to older mothers, which could be improved by psychological interventions. It reinforces our findings about the increased psychological challenges that young mothers face [ 1 ]. Teen mothers face higher risks of complications such as eclampsia, puerperal endometritis, systemic infections, low birth weight, premature labor, anemia, and pre-eclampsia compared to older mothers [ 46 , 47 ] Common physical health issues reported include severe nausea, vomiting, exhaustion, and musculoskeletal pain [ 47 ]. These findings are widely supported by both Iranian and global research, which highlight the increased vulnerability of adolescent mothers and their infants to adverse health outcomes [ 25 , 41 ]. However, our study also revealed positive aspects, such as “Establishing an Emotional Bond with the Fetus”, “Joy and Fulfillment of Motherhood” and “Increased Responsibility and Maturity,” which challenge the solely negative portrayal of adolescent pregnancy. This aligns with recent research emphasizing young mothers’ resilience and adaptive capacities. Teenage pregnancy, despite its challenges, can promote personal growth and empowerment. For some adolescents, motherhood provides satisfaction, hope, and a transition to adulthood, influenced by resources and support. Callister notes that pregnancy and motherhood can enrich spiritual and emotional development, shaping identity [ 2 ]. Lim interprets this as, “In addition to feeling evolved within themselves, adolescents experienced another evolution in the world around them. An important part of the participants’ experiences was focused on the social evolution of adolescents due to the social identity of becoming a mother, which caused them to feel valuable and socially acceptable as adults. They believe that they enjoy higher respect and social status by becoming pregnant and acquiring a maternal identity. They experienced a new self. Maternal identity is, actually, a unique individual and social event for every woman” [ 48 ]. Studies suggest positive outcomes of teenage motherhood in consensual marriages or planned pregnancies. Ebanks emphasized favorable views on planned pregnancies, Rosengard et al. linked positivity to life goals and maturity, and Montgomery highlighted themes of growth, responsibility, independence, and motherhood as a natural progression [ 41 ]. Thus, teenage pregnancy, often seen as challenging, can yield positive outcomes with proper support, optimism, and social networks. For some, motherhood fosters personal growth, satisfaction, and hope, while for others, outcomes depend on available resources and family or partner support [ 2 ]. Interestingly, while many studies focus on the role of the family and broader social network, this study found a significant shift in the reported emotional improvement of spousal relationships, with several adolescents noting an improvement in their marriages during pregnancy. This finding highlights the potential for positive relationship dynamics, which is often underreported in studies on adolescent pregnancy. The reported findings further suggests that pregnancy can act as a catalyst for positive relational shifts, a finding supported by studies on couple adaptation during life transitions [ 49 ]. Moreover, the enhancement of communication and shared responsibilities during this period may foster a deeper emotional connection between partners, reinforcing the notion that the transition to parenthood can serve as an opportunity for couples to strengthen their bond and navigate challenges together. Such insights underscore the importance of recognizing the multifaceted impacts of pregnancy on marital satisfaction, encouraging further research into the supportive mechanisms that can facilitate these positive outcomes [ 50 ]. The report “Motherhood and Future Aspirations” suggests that despite the challenges, certain teenage mothers often remain optimistic about their futures, their motivation stemming from a commitment to providing better lives for their children [ 51 ]. This underscores the critical role of psychosocial support in mitigating the negative impacts of adolescent pregnancy. Our results are consistent with studies advocating for comprehensive, culturally sensitive interventions that address the unique needs of this population [ 52 , 53 ]. Addressing mental stress in teenage pregnancy is crucial. Programs fostering coping skills, confidence, and resilience, alongside social support from family, peers, and the community, can help manage stress and lower depression risks [ 54 ]. Iran’s national health guidelines lack provisions for pregnant teenagers despite a large adolescent population. That’s why the results of the interviews emphasized that “Health Systems Support” includes starting pregnancy classes earlier than 18–20 weeks, increasing session frequency, and customizing content for adolescents. As stated in Macutkiewicz &amp; MacBeth, planned adolescent pregnancy is shaped by a complex interplay of personal desire, social context, and ambivalence, with both positive and negative perceptions reported. The findings indicate the complexity of adolescent pregnancy intentions and the need for context-sensitive approaches in health care and education [ 55 ]. They often need extra care and guidance, provided by cultural context and familial support, during pregnancy and postpartum, as their inexperience can make adjusting to the associated life changes more challenging [ 56 , 57 ]. Key solutions include enhancing support services, and adapting care frameworks to address the unique needs of pregnant adolescents in low- and middle-income countries, as recommended by Bhan N et al. [ 58 ] On the other hand, the Iranian education system lacks a definitive policy framework to address the needs of pregnant mothers. Furthermore, targeted support mechanisms are absent to assist this demographic [ 59 ]. Essential facilities like special schools for pregnant teens or dropouts and accessible online schools are still unavailable in many regions nationwide. Studies indicate that school programs effectively prevent unwanted pregnancies through teacher support, counseling, educational tools, financial aid, and home visits to ensure continued education [ 60 , 61 ]. Sex education is taboo in Iranian schools and society due to religious and cultural norms, with many parents and teachers lacking knowledge or fearing it may encourage undesirable behavior [ 62 ]. An effective approach to supporting adolescents involves integrating educational initiatives for their families and spouses while fostering collaboration with medical professionals. Husband support is crucial for boosting maternal self-esteem, reducing stress, and enhancing maternal self-efficacy, making them a key pillar during challenging times [ 9 ]. Encouraging family and community support can reduce stigma and isolation for pregnant teenagers while promoting support services helps them address challenges effectively [ 63 ]. Training spouses in childbirth preparation and support skills via media and social networks aligns with Mohammad Pour et al.‘s findings, where four 60-minute counseling sessions with fathers significantly improved social support scores [ 9 ]. In addition, Family-oriented interventions have been shown to enhance support networks and improve psychosocial outcomes [ 64 ]. Another significant finding was the strong emphasis on “Need for Specialized Healthcare Provider Training.” The lack of targeted support within Iran’s health and education systems was a critical issue. Healthcare providers emphasized the necessity for specialized training in adolescent pregnancy and parenting to enhance care. This training is crucial for addressing the unique challenges faced by pregnant adolescents, ensuring non-judgmental support, and improving overall healthcare outcomes for this demographic. As stated in the Miguel Vieira Martins study (2023) [ 65 ], “Training for primary care providers in adolescent sexual and reproductive health is inadequate, with only 22 out of 39 countries offering formal education. A study even showed improved training is essential for effectively addressing teen pregnancy and providing appropriate care and support” [ 66 ]. Another article explains that general practitioners also face challenges in addressing the complexities of teenage pregnancy [ 67 ]. In addition, Adolescent individuals experiencing pregnancy consistently highlighted “Value of Healthcare Provider Support”, the critical importance of receiving comprehensive support from healthcare providers. They emphasized the necessity of ongoing, empathetic care and engagement from medical professionals, which they regarded as equally vital alongside the support offered by their families and partners. Comprehensive programs that integrate education, psychosocial support, and medical care are essential to help them navigate this challenging period [ 68 – 71 ]. Strong emotional and social support systems are crucial for the positive experiences of teenage mothers. Support from family, trusted professionals, and community resources helps these young women navigate the challenges of motherhood and contributes to their overall well-being and resilience [ 51 , 72 ]. Teenage mothers’ psychological well-being improves with strong family support. Trust and attachment with caregivers enhance mood during pregnancy, leading to better birth outcomes and positive infant emotions [ 72 ]. One limitation of this study included only prim gravid adolescent pregnancies. While this focus allowed for a more in-depth exploration of specific experiences, it also facilitated better control over confounding variables, enabling a more precise analysis of the unique needs, risks, and complications associated with first pregnancies in adolescents. Additionally, this approach ensured the collection of consistent and reliable data. However, for future research, we recommend a comparative analysis that examines the experiences of first pregnancies versus subsequent pregnancies in both adolescent and adult populations. Such studies could provide valuable insights into the evolving needs and challenges across different pregnancy contexts and age groups. A notable strength of this study lies in the inclusion of diverse participants, such as healthcare providers, spouses, and families, which offered a multidimensional perspective on the research topic. This comprehensive approach enriched the findings by capturing insights from various stakeholders. Based on these findings, several future research directions are suggested. The design, implementation, and effectiveness of guidelines specific to Iranian pregnant adolescents should be investigated. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine the long-term impacts of adolescent pregnancy on maternal and child well-being. Studies exploring the effectiveness of specific psychosocial interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy or family-based counseling, are warranted. Furthermore, research should focus on developing and evaluating culturally tailored interventions that address the unique needs of diverse adolescent populations. Studies investigating the role of technology in delivering psychosocial support, such as online counseling or mobile health applications, are also needed. Finally, integrating these measures into national guidelines and creating a comprehensive care framework for pregnant adolescents is imperative. Conclusion This qualitative study offers a comprehensive understanding of the psychosocial experiences, needs, and expectations of pregnant adolescents in Ahvaz, Iran, drawing from the perspectives of adolescents themselves, their families, and healthcare providers. Adolescent pregnancy emerged as a deeply complex and multidimensional experience, shaped by emotional vulnerability, social stigma, limited autonomy, and health-related challenges, often intensified by insufficient support from families, communities, and healthcare systems. Yet within these challenges, the study also revealed striking capacities for resilience, emotional growth, and personal transformation. Many adolescents described moments of strength and adaptation, particularly when supported by spouses, mothers-in-law, and compassionate healthcare providers. The development of a maternal identity and the strengthening of familial bonds were identified not only as outcomes but as opportunities for empowerment and well-being. A critical insight from this research is the central role of family dynamics and culturally sensitive, adolescent-centered care. The absence of tailored psychosocial interventions, flexible educational opportunities, and specialized training for healthcare providers points to systemic gaps that hinder positive outcomes for this vulnerable population. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive, strengths-based, and culturally informed framework that integrates early and adaptable education, family-inclusive counseling, empowerment-focused prenatal support, and adolescent-specific reproductive health services. Policy-level interventions should prioritize evidence-based guidelines that foster resilience, prevent school dropout, and support the holistic development of both mother and child. Future research should examine the long-term impacts of psychosocial interventions and explore the potential of technology-driven support models. Comparative studies, such as those investigating first versus subsequent pregnancies or the effectiveness of culturally tailored care, will be essential in refining best practices and informing scalable solutions. Ultimately, adolescent pregnancy should not be seen solely through the lens of risk, but as a pivotal window for targeted support and meaningful growth. By acknowledging both the challenges and the capacities of these young women, we can build more responsive systems that uphold their dignity, safeguard their health, and expand their future possibilities.
