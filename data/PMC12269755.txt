Abstract The rising prevalence of youth-onset type 2 diabetes (T2D) in children necessitates early intervention with tight glycemic control, obesity medications, and metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS). Insulin and metformin are standard treatments, but additional therapies for obesity in youth are needed. A 4-year-old Hispanic female with early-onset obesity and newly diagnosed T2D presented with a hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of 8.0% (reference: &lt; 5.7%), a fasting glucose of 147 mg/dL (SI units: 8.17 mmol/L) (reference: 70-115 mg/dL [3.33-6.39 mmol/L]), and a body mass index (BMI) of 41 kg/m². She had rapid weight gain and hyperphagia. Initially, insulin therapy was started, and her HbA1c decreased to 6.7% over 6 months. Insulin was discontinued and metformin 1000 mg twice daily was started. Topiramate 75 mg nightly was prescribed for obesity with minimal effect and so semaglutide was started and titrated to 2 mg weekly. By 27 months, her HbA1c improved to 5.1%, and overall, she had a 12% reduction in her percent above the 95th percentile (%BMI p95 ; BMI 41 kg/m²→35 kg/m²; %BMI p95 = 205%→190%). Early intervention for early-onset obesity and diabetes, through tight glycemic control, intensive behavioral lifestyle programs, obesity medications, and metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS), is crucial to reversing or preventing and reducing life-limiting complications [ 4-6 ]. Currently, no medications are approved for treating nonmonogenic, nonsyndromic obesity in children younger than 12, although a phase 3a trial of liraglutide demonstrated significant reductions in body mass index (BMI) and body weight in children aged 6 to 12 years when combined with lifestyle interventions, despite common gastrointestinal side effects [ 7-10 ]. Weekly semaglutide is Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for children aged 12 and older, significantly reducing weight and improving metabolic markers such as glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) [ 11 ]. However, no glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are approved for younger children, representing a therapeutic gap [ 7 ]. When pharmacologic management and lifestyle interventions are insufficient, MBS may offer durable treatment for severe obesity [ 12 ]. The American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery recognizes MBS as highly effective in children and adolescents with severe obesity, particularly when performed early, before serious comorbidities develop [ 13 ]. However, stringent eligibility criteria limit MBS availability in younger children [ 14 , 15 ]. We present the case of a 4-year-old female with severe early-onset obesity and newly diagnosed T2D, highlighting challenges in diagnosing and managing pediatric obesity and diabetes at a very young age. We discuss a multi-modal treatment approach, including insulin, obesity medications, intensive lifestyle interventions, and consideration for MBS. Case Presentation A Hispanic girl aged 4 years 1 month presented with a HbA1c of 8.0% (ref: &lt; 5.7%) and fasting glucose of 147 mg/dL (8.17 mmol/L; ref: 70-115 mg/dL [3.33-6.39 mmol/L]), indicative of new-onset diabetes ( Table 1 ). Her weight was 205% of the 95th BMI percentile (%BMI p95 ), corresponding to BMI 41 kg/m², with acanthosis nigricans on the neck and axilla, with central adiposity ( Figs. 1 and 2 ). History revealed rapid weight gain from 1.5 years and hyperphagia since age 2. Parents noted cold extremities with excessive sweating regardless of ambient temperature. Family history was significant for obesity and hypertension. Diagnostic Assessment Autoantibodies (glutamic acid decarboxylase [GAD-65], insulin autoantibodies, islet antigen 2 [IA-2] antibody, and zinc transporter-8 antibodies) were negative. Detectable C-peptide and insulin were present with normoglycemia before insulin therapy ( Table 1 ). Genetic testing for monogenic diabetes (28 genes) was negative. Sleep study revealed mixed central and obstructive sleep apnea, requiring bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP). Additional genetic and DNA methylation testing for Prader-Willi syndrome were negative. Given hypoventilation, early-onset obesity, and hyperphagia, rapid-onset obesity with hypoventilation, hypothalamic dysfunction, and autonomic dysregulation (ROHHAD) was considered and discussed with the family. Pituitary and hormonal screening were normal. Treatment The initial presentation suggested type 1 diabetes (T1D), treated with multiple daily insulin injections (short- and long-acting analogs) and diabetes education. After negative antibodies and detectable C-peptide, discussion with family led to considering T2D and continuation of insulin for glycemic control, with possible future metformin. One month later, an obesity treatment plan was initiated including intensive lifestyle intervention, obesity medications, and MBS consideration. Topiramate was started and titrated to 75 mg nightly, with mild appetite reduction and no side effects. Outcome and Follow-Up Insulin therapy was continued for 6 months after her initial presentation, with blood glucose levels consistently within target. However, the family self-discontinued insulin due to concerns about potential hypoglycemia, as glucose levels remained within target at home. Her HbA1c decreased to 6.7%, and she was started on metformin 1000 mg twice daily (with standard titration to full dose), which she tolerated well. After 2 months of taking 1000 mg of metformin twice daily and 7 months of taking topiramate 75 mg nightly, her glucose levels remained stable, and her weight gain velocity stabilized (BMI = 39 kg/m²; 202% of the 95th percentile, Fig. 2 ). Challenges with medication adherence led to the decision to add weekly semaglutide for obesity treatment and glycemic control, with a goal of simplifying the regimen. The caregivers were counseled extensively on the use of the semaglutide pen device, including proper administration technique, storage requirements, and recognition of potential side effects; they were also advised on strategies to monitor and manage gastrointestinal symptoms, which are common with GLP-1 receptor agonists, to support treatment adherence and tolerability. Semaglutide was titrated to 1 mg weekly over 3 months (per standard titration), without gastrointestinal side effects reported. After 3 months of taking metformin 1000 mg twice daily, topiramate 75 mg nightly, and titrating semaglutide up to 1 mg weekly, her BMI trended down to 38 kg/m²; associated with a 4% reduction in %BMI p95 (∼201% of the 95th percentile, Fig. 2 ). All 3 medications were temporarily discontinued for 4 weeks prior to an electively scheduled tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy per anesthesia and parents' preference and then restarted 3 weeks postoperatively. During this 7-week hiatus, in which she was not taking any medications, her HbA1c increased to 7.3%, with an increase in BMI to 39 kg/m² (202% of the 95th percentile, Fig. 2 ) and parents reported significant worsening of her hyperphagia symptoms. It was then recommended that she restart metformin, topiramate, and semaglutide per standard titration protocols as she has tolerated previously. However, the family elected to only restart the topiramate and semaglutide at this time. The family desired to remain off metformin, if possible, due to challenges with palatability of both the liquid and pill preparation of the agent for the child. She consistently took topiramate 75 mg nightly and semaglutide 0.5 mg weekly for 1 month, at which time her parents reported increased food cravings, affecting the patient's school performance. Due to concerns from extended family regarding the off-label use of semaglutide at her age, the patient's caregivers expressed hesitation about dose escalation. In the context of a shared decision-making approach, the clinical team and family elected to initiate phentermine 15 mg in the morning to address early morning and afternoon hyperphagia. She remained on topiramate 75 mg nightly, phentermine 15 mg each morning (per parents the adherence for the phentermine was about 60% due to challenges with administering in the morning prior to school), and semaglutide 0.5 mg weekly for 5 months. Her HbA1c decreased to 6.1%, and her BMI decreased to 36 kg/m² (195% of the 95th percentile). The family continued to desire a simplification of her medical regimen and thus her phentermine was discontinued and her semaglutide was increased to 1 mg weekly to determine if a monotherapy approach would achieve adequate control and ensure all patient centered outcomes were met. For the subsequent 4 months, the child took topiramate 75 mg nightly, semaglutide 1 mg weekly. Her HbA1c decreased to 5.7%, and her parents reported significant improvements in hyperphagia and food focus. In addition to the medication regimen, the family also implemented health behavior lifestyle modification changes in the home, and her BMI decreased to 35 kg/m² (190% of the 95th percentile), achieving an 12% reduction in %BMI p95 since initial semaglutide initiation. Given the family's desire to simplify her medical regimen and promote long-term health while preventing life-limiting complications, she was referred to the pediatric MBS program for evaluation of potential long-term treatment options. The patient's family is actively considering MBS and is collaborating with a multidisciplinary team, including bariatric dietitians, psychologists, obesity medicine specialists, and bariatric surgeons, to address the complexities of her weight loss journey. Twenty-seven months after initial presentation, the family elected to stop topiramate due to concentration concerns at school. Her weight trajectory had plateaued and thus her semaglutide dose was increased to 2 mg weekly. In total, since starting semaglutide she has achieved a 12% reduction in %BMI p95 (35 kg/m²; 190% of the 95th percentile), with associated improvement in her HbA1c (most recent = 5.1%), liver enzymes, and lipid profile as well ( Table 1 ). Parents report decreased appetite, food responsiveness, and improved energy level. A repeat polysomnogram demonstrated significant improvement, with a decreased apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), enhanced oxygenation, and a progressive weaning of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) settings over time. Discussion This case illustrates the rare and complex clinical presentation of early-onset T2D in a young child, compounded by severe, rapidly progressive obesity and hyperphagia. The patient's phenotype, marked by a strong family history of metabolic disease, clinical insulin resistance, and early T2D onset, highlights the aggressive nature of metabolic dysfunction in pediatric populations [ 16 ]. Early diagnosis and intervention are essential to mitigating long-term complications, including cardiovascular disease, liver dysfunction, and beta-cell failure [ 1-3 , 17 ]. The introduction of semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, proved to be a pivotal intervention. The patient demonstrated a sustained reduction in %BMI p95 with improvements in glycemic parameters and lipid profiles. This aligns with findings from the STEP TEENS trial, which demonstrated a 16.7% greater reduction in BMI with semaglutide compared to placebo in adolescents, with 73% achieving ≥5% weight loss, and additional benefits in metabolic risk factors [ 11 ]. More recently, the SCALE Kids trial provided compelling data on liraglutide in younger children (ages 6 to &lt;12 years), reporting a mean BMI reduction of 5.8% vs a 1.6% increase in placebo (difference of −7.4% points, P &lt; .001), with 46% achieving at least 5% BMI reduction [ 10 ]. The current case extends these insights by demonstrating the safety and feasibility of semaglutide use in a preadolescent patient, showing not only tolerability but clinically significant improvement in hyperphagia and weight trajectory. While the patient did not experience the gastrointestinal side effects frequently associated with GLP-1 agonists, this is noteworthy given that in SCALE Kids, 80% of liraglutide-treated patients reported gastrointestinal adverse effects [ 10 ], and in STEP TEENS, 62% reported gastrointestinal symptoms with semaglutide [ 11 ]. This patient's favorable response suggests that younger children may tolerate GLP-1 therapy better than expected, especially with careful titration and monitoring. Given the patient's severe obesity, which persisted despite pharmacological management, MBS was considered as part of her treatment plan [ 14 , 18 ]. MBS is increasingly recognized as an effective long-term solution for youth with severe obesity, including those with T2D [ 14 , 16 , 18 ]. MBS has shown promise in preserving beta-cell function and improving glycemic control, making it a valuable option for children with early-onset T2D [ 19 ]. The patient's progressive weight gain despite medications and lifestyle interventions highlighted the need for a more durable solution, particularly given the aggressive nature of T2D in younger children. Although MBS in young children remains controversial and subject to strict eligibility criteria, early surgical intervention may be necessary in severe cases to prevent long-term complications [ 20 ]. Given the patient's clinical presentation, another consideration in the differential diagnosis was ROHHAD, a rare and life-threatening pediatric condition [ 21 ]. In this case, the patient's early-onset severe obesity, hypoventilation requiring BiPAP therapy, and parental report of autonomic dysregulation raised concerns for ROHHAD [ 21-23 ]. This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for ROHHAD in children presenting with rapid-onset obesity and associated symptoms such as central hypoventilation. The clinical acumen required to differentiate between conditions like ROHHAD and typical early-onset obesity with T2D is crucial in ensuring appropriate treatment. Aggressive multi-modal treatment of obesity is key in managing conditions like ROHHAD, and optimizing obesity through medication and lifestyle interventions remains essential in preventing life-limiting complications [ 24 ]. This case highlights the complexity of managing early-onset type 2 diabetes and severe obesity in young children, demonstrating the potential of combining semaglutide with phentermine to improve appetite, weight, and glycemic control. It underscores the emerging role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in pediatric obesity and emphasizes the need for ongoing research and multidisciplinary care to safely integrate these therapies into clinical practice. Learning Points Early-onset T2D in children requires careful diagnosis and management. GLP-1 receptor agonists, like semaglutide, can be effective in managing both T2D and obesity in pediatric patients. Multi-modal treatment strategies are crucial for managing severe obesity and T2D. Rare conditions like ROHHAD should be considered in differential diagnosis when obesity is accompanied by hypoventilation and other symptoms.
