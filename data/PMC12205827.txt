Abstract Systemic inflammation and dysregulated kynurenine (KYN) metabolism are increasingly recognised as key factors in various psychiatric disorders. Imbalances in the KYN pathway are associated with psychological and cognitive symptoms across disorders like depression and schizophrenia, yet remain unexplored in borderline personality disorder (BPD). This study is the first to compare KYN metabolism together with the myokine IL-6 in BPD patients and healthy controls and to examine acute exercise effects on this pathway in BPD. Fourteen patients with BPD and nine controls participated in two experimental conditions: cycling exercise and a control session. Serum samples were analysed for tryptophan (TRP), KYN, kynurenic acid (KA), and quinolinic acid (QA). Results revealed greater QA/KYN and lower KA/KYN and KA/QA ratios in BPD, indicating a shift toward the neurotoxic branch. Acute exercise increased KA, KA/KYN, KA/QA, and IL-6 levels, highlighting its potential to enhance neuroprotective KYN activity. These findings suggest KYN pathway dysregulation in BPD and support physical exercise as a promising intervention to rebalance this pathway. Further research should investigate long-term exercise effects and their impact on psychological outcomes in BPD. Introduction Recent research in psychiatry increasingly emphasises the roles of systemic inflammation and dysregulated kynurenine (KYN) metabolism as underlying triggers for various disorders. The KYN pathway, a product of tryptophan (TRP) catabolism, divides into two branches: one driven by kynurenine aminotransferase (KAT) enzymes, which produce kynurenic acid (KA – generally considered neuroprotective unless levels become excessively high), and another primarily influenced by the inflammation-mediated enzyme kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO). The latter generates several neurotoxic metabolites, such as quinolinic acid (QA), but also the versatile coenzyme nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), which is essential for multiple cellular functions ( Schwarcz et al., 2012 ). Although levels of imbalance differ between disorders, systemic inflammation and the KYN pathway appear to be integrated within both peripheral and central networks, with co-active metabolites (e.g., serotonin, glutamate, GABA) triggering various psychological symptoms ( Cervenka et al., 2017 ; Riβmayer et al., n.d. ; Schwarcz et al., 2012 ). For instance, patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) often present low-grade systemic inflammation accompanied by an overactive KMO. This results in increased levels of neurotoxic metabolites, such as 3-hydroxykynurenine and QA, alongside decreased KA levels coming from the KAT branch ( Cervenka et al., 2017 ; Ogyu et al., 2018 ). Conversely, in schizophrenia, the separation between the neuroprotective and neurotoxic branches becomes less distinct, as elevated central levels of the neuroprotective KA may exceed healthy thresholds, potentially triggering psychotic and cognitive symptoms ( Plitman et al., 2017 ; Riβmayer et al., n.d. ). This model also extends to other psychological and central nervous system conditions ( Savitz, 2020 ). However, despite these insights, no studies have specifically focused on patients with borderline personality disorder (BPD). Therefore, this study will first compare the KYN metabolism in BPD patients to matched healthy controls. Personality disorders are among the most common psychological disorders, with prevalence rates between 11 % and 14 %, and BPD being one of the most prevalent forms ( Eaton and Greene, 2018 ). Typically diagnosed in adolescence or early adulthood, BPD is a severe disorder characterised by dysfunction across emotional, behavioural, interpersonal, and cognitive domains ( Gunderson et al., 2018 ). Core symptoms include high impulsivity, emotional dysregulation, and self-image issues, which can lead to significant distress and functional impairment ( Gunderson et al., 2018 ). Additionally, BPD patients frequently face comorbid psychological conditions (e.g., depression, substance use) and physical health issues (e.g., cardiovascular and metabolic diseases), further impacting their daily functioning ( Doering, 2019 ; Shah and Zanarini, 2018 ). Most treatments for BPD demonstrate only small to moderate efficacy, with behavioural therapies being the most effective ( Cristea et al., 2017 ). The latest meta-analysis identifies dialectical behavioural therapy as the most empirically supported treatment, although responses vary widely, and many patients show little to no improvement ( Rizvi, 2011 ). This variability underscores the need for supportive or alternative treatments. Physical exercise is increasingly recognised for its benefits in reducing impulsivity, enhancing mood, and improving self-efficacy and self-image in both healthy individuals and those at risk ( Bernstein et al., 2019 ; Javelle et al., 2021 ). Exercise has also demonstrated moderate to large benefits for comorbidities associated with BPD, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, anxiety, and depression ( Posadzki et al., 2020 ). Moreover, exercise is cost-effective and has no known side effects. However, no exercise-based intervention has yet been specifically tested in BPD patients ( St-Amour et al., 2021 ). To our knowledge, only one acute pilot study has evaluated the effects of physical exercise in this context ( St-Amour et al., 2022 ). No adverse events were attributed to the exercise training, which was considered safe. However, this study focused on psychological outcomes, specifically negative affect following mood induction, and did not assess the acute effects of exercise on biomarkers that may influence psychological symptoms. Therefore, the second aim of this study is to investigate the acute effects of physical exercise on the KYN pathway in BPD patients. 2. Methods The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. It was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT05246527 ) and approved by the Ethics Committee from Bonn University before data collection. The results presented below constitute a preliminary sub-analysis of an ongoing larger randomised controlled trial project testing the effect of different exercise intensities in BPD patients and matched healthy controls, which also incorporates assessments of cognitive function, stress reactivity, and further psychological outcomes beyond those detailed here. The complete study protocol can be found here: clinicaltrials.gov/study/ NCT05246527 . 2.1. Participants &amp; experimental procedure Fourteen patients with BPD and nine age, sex, and educational attainment matched healthy controls participated in the study ( Table 1 ). According to assessments conducted using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5), patients were diagnosed with depression (n = 13), anxiety (n = 9), post-traumatic stress disorder (n = 7), attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (n = 6), substance use disorder (n = 3), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (n = 3). The severity of participants’ symptoms was assessed using the Borderline Symptom List-95 (BSL-95) and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). The clinical group showed moderate and severe mean scores on the BSL-95 and BDI, respectively ( Table 1 ), while the healthy controls fell within the minimal range on both measures. Severe or unstable endocrine or cardiovascular conditions were exclusion criteria. Patients were prescribed various medications, including antidepressants (n = 13), antipsychotics (n = 9), mood stabilizers (n = 4), anxiolytics (n = 1), ADHD medications (n = 3), and treatments for hypertension (n = 4), hormonal disorders (n = 2), or gastrointestinal conditions (n = 1). They participated in two 30-min experimental conditions on separate days at least 48 h apart in counterbalanced order. In the Exercise condition, they cycled on a stationary ergometer after random assignment to either a moderate- or a high-intensity protocol, guided by a video of a cycling instructor. Both protocols included a 5-min warm-up, 21 min of active phase, and a 4-min cool-down. During the active phase, the moderate-intensity group cycled at 64–76 % of their individually determined maximal heart rate (HRmax), while the high-intensity group cycled above 77 % HRmax. HRmax was determined through a pre-experimental maximal exercise test. HR was continuously monitored during exercise sessions to ensure adherence to target intensities. On average, the moderate-intensity group maintained 71.5 ± 3.1 % of HRmax, while the high-intensity group averaged 86.6 ± 5.4 % of HRmax. Since not enough patients have been tested to assess the differential effects of exercise intensity, the two types were combined into a single Exercise condition. In the control condition, all participants watched a movie about the benefits of physical activity on general health. The sessions were scheduled at the same time (at 2:00 to 3:00 p.m.) to control for circadian rhythm effects. Blood samples were collected before and after each condition (within 5–15 min). Women not using hormonal contraceptives were tested during the luteal phase of their menstrual cycle, while those using hormonal contraceptives (n = 3 for patients and n = 1 for controls) were not tested during menstruation. Participants were instructed to refrain from intense physical exercise, alcohol, drugs and unprescribed medications at least 24 h before testing and to refrain from nicotine, caffeine, food and drinks (except water) at least 1 h before testing. Participants received compensation of 10 Euros per hour for their involvement in the study. 2.2. Blood samples After blood collection, 9 mL S-Monovette tubes were left upright at room temperature for at least 30 min, then centrifuged at 3000 RPM for 10 min. The resulting serum was aliquoted and frozen at −40 °C. 2.3. Enzyme-linked ImmunoSorbent assay (ELISA) The pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin 6 ([IL-6], lower limit of quantification [LLoQ]: 0.031 pg/mL, intra-assay coefficient of variation [CV] was 4.1 %) was measured using commercial High-Sensitivity ELISA-kits (Bio-Techne, R&amp;D Systems) according to the manufacturer's protocol. 2.4. High-performance liquid chromatography – tandem mass spectrometry Based on previously published protocols ( Javelle et al., 2021 ; Joisten et al., 2020 ), serum levels of TRP, KYN, KA, and QA were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) following serum protein precipitation. The analyses were conducted on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC® system, equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 analytical column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) and coupled to a Xevo® TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters, Eschborn, Germany). Diagnostic ion transitions were identified through multiple reaction monitoring experiments. 2.5. Statistics Analyses were conducted using JASP Version 0.18.3. Data were winsorised at ±3z (0.4 % of the data adapted). A Bayesian statistical approach was chosen to provide more valuable inferences given the limited sample size compared to a frequentist approach. The BF 10 represents the likelihood ratio of the data under H1 compared to H0. The following rule of thumb can be used for interpretation: BF 10 &gt; 3 indicates moderate evidence, BF 10 &gt; 10 indicates strong evidence, and BF 10 &gt; 100 indicates very strong evidence. Uniform priors were used throughout the analysis due to the lack of prior experience with analysing the KYN pathway in BPD patients. Since there was a difference in BMI between the patient and healthy control groups, we included BMI as a covariate by adding it to the comparative null model. Differences between healthy controls and patients were assessed using Bayesian ANCOVA. Baseline correlations were tested using Bayesian Pearson's correlations. To improve measurement stability and reduce dependency between multiple measures in these tests, we used the average of the two pre-test conditions ( Exercise and Movie ). As oral contraceptives have been shown to influence KYN metabolite levels, metabolite levels in women taking oral contraceptives were compared to those not taking them in patients and in controls. This comparison was made using a Bayesian Mann-Whitney U test with a data augmentation algorithm, employing 5 chains and 1000 iterations. No differences were detected. Differences in intervention effects between Exercise and Movie were evaluated using individual baseline-adjusted Bayesian repeated measures ANCOVAs for each outcome. For repeatability purposes, the seed was set to 1 for all tests. 3. Results 3.1. BPD patients vs healthy controls BPD patients and healthy controls were compared for TRP, KYN, QA, KA, KYN/TRP, KA/KYN, QA/KYN, KA/QA, and IL-6. When considering single metabolites, only TRP was substantially greater in healthy controls than in BPD patients (BF 10 = 8.248; Cohen's d = 1.18). However, differences emerged more clearly when examining ratios that index the activity of the two KYN branches ( Fig. 1 – Cohen's d for A. KA/KYN = 1.10, BF 10 = 3.234; B. QA/KYN = 1.24, BF 10 = 3.625; and C. KA/QA = 1.23, BF 10 = 8.723). Notably, the QA/KYN ratio (reflecting the activity of the neurotoxic branch) was greater in patients than in controls, while the KA/QA ratio (reflecting the balance between the neuroprotective and neurotoxic branches) was significantly higher in healthy controls. IL-6 levels did not differ substantially between BPD patients and healthy controls (BF 10 = 0.452) Subsequently, BSL-95 and BDI scores were correlated with all kynurenine metabolites and IL-6 levels within the patient group. The KA/KYN ratio demonstrated a strong negative correlation with BDI scores (r = −0.683, BF 10 = 6.759). No other substantial correlations between biological and psychological variables, or between IL-6 and KYN metabolites. 3.2. Acute effects of exercise in BPD patients As displayed in Fig. 2 , acute physical exercise triggered a substantial increase in KA (R 2 = 0.516; BF 10 = 7.980; Time BF incl = 2.463; Condition BF incl = 4.066; Time ∗ Condition BF incl = 7.980), KA/KYN (R 2 = 0.505, BF 10 = 8.106; Time BF incl = 3.864; Condition BF incl = 2.779; Time ∗ Condition BF incl = 6.406), and KA/QA levels (R 2 = 0.862; BF 10 = 15.842; Time BF incl = 3.701; Condition BF incl = 9.565; Time ∗ Condition BF incl = 15.842) when compared to the Movie condition ( Fig. 2 ). Acute physical exercise also led to a substantial increase in IL-6 levels compared to the Movie condition (R 2 = 0.988; BF 10 = 3.58 x 10 4 ; Time BF incl = 1.017 x 10 4 ; Condition BF incl = 18.879; Time ∗ Condition BF incl = 29.072). No further interactions were detected. 4. Discussion This study is the first to compare KYN metabolism between BPD patients and healthy controls, revealing distinct metabolic imbalances. Specifically, BPD patients exhibited a higher QA/KYN ratio and lower KA/KYN and KA/QA ratios, suggesting heightened activity in the KMO (neurotoxic) branch relative to the KATs (neuroprotective) branch. This metabolic shift aligns with findings in individuals with mood disorders, depression, and suicide, where chronic low-grade inflammation is thought to upregulate KMO enzyme activity, thus driving the production of neurotoxic metabolites ( Ogyu et al., 2018 ; Savitz, 2020 ). Chronic low-grade inflammation has been repeatedly observed in BPD ( Saccaro et al., 2021 ) and likely contributes to the elevated peripheral activity of the KMO branch seen here. Nonetheless, resting IL-6 levels, an indicator of such inflammation, were not sufficiently elevated in BPD patients to differ substantially from those of healthy controls, suggesting a more complex regulatory mechanism that may involve additional co-inflammatory signals. It is also important to note that all BPD patients in this study had comorbidities, most notably depression. Baseline analyses further revealed a strong negative correlation between depressive symptom severity (indexed by BDI scores) and KA/KYN levels. Therefore, it remains unclear whether these dysregulations in the KYN system are independent of the common comorbidities seen in BPD ( Doering, 2019 ; Shah and Zanarini, 2018 ), and whether they may play a role in both peripheral and central network disturbances, potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of BPD. Following this line of thought, research is needed to explore central KYN and inflammatory levels in BPD, as peripheral and central levels may not directly correlate due to selective upregulation of cell types expressing only one class of enzyme, such as astroglial proliferation in schizophrenia ( Riβmayer et al., n.d. ). This study also explored the acute effects of physical exercise on KYN metabolism in BPD patients and highlighted the increased activity of the neuroprotective branch with three of its markers (KA alone, KA/KYN, KA/QA), along with a concurrent rise in IL-6 levels. This result aligns with our expectations. Elevated resting IL-6 has been shown to activate IDO enzymes, thereby enhancing the conversion of TRP to KYN and increasing the KYN pool ( Joisten et al., 2020 ). However, IL-6 released during acute physical exercise originates primarily from muscle cells and may exert different regulatory effects on TRP metabolism than IL-6 associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, which tends to be more systemic, sustained and accompanied by stronger pro-inflammatory signalling. Since we did not observe a significant increase (nor decrease) in KYN post-exercise, it is possible that KYN was rapidly down-metabolised. Indeed, acute exercise is a potent stimulus for the transcription factor PGC-1α (together with PPAR delta), which upregulates KAT4 expression via the 5′-adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase and the change ADP/ATP ratio and thus may potentially explain the increase KA production ( Agudelo et al., 2014 ; Joisten et al., 2020 ; Schiaffino et al., 2007 ). Nonetheless, the direct link between transcriptional changes and immediate modification in enzymatic activity remains to be established. Indeed, several studies have shown that those transcriptional changes inducing enzymatic modifications (and potentially activity changes) would only peak several hours post-exercise ( Kuang et al., 2022 ; Pilegaard et al., 2003 ), and thus may not solely explain immediate changes in KA. Further research suggests that KA may be considered a myokine ( Martin et al., 2020 ). Indeed, exercise activates the malate-aspartate shuttle (MAS), which supports cellular energy production by transferring cytosolic NADH into the mitochondria, increasing ATP production ( Martin et al., 2020 ). This enhanced energy metabolism creates favourable conditions for the KYN-to-KA conversion, as the increased activity in MAS enzymes complements the upregulation of KATs. Together, these changes help muscle cells efficiently metabolise KYN into KA, contributing to higher KA levels during and after exercise. Exercise not only reroutes certain TRP metabolites but also enhances interorgan and intercellular KYN signalling. Several studies have demonstrated that exercise increases the activity of large amino acid transporters immediately post-exercise, facilitating the movement of KYN through both vascular and lymphatic circulations, thereby supporting various cellular systems. This increase in KA synthesis enhances peripheral KYN clearance, as KA (but not KYN) cannot cross the blood-brain barrier. This mechanism may prevent central nervous system-specific KYN accumulation and associated neurotoxic outcomes while promoting immune resilience through increased KA binding to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, which supports CD4 + T-cell differentiation into regulatory T cells and resident memory CD8 + T cells ( Dean et al., 2023 ; Gandhi et al., 2010 ). When applied regularly, such exercise training will lead to an anti-inflammatory environment that is very beneficial for BPD patients where chronic low-grade inflammation may exist. The lack of change in the inflammatory-driven KMO branch (where an increase might have been expected due to the temporary inflammation triggered by exercise) could be attributed to variability across exercise conditions, as moderate- and high-intensity sessions were combined. These conditions elicit different physiological responses, with 30 min of high-intensity exercise producing a significantly stronger inflammatory signal and a higher release of exerkines by skeletal muscles ( Cerqueira et al., 2020 ). Once the entire study is completed, separating the two exercise types will provide valuable insights into which intensity produces the optimal physiological outcomes. It will also help evaluate which intensity is more likely to achieve high adherence and compliance when implemented as a long-term intervention. Previous research by Javelle et al. (2021) suggests that regular physical exercise may positively modulate the KYN pathway together with mitigating emotion and non-emotion-related impulsivity, core symptoms in BPD. Regular exercise could offer a promising approach for rebalancing KYN metabolism and alleviating symptoms over time. Further studies are warranted to explore whether the acute changes observed in this study can translate into lasting benefits for BPD patients, potentially through structured, chronic exercise interventions. Despite the clear and novel findings, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, the sample size remains a significant limitation, reducing the statistical power of our analyses and hindering sub-group analyses within the sports condition. Second, pooling the two exercise modalities introduces considerable heterogeneity in the results and limits the extent of our conclusions. However, both of these limitations will be addressed through ongoing data collection as part of our larger project. Third, the high prevalence of comorbidities in our sample (and in BPD patients overall)raises questions about whether the observed KYN changes can be attributed solely to BPD or are influenced by these comorbid conditions. Additionally, patients were prescribed various medications, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, anxiolytics, and treatments for somatic conditions (e.g., tension, hormonal, or gastrointestinal issues). Although psychotropic medications can influence the KYN pathway, our small sample size did not allow for stratification by drug class. This remains an important consideration for future studies with larger and more homogeneous samples. Fourth, restricting measurements to the pre- and immediately post-exercise periods prevents us from assessing the temporal dynamics of KYN metabolites or the transition from acute to chronic changes. Finally, since these analyses were not the primary focus of our RCT, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that the study design may have subtly influenced our findings. Nevertheless, this study represents the first to explore the acute effects of physical exercise on biological markers in BPD patients and should encourage further investigation into this underexplored field. In conclusion, this study is the first to report a peripheral metabolic shift toward the neurotoxic branch of the KYN pathway in BPD patients compared to healthy controls. This imbalance may be mitigated through increased activity in the neuroprotective KYN branch induced by physical exercise. These preliminary findings highlight the need for further research on exercise interventions in BPD, where positive physiological and immunological outcomes are to be expected.
