Background Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) affects mental health in the long term and is often associated with past psychological trauma. This systematic review was conducted with a purpose to investigate the correlation between adverse childhood experiences (ACE), experiences of child maltreatment, and the occurrence of PTSD in adults over 18 years to optimize treatment guidelines accordingly. Method The review utilized articles searched on PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and Cochrane. Inclusion criteria include original studies (RCTs, cross-sectional, and cohorts) involving adults over 18 years with PTSD with a history of ACE published during the last decade (2014 and 2025). This study was conducted according to preferred reported items in systematic reviews and meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. A quality assessment of the included studies was conducted using Newcastle Ottawa scale for cohort studies and the axis scale for cross-sectional studies. Results Out of 356 articles, 169 were screened, of which 26 studies were included in this review. These studies discussed various forms of childhood maltreatment, including sexual abuse, physical abuse, traumatic events, and neglect from parents. Most of these studies indicate a direct correlation between childhood maltreatment and Adulthood PTSD. Conclusion A robust association exists between ACE and childhood maltreatment and the development of PTSD in adulthood. There is a need for more literature to study the effects of maltreatment and comorbid mental health conditions for policymaking to protect child mental health. Adverse childhood Experiences (ACEs) consist of various forms of traumatic events that happened during one’s childhood, such as child neglect, abuse, and dysfunctional environment [ 2 ]. Initially, seven types of ACEs were examined: four were associated with family dysfunction (drug abuse, mental illness, mothers treated violently, and criminal conduct), and three were related to CM (sexual, physical, and psychological abuse) [ 3 ]. Exposure to ACEs increases risk of various mental and physical problems [ 4 ]. These individuals include younger and middle-aged persons but given the link between childhood trauma and early mortality, associations most likely continue into old life [ 5 ]. The long-term impact of CM is Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) development [ 6 ]. PTSD is a psychiatric disorder that can occur after a significant traumatic experience [ 7 ], often linked to major functional impairments in everyday life which include friendships, parenting, social functioning, intimate relationships, academic achievement, and financial problems [ 8 ]. Additional characteristics include mood shifts, avoidant behaviour, heightened arousal, and intrusive symptoms such as nightmares and flashbacks [ 9 ]. Research shows that people having PTSD are more likely to develop dementia [ 7 ]. There is a high risk of PTSD development in individuals who experience Childhood Sexual Abuse (CSA) [ 9 ]. According to studies, a proportion of 45–55% of CSA victims suffer from PTSD [ 9 ]. Research indicates that PTSD is linked to impaired cognitive functions in several domains, such as concentration, working and verbal memory, attention, mental agility, and language [ 10 ]. There is evidence that several factors, such as the kind of event, intensity, and age of the exposure, dosage of adverse events, and the socio-economic environment determine the impact of ACEs [ 11 ]. The existing literature, including numerous systematic reviews and meta-analyses, has demonstrated a well-established link between Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) and the development of PTSD. However, fewer studies have systematically explored this relationship across the full spectrum of adult populations, especially in regard to age-specific outcomes or demographic moderating variables. This review aims to synthesize recent evidence and identify under-researched sociodemographic factors influencing the PTSD-ACEs relationship. Results The systematic review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024466401). 356 studies from the following databases were found using the search strategy: PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. Following the elimination of duplicates and screening of articles based on our inclusion and exclusion criteria, 52 full-text papers were evaluated for eligibility. Twenty six of these were used in the research.This is illustrated in Fig. 1 (PRISMA Flowchart). Data extraction was conducted using the MeSH keywords from the PICO of the articles, and a filter was applied to include articles from the past 11 years in our initial search. There were 169 articles screened in all, and duplicates were removed using Rayyan software during screening. Only articles with full text and those that were freely accessible were included. This review found a strong association between ACEs and the development of PTSD in adults over 18. Various ACEs in the selected studies were considered culprits for the precipitation and development of these mental health disorders. These include emotional, physical, and sexual abuse, physical and emotional neglect, household dysfunction, witness to intimate partner violence, perceived discrimination, historical loss, community violence, maltreatment, and parent psychopathology. The findings of the included studies indicate that such events are not only associated with PTSD in adult life but also with several other psychiatric disorders such as depression, anxiety, and drug abuse. Notably, the cohort studies provided stronger evidence for the link between ACEs and PTSD compared to the cross-sectional studies. The prospective nature of cohort designs allowed for a more robust examination of the temporal relationship between childhood adversity and the subsequent development of PTSD. Among the specific ACEs examined, witnessing intimate partner violence (IPV) and experiencing physical abuse during childhood emerged as the most strongly associated with PTSD in adulthood. Children who witnessed IPV were found to have significantly higher odds of developing PTSD (OR = 2.96, p &lt; 0.001) and using multiple substances (OR = 5.3, p &lt; 0.001) later in life. Similarly, physical abuse during childhood was linked to a higher risk of depressive symptoms (OR = 3.68, p &lt; 0.001) and suicide attempts (OR = 2.90, p &lt; 0.001) in adulthood. Regarding demographic factors, the available evidence suggests that the impact of ACEs on PTSD may vary by gender. Some studies found that females who experienced maltreatment at an earlier age were more likely to develop PTSD and anxiety compared to males. However, other studies reported conflicting findings, with one nationwide poll of African Americans indicating a higher likelihood of experiencing any ACE among men compared to women. A dose–response relationship was provided by several studies between the severity or number of ACEs and the risk of PTSD development. A prospective study found that individuals who experienced a higher number of maltreatment events, such as historical loss, physical neglect, and emotional abuse, had a stronger association with PTSD (4% at 12 months and 6% over a lifetime). The included studies had several methodological strengths, such as the use of validated instruments to assess PTSD and ACEs, and the inclusion of diverse populations. However, common limitations included recall bias, location restrictions, and small sample sizes. Additionally, the lack of randomized controlled trials in the final set of studies should be noted. The findings of this systematic review have important implications for public health interventions and clinical practice. They underscore the need for early identification and targeted interventions to prevent the development of PTSD in adults over 18 with a history of childhood adversity. Clinicians should routinely screen for ACEs and provide trauma-informed care to address the long-term mental health consequences. Additionally, public health efforts should focus on strengthening child protection services and implementing community-based programs to mitigate the impact of ACEs and promote resilience. Discussion This systematic review included twelve primary observational studies consisting of both cross-sectional studies (66.67%) and cohort studies (33.33%). These were included in the review and were published between 2014 to 2025. It includes participants in their late adolescence and adulthood. The review focused on the correlation between childhood abuse and the origin of psychiatric disorders, including posttraumatic stress disorder, but overall, the studies also highlighted depression, anxiety, polydrug use, and suicide attempts as other concerning findings. Various ACEs were considered culprits for the precipitation and development of these mental health disorders. The ACEs included in the selected studies according to the criteria were: (a) Sexual abuse, (b) Emotional abuse, (c) Physical abuse, (d) Emotional neglect, (e) Physical neglect, (f) Witness to intimate partner violence, (g) household dysfunction, (h) perceived discrimination, (i) historical loss, (j) community violence, (k) maltreatment, (l) parent psychopathology, (m) low adversity. The literature focuses on the association of individuals who experienced childhood maltreatment and later developed post-traumatic stress disorder. An epidemiological study conducted by WHO proved a positive association among physical, and sexual abuse, parent psychopathology, neglect, and the odds of developing PTSD (OR = 1.8) after experiencing trauma [ 13 ]. It concludes that parent psychopathology and child maltreatment emerged as a significant risk factors for PTSD and shines light on the age at which trauma is experienced, childhood-adolescence and early-middle adulthood were found to have a stronger effect on the development of PTSD than latter-middle age [ 13 ]. This finding reflects that the development of PTSD deteriorates with age which suggests the increase in protective incidents with age, this can also be explained by recall bias, that as age proceeds the memory of trauma fades [ 13 ]. Furthermore, inaccuracies may have occurred when PTSD was assessed using a standardized interview as opposed to a clinician-administered assessment [ 13 ]. It was observed that physical and sexual abuse were most likely related to PTSD, depression, and anxiety [ 14 , 18 , 24 ]. Adams et al. conclude that even though there is no significant correlation between the duration and severity of abuse with mental health, the onset in early adolescence predisposes more females to develop PTSD as compared to males [ 18 ]. Lee et al. specifically found that community violence and maltreatment increased the odds of PTSD as compared to low adversity [ 14 ]. Breslau studied the severity of maltreatment, especially in the first ten years of life, severe abuse increased the risk of posttraumatic stress by (OR = 2.64, 95% CI (1.16–6.0) concerning moderate neglect or no abuse [ 24 ]. A birth cohort of 1037 followed up to 38 years of age in New Zealand observed that the physical and sexual abuse recalled by the adults, which they faced as a child raised the possibility of bias as they are influenced by adult viewpoints and experiences related to trauma and PTSD later in life [ 24 ]. The sample’s geographic limitations and—more significantly—the range of ages of the adults during the years that data was examined place restrictions on the findings’ generalizability [ 24 ]. Mental health correlations after witnessing IPV can be a difficult event, and each child is affected differently; some develop anxiety, PTSD, and depression, and some resort to polydrug use or even suicidal attempts [ 20 , 21 ]. Brockie et al. concluded that children who witnessed IPV developed PTSD (OR = 2.96, p = &lt; 0.001), some used substances (OR = 5.3, p = &lt; 0.001) in their adult life; children who were physically abused later were diagnosed with depressive symptoms (OR = 3.68, p = &lt; 0.001), some of them also tried to attempt suicide (OR = 2.90, p = &lt; 0.001) [34]. They also explored specific types of abuse (historical loss and discrimination) which showed a strong correlation with mental health. Still, the actual incidence of child abuse has likely been understated due to the use of judicially determined cases [ 20 ]. Kisely et al. is a prospective study where 3778 mothers and children were included, and 4.5% ( n = 171) children suffered from maltreatment. The most prevalent one was emotional abuse ( n = 91), other abuse included physical and emotional neglect, historical loss, and discrimination. [ 21 ] It conducted CIDI Auto (Composite International Diagnostic Interview-Auto version) to diagnose psychiatric disorders at 12 months and over a lifetime, PTSD was noted (4% ( n = 111) and 6% ( n = 161)) to have a strong association with childhood trauma [ 21 ]. Compared to [ 17 , 21 ] presented a retrospective study of PTSD with early developmental trauma ( n = 17), and compared them with health control ( n = 31). Although no statistically significant data was extracted, certain findings were essential to consider [ 21 , 24 ]. Physical, emotional, and sexual abuse was between 6 and 11 years of age, emotional abuse was more seen in people who developed PTSD in their adulthood than in children who faced physical and sexual abuse [ 17 , 21 ]. This study also evaluated the gender and the role of the physical, sexual, and emotional abuser which was found to be male caregiver, outsider male, and female caregiver respectively [ 17 , 21 ]. Whereas [ 18 ] concluded that females when maltreated at an earlier age have a higher propensity to develop PTSD and anxiety. These results are eye-openers and can aid in changing the current preventive measures. Macpherson et al. found a strong connection between childhood maltreatment and mental disorders, with PTSD being most closely correlated (HR 1.68, 95% CI: 1.27–2.20) [ 23 ]. Participants who frequently binge drank or made few social contacts were more likely to exhibit this connection [ 23 ]. The possible mental health effects of abuse were evaluated using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), and it was discovered that these effects persisted 20–70 years after the traumatic incident, even in people who had no prior history of mental problems in their early adult years [ 23 ]. In a separate study, Chen et al. included trauma-exposed adults who sought treatment at an emergency department following a traumatic event [ 16 ]. In each category of reported maltreatment, the participants, who were 33.1 years old on average, claimed varied degrees of abuse throughout their childhood [ 16 ]. Three months after their traumatic event, the criteria for PTSD diagnosis was met by 27.8% of participants based on the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5), and dispositional optimism was found to mediate the relation between PTSD and childhood maltreatment [ 16 ]. Adams J. et al. concluded that the initiation of physical abuse during childhood increased odds of all forms of psychopathology (β = 0.07 for anxiety, β = 0.19 for PTSD, and β = 0.13 for depression, all p &lt; 0.05) and the commencement of physical abuse during childhood and teenage years was linked to a higher number of PTSD symptoms (β = 0.08 and β = 0.11, p &lt; 0.05, respectively), without affecting other kinds of psychopathology [ 18 ]. Additionally, there was a clear correlation between the onset of sexual abuse in adolescence and childhood, and higher levels of symptoms of depression, anxiety and PTSD [ 18 , 19 ]. Lee H. Et al. identified four heterogeneous ACE classes, of these, the “child maltreatment” class had a greater probability of adult PTSD, depression, and anxiety than the “low adversity” class [ 14 ]. A major toxic threat that can be associated with PTSD is community violence exposure, and the effects of various forms of ACEs on young adults’ mental health outcomes vary [ 14 , 25 ]. Kim M. et al. used self-report questionnaires and organized clinical interviews to assess the severity of PTSD concluding that childhood abuse experiences were linked to heightened lifetime PTSD symptoms, with the influence of low resilience levels and the adoption of dysfunctional coping strategies mediating this association [ 15 ]. Rachel A. Maja, et al. assessed participants for ACEs and post-traumatic stress symptoms and presented that adverse PTSD symptoms and worse executive functions related to oneself were connected to an increased number of ACEs that were documented [ 18 ]. ACEs have a positive correlation to the development of PTSD after trauma exposure in adults, which was measured at 2 weeks ( p &lt; 0.01) and 3 months ( p &lt; 0.001) following a traumatic exposure [ 22 ]. This is a unique cohort study that compares and contrasts thalamic nuclei and their volume to the exposure to childhood trauma, the volume of 22 nuclei (n = 50 nuclei) in the PTSD group was found to be decreased as compared to the non-PTSD group [ 22 ]. Points out that the inverse proportionality is only seen in 3 months post-trauma and not in 2 weeks, which signifies several factors that influence and reduce the thalamic volume [ 22 ]. Limitations It is essential to recognize the limitations of these studies. Recall bias in people who do not develop PTSD symptoms is possible, as they might not remember the extent of trauma. Location restrictions and sample size limits also affect how broadly the results can be applied. Some articles were excluded because they were inaccessible or were written in languages other than English. Furthermore, biases should be carefully considered when interpreting data, as there were no randomized controlled trials in the finalized articles. Subsequent investigations should address these constraints while examining innovative interventions and preventive measures. To lessen the long-term effects of ACEs on mental health, we may create more effective therapies using a holistic approach that considers both environmental and individual aspects. Conclusion The systematic review gathered data from 547,068 children, adolescents, and adults, reflecting a wide variety of racial and geographical backgrounds. It unravels the positive relationship between the history of CM and the occurrence of several psychiatric disorders in adulthood. This qualitative study encapsulates the evolution of PTSD signs and symptoms in people with lived experiences of childhood adversities. Hence, it is crucial to evaluate children for abuse, and in case of abuse child protective services should be set up to help them recover. Future reviews should examine and evaluate the impacts of maltreatment on personal growth, self-esteem, self-identity, resilience, and impulsivity to establish concrete associations. ACEs have been associated with mental illnesses such as addiction, anxiety, and depression, all of which can coexist with PTSD. Future research ought to take these comorbidities into account. As a result, more effective health and policy-based interventions can be developed to protect the health of both children and adults [ 26 ].
