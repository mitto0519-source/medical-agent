ABSTRACT The transport system influences health, including the onset of new cancers, in both positive and negative ways. Transport affects the frequency of cancer via access to healthcare, employment and other goods and services of society; physical (in)activity; exposure to transport-related pollution and climate change. In addition, avoidable transport health impacts overload the health system that delivers cancer care. The current transport system in Aotearoa/New Zealand is the result of policy choices made in the last 50–70 years which have privileged the private motor vehicle. We discuss examples of local work to support a healthier transport system, including action research with government partners creating infrastructure to support healthy travel, health professional advocacy in transport policy processes and involvement in coronial processes and a subsequent government advisory group. These, and other attempts at change, have had limited impact because of the elements that hold the current system in place; the weak regulatory architecture and institutions, commercial influences on policy and funding arrangements. Creating a healthy, cancer-preventing transport system requires not just focus on specific policy changes but reform of the policy making apparatus to ensure healthy people and planet are at the centre of decision making. KEYWORDS Cancer prevention climate change population health public policy transport system Access: The transport system affects the onset of new cancers through its influence (which may be positive or negative) on access to the determinants of cancer. For example, the transport system enables access to employment (which may increase or decrease cancer risk depending on the type of employment, money earned etc) and health care (including cancer prevention activities, such as smoking cessation and HPV vaccination). To our knowledge no one has attempted to quantify the health impacts of this pathway and the complexity of pathways may make quantification impossible. Air pollution and contamination : Motor vehicles cause pollution in many forms. Burning fossil fuels releases fine particles, nitrogen dioxide and other compounds such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Photochemical oxidants such as ozone form when tail-pipe emissions interact with sunlight. Brake and tyre wear and road dust also contribute to this mix of contaminants. Many of these pollutants linked with use of motor vehicles are registered by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as carcinogens (that is, there is a plausible association between human exposure and cancer) (Loomis et al. 2013 ). Reviews have consistently confirmed the association between transport-related air pollution and lung cancer (Hamra et al. 2015 ; Boogaard et al. 2022 ). Globally it is estimated that pollution (mostly in the form of air pollution) is responsible for around 43% of lung cancer deaths (Landrigan et al. 2018 ). The most comprehensive New Zealand study to date found a positive but not statistically significant association between exposure to NO 2 (which in Aotearoa is substantially due to motor vehicles) and lung cancer deaths (Hales et al. 2021 ). There is also emerging evidence linking particle air pollution to a wider set of cancer outcomes than just lung cancer. These include breast, bladder and prostate cancer (Kayyal-Tarabeia et al. 2024 ). Most studies of cancer risk and transport emissions have included the general population, recognising that air pollution due to this cause may be widely disseminated (this is true especially for photochemical oxidants). However, research has also been conducted with highly exposed subgroups. For example, it is reported road transportation workers have increased risks of a wide range of cancers that may be related to exposures from vehicles, including liver, respiratory and bladder cancers (Lee et al. 2020 ). Physical activity: Transport is a key source of sustainable physical activity. Research shows that changing to active modes of transport increases overall physical activity (Wanjau et al. 2023 ) as a result of integrating physical activity into daily routines. In contrast, using private vehicles/cars can increase sedentary behaviour (McCormack and Virk 2014 ; Bista et al. 2020 ). Physical activity is associated with numerous health conditions, including cancers. Evidence indicates that increased physical activity reduces the risk of many cancers, including colorectal, breast, endometrial, gastric, oesophageal (adenocarcinoma), renal, bladder, head and neck, lung, liver, ovarian and pancreatic cancers, myeloid leukaemia and myeloma (Moore et al. 2016 ; Rezende et al. 2018 ; McTiernan et al. 2019 ). Potential mechanisms for this association involve a range of physiological processes. Being physically active increases gut motility (reducing faecal transit time), boosts immune function and energy balance, increases the sensitivity of the body to insulin thus reducing high levels of insulin in the blood, modulates sex steroid hormone levels, reduces inflammation body-wide and may also support DNA repair (Peters et al. 2001 ; Jurdana 2021 ). Physical activity may also act through reduced body mass, a known risk factor for many cancers (Bhaskaran et al. 2014 ). On the other hand, sedentary behaviours, such as driving a car, are associated with increased risk of cancer independent of physical activity (de Rezende et al. 2014 ). This may result from metabolic dysfunction, alterations in sex hormone levels, adiposity and associated reduction in vitamin D level (Lynch 2010 ). A recent systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by some authors of this paper found that levels of physical activity recommended by the World Health Organization and Te Whatu Ora (150–300 minutes of moderate-level activity per week (e.g. walking for commute) or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity (e.g. cycling for commute)) could reduce the risk of common cancers, such as breast, colorectal and endometrial cancers (Thu et al. 2024 ). Greenhouse gas emissions/climate change: The transport system produces 16% of greenhouse gas emissions (GHGe) in Aotearoa (Ministry for the Environment 2023 ). Health risks from climate change occur through at least three pathways: acute events (such as floods, storms and droughts), ecosystem-mediated impacts (e.g. through changing patterns of vector-born disease and infectious disease risks, or disruption in production and distribution of food) and through disseminated impacts (e.g. social disruption, conflict, forced migration etc) (Corvalan et al. 2005 ). There are links to cancer in each case, and it is argued climate change will adversely affect the entire cancer control continuum and is likely to increase cancer inequities (Hiatt and Beyeler 2020 ; Nogueira et al. 2023 ). From a cancer prevention perspective, climate change will alter cancer risk through changed exposure to cancer risk factors and disruption of health promotion/cancer prevention services. For example: Acute weather events may lead to disruptions in important cancer prevention services, such as population screening, vaccination and tobacco control (Ortiz et al. 2021 ; Nogueira et al. 2023 ). Medical supply chains may also be impacted, meaning events distant to Aotearoa may impact on cancer prevention here (Nogueira et al. 2023 ). Altered growing conditions combined with acute weather events may result in long-term reductions in fruit and vegetable production. The resulting increase in price of fruit and vegetables will decrease intake, thus increasing the risks of colorectal and breast cancer (Springmann et al. 2016 ; Hiatt and Beyeler 2020 ). Altered patterns of infectious diseases may affect future cancer risk. Although it is not clear if known cancer-causing agents e.g. H. pylori, HPV are impacted by climate change (Hiatt and Beyeler 2020 ), recent work indicates that climatic conditions influence over half of infectious diseases (Mora et al. 2022 ). Increased exposure to particulate air pollution from altered climatic conditions and events such as wildfires may result in increasing lung cancer risk (Hiatt and Beyeler 2020 ). Increases in the frequency and severity of extreme weather events, such as wildfires and floods, are already resulting in increased dispersal of carcinogenic chemicals into the environment (Hiatt and Beyeler 2020 ; Nogueira et al. 2023 ). Importantly, but less directly, the wider burden of avoidable transport-related harm places pressure on the whole healthcare system, in turn creating competing demands for services across the cancer control continuum. These competing demands are strongly influenced by transport system design, policy settings and rules. For example, recent policies in Aotearoa to increase speed limits will result in increased deaths and serious injuries from crashes (Vecino-Ortiz et al. 2022 ; Yannis and Michelaraki 2024 ). Moreover, collectively the removal of the clean car discount and weakening of pollution standards for imported vehicles will perpetuate the high levels of health harm from transport air pollution (Metcalfe and Kuschel 2023 ) and may exacerbate them if these policies result in the ‘dumping’ of vehicles with poor emissions profiles into the local market as a result of the weak standards. What would a healthy transport system look like and why do we not have one? Following on from the links between transport and cancer, we can see that a transport system that supports cancer prevention, and health more generally, would be one that promotes high levels of walking, cycling and use of public transport and correspondingly low levels of private car use (Nieuwenhuijsen 2020 ). Not only would this system prevent cancer through reduced pollution, physical inactivity and greenhouse gas emissions, it would also come with the benefit of drastic reductions in road injury and economic, social and transport benefits (Ding 2024 ). Coordinating our transport system with where we choose to build houses, places of employment, shops and other important services (integrated transport and land use planning) can ensure that all of these benefits also come with enhanced, and more equitable, access benefits, allowing everyone, even those who need to drive, to get to the destinations they need to (Mueller et al. 2021 ). However, due largely to the reinforcing and interconnected characteristics of car dependence that keep transport stuck in the status quo, we have a transport system that is very far from healthy and sustainable (Macmillan et al. 2021 ). We have seen the forces of car dependence play out over the last 70 years, starting in the post-war era of the 1950s to the 1970s, which saw reductions in private car production costs, increased global political power of automotive and oil industries, a shift to suburban housing development and a corresponding increase in road building to support estimated levels of future car use and the start of the decline in public transport, walking and cycling levels (Coleman 2010 ; Mattioli et al. 2020 ). Other transport policy in the 1980s and 90s further entrenched the dominance of the car by reducing investment in public transport, reducing vehicle import costs and removing regulation that previously constrained urban sprawl (Lee and Rivasplata 2001 ; Imran and Pearce 2015 ). These effects continue today with the so-called ‘light touch’ approach to government intervention in transport that favours individual responsibility and market-led solutions to decarbonising the transport system, despite also heavily investing in roading infrastructure to support the status quo (Macmillan et al. 2021 ). Similar patterns have been seen in many car-dependent societies around the world where strong car cultures have developed that allow for large-scale use of public land for the movement and storage of private vehicles and the shifting use of economic arguments that justify these (Urry 2004 ; Mattioli et al. 2020 ). Over many years of working on transport and health, we have seen many of the reinforcing and interdependent forces of car dependence work against good-quality, evidence-based transport decision making. The next section presents three case studies from our collective work that demonstrate this and highlight the barriers to sustainable transport from the social, political and commercial determinants of car dependence. Part 2: case studies of attempts to create policy change within the transport system The previous section outlined how the current unhealthy transport system is the result of a series of deliberate political decisions that have been made over time. These decisions have led, rather than responded to, changes in population transport behaviours. The implication of this is that there is nothing inevitable about an unhealthy transport system; different decisions can lead towards a healthier transport system. This section outlines three case studies the authors have been involved in to try to shift towards a healthy, cancer-preventing transport system in Aotearoa and the barriers these face within the current system. Working with local government to change local infrastructure: Te Ara Mua project overview Te Ara Mua Future Streets has been a community-based epidemiological study and a piece of long-term action research (Macmillan et al. 2018 ). The aims of the project were to address the complex transport–health equity links described above by retrofitting existing streets to enhance the suburban physical environment. The project is sited in Ngā Hau Māngere in Tāmaki Makaurau, an area with long-term experience of social, transport and health inequities, including high road traffic crash risks (Auckland Transport 2012 unpublished data). Over time, the project has brought together research funding (MBIE, HRC, Healthier Lives and Aging Well National Science Challenges), with local board, council and transport agency infrastructure funding. Transdisciplinary partnership is at the heart of the project, bringing mana whenua, community, local government and academic knowledges together in a co-designed street retrofit (Mackie et al. 2018 ). Co-design workshops identified the destinations and routes that residents wanted to use and described the barriers to walking and wheeling (by bike, wheelchair, scooter, mobility scooter etc.), including speed and volume of traffic, fear of crime and lack of accessibility. Infrastructure was then designed and put in place to improve this experience. This was funded by the local board and the transport agencies. Most of the $11M spent was on improving walkability, slowing traffic, re-Indigenising streets and places and making the area safer from crime through environmental design (e.g. lighting, fencing, planting). Bike lanes were also created, including physically separated lanes on the busiest roads. The infrastructure changes in Māngere Central then became a foundation and stimulus for further community-led action. This included the Māngere Bike Hub, researcher–community partnerships, e-bike trials and wider network-level walking and wheeling infrastructure investment by Auckland Transport. A range of baseline and follow-up measures in the intervention and control areas have helped to understand the effects on transport and health outcomes. We have been able to demonstrate the early benefits of the changes on reducing the speed and volume of traffic on local streets (Hosking et al. 2023 ), leading to reductions in traffic crashes, particularly between cars and people walking and wheeling. In turn, mixed effects have been seen so far on people’s perceptions of safety (Witten et al. 2022 ), with survey data suggesting improvements in sense of safety from crime but not traffic and qualitative interviews suggesting the opposite. Analysis of longer-term follow-up data about active travel is beginning to show promising signs, despite COVID disrupting both travel patterns and data collection. The combination of safer biking infrastructure and community-led activation of cycling has led to an increase in cycle counts in the intervention area compared to the control area. Wheelchair and small-wheeled vehicle trips have also seen a substantial increase, suggesting improvements in accessibility. Walking, already a highly used mode in the area, has not seen a consistent increase. The research status of Te Ara Mua Future Streets enabled the demonstration of approaches that, at the time, were outside standard transport sector design guidance in Aotearoa. These included raised side-road crossings and novel bike lane/bus stop designs. These have now become accepted practice. Moreover, subsequent institutional changes in transport agencies and councils were, at least in part, stimulated by Te Ara Mua Future Streets, including a new focus on suburbs, the creation of structures to support ‘innovation' and the beginnings of what has now become greatly improved community engagement. Despite its success, Te Ara Mua Future Streets also highlights some important challenges. Flow-on suburban retrofit programmes, (e.g. Waka Kotahi’s Innovating Streets programme) continue to reinforce the notion that healthy streets are ‘experimental' and outside business-as-usual. Despite wider investment, the intervention area remains an ‘island’ of traffic calming in a ‘sea' of transport system signals reinforcing car dominance in suburban environments. Convincingly evaluating benefit requires systems thinking, a mixture of data collection methods, long-term follow-up, combined with modelling. For a successful just transition towards a cancer-preventing transport system, this work suggests a mixture of street changes, social activation programmes, institutional change and wider disincentives to car use will be needed. Health professional advocacy Doctors for Active Safe Transport (DAST) is a collective of hospital-based specialists originating in Wellington Hospital. The focus of DAST has been on translating the evidence about how to create a healthy transport system into policy and to ensure that health is considered in decision making around transport in Aotearoa. Their activities cover three broad domains. Firstly, DAST has specifically identified the different levels at which transport investment decisions occur and made numerous submissions at each of these levels. These are at national (e.g. the Government Policy Statement on Land Transport), regional (e.g. the Wellington Regional Policy Statement) and local levels (e.g. the District Plans of the cities in the Wellington Region). In DAST’s experience there has not yet been widespread willingness to broaden the decision criteria for transport investment to meaningfully include health outcomes. Secondly, DAST has submitted on numerous annual and long-term plans of local authorities. These local government plans encompass a vast array of responsibilities (e.g. water and transport infrastructure) within very constrained budgets. DAST has had some successes here (for example, supporting projects for on-street cycleways that involved re-allocation of street space away from on-street parking) with health professionals able to use the health evidence and perspective to create an alternative viewpoint to entrenched and polarised views that often dominate these processes. Finally, DAST has submitted on numerous individual transport projects. In the case of new cycle infrastructure, DAST endorsement has supported several successful projects, generally where there was already political willingness to consider change. In other settings, such as consents for large projects or for private infrastructure developments, the weight that the Resource Management Act gives to experts over public submitters (e.g. communities and advocacy groups such as DAST) strongly favours commercial interests or government agencies who have the financial capacity to fund expert and legal support. These experiences have collectively resulted in some wins and, at times, considerable frustration. Our reflections on this experience include: Existing policy frameworks for transport are complex. While ‘health outcomes’ are often referenced in the narratives of these documents, in DAST’s experience there has not yet been widespread willingness to broaden the decision criteria for transport investment to meaningfully include health outcomes. The absence of this means that health (over and above injury impacts) will always remain an optional extra on decision making and certainly not integral to project objectives or design. While DAST has had more success with local government processes than central government, it is also obvious that the dramatic impact of active transport on health outcomes is little appreciated by most decision makers. The ‘rules’ for transport system decision making are systematically biased when it comes to considering health impacts. For example, economic assessments of transport infrastructure consider the physical activity-related health benefits of projects that increase walking and cycling but do not include the physical activity dis-benefits (i.e. reductions) of projects which increase private vehicle use. (New Zealand Transport Agency 2024 ) Participation in coronial inquiry and National Cycling Safety Panel In 2011, an unusually high number of cyclist deaths across the country prompted a coronial inquiry into cycling safety in Aotearoa. The following year, Coroner Matenga examined the 13 deaths from 2011 (Matenga 2013 ). During the progress of the review, the coroner was encouraged to put these deaths in the context of cycling deaths over a longer timeframe and to look at wider factors than the circumstances of each particular death. Submissions from cycling safety experts, cycling advocate groups and a public health physician attempted to shift the coroner’s gaze from individual mistakes to the transport system context and to consider both the benefits and harms of cycling. This is relevant to a wide range of health outcomes, as improving the safety of cycling facilitates wider uptake of cycling and thus increases physical activity (with associated health benefits). These submissions and analysis, alongside an earlier Canadian coronial review, had a major influence on the direction of the review report and resulted in the coroner recommending that a government cycling safety panel be convened, ‘with a view to compiling a list of recommendations to central and local government which will improve cycling safety in New Zealand and prevent further cycling crashes and fatalities' (Matenga 2013 ). As a result, the National Cycling Safety Panel was convened by the Minister of Transport in 2014 (National Cycling Safety Panel 2014 ), comprising national expertise in cycling safety, including public health expertise. Along with health expertise, the panel also included transport safety engineers and researchers, cycle skills training leaders, an Olympic cyclist and a representative of the Automobile Association. Cycling advocacy groups were excluded from the panel. Over nine months, evidence and research was summarised and debated, workshops to garner wide-ranging views were held and recommendations were drafted that took a whole-system approach to making cycling safer and more attractive. Sixteen recommendations were made in the final report (National Cycling Safety Panel 2014 ). The highest-priority recommendation was to substantially increase investment in active travel infrastructure, especially in cities, by giving greater priority to walking and cycling in all land transport planning and investment decisions. The second priority was to implement a safer speeds programme. Mandates for improved driver behaviour through minimum passing distances and driver education were third. The panel's recommendations were largely accepted by the government of the day (led by John Key), who went on to make the largest investment in urban cycleways of any Aotearoa government (Bridges 2015 ). Improving active travel infrastructure and reducing speeds were also a strategic priority for subsequent governments. However, more recently, active travel infrastructure projects have been halted (Martin 2023 ), while progress on speed management has been reversed (Brown and Seymour 2024 ). These reversals underscore the need for ongoing evidence-informed advocacy to continue progress. Part 3: key barriers to change It is important to understand that there has been clear evidence of both the links between transport and health and the policy needed to deliver a better system for a number of decades (Santos et al. 2010a ; Santos et al. 2010a ; Jaramillo et al. 2023 ). Despite this, and despite efforts to create change made by researchers, advocates and civil society over many decades, progress towards a healthier transport system has been patchy, and there is significant inertia and, at times, regression. Many individuals are already aware of the health, social and environmental benefits of active travel and demonstrating leadership by making individual change in taking fewer trips by car. Often this experience is a positive one for individual health and happiness. On the other hand, attempts to change also come up against substantial structural barriers, making walking, biking and wheelchair trips feel unpleasant, dangerous and inconvenient, as well as socially abnormal, even strange, within a dominant car culture. Ultimately, to create the shift in the transport system required to address health and sustainability, deeper structural and systemic changes are needed that are beyond what can be achieved by individuals changing their travel choices (Faherty and Morrissey 2014 ). There are many specific barriers that are preventing change, some of which are illustrated in the case studies. However, from the authors’ experience, we recognise three important deeper structural features that serve to hold in place the current unhealthy system of car dependence and automobility. This section outlines these. Weak regulatory and institutional architecture A transition to a sustainable and healthy transport system will involve a wide range of actors. Governance and policy institutions are just some of these actors. However, these institutions have consistently held back the large-scale, sustained shift in policy, planning and practice required to create the type of transport system we need. Legislation, such as the Land Transport Management Act 2003 and the Resource Management Act 1991 , have seen weakened provisions for health and sustainability over successive governments. The reliance on high-level policy documents, such as the Government Policy Statement on Land Transport and the Transport Outcomes Framework, that can be changed or discarded easily has supported the politicisation of transport planning issues and short-term policy cycles that slow (or reverse) progress. This is further complicated by the complex regulatory system that governs transport (illustrated in Case Study 2), involving the Ministry of Transport, NZTA, regional councils and city or district councils, among others, that often cannot shift fast enough to keep up with the frequent changes in high-level policy direction, as evidenced by underspent budgets for active travel (Macmillan et al. 2021 ; Crossland et al. 2022 ). On top of this, the siloed approach to transport, land use, health, climate change and environmental management sees institutions remaining entrenched in outdated transport planning and engineering paradigms, focusing on technical road design and systematically biased economic analysis with limited expertise in innovative and participatory planning that would support active and sustainable transport development (Broaddus 2020 ; Macmillan et al. 2021 ; Crossland et al. 2022 ). Funding model If we want to shift to a healthy transport system, we need to put in place the policies, infrastructure and support to deliver it. Much of this will require sustained funding. However, as illustrated by the case studies, sufficient expenditure on sustainable transport is not ‘business-as-usual’, instead needing considerable effort to justify it on a case-by-case basis. Moreover, even ‘record’ amounts of funding in active or public transport have been inadequate, may be tied to a single project rather than the wider change needed and have not been effectively bedded into business-as-usual, thus risk being easily removed when government priorities change (Macmillan et al. 2021 ). One of the reasons that sustained funding for modes other than cars have struggled is the hypothecated funding structure of transport in Aotearoa. Since the 1950s, road-building has been the direct result of a funding model that is underpinned by the view that taxes should directly benefit those who paid them (as opposed to the whole of society). Fuel taxes, road user charges and motor vehicle registration charges have all been fed directly into a specific fund: the National Land Transport Fund (NLTF). This hypothecated funding model has created a ‘social contract’ whereby the expectation is that the fund be mainly used for road-building and maintenance, and reducing congestion (Ministry of Transport 2014 ). The design also creates a relationship of influence between transport decision makers and their main sources of NLTF revenue: representatives of private vehicle owners like the Automobile Association and road freight industry companies and their representatives. It has also become increasingly clear that the funding model for transport is inadequate. The NLTF fund is insufficient as a source of income for the demands of the transport system and is itself under strain as the light fleet moves to electric vehicles. The proportion of transport expenditure funded directly from government rather than the NTLF has been rising in the last decade and is now around 30% (Ministry of Transport 2021 ). In the next decade, to meet the promises made in the GPS on Land Transport 2024, the government needs over $6 billion in government grants and loans, an additional $1 billion from the budget and private finance through public–private partnerships. This is on top of the money collected from road users and fuel taxes (Ministry of Transport 2024b ). The Ministry of Transport is aware of the issues with transport funding and has been working for the past decade on alternative funding options (Ministry of Transport 2024a ). It is still unclear how transport will be funded in the future. However, a change in funding model represents an opportunity to consider how funding might support a healthy transport system. This funding system should be designed to produce maximum social (including health) and environmental benefits and ensure a fairer distribution of the costs and benefits across all of society (Randal et al. 2020 ). Commercial influences The commercial sector is integrated into the existing transport system as both users and providers of transport services and infrastructure. For example, businesses need people and products to be mobile. Several major commercial sectors are also involved with the provision of transport, including the automotive, freight, fossil fuel and civil construction industries. It suits many of these economically powerful interests to have substantial political support and public subsidies for roads and parking, as this infrastructure is fundamental to their business model (Woodcock and Aldred 2008 ; Mattioli et al. 2020 ). We know from research into other unhealthy industries (such as tobacco, alcohol and junk food) that when profits are threatened, commercial actors may employ a range of methods to ward off or weaken policy intended to benefit public health (Ulucanlar et al. 2023 ). The fossil fuel industry is well known for ongoing elaborate strategies of climate denial and delay (van Schalkwyk et al. 2023 ). Internationally and locally, automotive and freight lobby groups are also involved in shaping transport policy which has significant health impacts, such as vehicle emissions regulations (Sick 2023 ; Influence Map 2024 ; Pullar-Strecker 2024 ). It is therefore increasingly acknowledged that transport industries must be viewed as another ‘commercial determinant of health' (Gilmore et al. 2023 ), with urgent questions to be answered about how these commercial actors may be involved in sustaining the dominance of private road transport at the expense of health (Macmillan et al. 2021 ). Conclusion The current transport system in Aotearoa is unhealthy (its effects include increasing the incidence of cancer and overloading in other ways the health system that delivers cancer care), inequitable (both in who enjoys the benefits and who experiences the adverse health outcomes) and directly contributes to destruction of the planetary systems that sustain human life. In general, we know what needs to be done; there are plenty of evidence-backed policies that would make transport healthier, fairer and more sustainable. But meaningful progress to change the system is lacking. We have argued in this paper that the institutions and actors involved in the system are part of the barriers to change. Solutions to this impasse require more than changes in policy instruments and may also need to involve new institutions, new funding arrangements, stronger identification and management of conflicts of interest and legislation that places healthy people and planet at the centre of the transport system.
