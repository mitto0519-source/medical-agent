See: http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/ . Abstract Insufficient physical activity is associated with numerous widespread diseases, productivity loss, and sick leave at the workplace. A physically active way to work is a potential resource for implementing regular physical activity into daily life. However, there is a lack of data on the duration and physical exertion involved and the potential health benefits of physically active commuting compared with passive commuting. Digitalisation for Sustainable Mobility (DiNaMo)-active is a prospective, observational trial to characterise commuting habits among employees and students at four higher educational institutions in Hannover (Germany) and estimate their short and long-term impact on health-related and economic issues. We will use wearable activity trackers and questionnaires to examine the long-term influences of active (by foot or by bicycle) or passive (eg, private cars, motorbikes, scooters or public transportation) commuting on physical and mental outcomes among students and university employees. We will also apply more in-depth physiological measures like continuous glucose monitoring, ambulatory blood pressure recordings and particulate matter concentrations to compare the short-term effects of active versus passive commuting on physiological regulations during commuting, working and time after work. Facilitating physically active and sustainable transportation could contribute to a healthier lifestyle and reduce road traffic and greenhouse gas emissions. The way to work as a potential resource for a physically active and sustainable form of commuting is rarely studied, and our results might provide relevant information for policymakers and occupational health experts. This observational trial is prospectively registered (DiNaMo-active NCT06493006 ). Protocol version 1.0 (15 April 2025). WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC Insufficient activity increases during life span, especially for the working population. The type of commuting to work affects employees’ health, well-being and economics. WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS Digitalisation for Sustainable Mobility-active provides observational data on commuting behaviours using wearables and surveys, related to physiological measurements (eg, heart rate variability, blood glucose, blood pressure and air pollution exposure). It combines long-term and short-term assessments of physical and mental health in students and employees of universities. HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY Findings may support policies encouraging active and sustainable commuting. It may help develop targeted interventions for improving employee well-being and reducing environmental and infrastructural burdens. Background The United Nations developed binding recommendations for governments to address the growing challenge of sustainable development. 1 Companies, institutions and individuals worldwide are called on to promote prosperity while protecting the planet. Higher education institutions have an explanatory function in developing strategies to achieve the mentioned objectives, 2 and they could help test promising measures for implementing and transferring sustainability into practice. In 2013, the German Federal Ministry of Education and Research (BMBF) launched the ‘Sustainability in Science’ initiative to address sustainability at German universities. This initiative is part of the BMBF’s broader social-ecological research agenda and is intended to contribute to achieving the ‘Sustainable Development Goals’ of the United Nations 2030 Agenda. The type of commuting, whether by motorised vehicles, public transport or more active modes (eg, walking or cycling), touches on several sustainability issues, from environmental to health-economical aspects. Physically active commuting can decrease mobility-related use of fossil fuels and air pollution and affect health-related conditions and well-being. 3 , 6 Economic aspects of reduced physical activity (PA) include workers’ health status and work ability. 7 Both are related to productivity, sick leave, and healthcare costs in the working population. 8 9 Currently, more than 30% of adults worldwide do not meet the WHO activity recommendations, 6 which is an increase of 23% compared with 2000 and in contrast to the 2030 World Health Assembly target 10 of a 15% reduction of insufficient PA. The ongoing development of motorised transportation, which reduces the need to commute physically, likely contributes to the high prevalence of inactive and sedentary behaviour. In this regard, the active way to work is an untapped resource to increase PA for the working population, whereas leisure-time PA is often time-limited. Whether active commuting influences mental and physical aspects of health and subjective quality of life is understudied and not certain. 4 The objective of the current observational study is to investigate if a physically active way to work has positive acute and chronic effects on mental and physical health-related outcomes of employees and students at university institutions when compared with passive commuting. Methods Study design DiNaMo (‘Digitalisation for Sustainable Mobility’) is a prospective, multisectoral, observational trial that aims to develop and test strategies to increase sustainable mobility through digitalisation at higher educational institutions in Hannover (Lower Saxony, Germany). The study is led by the Leibniz University Hannover (LUH) as a study centre with the Hannover Medical School (MHH), the University of Applied Sciences and Arts Hannover and the Leibniz Information Centre for Science and Technology as study sites. As part of this project, the Department of Rehabilitation and Sports Medicine of the MHH will conduct the substudy DiNaMo-active, which will evaluate data on mobility characteristics and physiological parameters during active versus passive commuting. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov initially on 2 July 2024, with the identifier NCT06493006 . This protocol concurs with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials reporting guidelines. 11 Recruitment and allocation of participants The study centre leads the first contact and screening of potential study participants. The LUH will send an invitation to participate in DiNaMo to all employees and students of the study sites. The invitation email is linked to an initial questionnaire, which includes information about the study’s background, purpose, risk and data privacy aspects and giving consent to participate in DiNaMo. The questionnaire collects general sociodemographic data and information about different habits and transport modes of commuting to work. At the end of the questionnaire, the participant is asked to agree to be contacted again for further evaluations like the substudy DiNaMo-active. To be eligible for the study, volunteers must be employees or students of one of the study sites with a minimum age of 18 years and a place of residence within 1–50 km of their place of work or study. Exclusion criteria are contemporaneous participation in another interventional trial. The participants who agreed to be contacted again received specific information and a questionnaire for the substudy DiNaMo-active via email ( online supplemental file 1 , online supplemental file 2 ). All participants will be divided into two subgroups according to their commuting habits. Subgroup A: Participants use physically active and passive types to work. Aim: to examine short-term effects of active vs passive commuting within the same individual. Subgroup B: Participants who do not change their type of commuting. Aim: to examine the long-term effects of permanently commuting physically active or passive between individuals. Measurements and data collection All collected data will be recorded and stored pseudonymised, and the trial staff must comply with the requirements of the data protection law of the European Privacy legislation. The data are inaccessible to third parties; only anonymised data will be published. The overall health conditions of all participants will be collected using a regular anamnesis form, which will collect sociodemographic and health data, including personal medical history and musculoskeletal complaints. The health-related quality of life will be estimated by the Short Form 36 (SF-36), 12 in eight subscales representing mental and physical quality of life. Both sum scales range from 0 (minimum quality of life) to 100 (maximum quality of life). The total and exercise-related PA will be assessed by the Freiburger PA questionnaire 13 and represented as metabolic-equivalents-of-task hours per week. The Work Ability Index (WAI) 14 represents work ability and work-related health with a total score ranging from 7 to 49, and higher scores represent higher work ability). An activity tracker worn on the non-dominant wrist (Forerunner 55, Garmin, Olathe, Kansas, USA) will be used to measure heart rate, heart rate variability (HRV), duration and distance of commuting, daily PA and steps in both subgroups. The collected data will be retrieved and processed via the software Fitrockr (Digital Rebels GmbH, Berlin, Germany). Subgroup A: To examine intraindividual short-term effects of active or passive commuting, participants in subgroup A will be equipped with the above-mentioned activity tracker and additional sensors on two separate days. Measurements for the more specific evaluation of acute effects of the mode of commute include arterial blood pressure, blood glucose concentrations and the exposition to air pollution assessed by fine particulate matter (PM) concentrations during commuting. The 24-hour blood pressure monitoring is conducted by the TM-4250 boso (Bosch+Sohn Gmb, Jungingen, Germany). The systolic and diastolic blood pressure will be measured every 20 min between 6:00 and 22:00 and every 30 min at night. The participants will start additional measures manually before and after the commute. The measured data will be exported through the boso profilmanager XD Software and edited by Office Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond Washington, USA) and SPSS Statistics V.29 (International Business Machines, Armonk New York, USA). For continuous blood glucose monitoring, the Freestyle Libre 3 (Abbott, Wiesbaden, Germany) will be used. Via FreeStyle Libre 3 and FreeStyle Libre View, participants and researchers can view the blood glucose levels throughout the day. The data will be exported to Excel and SPSS. To estimate the PM air pollution during the commute, the Central Research Devices Service Unit of the MHH developed a specified sensor. These data logger captures the PM concentration (particle sizes PM 1.0, PM 2.5, PM 4.0 and PM 10) and records temperature, humidity, global positioning system (GPS) logs, date and time. The PM 1.0, PM 4.0 and PM 10 are calculated. Further evaluation will be done in Excel and SPSS. Subgroup B: To examine interindividual long-term effects of generally commuting physically active or passive, participants in subgroup B will be equipped with the activity tracker for a consecutive period of 7 days. The organisation of the measurement period for both subgroups All participants will be contacted by email for an initial face-to-face consultation (V1). The following day at work should be the examination day (T1), which will record the commute from home to the university and back. For this purpose, a detailed introduction to the measuring devices will be provided during V1. The devices will be applied and checked for proper functioning, and participants will be trained on usage (manually starting and stopping recordings) and potential sources of errors and troubleshooting. Table 1 provides a detailed overview of the measurement schedule. Subgroup A receives the date for the second measurement day within 2–14 days, scheduled on the same weekday/shift, for a higher comparability. Subgroup B completes the observational timeframe within 1 week (five workdays, two weekend days) with no further visits needed. The same procedures as those performed on the way to work should be followed on the way home. The activity device and the blood pressure monitor have to be worn over 24 hours and may be taken off the next morning. Subgroup B will continue to wear the activity tracker and repeat the measures and protocolling from T1 for the following five workdays. The activity device should be worn during the weekend without additional recordings as necessary for the commute. Subgroup B will return the activity device and documentation diary to the study team to complete the trial. Subgroup A will repeat the measures with another type of commuting 1 week afterwards (T2) in the same manner as for T1. Sample size calculation To our knowledge, there is no appropriate previous literature on conducting a formal sample size calculation for our study. We aim to enrol an overall sample of 500 participants within 18 months. Study outcomes The primary outcome is the HRV, an objectively measurable variable of the autonomic balance of the nervous system and a general indicator of the participant’s health status. Secondary outcomes include the daily minutes of moderate to vigorous PA to assess the possibility of fulfilling the WHO recommendations for PA of 150 min per week, the scores of the questionnaire surveys (SF-36, WAI), as well as acute changes in heart rate, blood glucose concentration, 24-hour blood pressure and exposure to fine PM concentration during commuting. Data management and statistical analysis Data are acquired using the above-mentioned device’s software and merged in Excel. Statistical processing and analysis are performed using SPSS and GraphPad Prism V.8.0 (GraphPad Software, California, USA). Normality distribution was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. In descriptive analysis, absolute frequencies were calculated for categorical variables and mean and SD for continuous variables. The significance level is set at p&lt;0.05. The effect size of significant changes will be calculated by Eta 2 (&lt;0.06 small effect; 0.06–0.14 medium effect; &gt;0.14 large effect). For the (outcome) analysis in subgroup A, within-subject differences between the two observation periods were tested using a two-sided Student’s t-test for dependent samples. Group comparisons in subgroup B will be done using a Student’s t-test for independent samples, and we will use adjusted regression analyses to determine associations between the active/passive way of commuting and our defined outcomes. In the case of missing data (eg, due to measurement errors), participants are asked to repeat the measurement. Participants are excluded from the analysis if they wish to discontinue, withdraw their consent or refuse to repeat a measurement. Discussion Sustainable and physically active commuting is highly researched worldwide. 15 , 17 Active commuting by bicycle is associated with the lowest risk of cardiovascular disease incidence and mortality, followed by commuting by walking and mixed commuting modes. 18 An active way to work has also been linked to increased health-related quality of life and decreased sickness-related absence from work. 16 19 20 Whereas the proportion of non-active commuters normally ranges between 97% and 72% in previous trials, 18 21 the highest rates for physically active transportation are reached in European countries compared with the USA, Australia and Canada. 22 For this real-world observational study, we chose HRV as our primary outcome, as it is a non-invasive and inexpensive marker of an individual’s autonomic regulation and stress level. Reduced HRV is a known independent risk factor for sudden cardiac events and death in the general population 23 24 in the elderly. 25 It is also related to the number of metabolic syndrome components and manifest diabetes mellitus. 26 Shortcomings of HRV measurements in daily life include the lack of specificity due to the high complexity of sympathetic and parasympathetic cardiac modulation. 27 In addition, lower resting HRV suggests increased physiological and psychological arousal 28 29 but has to be interpreted with caution because the results of different trials are inconsistent. 30 Our secondary outcomes allow observing more precise physiological reactions as a function of commuting behaviour. This could lead to clinically relevant results and further research questions to set up studies with a predefined number of cases and appropriate statistical power. This study is the first to evaluate the effects of active vs passive commuting to work in German higher educational institutions using objective PA and physiological measures in addition to self-reported questionnaire-based outcomes. This project might help policy-makers and occupational health experts to discuss measures for implementing sustainability goals through individually beneficial behavioural changes in the working population. Trial status The screening started in August 2024 and the recruitment will be approximately completed in December 2025.
