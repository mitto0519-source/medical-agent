Abstract Introduction Direct comparisons of both short-term and long-term auxological outcomes of growth hormone therapy (GHT) between growth hormone deficiency (GHD) and small for gestational age (SGA) are scarce. Methods One hundred three patients with GHD and 53 patients with SGA treated at our tertiary center were investigated. Short-term and long-term outcomes were compared between these groups using multivariable linear regression models with adjustment for age, sex, and height at therapy start, also allowing for sex-specific group comparisons. Results Mean delta height standard deviation scores (SDS) after 1 year of treatment were significantly higher in GHD (0.90, CI: 0.82–0.99) compared to SGA (0.67, CI: 0.54–0.79) ( p = 0.003) with no sex difference. As expected, the mean increase in height SDS at final height (FH) was significantly higher in GHD (2.21, CI: 2.00–2.42) compared to SGA (1.05, CI: 0.75–1.35) ( p &lt; 0.001), leading to a target height corrected FH of −0.39 SDS (CI: −0.62 to −0.15) in GHD and −1.22 SDS (CI: −1.57 to −0.87) in SGA ( p &lt; 0.001). Girls with GHD had a better long-term outcome, as did boys with SGA when compared to the respective opposite sex. The cut-off of delta height of 0.5 SDS during the first year had a low sensitivity to detect long-term non-responders. We found a relation between short-term and long-term outcomes in GHD but not in SGA (adjusted R 2 = 0.66 vs. 0.01). Conclusion In contrast to GHD, we observed practically no relationship between 1st-year and long-term outcomes in SGA patients treated with GH. Keywords Growth hormone therapy Growth hormone deficiency Small for gestational age Final height Response This study was not supported by any sponsor or funder. The majority of patients had the complete work-up for short stature at our Pediatric Endocrine outpatient clinic following evidence-based procedures [ 13 ] performed by the core team consisting of four experienced Pediatric Endocrinologists over the years. The final sample included 53 patients with SGA and 103 patients with GHD. In all of these patients, data on at least the first year of GHT were available. Final height data (see below) at the time of analysis were available in 61 patients with GHD and 33 patients with SGA; ongoing GHT is documented for 28 patients with GHD and 8 patients with SGA. The diagnosis “Small for gestational age without catch-up growth” (short SGA) followed definitions in consensus guidelines [ 3 , 4 ]: birth length and/or birth weight below −2.0 SDS, calculated on the basis of national reference data [ 14 ], actual height of &lt; −2.5 SDS and a discrepancy to target height (TH) of at least 1.0 SDS. Patients who met the auxological definitions were not routinely tested for GHD by stimulation tests if their IGF-I level was in the normal range and/or prenatal patient history documented placental insufficiency, calcifications, maternal illness or drug consumption. Results of GH testing in SGA were available in 21 of originally 57 patients and abnormal in 4 of 21. In case of pathological GH testing (diagnosis of GHD, see below) in a patient born SGA, the diagnostic group definition was declared GHD. Genetic evaluations were performed when a postnatal growth disorder was present with (SGA, see above) or without a prenatal growth disorder. This included cytogenetic studies and, whole exome and clinical exome investigations once it became available (a total of 25 patients with genetic investigations and normal results). The diagnosis of GHD was made after structured clinical and laboratory exclusion procedures as described previously [ 15 ]. One stimulation test was considered sufficient if the first showed a response &lt;3 ng/mL and IGF-I was below −3.0 SDS ( n = 3). When two tests were performed, the higher response of the two was used for the classification of GHD. Serum GH was initially determined by in-house RIA, followed by the Medignost R assay and finally by the IDS-iSYS R assay. The cut-off for biochemical GHD was 7.0 ng/mL [ 16 , 17 ] using the IDS-iSYS R assay. Results obtained by previously used assays were adapted according to published references [ 16 ]. In the GHD group, 23 patients had additional TSH/thyroxin, 4 patients had additional ACTH/cortisol deficiency, and no patient developed additional gonadotropin deficiency. All deficiencies were substituted in physiological concentrations and monitored regularly. GHT and Core Visits GH starting dose was 25 μg/kg/day for GHD and 35 μg/kg/day for SGA and was weight adapted in the further course. IGF-I levels were determined every 6 months in GHD to document the expected rise to average serum concentrations and eventually prompt the increase in GH dose. In some young children with genetic or radiological abnormalities IGF-I normalized and the “target height channel or percentile” was reached although GH dosage was not weight adapted. In patients with SGA, IGF-I levels in the upper normal range were expected; therefore, in case of higher levels (&gt;2.0 SDS), the dosage was not adapted to weight until serum IGF-I dropped below 2.0 SDS again. The ranges of GH doses were 16–30 μg/kg/day in GHD and 28–38 μg/kg/day in SGA. GH dose was not increased during puberty. GHT was ended when height velocity dropped to &lt;2 cm/year and/or bone age (Greulich&amp;Pyle, Lit) was &gt;15 years in girls and 16 years in boys, except in 8 patients with genetic or radiological abnormalities and 2 or more other pituitary deficiencies in whom GH dose was switched to 10 μg/kg/day. A further visit at least 12 months after the end of therapy was used to define “final height” (FH), if growth since the end of therapy was less than 6 mm and/or bone age was 16 years in girls or 17 years in boys. In patients who did not participate in this visit ( n = 14), a height prognosis was based on the last registered bone age and used for the calculation of final height. Auxology Standing height (≥2 years) and body length (&lt;2 years) were measured using the respective Harpenden stadiometer. Standard deviation Scores (SDS) at birth and from 4 to 19 years were calculated using Austrian references [ 18 , 19 ] and based on WHO standards between 0 and &lt;4 years [ 20 ]. For the calculation of final height (SDS), adult references at 19 years were applied irrespective of the actual age when the final height was attained. Parameters to Evaluate Short-Term and Long-Term Outcome under GHT For the quantification of the short-term therapy outcome, we used height velocity (in cm/year and SDS) and the change in height SDS as follows: for calculation of the annualized height velocity during the first year of therapy, measurements taken 9–15 months after therapy started were allowed in cases, where the 12-month visit was not available (differences referring to an interval unequal to 12 months were annualized by division by the length of the interval). Eight (7 GHD, 1 SGA) patients with an even larger time interval were excluded from the primary analysis but included in an extended analysis. For the group of GHD, disorder-specific first-year height velocity was transformed to age- and sex-specific SDS using references provided for a Belgian cohort which is comparable to our GHD group with respect to GH dose [ 21 ]. For the SGA group, the same references were used for comparison with GHD due to a lack of SGA-specific references. Delta height SDS for the first year of therapy is calculated as the annualized difference of height SDS after 1 year minus height SDS at the start of therapy (annualizing by division by the length of the time interval in years); a positive value thus indicates a gain in height SDS. For the evaluation of the long-term outcome after GHT, we calculated the following: Final height (SDS), based on the references at age 19 [ 19 ]. The deviation of FH (SDS) from target height (TH) (SDS) (“ FH minus TH SDS ”) where TH equals the arithmetic mean of father’s and mother’s height with sex correction by adding 6.5 cm for boys and subtracting 6.5 cm for girls. The delta height SDS from the start of therapy to FH as the difference of FH SDS minus height SDS at the start of therapy. The gain in height (cm), estimated as the difference in cm between the attained FH and extrapolated adult height (EAH; in cm) at therapy start. The EAH is obtained by transforming the height SDS at therapy start into an extrapolated height in cm by using the Austrian references at the age of 19 years [ 19 ]. Statistical Analyses Continuous baseline patient characteristics as well as raw data for short- and long-term parameters of growth are described for each group (GHD, SGA) by median and quartiles to account for non-normal distributions. Details on all statistical models and sensitivity analyses are summarized in the online supplementary material. Short-term parameters are compared between groups using multivariable linear regression models to adjust for age, sex, and height SDS at start of therapy. Long-term parameters of growth were missing for patients who are already known to have dropped out after the first year of therapy and for patients who are still under therapy. Based on the 94 patients with available final height and the 26 patients who dropped out after their first year, a logistic regression model is used to investigate the association of dropping out with baseline and first-year parameters. All analyses for long-term outcomes are based on observed FH measurements and imputed values of final height for patients under ongoing therapy (28%). Linear regression models for long-term parameters of growth as dependent variables contain the same baseline adjustment variables as used for short-term parameters (see above) plus the interaction of group and sex, which allows to report p values for sex-specific group comparisons. In order to evaluate the potential of published dichotomous short-term criteria of non-response to predict dichotomous long-term criteria of non-response we calculate sensitivity and specificity. Finally, we estimate linear regression models using the baseline and short-term parameters as continuous independent variables in order to assess their potential for predicting the long-term outcome parameter delta height SDS from the start of therapy to FH (as a continuous dependent variable). We report apparent Adjusted R 2 values as measures of explained variation when the potential predictors (at baseline and for the first year of growth) are sequentially added. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., 2016) is used for all statistical analyses. Two-sided p values below 0.05 are regarded to indicate statistical significance. No correction for analyzing multiple short-term and long-term parameters of growth is applied since their list has been pre-specified and all results are reported irrespective of statistical significance; p values and confidence intervals have to be interpreted accordingly. Results Patient Characteristics at the Start of GHT As expected, birth weight and length SDS were considerably lower in SGA (median SDS of −2.2 compared to −0.6 in GHD, p &lt; 0.001). However, length and weight in GHD were also below the population average (upper quartile of birth weight (SDS) and birth length (SDS) at 0.0 and −0.24, respectively). Height (SDS) at start of GHT was comparable between GHD and SGA (median of −3.1 and −2.9 SD, resp). Patients were younger on average in GHD (6.5 vs. 7.7 years), albeit not significantly. Target height and, consequently, the difference to FH were significantly lower in patients with SGA. Details are summarized in Table 1 . These patient characteristics were not significantly different between females and males. Furthermore, the differences between GHD and SGA regarding these patient characteristics were not significantly different between females and males. Sex-specific patient characteristics are summarized in online supplementary Table 1. Growth during the First Year of GHT We observed a large variation in all three parameters of growth, delta height SDS, height velocity (cm/year), and GHD height velocity (SDS), during the first year of therapy. Unadjusted data are summarized in Figure 2 and online supplementary Table 2. In patients with GHD, the disorder- and GH treatment-specific height velocity during first year [ 21 ] was close to the population average (adjusted mean 0.05, 95% CI: −0.14 to 0.23). Height velocity and delta height SDS during the first year of GHT were significantly higher in GHD compared to SGA by 1.3 cm/year (CI: 0.6–1.9, p &lt; 0.001) and 0.24 SD (CI: 0.08–0.39, p = 0.003), respectively ( Table 2 ). A sensitivity analysis removing influential observations resulted in a slightly lower mean group differences of 1.0 cm/year ( p &lt; 0.001) and 0.17 SD ( p = 0.005), respectively. Group differences of first-year growth parameters were not significantly different between females and males, and there was no statistically significant difference in short-term response between sexes (see Table 2 ). Long-Term Outcome after GHT Before analyzing long-term parameters of growth, potential reasons for drop-out were investigated. While age and height SDS at the start of therapy are not significantly associated with drop-out, each additional 0.1 SD in the first-year growth reduced the odds for drop-out by 28% (CI: −40% to −12%, p = 0.001). All analyses reported below for long-term outcomes are based on observed FH measurements (94 patients) and imputed values for patients under ongoing therapy (36 patients). All analyzed long-term response parameters showed high variability in both groups (unadjusted data are given in Fig. 3 and online suppl. Table 2). Adjusted mean FH was −0.94 SDS (CI: −1.15 to −0.73) in GHD, corresponding to the 17th percentile, and −2.10 SDS (CI: −2.41 to −1.80) in SGA, corresponding to the 1.8th percentile of the general Austrian population. Adjusted mean final height corrected for target height (FH minus TH) was significantly negative in GHD (−0.39 SDS, CI: −0.62 to −0.15) and SGA (−1.22 SDS, CI: −1.57 to −0.87) with a significant adjusted mean difference of 0.83 SDS (CI: 0.41–1.25, p &lt; 0.001) between the two groups. The adjusted mean increase in height SDS from start of GHT to final height was 2.21 SDS in GHD (CI: 2.00–2.42) and 1.05 SDS in SGA (CI: 0.75–1.35), with a significant adjusted mean difference of 1.16 SDS (CI: 0.80–1.53, p &lt; 0.001) between the groups. The adjusted mean gain in centimeters, based on extrapolated adult height at baseline was 12.5 cm in GHD (CI: 11.1–13.8) and 6.5 cm in SGA (CI: −4.7 to 8.2) with a significant average difference of 6.0 cm (CI: 3.8–8.1, p &lt; 0.001). For all long-term outcomes, the sensitivity analyses after removing potentially influential observations neither changed the mean group difference nor p values to a meaningful degree. Group differences of all long-term parameters (after adjustment for height SDS at start of GHT) were considerably larger for females compared to males. Notably, for all long-term outcomes, male patients on average show a smaller adjusted outcome under GHT compared to females in GHD, but a larger one in SGA. For all long-term parameters except FH minus TH (SDS), this qualitative interaction between group and sex was statistically significant (see Table 3 for details). To summarize the group comparisons, we observed a clear and statistically significant advantage for GHD patients compared to SGA, in short-term as well as long-term outcome parameters. Evaluation of Previously Published Criteria for Short-Term and Long-Term Responses in GHD In the literature, cut-offs for parameters of therapy effect in GHD have been suggested for the definition of responders/non-responders during the first year of therapy [ 22 ] as well as for the long-term perspective (start of therapy to final height), reviewed in [ 23 ]. Using our data, we evaluate two indicators of long-term non-response (“poor final height outcome”) also recently evaluated in the literature [ 24 ]: A. An increase of &lt;1.0 SD from start of therapy to final height, which indicates non-response for 12 of our 61 GHD patients with available final height; B. “Not reaching genetic height potential”, expressed by FH − TH &lt; −0.5 SD, which indicates non-response for 28 of 59 GHD patients with available final height and target height. In the set of 59 patients with available FH and TH, criteria (A) and (B) agree in 9 non-responders and 28 responders, while 19 patients are declared non-responders only according to (B) and 3 patients only according to (A). We estimated sensitivity (i.e., probability to correctly identify non-responders) and specificity (i.e., probability to correctly identify responders) applying the following published criteria for short-term non-response to our 89 GHD patients with observed or imputed indicator for long-term non-response (see Table 3 in [ 25 ]): a) delta height SDS first year &lt;0.5 SD (satisfied for 17 of 89 GHD patients); b) delta height SDS first year &lt;0.37 SD (satisfied for 10 of 89 GHD patients); c) height velocity first year &lt;6.7 cm/year (satisfied for 4 of 89 GHD patients); d) GHD height velocity (SDS) &lt;−1.0 SD (satisfied for 12 of 83 GHD patients with allowable measurement time interval and satisfied for 13 of all 89 GHD patients with extended interval); As shown in online supplementary Table 3, for both long-term criteria, A and B, the probability to correctly predict non-responders (sensitivity) was below 50% for all evaluated first-year criteria (a–d), with the highest probability of 48.4% for short-term criterion a (delta height SDS first year &lt;0.5 SD). The probability of correctly predicting responders (specificity) was above 80% for all short-term criteria (a–d) and above 90% for b and c. The results for long-term criterion (A) are comparable to those reported by Straetemans et al. [ 25 ], while sensitivity is approximately cut in half for criterion (B). Extending the allowable measurement time interval to include all 89 GHD patients leads to similar results (not shown). Instead, we estimate linear regression models using the baseline and short-term outcome parameters as continuous independent variables (thus without proposing any new cut-offs) in order to assess their potential for predicting the long-term outcome parameter delta height SDS from start of therapy to FH (as continuous dependent variable). Discussion The growth promoting effect of GHT in patients with various disorders has been a focus of research for decades. Analyses of NAH or FH after GHT in GHD and SGA in national and international multicenter or single-center studies have been published since 1997. Only very recently long-term data on safety and efficacy of GHT in various diagnoses including GHD and SGA were investigated in the largest international multicenter study [ 11 ]. GHD and SGA represent the two largest groups of patients treated with biosynthetic growth hormone during childhood and adolescence. The designs of studies that investigate one of these two patient groups are heterogeneous. Further, the definitions of diagnosis and of investigated parameters of short-term and long-term response vary across studies. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses are scarce and restricted to earlier years, when the first reports on long-term results (FH and NAH) became available [ 5 ]. Although some earlier studies had compared short-term response [ 27 , 28 ], the relation between short- and long-term response has not been directly compared between SGA and GHD within one center or multicenter analysis in previous studies. As our study was carried out at a single tertiary center with homogeneous criteria for diagnostic work-up, diagnostic laboratory methods and cut-offs as well as doses of GH treatment, we consider our cohort to be of interest for a direct comparison between the two groups. Moreover, we paid attention to sex differences not only with regard to response to GHT but also regarding group differences in GHT response. Evaluation of Growth during the First Year of GHT - Parameters, Definitions and Limitations Traditionally, the first-year growth response to GHT has been the central parameter of interest from two perspectives: 1. At a time, when long-term outcome data were still sparse “response to GHT” was primarily defined as growth during the first year of GHT. Different measures were applied to define first-year growth during GHT (reviewed in [ 29 ]). First-year growth after start of GHT was thus the target of prediction models, with predictors including patient characteristics like age, height, peak stimulated GH, bone age delay at start of GHT and parental height (reviewed in [ 8 , 9 ]). 2. Subsequently, when final height data became increasingly available, first-year growth after start of GHT as the short-term parameter was used to predict long-term outcome. The increase in height SDS (delta height SDS) during the first year of GHT has turned out as the most frequently used parameter, as an assessment at the level of SDS allows direct comparability across different sexes and ages. Positive values indicate catch-up growth (CUG) in GHD and enhanced growth in SGA [ 9 ] and other disorders in which GHT is not applied as a substitution therapy. In our study, the difference in short-term response between GHD and SGA was statistically significant in the unadjusted as well as the adjusted analysis of all short-term parameters, but only small in terms of SDS or height velocity. This is in line with the difference reported by Bang et al. [ 22 ]. We think that the following reasons contribute to the observed difference of short-term response between GHD and SGA in our study that persisted after adjustment for sex, age, and baseline height: concerning the “degree of GHD,” often classified as partial or complete [ 27 , 30 , 31 ] and matter of debate since decades, we apply the clinically-based cut-off of 7.0 ng/mL for the definition of GHD [ 16 ], whereas 10 ng/mL has been used in most other studies [ 6 ]. This stricter classification of GHD might contribute to a better discernibility between GH deficiency and GH sufficiency (which is expected in SGA). In the same line, we also use the SGA definitions recommended in consensus guidelines [ 3 , 4 ], which differ from less strict definitions in earlier and also recent studies [ 12 ]. A common finding in our study, as well as in other studies reporting on first-year response in either GHD or SGA [ 11 , 27 ] is the large variation of responses even within each group. This might in particular be due to a subset of excellent responders in the GHD group in our study. As we used a fixed starting dose, which was weight adapted in the following course, in both GHD and SGA we cannot add new evidence for the effect of GH dose in the variation of short-term effects of GHT. Remarkably, however, this dose in the lower dose segment (see [ 32 ] for international comparisons) led to results that are roughly comparable to those of the previous reports cited above. Evaluation of Long-Term Outcome under GHT - Parameters, Definitions and Limitations Several parameters have been defined to evaluate long-term response: Height after GHT per se (SDS, rarely in percentiles), height corrected for TH, the increase in height from start to FH or, more frequently, to NAH - either reported in SDS, or, less frequently, in cm. “True” final height data, measured in young adults, are exceptional due to loss to follow-up once therapy has been stopped, especially in SGA. Therefore, in the respective studies, we find definitions for NAH based on chronological ages, bone age, and frequently growth rates in cm during the last 6–12 months. While attainment of 98–99% of growth potential is in most centers the timepoint to stop GHT, the age at this point may vary considerably. In addition, it is important to carefully choose the age-basis of SDS calculations (calculated at actual age or adult age of the respective reference), as treatment effects may be overestimated. To give an extreme example of a 13 year old girl after rather early puberty and a bone age of 15 years who has achieved 99% of adult height but height SDS are calculated based on 13 years instead of 18, 19, or 21 years (depending on the last available height of the underlying reference). In GHD, the most straightforward definition of a successful therapy is some quantification of “normalization of height”; in large samples with successful GHT, a mean FH around 0 SDS would thus be expected. Correction for TH is frequently used to account for possible additional familial factors independent of GHD. Still, many studies, including tours, report sample means lower than population average adult height in GHD after GHT (summarized in [ 11 , 33 , 34 ]). Again, we want to point out that heterogeneity of the results for final height SDS across studies can be caused not only by different national (or other) references but also by different ages used for calculation of SDS. In SGA, a good long-term outcome of GHT has been defined in various ways: reaching a height within the normal range (3rd percentile, −2.0 SDS, also often called “normalization,” but different from the term used in GHD!), a height close to population average after adjustment for target height or sufficient gain in height SDS (definitions of long-term response: see below). Comparability between studies is even more difficult than in the case of GHD, as SGA patients attain final height even earlier [ 35 , 36 ], raising the question of a suitable age for SDS calculation again. Results in “pure FH” or TH-corrected FH in our study are well within the range of previous and most recent reports [ 5 , 11 , 12 , 34 ] both in GHD and SGA, though, as mentioned before, direct comparisons between studies are questionable and might impede meta-analyses. In our study, the unadjusted mean increase in cm between EAH and final height in SGA was 5.6 cm, while on the level of SDS an unadjusted mean increase by 1.0 SDS- was observed. These numbers might be helpful when discussing treatment options with parents. Interestingly, sex had a different impact on long-term outcomes in GHD compared to SGA, with better results in girls with GHD as compared to boys with GHD and better results in boys with SGA when compared to girls with SGA. Most studies, in which long-term data have been reported separately for males and females, have evaluated NAH but calculated the respective height SDS on the basis of chronological age [ 34 ]. Therefore, sex differences in outcome under GHT are difficult to compare. We do not have sufficient data on prepubertal and pubertal height gain, which could help to explain this highly interesting interaction of diagnosis and sex. Furthermore, besides the effect of sex steroids, also other factors inherent to the growth-plate or the likely different mechanisms behind the growth promoting action of GHT in GHD and SGA may be relevant. Definition of Short-Term and Long-Term Responders, Predictions, and Their Underlying Methodology Cut-Offs for 1st Year Response to GHT Data of large multicenter trials provided GHD-specific first-year height velocity as percentiles or SDS. The definition of a poor response was suggested to be below the 10th–25th percentile of the observed GHD-specific height velocity (cm/year) [ 37 ] and subsequently transformed to an equivalent of a delta height increase of less than 0.4 SDS on the basis of the KIGS data [ 38 ]. Bang et al. [ 22 ] evaluated an arbitrary “cut-off” of 0.5 SDS based on the rather homogeneous first-year responses in their 2011 study and performed a literature review of available data at that time. The usefulness of this cut-off of 0.5 SDS first-year height gain has also been investigated in GHD more recently [ 25 ], both to define the proportion of short-term non-responders to GHT and to predict long-term outcome by means of this 0.5 SDS short-term cut-off. In their analyses of patients in a Belgian multicenter study, Straetemans et al. [ 29 ] found that the various short-term response cut-offs yielded similar percentages of non-responders (around 25%) in GHD and SGA. Applying the &lt;0.5 SDS first-year criterion to our data, we found 19% short-term non-responders in GHD and 29% in SGA. We consider our samples not large enough to derive own cut-offs [ 26 ]; therefore, we compared specificity and sensitivity of these previously published cut-offs for GHD [ 25 ] with our data set. Besides the frequently applied long-term non-response parameter delta height SDS &lt;1 SDS we also used a stricter criterion for long-term non-response defined by a final height that is not sufficiently close to target height (FH &lt; TH −0.5 SD). Our results confirm those from Straetemans et al. [ 25 ] with poor overall performance of the “&lt;0.5 SDS short-term cut-off” to predict non-response on the long-term in GHD and add that a “sharper” definition of non-response (FH &lt; TH −0.5 SD) does not improve sensitivity. Prediction Models Continuous parameters reflecting first-year increase in height SDS have been part of more complex prediction models (reviewed in [ 8 , 9 , 39 ]): first-year response to GHT has been reported to be an important predictor of long-term response in GHD [ 28 , 40 ] but also SGA [ 38 ] and in samples comprising both diagnoses [ 41 ]. In our study, long-term response, expressed as total height SDS gain from start of therapy to FH was clearly related to first-year growth response in GHD, but not in SGA. In GHD, prediction of long-term response by a model including previously described important prediction parameters of long-term response (height and age at start of GHT) was further improved by adding 1st year response as a parameter. In contrast, none of these parameters predicted long-term response in SGA in our study. The conflicting results concerning predictability between our study and the larger multinational studies in which a relationship between 1st year and long-term response was shown for SGA [ 38 ] might be due to the obvious differences in sample sizes. An alternative explanation, unrelated to sample size of analyzed SGA cohorts, might be related to methodology: in our study, we defined final height by achievement of 99% of height potential and/or a growth rate of less than 6 mm/12 months, whereas in the KIGS study, near adult final height was defined by chronological age (14 years in girls, 16 years in boys) and a growth rate of &lt;2 cm in 12 months [ 11 , 42 ]. Furthermore, we have calculated FH SDS on the basis of adult reference data (at age 19 years) while in the KIGS study, NAH SDS was calculated on the basis of the respective chronological ages. Thus, the reported characteristic pubertal growth pattern in SGA, with accelerated bone maturation despite normal pubertal age and duration [ 35 ] and early deceleration of height velocity [ 36 ], is only incompletely depicted in the KIGS cohort - in other words, long-term outcomes under GHT in SGA is likely to be overestimated. This might also affect prediction models. In conclusion, the short- and long-term outcomes of GHT treatment in our study was significantly better in GHD than in SGA in all investigated parameters. Our results are in favor of a diagnosis-specific growth pattern during GHT. In accordance with previous studies, long-term outcome was related to short-term response in GHD, although there was – also in accordance with previous studies – no simple cut-off rule derived from 1st year change in height SDS to predict long-term non-responders. In SGA, we observed practically no relationship between 1st-year and long-term responses to GHT. The strength of our study is the homogeneity of the diagnostic pathway, the emphasis on the definition of diagnoses and the design to directly compare GHD and SGA. At the same time, the single-center study design did not offer a sample size sufficiently large in order to propose new cut-offs or compare predictability of long-term response in GHD and SGA in subgroups related to pubertal onset.
