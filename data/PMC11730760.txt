Abstract Issue Addressed Public transport (PT) users often accumulate more physical activity (PA) than private motor vehicle users and financial incentives may increase PT use. Responding to rising petrol prices, from 28 March to 1 May 2022, the Tasmanian government made public bus use fare‐free. This exploratory study examined the perceived impact of fare‐free buses on bus use and PA. Methods Tasmanian adults who had used the fare‐free buses ( N = 548) completed an online survey (4 May–14 June 2022). Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive and inferential methods and responses to open‐ended questions categorised. Results Over the fare‐free period, 46% of participants reported more bus use—average weekly bus trips increased from 3.0 to 4.0 ( p &lt; 0.001). Most (81%) participants, including 36% ( n = 33/93) who were not previous bus users, planned continued bus use despite financial cost. Total PA was reported higher (36%), the same (60%) and lower (4%) than usual during the fare‐free period. Higher total PA was more common amongst those reporting increased bus use ( n = 186/240; 78%). Responses to open‐ended questions revealed: bus use‐related PA gain was through walking to/from bus stops, more frequently attending PA settings (e.g., the gym) and unexpected walking due to bus service limitations; household‐level cost savings, improved travel opportunities and better social/mental health were additional fare‐free benefits. Conclusions/So What Fare‐free buses were commonly perceived to increase bus use and PA. Intervention studies would determine if PA and financially incentivised PT have a causal relationship. Free bus fares, bus use and physical activity: An exploratory cross‐sectional study . Health Promot J Austral . 2025 ; 36 ( 1 ): e881 . 10.1002/hpja.881 PMC11730760 38812377 Handling editor: Joshua Trigg 1 INTRODUCTION In Australia (and globally), many adults are insufficiently physically active for health. 1 , 2 Australian guidelines recommend adults accumulate 150 min of moderate or 75 min of vigorous physical activity per week for health benefit. 3 The World Health Organization recently estimated that the global cost of ill‐health associated with physical inactivity (non‐communicable disease such as heart disease and diabetes) is US$27 billion annually. 4 Most initiatives to improve population level physical activity have focused on leisure time, with less attention given to the way people travel in their daily lives. 5 Focusing on the way people travel to increase physical activity levels is worthy of greater investigation. Improving access to safe public transport is promoted by the World Health Organization as a strategy to increase population level physical activity. 4 A systematic review found that public transport users accumulate 8–33 min/day of additional/incidental walking, which makes a substantial contribution to weekly physical activity targets. 6 , 7 , 8 Furthermore, several studies have found that public transport use generated more physical activity compared with private motor vehicle use so finding ways to increase public transport utilisation for physical activity gain has merit. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Another important co‐benefit of increasing public transport use in exchange for private motor vehicle use is the environmental advantage this change in transport behaviour confers—a significant consideration in the context of climate change. 13 In 2016, only 11.5% of Australians used public transport to commute to work on census day. 14 In 2021, this figure dropped to 4.6%, likely due to the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. 14 , 15 Use of public transport is lower again in less densely populated Australian jurisdictions, such as in the island state Tasmania where only 3% of the population used public transport to commute to work on census day in 2016 and 2021. 14 Financial incentives show promise in positively influencing heath behaviours such as physical activity. 16 , 17 Financial incentives as low as $.10 US/day increase physical activity which is sustained after incentive removal. 18 As purported by behavioural economics theory, humans are more likely to change behaviour if gratification is immediate—a recent systematic review confirmed that the more immediate the financial incentive the more likely a gain in physical activity. 18 , 19 , 20 In Australia and elsewhere, financially incentivising public transport use (e.g., fare‐free or fare reduction) can result in increased ridership but the effect is moderated by correlates of public transport use such as individual (e.g., age), social (e.g., population density) and environmental characteristics (e.g., the built environment). 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 For example, a reduction in public transport fares in a major Australian region (south‐east Queensland) resulted in an overall increase in ridership, but the effect was greatest amongst full‐fare paying adults whereby the absolute financial gain was highest. 23 Despite the promise of financial incentives on healthful behaviours, including public transport use, an important knowledge gap remains regarding the relationship between financially incentivised public transport use and physical activity. Furthermore, the impact of financial incentives on bus use and physical activity in a low density setting with limited public transport is also unknown. Filling these knowledge gaps would benefit public transport providers, policy makers and public health experts. This study aimed to examine the perceived impact of temporary fare‐free public buses on bus use and physical activity in a regional Australian setting. Behavioural economics, specifically Nudge Theory, informed the study. 25 In public health, Nudge Theory has been applied by creating environments that make it easier for individuals to engage in healthful behaviour without needing to influence individual‐level knowledge or attitudes—the healthful behaviour occurs in the absence of complex decision making (e.g., healthier food is purchased because it is displayed more prominently compared with less healthy food). 25 , 26 In this study, provision of fare‐free public transport provides a nudge environment for physical activity. 2 METHODS 2.1 Study design Cross‐sectional online survey. 2.2 Study setting From 28 March 2022 to 1 May 2022 (inclusive), Tasmanian public transport was free through a state government initiative to grow ridership in response to cost‐of‐living pressures and rising petrol prices. Tasmania is an island state of Australia, with a population of approximately 550 000, with almost half the population residing around its capital city, Hobart. Public transport is predominantly buses, with Metro Tasmania providing most metropolitan services. Census data from 2016 and 2021 indicate that Tasmania's public transport use for commuting is one of the lowest when compared with the remaining seven Australian jurisdictions. 27 Relevant COVID‐19‐related restrictions during the fare‐free period were a quarantine period of at least 7 days for positive cases and compulsory mask wearing on public transport. 28 2.3 Study population Residents of Tasmania (18+ years) who had used a public bus during the fare‐free period. 2.4 Recruitment methods Participants in this convenience sample were recruited through social/traditional media, word‐of‐mouth, bus advertising and professional networks. Participants in our past transport‐related physical activity studies were invited by email if they had previously indicated they wanted to be informed of future relevant projects. 2.5 Procedure Ethics approval was granted by the University of Tasmania Human Research Ethics Committee (30 April 2022). A weblink and Quick Response code connected prospective participants to the online survey. The survey was prefaced with an information sheet and informed consent was required to commence the survey. The survey was open for 6 weeks (4 May–14 June 2022) after the fare‐free period had ended. To encourage survey completion, participants could enter a draw to win one of five $100 credit card vouchers. 2.6 Measures The online survey was designed to be brief to enable a quick response to the unexpected fare‐free initiative, maximise response rates by minimising participant burden and capture information directly relevant to the research aims. The survey contained questions based on or adapted from existing measures used in our previous transport‐related physical activity studies or were developed in direct response to the research questions where no items existed. 29 , 30 The survey was reviewed by Metro Tasmania prior to dissemination to ensure policy‐relevance. The survey questions related to demographics ( n = 5; age group, gender, residential suburb and postcode, highest level of education), bus use ( n = 7; bus service used, time to walk from home and place of work/study to bus stop, number of days per week used bus before and during the fare‐free period, reasons for no increase in bus use, intentions to continue using bus), physical activity ( n = 5; walking and total physical activity related to bus use compared with motor vehicle use, walking to and from a bus stop before and during the fare‐free period, change in physical activity during the fare‐free period) and a final open‐ended question inviting comments about the connection between the fare‐free buses, bus use and physical activity. 2.7 Analysis Descriptive statistics, percentages and frequencies of categorical variables and mean/median and standard deviation/interquartile range of continuous variables are used to describe the characteristics, bus use and transport‐related physical activity of the sample before and during the fare‐free period. Mean differences (with 95% confidence intervals) in bus use and time spent walking to and/or from a bus stop during compared with before the fare‐free period are reported. All counts and estimates were unweighted. Data analysis was completed in 2022–2023 using Stata Statistical Software version 17 (Statacorp, College Station, Texas). Responses to open‐ended questions were categorised, facilitated by use of qualitative data management software NVivo 12 (QSR International, Doncaster, Victoria, Australia). 3 RESULTS Five hundred and forty‐eight surveys were completed by 706 people who at a minimum viewed the online participant information sheet. Partial responses were defined as those who did not enter the final section of the survey (which contained the physical activity questions) and these ( n = 158) were excluded from the analysis. Thirty‐two participants entered the final physical activity section but did not click the ‘submit survey’ button at the end of the section which resulted in the final section not being saved, thus leaving a sub‐sample of n = 516 for analysis of physical activity. Compared with the 2021 Tasmanian census data characterising those who travelled to work by public transport, the study sample was generally representative of public transport user—women (73% vs. 60%), those 18–44 years old (74% vs. 67%), urban‐based residents (89% vs. 90%) and those with a university degree (42% vs. 42%) (Table 1 ). 31 Across all demographic characteristics, bus use and physical activity increasing or remaining the same was more commonly reported than either behaviour decreasing during the fare‐free period (Table 1 ). An increase in bus use and physical activity was more commonly reported by men, those in the youngest and oldest age brackets (18–24 years and &gt;64 years), those living outside an urban area and those with low education levels. On average, participants reported that the walk from home to the bus stop took less time than the walk from work/place of study to the bus stop (mean = 5.8 min from home, 6.6 min from work/place of study), although the median (3 min), and interquartile ranges were the same (3–8 min) (Figure S1 ). Almost half of participants (45.8%; Table 1 ) reported using the bus more during the fare‐free period than they did before it, with average days per week of bus use significantly ( p &lt; 0.001) increasing from 3 days (before the free bus fares) to 4 days (during the free bus fares) (Table 2 ). Just over a third of participants (36.1%; Table 1 ) reported an increase in physical activity during the fare‐free period compared with before it, with average total minutes of walking to and/or from a bus stop significantly ( p &lt; 0.001) increasing from 39 min/week (before the fare‐free period) to 49.3 min/week (during the fare‐free period). Amongst those whose bus use did not increase ( n = 297), their reasons for this were (multiple responses accepted): The cost of using the bus does not influence my decision to use it (51.5%); It does not suit me to use the bus more often (24.6%); The cost of fuel does not influence the way I choose to travel (10.4%), and I have cut back on spending on other things (e.g. eating out) due to the increase in fuel prices (11.1%). Other common reasons for bus use not increasing, extracted from the comments section of the survey, were that they were already using the bus maximally (8.1%) and that service provision limitations prevented more bus use (7.7%). Most participants (81%) planned to continue using the bus, although it was no longer free, 9.1% were unsure and 9.5% did not intend to. Of the n = 93 who did not use public buses prior to the fare‐free period, 35.5% planned to continue using the bus, 39.8% did not and 24.7% were unsure. Amongst those with complete physical activity data ( n = 516) over half of the participants reported that they walked more or were more physically active overall on days they used the bus compared with days they drove (Table 3 ). Over a third of participants reported an increase in total physical activity during the fare‐free period, with only a small proportion (3.9%) stating it was lower. Participants reporting more physical activity during the fare‐free period compared with before it demonstrated an increase (26.5 min/week) in average walking to/from bus stops, those reporting no change in physical activity had little change (2.7 min/week) in average walking to/from bus stops and those reporting they were less physically active demonstrated a decrease (−21 min/week) in average walking to/from bus stops (Table 4 ). The greatest change in average walking to/from bus stops (31.7 min/week) was observed amongst participants who reported both an increase in bus use and an increase in total physical activity. Analysis (Table 5 ) of responses to the final open‐ended survey question inviting comment regarding the perceived connection between fare‐free buses, bus use and physical activity ( n = 310/516 responses; 60% of total sample, with generally proportionate response rates by age and gender), revealed that the fare‐free bus initiative was popular with a desire for it to occur again or operate in perpetuity—“ Public transport needs to be free in order for it to be valued and utilised properly” (Woman, 35–44 years) . However, limitations in bus service provision thwarted its reach and was a reason given for no increase in bus use. For some, COVID‐19 impacted their use of the fare‐free buses due to required isolation, self‐limited socialisation or concerns about transmissibility in the enclosed bus environment. Participants stated that physical activity gain related to fare‐free buses was through walking to and from bus stops, attending physical activity settings more often, such as the gym and walking to a destination because of buses not arriving or being late or over‐crowded. Although physical activity loss was reportedly related to using the bus instead of walking, or using a motor vehicle, parking was further from their destination than the bus stop. Individual‐ or household‐level cost savings and improved travel opportunities and social and mental health were other related benefits. The free bus fare initiative really impacted me in a positive way, I was able to be more social with friends and I was able to go to the gym more often and even do more in person job searches, free bus fares really took a bunch of stress off of my shoulders (Woman, 18–24 years). However, individual‐level cost savings could be undermined by an unreliable service if an alternate and more expensive transport option was needed (e.g. Uber use because the bus did not show). 4 DISCUSSION The objective of this exploratory cross‐sectional study was to examine the perceived association between fare‐free buses, bus use and physical activity. In this sample of adults living in Tasmania, Australia, temporary financial incentives were reported to attract new bus users, increase bus use, increase physical activity and encourage commitment to ongoing bus use in a full‐fare environment. Perceived increases in physical activity were most common amongst those who reported an increase in bus use, suggesting a relationship between these two behaviours. Reporting increases in both bus use and physical activity were most common amongst men, the youngest and oldest adult categories, those living outside of an urban area and those with a low education level. Other co‐benefits of bus use were noted, including those related to social and mental wellbeing. The reported benefits of fare‐free buses were despite COVID‐19 continuing to negatively impact bus service provision. The findings support the growing evidence that financial incentives can positively influence public transport use in various ways—attracting new, more or an intention for ongoing bus use even in a post‐pandemic environment. 21 , 22 , 23 Data publicly shared by the Tasmanian state government demonstrated an approximate increase in public transport patronage of 15% during the fare‐free period with some services experiencing an approximate 60% increase in use. 32 An upward trend in Tasmanian public transport use during the study period in the month of April 2022 was also apparent in publicly available Google mobility data. 33 However, similar to other studies, our findings also highlighted that not everyone is motivated to use public transport purely as a cost saving endeavour—other individual factors (e.g. physical activity gain and psychological mechanisms such as personal norms, attitudes and perceived behavioural control) or environmental factors (e.g. reducing motor vehicle related emissions) can be influential. 34 , 35 Furthermore, variability in both participant characteristics associated with more bus use and the motivations to use public transport indicates that tailoring public transport use promotion strategies may capture broader and ongoing patronage. Around 60% of participants indicated that they walked more or were more physically active overall when they caught the bus compared with when they drove, providing further evidence of the potential of public transport to improve health via increased physical activity. This finding also supports the application of Nudge Theory in public health—participants made a conscious choice to use the bus resulting in an unconscious physical activity gain. 25 , 26 Furthermore, 77% of those whose bus use increased reported increases in total physical activity. Minutes per week spent walking to and/or from bus stops was perceived to increase by 5–10 min per week in the fare‐free period, which is consistent with the average increase of one bus trip per week. Our earlier work found that the average time bus users spent walking to/from bus stops in Hobart (the capital city of Tasmania), was 30 min per week and 6 min per trip. 36 Although this level of physical activity may seem small at an individual level, it can have a large impact on health and wellbeing at a population level. For example, 15 min a day or 90 min per week of low volume physical activity reduces the risk of all‐cause mortality by 14%, cardiovascular disease by 19%, and ischemic heart disease by 25% and increases life expectancy by 3 years. 37 Participants revealed in an open‐ended question that the physical activity gain related to fare‐free buses commonly reported by participants was not limited to walking to and from bus stops, but for some also resulted from travelling to recreational physical activity settings, such as the gym, more often. This finding highlights the potential impact of increased public transport use on other physical activity domains, not just transport‐related physical activity. Less commonly, 3.9% of participants reported physical activity loss during the fare‐free bus period, with the greatest proportion (13%) amongst those who reported less bus use compared with those with the same (3%) or increased bus use (5%). Physical activity loss reportedly occurred by using the bus instead of walking to/from their destination or using the bus instead of a motor vehicle because the bus stop was closer to their destination than the car park. This unintended negative consequence of fare‐free buses may be mitigated by concomitant health promotion messaging encouraging walking to/from a more distant bus stop—the health benefits of physical activity gain in this way can be overlooked by bus users. 38 Co‐benefits of public transport such as reduced traffic and lower greenhouse gas emissions provide further justification for public transport use promotion. 34 Another reason for physical activity loss connected with bus use, that was not captured in this study, could be that the time allocated for recreational physical activity had been displaced. The reported benefits of fare‐free buses were far broader than individual‐level fiscal gain, which was the impetus behind this state government‐led initiative and the reason given for its short‐term nature. Rigorous evaluation of the breadth of outcomes from financially incentivised public transport is necessary. Participants reports of gains in physical activity and social (e.g. greater social connectedness) and mental (e.g. less financial related stress) health related to fare‐free buses are important public health considerations for decision makers regarding the longevity of such initiatives. For instance, a recent high quality quasi‐experimental study conducted in England found that ongoing fare‐free bus passes for eligible older adults was associated with improved mental health and less social isolation. 39 Findings from a Scottish‐based evaluation 1‐year post‐initiation of fare‐free buses for those aged 5–21 years also found broader reported benefits of the scheme, in addition to more bus use and less private motor vehicle use, such as travelling to new places for social, education, employment and health reasons and reducing stress associated with transport costs. 40 The environmental benefit of more public transport use because of financial incentives is another important consideration for decision makers. 13 Box 1 provides a summary of recommendations for public health and public transport stakeholders based on the study findings. BOX 1 A summary of recommendations for stakeholders based on the study findings Recognise the potential broader health, social, economic and environmental benefits of fare‐free buses in addition to the individual‐level cost savings. Consider the merits of similar short‐term interventions to attract and potentially retain new bus users as well as encourage existing riders to use the bus more. Enable adequate bus service provision when fare‐free initiatives occur. Public health researchers and appropriate agencies work together in the design of future fare‐free public transport initiatives, so the intervention effect can be comprehensively and rigorously evaluated. Embed promotion of the potential health/physical activity benefits alongside the individual cost saving benefit in strategies to increase public transport use. 4.1 Limitations The study had a small convenience sample, so the generalisability of the findings is unknown. However, participant characteristics generally represented the characteristics of Tasmanian public transport users. Consequently, there was reasonable diversity in the age of respondents, and good representation from lower and middle education groups, which are often under‐represented in convenience samples. The non‐randomised and non‐controlled post‐intervention retrospective design means conclusions about the causal effect of the fare‐free period on bus use and physical activity cannot be made, although reverse causality—that the increases in bus use and physical activity observed would lead to greater uptake of the fare‐free initiative—seems unlikely. Due to time constraints and the opportunistic nature of the study, the number of questions were deliberately minimised to ensure that the study could be conducted immediately post the fare‐free period and to maximise responses. Some survey questions were limited in scope—future studies conducted over the longer term that determine travel purpose and shift in travel mode would add important information. Bus use and physical activity were self‐reported which has known limitations. 41 , 42 The fare‐free period occurred when COVID‐19 continued to impact Tasmanian public transport service provision due to bus service staff shortages, which may have influenced the study findings (e.g., less bus use associated with service cuts). 43 4.2 Strengths This opportunistic study has provided useful insights into the perceived impact of fare‐free buses on bus use and physical activity behaviours. Other strengths include the rapid responsiveness to the state government initiative, large number of survey responses and reasonable sample diversity. Furthermore, the representation of public transport users from both rural and urban areas in Tasmania was consistent with that reported elsewhere. 44 The inclusion of open‐ended survey questions added important contextual information. 5 CONCLUSIONS This opportunistic study provides useful information for government, public health and public transport agencies. The findings add to the building evidence that incentivising public transport improves its usage and that pursuing physical activity gain through travel behaviour interventions has merit. However, the need for efficient, reliable, and frequent public transport services is also highlighted. Furthermore, the perceived benefits of free public transport were broad and extend beyond the individual‐level fiscal benefit underpinning the genesis of the initiative. Intervention studies and comprehensive individual‐ and societal‐level health economic analysis are recommended to determine the causal relationship between financially incentivised public transport and physical activity and its wider benefits, respectively.
