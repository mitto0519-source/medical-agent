Arthritis often results in unmet healthcare needs for older adults with cognitive decline, who may struggle to communicate pain or recall symptoms. However, the risk factors for arthritis in this group remain underexplored. We addressed this gap by identifying and comparing arthritis risk factors among older adults with varying cognitive statuses. Data from 334 participants with cognitive decline and 808 participants with normal cognition were analysed using the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging, tracking arthritis diagnoses over 12 years with Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards regression. Results showed 47.6% of older adults with cognitive decline developed arthritis, compared with 30.1% with normal cognition. Key risk factors for the cognitive decline group included depressive symptoms (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.87), living alone (HR: 1.66), infrequent social interactions (HR: 1.42), and greater dependency in daily activities (HR: 1.41). In the normal cognition group, additional chronic illnesses (HR: 1.41) and higher body mass index (HR: 1.09) were significant risk factors. Understanding these distinct risk factors is crucial for preventing and managing arthritis among at risk groups. Moreover, these findings can assist in developing comprehensive public health strategies integrating mental health and social support to improve health outcomes for older adults with cognitive decline. They are particularly vulnerable to severe chronic illnesses and receive a higher number of diagnoses 3 . Managing health is complex for those with mild cognitive impairment—a condition characterized by significant declines in cognitive abilities that do not disrupt daily functions but increase the risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease or other forms of dementia—which can exacerbate the severity of other chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases and metabolic disorders 4 – 6 . Additionally, the risk of developing more chronic conditions is elevated among those with dementia, with over 60% of individuals with dementia having three or more concurrent chronic illnesses 7 , 8 . Among chronic illnesses affecting older adults with cognitive decline, arthritis (including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis) is a significant concern 9 – 14 . Osteoarthritis involves the deterioration of joint cartilage, primarily affecting load-bearing joints, whereas rheumatoid arthritis is a systemic inflammatory condition affecting multiple joints 13 , 15 . These impair physical function, leading to chronic pain, limited mobility, and a significant reduction in quality of life, often resulting in increased disability and long-term care 14 , 16 , 17 . From 1990 to 2019, the global burden of osteoarthritis has increased overall, with the age-standardized incidence rate rising by approximately 6.2% and years lived with disability rate increasing by 7.8% 18 . Similarly, the global incidence rate of rheumatoid arthritis has increased between 1990 and 2019, with the highest rate of age-standardized disability-adjusted life years in the 60–69 years age group 19 . In East Asia, the convergence of cognitive decline and arthritis presents a critical healthcare issue, further exacerbated by the region’s rapidly aging population 20 . The age-standardized prevalence rates of rheumatoid arthritis in East Asia have changed significantly from 1999 to 2017, with a 25% increase observed in 2017 21 . East Asia reported the highest incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years for osteoarthritis, potentially contributing to a dramatic increase in the number of cases predicted in the coming decades 14 . For example, by 2025, one in five people aged over 65 in Japan may be affected by dementia 22 , while dementia prevalence in China is estimated at 6.19%, with regional variations 23 . In Korea, the prevalence of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis in 2021 was 8.27% and 1.36%, respectively, among 163,221 individuals 24 . Given South Korea’s aging population, projected to rise to 46.4% by 2070 25 , 26 , understanding the relationship between arthritis risk and cognitive decline is vital. Previous research has predominantly focused on individuals with arthritis, identifying the relationship between their condition and the subsequent risk of cognitive decline. For instance, a study using Mendelian randomization analysis with Genome-Wide Association Study demonstrated that individuals with rheumatoid arthritis had a higher risk of cognitive decline 27 . Accordingly, older individuals with rheumatoid arthritis exhibit an increased risk of cognitive decline, as shown in a two-decade population-based study 9 , nested case-control study 10 , and mediation analysis study 28 . Similarly, mild cognitive impairment has been associated with an increased risk of radiographic knee osteoarthritis in a three-year follow-up longitudinal study 29 . Most research has concentrated on factors contributing to cognitive decline in older adults with arthritis, including psychological states like depression, physical limitations, and demographic factors such as age and educational level 28 , 30 – 32 . Furthermore, osteoarthritis has been linked to dementia progression, influenced by factors like sex, age, obesity, and vascular comorbidities 13 . However, some studies have yielded controversial results, finding no significant relationship between arthritis and cognitive decline, and suggesting the need for more detailed studies to explore the underlying mechanisms 33 , 34 . Most studies have focused on individuals with arthritis at baseline, examining their subsequent risk of developing cognitive decline. Consequently, specific risk factors for arthritis onset in older adults with cognitive decline remain largely underexplored. Our study addresses this gap by tracking arthritis development in older adults with cognitive decline as the baseline. This novel perspective is critical because older adults with cognitive decline face unique challenges, such as difficulty recalling past pain episodes and identifying the source of pain from one or more comorbid conditions 12 , 35 , 36 . Furthermore, while some hypothesised factors, such as reduced physical activity and increased dependence in daily activities, have been suggested 37 , 38 , there has been a lack of research directly comparing these risk factors across different cognitive statuses. Our study identifies and compares arthritis risk factors between older adults with cognitively decline and those with normal cognitive status. We analyse 12-year transitions in arthritis diagnoses and compare these risk factors among older adults in South Korea using nationally representative longitudinal data. This approach is essential for developing targeted preventive strategies to mitigate the compounded health burdens faced by this vulnerable population. By comparing arthritis risk factors by cognitive status, our findings underscore the urgency of tailored interventions to effectively prevent and manage potential chronic pain and diminished quality of life 39 – 41 . Results Characteristics of the participants Table 1 presents the characteristics of the total study participants, stratified by cognitive function. Among those with cognitive decline ( n = 334), the average age was higher (71.7 ± 4.9 years), with a larger proportion being less educated (93.4%), predominantly unemployed (83.5%), and residing in rural areas (69.2%). They participated more frequently in social activities at least once per week (70.4%) and were more likely to live alone (13.8%) compared to their counterparts with normal cognition ( n = 808), although these differences were not statistically significant. Regarding health-related factors, older adults with cognitive decline had a relatively lower mean body mass index (22.7 ± 2.9) compared to those with normal cognition (23.1 ± 2.6). They also exhibited weaker grip strength, falling into the lower tercile (49.1%), and had higher rates of depressive symptoms (18.3%). Furthermore, this group was more likely to exercise less than once per week (79.3%), and these individuals were more often non-smokers (81.4%) and non-drinkers (73.0%). Regarding functional dependency, they showed higher dependency in basic (mean Korean Activities of Daily Living (K-ADL) score: 0.13 ± 0.67) and complex activities (mean Korean Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (K-IADL) score: 0.67 ± 1.78) compared to those with normal cognition (mean K-ADL score: 0.03 ± 0.34, mean K-IADL score: 0.23 ± 0.89). Kaplan–Meier survival curves for remaining without an arthritis diagnosis A Kaplan–Meier curve showed a 168-month survival rate (remaining without a diagnosis of arthritis) of 0.64, with 740 individuals surviving (Fig. 1 ). Participants were stratified according to sex because of arthritis incidence differences 42 , 43 . Significant differences in survival rates were observed between older adults with cognitive decline (men: 0.6, women: 0.5) and those with normal cognition (men: 0.8, women: 0.5) at 168 months (Fig. 2 ). Fifty men and 125 women with cognitive decline remained without arthritis diagnosis, while 384 men and 181 women with normal cognition survived without arthritis. Risk factors for arthritis by cognitive function Table 2 demonstrates risk factors for arthritis among total participants. The adjusted Cox regression model showed an increased risk of arthritis among participants who were women (hazard ratio [HR]: 2.51), had depressive symptoms (HR: 1.49), had chronic illnesses (HR: 1.32), were socially inactive (HR: 1.24), had a higher body mass index (HR: 1.05), and were older (HR: 1.05) (Table 2 ). Table 3 presents specific risk factors for older adults with normal cognition and those with cognitive decline. Among both groups, we observed an increased risk of arthritis in participants who were women (HR: 2.05 for cognitive decline; HR: 3.18 for normal cognition) and older (HR: 1.05 for cognitive decline; HR: 1.06 for normal cognition) (Table 3 ). Specifically, older adults with cognitive decline had an increased risk of arthritis and exhibited depressive symptoms (HR: 1.87), lived alone (HR: 1.66), were socially inactive (HR: 1.42), and displayed higher dependency in activities of daily living (HR: 1.41). In contrast, older adults with normal cognition showed higher rates of risk of arthritis, associated with the presence of a comorbid chronic illness (HR: 1.41) and an increased body mass index (HR: 1.09). Discussion We utilised a nationally representative South Korean sample to compare the risk of general arthritis between older adults with normal cognition and those with cognitive decline. The normal cognition group showed an increased risk associated with comorbidities and high body mass index, whereas the cognitive decline group exhibited heightened risks due to social inactivity, living alone, increased dependency in activities of daily living, and depressive symptoms. These findings highlight the necessity for tailored arthritis prevention strategies that address specific needs based on cognitive status. Our study aligns with previous research indicating that women and older adults are more susceptible to arthritis, regardless of cognitive status 32 , 43 – 45 . Gender differences have been identified as significant risk factors for Alzheimer’s disease, related dementia, and arthritis; previous studies show that women have a higher risk of developing these conditions 46 , 47 . The underlying mechanisms for these gender differences have been explored through several biological pathways, including higher DAS28-ESR levels in women compared to men 48 and lower oestrogen levels among menopausal women, which reduces the protective effects of oestrogen on cartilage homeostasis 43 . Consistent with these findings, our study emphasises the importance of gender-specific and age-appropriate preventive measures and the need for more targeted, personalised management strategies for this at-risk group. For older adults with normal cognition, the correlation between body mass index and the presence of comorbid conditions underscores the critical role of weight management and comorbidity control in mitigating arthritis risk. This result aligns with previous studies connecting weight gain with arthritis through biomechanical stress and inflammatory pathways 49 , 50 . Addressing multiple chronic conditions is crucial for reducing the overall health burden and worsening arthritis outcomes in this demographic 51 , 52 . Optimizing preventive strategies to avoid multimorbidity is essential, and further research is needed to understand the causal mechanisms by which the status and severity of multimorbidity influence the development of arthritis. For older adults with cognitive decline, our study underscores the importance of addressing functional dependency, and non-physical factors, including social inactivity, living alone, and depressive symptoms. We highlight the need for a multifaceted approach to support older adults, particularly those with limited self-care abilities. Greater dependency in activities of daily living significantly increases the risk of arthritis in this demographic, emphasising the importance of effective self-care management in reducing arthritis risk 38 , 53 . Functional dependency can lead to inadequate self-care, poor management of existing health conditions, lack of adherence to treatments, and neglect of preventive measures, all of which contribute to the progression of arthritis 54 . Our study also supports the association between functional decline and arthritis risk, particularly in the absence of regular vigorous physical activity, which is a recommended approach for managing the effects of arthritis 38 . Therefore, functional dependency may indirectly increase arthritis risk by reducing mobility, increasing joint stress, and hindering effective self-care in older adults with cognitive decline. Furthermore, we highlight that social inactivity heightens the risk of arthritis among older adults with cognitive decline. A small social network with few social contacts has been associated with poor physical and mental health and reduced physical function 30 . Previous research has revealed associations between lack of social contact, loneliness, and poor cognition 55 , suggesting that insufficient intellectual social, and physical activity stimulation may contribute to arthritis risk. Social isolation can lead to depression by influencing lifestyle choices, possibly contributing to the development of non-communicable diseases 56 . Similarly, older adults with cognitive decline who live alone are at higher risk of adopting unhealthy lifestyles and experiencing inadequate health management services 57 While our study did not directly control for dietary habits, the lifestyle challenges faced by older adults living alone, such as potentially unhealthy dietary behaviours, may further aggravate their risk of adverse health outcomes 58 , 59 . Future research should account for specific lifestyle factors, including daily dietary patterns, to identify concrete risk factors contributing to arthritis risk among older adults based on their living arrangements and ability to maintain independent living 30 . Additionally, our study supports previous findings on the role of depression in the relationship between arthritis and cognitive decline. Prior research has explored this link from inflammatory and mediating perspectives. For example, Nerurkar et al. demonstrated that immune alterations associated with rheumatoid arthritis may contribute to depression, potentially explaining its higher prevalence in individuals with rheumatoid arthritis compared with the general population 60 . Similarly, He et al. identified that depressive and anxiety symptoms mediate the association between arthritis and cognitive impairment 28 . Our study builds on these findings by identifying depressive symptoms as key arthritis risk factors for older adults with cognitive decline, highlighting the need to incorporate mental health assessments into arthritis care management programs. Given that late-life depression can range from mild cognitive impairment to dementia, and that individuals in this at risk group may be less responsive to treatment 61 , 62 , a tailored monitoring system should be prioritised in comprehensive arthritis management. We acknowledge the following limitations. First, the participants attrition over time, which is often unavoidable in longitudinal studies on older adults, may introduce bias in health-related outcomes 63 . This attrition could potentially affect the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of our findings. Second, our study predominantly involves women, which may limit the generalisability of the results to the broader population. The overrepresentation of women in our sample suggests that the findings may not fully capture the arthritis risk factors among men or provide a comprehensive view of gender differences. Future studies should aim for a more balanced gender distribution to enhance the generalisability of the findings. Third, our assessment of multimorbidity was based solely on the number of chronic illnesses, without considering the severity of these conditions, due to data limitations. Similarly, our study could not account for potential biological or dietary factors, such as genetic information, dietary habits, or detailed information about medication use. Therefore, future studies that control for a wider range of confounders, including biological domains, are needed. Finally, our inability to differentiate between arthritis types necessitates a cautious approach in interpreting our findings. The lack of detailed arthritis classification may obscure specific risk factors associated with different forms of arthritis, highlighting the need for future research in more diverse populations with detailed diagnostic information. Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful insights as the first to trace the development of arthritis diagnosis among older adults by cognitive status and to identify associated risk factors. The distinct risk profiles identified in our study for older adults with and without cognitive decline emphasize the need for multifaceted prevention and management strategies. These approaches should encompass medical interventions while integrating public health initiatives that consider social support, mental healthcare, and lifestyle modifications. Public health strategies need to effectively allocate resources for tailored arthritis prevention programs, factoring in older adults’ cognitive status. Future research should focus on refining these strategies to be more personalized, enhancing their effectiveness and public health impact. Conclusion This study provides a new perspective on arthritis risk by highlighting older adults with cognitive decline as a baseline population and vulnerable group that needs special focus. While most research has explored how arthritis may lead to cognitive decline, our study reverses this approach, identifying critical risk factors that predispose cognitively declined older adults to arthritis, such as depressive symptoms, limited social interaction, living alone, and diminished self-care abilities. By analysing 12-year transitions in arthritis diagnoses using nationally representative longitudinal data from South Korea, we highlighted the specific challenges faced by this vulnerable population. These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive public health strategies that integrate mental health support, social engagement, and personalised assistance into arthritis prevention and management programs. Clinically, tailored interventions targeting depression and enhancing social engagement should be prioritised for older adults with cognitive decline to mitigate their increased arthritis risk. On a policy level, the aging population in South Korea and similar contexts calls for coordinated efforts across healthcare, community services, and policymaking to develop and implement these integrated strategies. Such efforts are crucial for reducing health burdens and improving the quality of life in aging societies. To further advance this research, prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to examine the causal pathways between severity of cognitive impairment and arthritis, and to explore other modifiable risk factors that could lead to effective interventions. These future studies will be essential for developing targeted strategies to prevent and manage arthritis in older adults with varying cognitive statuses. Methods Data source We utilised the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) data spanning from Wave 1 (2006) to Wave 8 (2020). It is a nationwide longitudinal panel survey of South Koreans aged 45 years and older, that collects information about their demographic characteristics, physical and psychological health status, health-related behaviours, family structures, and economic activities. A total of 10,254 participants in the baseline wave (2006) were randomly selected using a multi-stage, stratified probability sampling design to generate a nationally representative sample 64 . Trained researchers conducted biennial follow-ups with the participants using computer-assisted personal interviews and consistent questionnaires. Participants We used Wave 1 (2006) as the baseline. Among the 10,254 participants, those with baseline arthritis (1,695), those aged under 65 (5,476), those with missing covariate data (246), and one participant lacking arthritis status were excluded from the study. An additional 1,694 participants were excluded for missing arthritis status by Wave 8 (2020). The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) is a widely used screening tool both in clinical practice and research, for assessing cognitive function 65 . It is favoured for its ease of administration, minimal training, diagnostic utility in distinguishing between individuals with cognitive decline and those with normal cognition 66 , and tracking cognitive changes over time 67 . Given these advantages, the K-MMSE is a cost-effective tool for continuously monitoring cognitive function among community-dwelling older adults, as opposed to more complicated and time intensive diagnostic tools. We used the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE), a valid and widely recognised screening tool for measuring general cognition and cognitive function research 68 – 70 in South Korean populations. The K-MMSE assesses cognitive function across six domains: (1) orientation to time and place; (2) registration; (3) attention and calculation; (4) recall; (5) language; and (6) visuospatial ability. Each domain is scored out of a maximum of 5 points, with a total score of 30 points; higher scores indicate better cognitive function. Conventional classification criteria categorize conditions into the following: normal (K-MMSE ≥ 24); mild cognitive impairment (K-MMSE 18–23); and moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment (K-MMSE ≤ 17) 65 , 68 . The sample size for those with moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment receiving arthritis diagnoses accounted for 52 events, while those without arthritis accounted for 42. Given the minimum requirement of 10 events per variable to maintain the stability and reliability of robust estimates in the Cox regression model 71 , we grouped participants into 2 criteria for cognitive function status: (1) normal (K-MMSE ≥ 24) and (2) cognitive decline (K-MMSE ≤ 23) 69 , 72 . Therefore, our final cohort consisted of 1,142 participants, including 334 with cognitive decline and 808 with normal cognitive function (Fig. 3 ). Arthritis diagnosis In this study, ‘arthritis’ refers to a general category of arthritis. Its diagnosis was identified through a single question: ‘Have you been diagnosed with arthritis or rheumatoid arthritis by a physician since the previous survey?’ Additionally, the onset of arthritis was calculated using physician-reported diagnosis time. The period from the baseline to the time of an arthritis diagnosis was converted into months. For example, if arthritis was diagnosed in June 2014, the total period was calculated as 90 months, using the baseline (2006) as the starting point. If only the year of arthritis diagnosis was investigated, the time of diagnosis was uniformly set to December. We confirmed that none of the participants had fully recovered from arthritis by the end of the investigation. Predictors of arthritis We considered participants’ demographics, health behaviour, and health-related characteristics as potential risk factors 73 . Demographics included sex, age, educational level (≥ 9 years/&lt;9 years), employment status (employed/unemployed), region of residence (urban/rural), and living arrangement (living alone/residing with others). Social interaction was assessed by evaluating participation in social gatherings, such as religious events, meeting friends, or volunteer activities (&lt; 1 per week/≥1 per week). Health behaviour characteristics included history of smoking or drinking (former or current experience/no experience) and regular exercise habits (&lt; 1 time per week/≥1 time per week). Physical health status was evaluated using body mass index, grip strength, and number of physician-diagnosed chronic illnesses. The KLoSA utilises a handgrip dynamometer (Hand Grip Meter 6103, Tanita, Tokyo, Japan) to assess handgrip strength in kg. During the test, participants squeeze the dynamometer twice with each hand, and the average handgrip strength from all four attempts is then recorded. Considering sex difference 74 , grip strength was divided into terciles for men and women: (1) lowest tercile (men: 10.5–27.5 kg, women: 1.3–16.0 kg), (2) middle tercile (men: 27.8–31.5 kg, women: 16.3–19.8 kg), and (3) highest tercile (men: 31.8–46.5 kg, women: 20.0–29.5 kg). The total number of chronic illnesses, including hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, prostate disease, and psychiatric disease, was classified into three groups (none/one illness/two or more illnesses). Psychological health status was evaluated using the short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 10 and higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. For older adults, a cut-off score of 4 was used to identify significant depressive symptoms; it demonstrated 100% sensitivity and 92% specificity 75 , 76 . We used this cut-off to categorize the participants as either normal or experiencing depressive symptoms. Functional limitations were assessed using the Korean Activities of Daily Living (K-ADL) and Korean Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (K-IADL) scales. The K-ADL scale is a standardized assessment tool for measuring the functional status of older adults, which includes eating, bathing, grooming, walking, dressing, transferring, and toileting 77 . Each domain is rated on a scale from independent (0) to dependent (1). In a validation study conducted with older South Korean adults, including a healthy function and patient group, the K-ADL demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94), strong inter-rater reliability (kappa value = 0.86‒1.00), and a test-retest reliability correlation (≥ 0.70) 77 . Similarly, the K-IADL scale evaluates functional status in more complex activities, including shopping, cooking, grooming, taking short walks, managing finances, using transportation, laundry, housekeeping, making phone calls, and medication management 78 . Each K-IADL item is rated on a scale from independent (0) to requiring assistance (1). The validation study for the K-IADL, involving the same demographic cohorts, showed robust internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94), inter-rater reliability (kappa value = 0.81‒0.95), and test-retest reliability correlation (≥ 0.70) 78 . In this study, higher scores on the K-ADL and K-IADL scales indicate a lower level of functional abilities, and both scores were treated as continuous variables. Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were used to present categorical variables as numbers with proportions (%) and continuous variables as mean and standard deviations. Differences between older adults with and without cognitive decline were assessed using χ 2 tests for categorical variables and analysis of variance for continuous variables. To estimate the probability of arthritis diagnosis over time among all participants, we conducted a Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank test comparing the two groups. We further employed multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to compare risk factors for arthritis among older adults with and without cognitive decline. We analysed the total sample, and then further stratified it by cognition group. These models included potential factors such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, physical and psychological health conditions, functional limitations, and health-behavioural factors. The selection of these factors was based on their established relevance in existing literature and availability in our dataset. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residual tests; the assumption was satisfied for all models. The probability level indicating statistical significance was set at P &lt; .05. Stata version 16 (Stata Corp., Texas, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.
