Abstract This study investigated maternal and their pup neurobehaviour following nicotine exposure during gestational and postnatal periods and neuroprotective effect of Omega-3 fatty acids. Thus, the study used thirty pregnant female Wistar rats for this study. Groups I and II (nonpregnant and pregnant) were treated with 1 ml/kg/day of normal saline for 42 days; III was treated with 4 mg/kg/day of nicotine for 42 days; IV-VI were co-administered nicotine 4 mg/kg and 100, 300, 600 mg/kg/day of Omega-3 fatty acids respectively for 42 days. Results: The beam walk time of the mother rats in groups III and IV were significantly higher when compared with other groups. Similarly, the beam walks time of the pups of groups III and IV were significantly higher when compared with the pups of the mother rats in other groups. The brain dopamine and serotonin levels of mother rats in groups III, IV and V were significantly higher when compared with other groups. Also, the brain dopamine and serotonin levels of the pups in groups III and IV were significantly higher when compared with the pups in other groups. The reduced glutathione and catalase of mother and pup rats in groups III and IV were significantly lower when compared with other groups. Photomicrographs of cerebellum and hippocampus of the rats treated with nicotine showed scattered arrangement of pyramidal cells with vacuolated neurons. These alterations were significantly reversed with Omega-3 fatty acids following nicotine exposure. Conclusions. Omega-3 fatty acids at 300 and 600 mg/kg ameliorated nicotine-induced neurotoxicity in mother rats and their pups. Introduction Nicotine has been linked to etiopathologies of different diseases, including neurobehavioral toxicity caused by smoking exposure ( Law et al., 2003 ). Brain cellular loss throughout development has been linked to prenatal exposure to nicotine in previous study ( Abdel-Rahman et al., 2005 ). It is a direct cholinergic agonist at the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) site, leading to behavioral effects and cellular loss ( Abdel-Rahman et al., 2005 , Betnowitz and Jacob, 2000 ). Exposure to nicotine early in life causes oxidative damage to nicotinic receptors in the brain and subsequently abnormal behavior in adulthood ( Abdel-Rahman et al., 2005 ). The increase dietary nicotine consumption nevertheless has been reported to increase the risk of various behavioral defect and other neural diseases ( Dong et al., 2020 , Nielsen et al., 2013 ). This study suggested that nicotine might have opposite functions with respect to neuroprotection and neurodegeneration. An experimental study revealed that nicotine stops dopaminergic neuron loss in a rodent neural disease of memory and cognitive model ( Dong et al., 2020 , Liu et al., 2017 ). Moreover, nicotine has been associated with a decreased risk for Alzheimer’s disease ( Townsend et al., 2004 , Echeverria and Zeitlin, 2012 , Lombardo and Maskos, 2015 , Moreno-Gonzalez et al., 2013 ). Nicotine positively influences different aspects of cognition, including learning, memory, and attention ( Ferrea and Winterer, 2009 ). It also improves mood, stress regulation, anxiety ( Ferrea and Winterer, 2009 ), Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease. According to cell culture experiments, nicotine has neurotrophic properties, and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) play significant roles in the control of neurodevelopment and maturation of the central nervous system before parturition. ( Levin, 2002 , Ferrea and Winterer, 2009 ). Omega−3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) with a double bond three atoms away from the terminal methyl group in their chemical structure. They are extensively distributed in nature, are vital constituents of animal lipid metabolism, and perform vital functions in the human diet and in human physiology ( Scortletti and Byrne, 2013 ). Omega-3 fatty acids are eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), both of which are often found in marine oils, and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), which is found in plant oils. ( Scortletti and Byrne, 2013 ). Alpha-linolenic acid contains walnut, edible seeds, algal oil, clary, sage seed oil, flaxseed oil, hemp oil, sacha Inch oil and Echium oil, while sources of the animal omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA include fish, fish oil eggs from chickens fed EPA and DHA, krill oil, squid oil and certain algae. Mammals are unable to produce the essential omega-3 fatty acid ALA but acquire it through the diet. Nevertheless, ALA can be used to form EPA and DHA by generating additional double bonds along its carbon chain (desaturation) and extending it (elongation). ALA is subsequently used to produce EPA, which is subsequently used to produce DHA ( Scortletti and Byrne, 2013 ). The omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (particularly, docosahexaenoic) is uniquely enriched in the brain and retina and is required for proper human brain development and visual functions ( Birch et al., 2000 , Innis and Friesen, 2008 ). These findings support the beneficial effects of omega-3 fatty acids in different neurological and neurodegenerative disorders ( Dennis et al., 2015 ). DHA, a principal member of the omega-3 fatty acid family, is highly concentrated in the brain and the outer segments of retinal cones and rods, accounting for approximately half of the total omega-3 fatty acids ( Parletta et al., 2013 ). Several studies have revealed that supplementation with DHA, an omega-3 fatty acids during pregnancy has neuroprotective effects on prenatal stress-induced brain disorders in rats and prevents hypertoxic and ischemic injuries of the rat brain ( Wainwright, 2002 , Feng et al., 2012 , Tuzun et al., 2012 ). Evidence from animal and human studies has shown that the DHA diet supplement reduced hippocampal oxidized protein level thereby positively activate hippocampal Akit and CaMKII levels in improving the cognitive, memory and learning capacity of the rats exposed to brain stress model ( Wu et al., 2008 ). Omega-3 FAs participates in synaptogenesis, synaptic transmission, learning, and memory processes ( Cao et al., 2009 ). Omega-3 FAs have been reported in previous studies to possess antioxidant ( Bazan, 2005 , Calon et al., 2004 , Hashimoto et al., 2002 ), anti-inflammatory ( Bazan, 2005 , Marcheselli et al., 2003 ) and anti-apoptotic properties ( Calon et al., 2005 ). Hence, this study investigated the impact of omega-3 fatty acids on nicotine-induced neurotoxicity in pregnant Wistar rats and their pups. Products Inc., Green Bay, W1 54311, USA; nicotine (11 mg/ml) was purchased from Alchem Europe SA. Via al flume 1,6929. Gravesano. Ketamine hydrochloride (50 mg/10 ml) was purchased from SWISS Parenterals Ltd., 809, Karala GIDC. Dist. Ahmedabad 302220. Gujarat, INDIA. Assay kits for biochemical parameters were purchased from Elabscience, Randox Manual/RX Monza, Cusabio and Abcam. Animal care Male and female adult Wistar rats weighing between 140 and 150 g were used for this research. They were obtained from a reputable animal care vendor, Ile-Ife, Nigeria. All the animals were kept healthy at favorable temperature and they were allowed free access to food and water. The rats were kept in the animal facility for 2 weeks before the commencement of the experiment. Experimental design Thirty (30) adults female and ten (10) adults male Wistar rats weighing between 140 and 150 g were used for this research. The female rats were randomly distributed into six (6) groups such that each group contained 5 female rats. After the distribution of the female rats, each rat was weighed before the commencement of the study. Male rats were introduced into each of the female rat cage in ratio 2:5 for copulation. The pregnancy was tested by the method of Ochiogu et al., 2006 via checking the vaginal smear grossly by hand and under microscope for the presence of protein coagulate plug which was the confirmatory test for the rat pregnancy. Once the protein coagulate was observed on the vaginal smear of each rat (which was regarded as day 1 of conception), the rat was weighed at four-day intervals to check the progress of the pregnancy. During the course of the experiment, the female rats were administered nicotine and omega-3 fatty acids immediately pregnancy was confirmed, at 21 days of delivery and continued at post-delivery for 21 days. Grouping of experimental animals Group I included nonpregnant female Wistar rats . The rats were treated with 1 ml/kg physiological saline (0.9 % NaCl) orally daily for 42 days. Group II included pregnant female Wistar rats . The rats were treated with 1 ml/kg physiological saline (0.9 % NaCl) orally daily for 42 days. Group III included pregnant female Wistar rats . The rats were treated with 4 mg/kg nicotine orally daily for 42 days ( Neogy et al., 2008 ). Nicotine was given during (21 days) and after pregnancy (21 days). Group IV included pregnant female Wistar rats . The rats were orally administered 4 mg/kg nicotine daily and 100 mg/kg omega-3 fatty acids orally daily for 42 days. Both nicotine and omega-3 fatty acids were administered together during (21 days) and after pregnancy (21 days) ( Abdel Moneim et al., 2011 ). Group V included pregnant female Wistar rats . The rats were treated with both 4 mg/kg nicotine orally and 300 mg/kg omega-3 fatty acids orally daily for 42 days. Group VI included pregnant female Wistar rats . The rats were treated with both 4 mg/kg nicotine daily and 600 mg/kg omega-3 fatty acids orally daily for 42 days ( Singh et al., 2016 ). The male pups of all the mother rats in the study design were selected for the study along their mother. Each male pup was assigned according to the group of their mother as follows; Group II: This comprises of four male pups, belong to the female rats treated with 1 ml/kg physiological saline (0.9 % NaCl) orally daily for 42 days. Group III: Four male pups, belong to the mother rats treated with 4 mg/kg nicotine orally daily for 42 days. Group IV: Four male pups, belong to the mother rats treated with both 4 mg/kg nicotine daily and 100 mg/kg omega-3 fatty acids orally daily for 42 days. Group V: Four male pups, belong to the mother rats treated with both 4 mg/kg nicotine daily and 300 mg/kg omega-3 fatty acids orally daily for 42 days. Group VI: Four male pups, belong to the mother rats treated with both 4 mg/kg nicotine daily and 600 mg/kg omega-3 fatty acids orally daily for 42 days. Neurobehavioral evaluations Neurobehavioral tests were carried out for each rat (mothers and their pups) in each group following the standard protocol as described below, and the results were documented. Inclined plane test The inclined plane test was used to evaluate limb motor function in the Wistar rats. This procedure followed the methodology of Abou-Donia et al., 2001 . The mother rats and their offspring were placed on a flat plane separately in the horizontal position, with the head facing the side of the board to be raised, which was measured to the nearest 5 ° . The angle at which the rat began to slip downward was recorded. Three trials were conducted for each exposure. The intertrial period was one hour. The results of the three trials were averaged and recorded. Forepaw grip test This method was adopted from Abou-Donia et al., 2001 procedure, where the grip strength of the mother and their pups were measured to ascertain appropriate neuromuscular function of the hand and forearm of the rats. This model was carried out on an 8 mm diameter wood dowel that was raised horizontally so that the body weight of the rat is supported on the wood dowel. The time to release the grip by the animal was recorded (in seconds) by a stopwatch. Three successive trials were carried out to measure forelimb strength only. Beam-walking test This procedure was experimented as described by Carter et al., 1999 and Perry et al., 1995 . The beam-walking or beam balance test measures fine motor balance and coordination in Wistar rats. The goal of this test was for the rat to walk across a horizontal narrow beam to a safe platform without deviating from normal route. Step-down latency in the active avoidance test This procedure followed the methodology of Jarvik and Essmann, 1960 , which was modified by Hudspeth et al. (1964) . A plain block of wood was placed on one side of the grid floor of a continuous avoidance apparatus, which served as a shock-free zone. Each rat was placed in the shock-free zone, and upon stepping down, it received an electric shock (5 V) through the grid floor. The time taken for each rat to step down was recorded with a recorder. Biochemical analysis The animals were sacrificed through ketamine and the brain tissues of all the animals (mothers and pups) were carefully removed, washed, and weighed. The tissues were homogenate and centrifuged to separate the wanted from unwanted substance. The supernatant was obtained for the estimation of serotonin, dopamine, superoxide dismutase (SOD) catalase (CAT), reduced glutathione (GSH), acetylcholinesterase, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), inflammatory marker (interleukin 6 and TNF-alpha) and apoptotic marker (caspase-3) activity. Evaluation of Serotonin The serotonin concentration in the brain tissue of each rat was measured following the manufacturer’s protocol (Sandwich-ELISA kit, Elabscience; E-EL-R1140). Wistar rat brain tissue was collected for the estimation of serotonin. At the end of this protocol, the serotonin concentration in the brain tissue homogenate was then calculated by extrapolating the OD of the samples from the standard curve. The concentration of brain serotonin was expressed as ng/ml. Assessment of the reduced glutathione (GSH) level GSH was quantified following the protocol reported by Beutler et al. (1963) . In brief, 1 ml of the supernatant was added to 0.5 ml of 10 mM Ellman’s reagent. Thereafter, 2 ml of phosphate buffer was added, and the resulting yellow precipitate was read as an absorbance against a blank. Serially diluted standard concentrations were analyzed similarly, and GSH was determined in mg/100 g of the tissue. Determination of superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity SOD activity was examined using the method reported by Misra and Fridovich (1972) . Two hundred microliters of the supernatant were mixed with Tris–HCl buffer, 300 μL of 2 mM pyrogallol and 30 mM EDTA. Changes in absorbance were recorded for 3 min to obtained SOD activity. Catalase activity The brain homogenate of catalase was measured using the Aebi (1984) laboratory method. A total of 2000 μL of phosphate buffer and 200 μL of H 2 O 2 were added to 40 μL of the above brain supernatant. The disappearance of hydrogen peroxide (H 2 O 2 ) was monitored for 3–4 min at 30 s intervals using a spectrophotometer to obtained catalase activity. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase concentration This enzyme was analyzed according to the laboratory protocol of Kornberg et al. (1955) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Randox Manual/RX Monza, G-6-PDH). This colorimetric method used a cuvette with a 1 cm light path, a temperature of approximately 37°C, and a wavelength of 340 nm. The concentrations of enzyme in the homogenates were determined by the standard concentration at U/g Hb. Evaluation of acetylcholinesterase This activity was assayed by a laboratory protocol according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Abcam; acetylcholinesterase assay kit, colorimetric). Then, 0.6 ml of the assay solution was added to buffer in a vial containing DTNB to make a 20X DTNB stock solution. The solution was incubated at 37°C. A total of 1.0 % of the sample was added to the stock solution, 0.6 ml of ddH2O was added to the vial of acetylthiocholine, and 100 µl of ddH2O with 0.1 % BSA was added to the vial of acetylcholinesterase standard to make a 50 units/ml acetylcholinesterase reaction. The concentration of acetylcholinesterase was obtained and plotted. Evaluation of Dopamine This was assayed by a laboratory protocol according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Abcam; Dopamine kit, colorimetric). All the reagents, samples and standards were prepared. The plate was washed 2 times with 1X wash solution before standard, sample and control wells were added. Then, 50 µl of biotin-conjugated antibody working solution was added to each well, and the plate was covered with a plate sealer. All the samples were incubated and washed with IX solution after incubation, and SABC working solution was added to each well and incubated. The samples were then washed again, and 90 µl of TMB substrate was added to each well and incubated in the dark within 15–20 min. The shades of blue were observed in the 3–4 wells at the end of the incubation, and 50 µl of stop solution was added to each well. The wavelength was read at 450 nm within 20 min. Evaluation of Caspase-3 This was assayed by a laboratory protocol according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cusabio; caspase-3 assay kit). The prepared reagents, working standards, and samples (brain homogenates) were prepared as described in the protocol manual. Then, 100 μl of standard or sample was added to each well, and the plates were covered with the adhesive strips provided. At the end of the protocol, the optical density of each well was determined within 5 min, and a microplate reader set to 450 nm was used to determine the caspase-3 concentration in the samples. Evaluation of tumor necrosis factor Alpha and IL-6 The levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in the homogenate from each rat were measured following the manufacturer’s protocol (Sandwich-ELISA kit, Elabscience). Rat brain homogenates were obtained for the estimation of TNF-α and IL-6 levels. Then, 100 μL of brain homogenate was added to each well with diluted TNF-α and IL-6 detection antibodies. At the end of the protocol, the concentrations of TNF-α and IL-6 were expressed as pg/ml. Histological processes The brain tissue biopsies (hippocampus and cerebellum) of the rats were fixed in 10 % formalin for staining using hematoxylin-eosin. The processed tissues were observed under microscope and photomicrographs were captured with a camera at 400x magnification. Changes in each subfield (CA1, CA2 and CA3) of the hippocampus were observed under a light microscope and the neuron cells were counted and quantified by image pro software. Statistical analysis The data represented in mean ± standard deviation (SD) and one–way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. The results were considered significant when p ˂ 0.05. GraphPad 5.03 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). Discussion Omega-3 FAs showed adequate neuroprotective in pregnant Wistar rats and their male pups following maternal exposure to nicotine during and after pregnancy periods. Testing for neurobehavioral deficits, the beam walk time, forepaw grip strength, inclined plane and active avoidance showed alteration pattern in the male pups from nicotine-exposed mothers on postnatal day 21. These neurobehavioral deficits have been demonstrated initially by the nicotine exposed mother rats during gestational and postnatal periods of the study. These neurobehavioral anomalies demonstrate treatment-related sensorimotor deficits that may be justified by the histopathological alterations present in the cerebellum and hippocampus ( Abdel-Rahman et al., 2005 ). Since significant alterations exist in the cerebellum, cerebellar neuronal and glial inadequacies might cause beam walking deficits. These neuropathological alterations might be linked to nicotine-induced oxidative stress ( Guan et al., 2003 ). Moreover, nicotine agonists have been shown to promote synaptic regulation in developing cerebellar Purkinje neuronal cells ( Kawa, 2002 ). Neurobehavioral abnormality has been reported in previous study ( Adriani et al., 2003 ) to involved nicotine exposure during peri-adolescence in rats. However, the neurobehavioral deficits in the beam walk time, beam walk score, forepaw grip strength and inclination plane caused by nicotine-induced oxidative damage were reversed following treatment with omega-3 FAs as a neuroprotective supplement. There was a significant increase in dopamine and serotonin in the brains of nicotine-treated rats, and these changes were reversed by coadministration of omega-3 FAs. This finding is in agreement with Song et al. (2009) and Taepavarapruk and Song (2010) , who reported ameliorative effects of omega-3 FAs against olfactory bulbectomy and IL-1 beta induced neurobehavioral deficits in rats. In the present study, the activities of the enzymes, acetylcholinesterase and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) were significantly reduced in nicotine-treated rats compared to control and omega-3 FAs rats, which might be attributed to the oxidative stress caused by nicotine on the neuronal activity. The nicotine-induced reduction in acetylcholinesterase and G6PD activities has been shown to be protected following cotreatment with omega-3 FAs, indicating the neuroprotective and antioxidant efficacy of omega-3 FAs ( Ross et al., 2007 ). In the present study, elevated levels of caspase 3, TNF-alpha and IL-6, were linked to neuroinflammation in nicotine-treated maternal rats and their pups, revealing ongoing inflammation. Inflammation in the brain could be advantageous in maintaining its homeostasis in response to infection. However, overwhelming of inflammatory process during undergoing infection on brain cells over the protecting mechanism of the neuronal tissue resulting in damaging of brain cells that are vital for normal functions of the brain activity. The undergoing brain inflammation involves microglia, which is the protecting mechanism of the brain cells ( Aloisi, 2001 ). When triggered, these cells synthesize pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. However, as result of continuous production of proinflammatory cytokines, these molecules become neurotoxic, leading to neuronal injury involved in numerous brain pathologies ( Blais and Rivest, 2003 , Layé, 2010 , Solito and Sastre, 2012 ). This inflammatory action of nicotine on the neuronal cells readily linked with brain oxidative injury. The anti-inflammatory effect of omega-3 FAs was demonstrated in this study by the significant reduction in the elevated levels of TNF-alpha and IL-6 induced by nicotine. Numerous epidemiologic and observational studies have revealed that subjects with greater blood omega-FA levels have decreased proinflammatory cytokine production ( Ferrucci et al., 2006 , Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 2011 , Farzaneh-Far et al., 2009 , Alfano et al., 2012 ). In addition, a diet rich in EPA decreased the synthesis of the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin (IL)-1b and ameliorated synaptic plasticity impairment in the hippocampus of old rats ( Martin et al., 2002 , Lynch et al., 2007 ). In contrast, low dietary intake of omega-3 FAs has detrimental effects on the brain, particularly during the perinatal period of brain development. For example, dietary omega-3 FA deficiency beginning on the first day of gestation reduces the DHA level, changes the microglial phenotype and motility, and increases the expression of the brain proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-alpha in the pup brains of mice and rats ( McNamara et al., 2010 ). Caspase 3 was significantly lower in omega-3FA-treated mothers and pup rats than in nicotine-treated mothers and pup rats. This demonstrated the antiapoptotic efficacy of omega-3 FAs against the neurotoxic effect of nicotine. This indicated that omega-3 FAs prevented apoptosis in protected rats. This finding is supported by a previous study ( Suphioglu et al., 2010 ), which demonstrated a direct association between DHA treatment and caspase-3 protein levels. In the present study, the activities of SOD and CAT and the amount of reduced glutathione were significantly reduced in nicotine-treated rats, suggesting membrane injury, which may be due to free radical production. SOD and CAT oppose radicals such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, respectively. Glutathione peroxidase has a scavenging effect against peroxyl, alkxyl and alkyl radicals that can form from oxidized membrane compounds and uses GSH as a substrate ( Das et al., 2009 ). The present study corroborates the earlier work of Jain and Flora (2012), who discovered that increasing the concentration of lipid peroxidation in nicotine-treated rats is linked to reducing the activities of CAT and SOD. In the present study, nicotine-induced decreases in the levels of CAT and SOD and a decrease in the level of glutathione were protected following coadministration of nicotine and omega-3 FAs, suggesting that nicotine has a neuroprotective effect. The nicotine-induced reduction in acetylcholinesterase and G6PD activities has been shown to be protected following cotreatment with nicotine and omega-3 FAs, showing its neuroprotective efficacy. Histopathological findings in this study revealed a significant reduction in the number of surviving Purkinje neuronal cells in the cerebellum and CA1, CA2 and CA3 subfield of the hippocampus in pups on PND 21 from mothers exposed to nicotine on gestational days (GDs) 1–21. These findings indicated that these Purkinje cells in the cerebellum and subfields of the hippocampus are sensitive to the deleterious effects of nicotine. The cerebellum is a well-organized structure in which neuronal purkinje signals are the major outputs of the cerebellar cortex ( Altman, 1972a , Altman, 1972c , Altman, 1972b ), and it is one of the first brain structures to transform. The hippocampus is a temporal lobe brain structure peculiarly linked to memory, emotion, and neurogenesis. It appears to be sensitive to the effects of drugs abuse ( Kutlu et al., 2016 ). Nicotine exposure is particularly damaging to the antenatally developing hippocampus, as cholinergic activity modulates relocation and constituents of nascent hippocampal circuitry ( Lauder and Schambra, (1999) . In the subfield of the hippocampus, prenatal nicotine exposure is linked to an increase in dying neuron density, evidence by the reduction in the hippocampus neuronal cells quantification in various subfields of the histophotomicrographs. These decreased in neuron cells counts observed in the nicotine exposed rats is due to cellular injury. This histopathological study was in conformity with the previous study which revealed reduction in the size of cells in the hippocampal at CA3 region following maternal exposure to nicotine during the gestational period ( Abdel-Rahman et al., 2005 ). In the present study, the groups treated with omega-3 FAs, particularly, the groups that received highest doses showed improvement in number of cells in the subfields of hippocampus. Thus, cotreatment of rats with omega-3 FAs reversed brain injury in the cerebellum and hippocampus compared to that in rats exposed to nicotine only. Conclusion In conclusion, the results of the present study indicated that omega-3 fatty acids (FAs) at 300 and 600 mg/kg/body weight ameliorate nicotine-induced brain oxidative stress through direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and prevention of compromise brain inflammation. Cotreatment with nicotine and omega-3 FAs minimized the effects of nicotine-induced biochemical and histopathological alterations. However, 600 mg/kg of omega-3 FAs was more effective at ameliorating these effects. This study revealed that omega-3 fatty acids could be a good supplement for both expectant and nursing mothers during the antenatal and postnatal periods and could support cognitive improvement and prevent total neurodegeneration during the developmental stages of the offspring. Therefore, further studies on the impact of omega-3 fatty acids on the prevention of nicotine or any other environmentally toxic agents during pregnancy and during the developmental stages of offspring are needed.
