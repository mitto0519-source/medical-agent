Abstract Background and Objectives Despite a growing body of research on the effects of spousal bereavement, little is known about whether spousal loss is longitudinally associated with changes in loneliness among widowed individuals. Furthermore, few studies have examined the roles of spousal caregiving and gender in this context. The present study investigates (a) trajectories of loneliness before and after the death of a spouse and (b) whether these trajectories of loneliness differ by gender and caregiving involvement of surviving spouse. Research Design and Methods Using data from nine waves of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), the analysis employed fixed effects models to account for individual heterogeneity. Moreover, stratified analyses were conducted to investigate the moderating effect of gender and spousal caregiving. Results The study revealed that spousal loss significantly increased loneliness during the first-year postbereavement, with levels returning to baseline thereafter. Gender-stratified analyses indicated that female survivors experienced a temporary increase in loneliness, whereas male survivors showed no significant changes. Moreover, male spouses, who provided care, experienced an increase in loneliness within the first year after the death, which persisted through the second year. Discussion and Implications The findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive, gender-specific support programs for bereaved individuals, particularly targeting the first year of loss and addressing the unique challenges faced by male caregivers. Policies promoting social engagement and community support for widowed older adults are essential to mitigating the emotional burden of bereavement in Korea. Despite the resilience developed through lifetime experiences, the loss of a spouse represents a profound disruption to psychosocial and physical well-being ( Gyasi &amp; Phillips, 2020 ; King et al., 2019 ). For instance, previous research has demonstrated the association between losing a spouse and increased loneliness among surviving spouses ( Kung, 2020 ). In the absence of a beloved figure, bereaved individuals experience a profound sense of loneliness ( Bowlby, 1980 ) that differs fundamentally from voluntary solitude ( Florczak &amp; Lockie, 2019 ). Emotional loneliness represents an affective status of isolation due to the lack of intimacy ( Weiss, 1973 ), and its implications extend beyond mere isolation. Within the grieving process, loneliness emerges as a critical dimension of psychological adaptation to loss, particularly in relation to identity reconstruction following the death of an attachment figure. The temporal dynamics of spousal loss significantly influence the experience of loneliness. Recent empirical findings suggest that the timing of loss substantially shapes psychological adaptation patterns before and after the spousal loss ( Sheftel et al., 2024 ). In line with this, the pattern of loneliness before and after the death of a spouse presents a complex picture. The anticipation of impending loss, particularly in cases involving chronic illness, can facilitate psychological preparation, potentially moderating the immediate emotional impact of bereavement ( Chen et al., 2020 ). This emotional readiness allows surviving spouses, especially those in caregiving roles, to experience less steep increases in loneliness ( Kotwal et al., 2024 ). On the other hand, research has demonstrated that prolonged forewarning of spousal loss can be associated with elevated stress-related hormones during the initial 6 months of bereavement, compared to sudden loss situations ( Chen et al., 2020 ). This finding suggests that while pre-loss contexts may provide opportunities for psychological preparation, they might also intensify the negative effects of the death of a spouse on the trajectories of loneliness. The immediate aftermath of spousal loss typically manifests as an acute surge in loneliness, representing a profound disruption to both emotional and practical aspects of daily life ( Freak-Poli et al., 2022 ; Jiao et al., 2024 ; van Baarsen, 2002 ). This period is characterized by the abrupt termination of a significant romantic relationship, creating an immediate void in the surviving spouse’s life. This disruption is particularly significant considering the interdependence of marital relationships ( Kim &amp; Hwang, 2024a ; Kim &amp; Kwon, 2023 ). sosited that marriages function as social contracts built on complementary abilities and labor division. In the absence of the deceased, surviving spouses struggle to manage previously shared responsibilities ( Schmitz, 2021 ). Research indicates that the physical absence of a spouse in daily routines often precipitates an intense “sense of emptiness,” directly contributing to heightened feelings of loneliness ( Fogarty, 2020 ). The severity of this immediate effect is further related to the increased short-term mortality rates among surviving spouses ( Brenn &amp; Ytterstad, 2016 ), underlying the profound physiological and psychological consequences of spousal death. Despite the long-term effects of spousal loss on various aspects of the well-being of surviving partners, there are mixed findings regarding the persistence of loneliness. Some studies have identified enduring patterns of loneliness among bereaved individuals ( Seltzer &amp; Li, 2000 ). In cases where there is no proper redefinition of the relationship, for instance, the continuing bond with the deceased can prolong high levels of loneliness ( Florczak &amp; Lockie, 2019 ). In contrast, emerging research suggests that loneliness may not necessarily persist as a long-term consequence of spousal loss. Sanders (1983) described loneliness as initial “anger-out” reactions, further linked to the “empty hand syndrome” (characterized by acute awareness of the deceased’s physical absence) among surviving partners. These emotional responses often resolve over time as they are actively processed and externalized rather than internalized, suggesting that elevated levels of loneliness may be a transitional rather than permanent feature of the bereavement experience. The dual process model offers a theoretical framework for understanding diverse patterns of grief adaptation ( Stroebe &amp; Schut, 1999 ). This model outlines two distinct but interrelated coping processes: loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping. Loss-oriented coping involves actively engaging with grief, processing emotions, and maintaining psychological connections with the deceased. In contrast, restoration-oriented coping focuses on adapting to life changes, establishing new routines, and developing new roles and relationships. Crucially, the model underscores that both processes are essential for healthy adaptation, with effective coping characterized by the ability to oscillate between these two orientations ( Stroebe et al., 2001 ). Successful grief processing involves a dynamic balance between engaging with loss-related aspects and embracing restoration-related changes, with the timing and intensity of each process naturally varying across individuals and circumstances. Previous studies have provided substantial evidence of gender-specific coping patterns, showing that women are generally more inclined toward loss-oriented coping than men. This gender difference highlights the importance of considering both coping styles to gain a comprehensive understanding of bereavement adaptation. Research on bereavement experiences has highlighted distinct gender differences in responses to spousal loss. Studies have shown that women often exhibit a stronger tendency toward loss-oriented coping processes ( King et al., 2019 ). This heightened engagement with loss is associated with higher reported loneliness among bereaved women ( Olawa et al., 2021 ), particularly when they struggle with identity reconstruction ( Yang, 2021 ). However, the gendered impact of spousal loss is complex. Some studies suggest that widowers may be particularly vulnerable, especially when they lack internal support resources ( King et al., 2019 ). Due to a lower propensity for help-seeking behavior, men may experience prolonged loneliness ( Jiao et al., 2024 ). Adding to this complexity, other research has found no significant gender differences in bereavement-related loneliness ( Yang &amp; Gu, 2021 ). These mixed findings indicate that sociocultural and personal factors—beyond gender alone—play critical roles in shaping how individuals navigate between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping processes following spousal loss. Furthermore, the role of spousal caregiving emerges as a determinant factor in shaping trajectories of loneliness with its effects, presenting both challenges and potential benefits. Spousal caregiving encompasses social, emotional, and psychological dimensions in maintaining the well-being of an ill partner ( Pinquart &amp; Sörensen, 2003 ). Therefore, caregiving can provoke caregiving burden due to the active provision of time and effort ( Kim &amp; Yoon, 2022 ; Yee &amp; Schulz, 2000 ). Despite the prevailing evidence of the negative effect of caregiving on psychological health, caregiving may offer protective elements in the bereavement process. In contrast to the previous notion, a recent study failed to detect a negative effect on self-esteem. Moreover, it was pointed out that the caregiving experience can facilitate better post-loss adjustment, as bereaved caregivers often experience a sense of self-reclamation when reestablishing their pre-caregiving lives ( Peacock et al., 2018 ). The intersection of gender and caregiving roles presents a critical dimension in understanding loneliness trajectories following spousal loss ( Pinquart &amp; Sörensen, 2003 ). This is attributed to the notion that caregiving itself is a gendered phenomenon ( Yoon &amp; Kim, 2022 ). Although male participation in spousal caregiving has increased, caregiving remains predominantly associated with traditional female roles ( Yee &amp; Schulz, 2000 ). Similarly, it was found that spousal caregiving provokes a sense of meaning among women ( Yee &amp; Schulz, 2000 ). Moreover, women often benefit from robust social support networks, which help them avoid social loneliness after a loss ( Hwang &amp; Hong, 2021 ). In contrast, bereaved men may experience loneliness differently due to their role as breadwinners ( Seok &amp; Jang, 2017 ). For instance, financial resources can mitigate the sense of loneliness only among bereaved men ( Freak-Poli et al., 2022 ), potentially emphasizing their roles as the heads of households. However, this gendered dynamic in caregiving and its implications for post-loss adjustment remain understudied, particularly concerning the experiences of male caregivers. Previous literature has elucidated the adverse effects of spousal death among older adults in Korea, including depression ( Lee et al., 2017 ) and cognitive decline ( Lyu et al., 2019 ). Furthermore, multiple studies have indicated heterogeneity in these associations. For instance, Yoon et al. (2022) observed the effect of spousal loss on psychosocial well-being, highlighting the particular vulnerability of bereaved men. Similarly, Song and Kim (2024) further pointed out the association between spousal loss and reduced physical capabilities. Conversely, Min and Kim (2020) demonstrated diminished cognitive abilities among bereaved women. Despite the substantial evidence of the detrimental impacts of spousal loss, there remains a paucity of quantitative research exploring this effect on loneliness within the Korean context. Notably, Sheftel et al. (2024) conducted cross-national comparisons to investigate the association between life events and loneliness, finding no significant effect of spousal loss on loneliness in Korea. This absence of significant findings may be attributed to the study’s focus on general patterns of loneliness across multiple countries. In Korea, the experience of spousal loss is distinctly shaped by cultural norms and social structures that may intensify feelings of loneliness among the bereaved. For instance, discussions of death and expressions of grief are stigmatized, creating barriers for those seeking emotional support or openly processing their loss ( Cacciatore &amp; DeFrain, 2015 ). This cultural context of emotional restraint can lead to social isolation precisely when individuals most need connection and support. The challenges are particularly acute for older adults, who face limited access to formal support services specifically designed for widowed individuals in Korea ( Lee et al., 2017 ). The scarcity of community-based resources, combined with cultural barriers to seeking help, can create a cycle of isolation that perpetuates and intensifies feelings of loneliness. Moreover, cultural attitudes toward remarriage after widowhood present additional challenges. With traditional social norms emphasizing loyalty to the deceased spouse, the surviving spouse may be reluctant to form new romantic relationships ( Lee &amp; Park, 2021 ). This cultural tendency generates psychological and social challenges for bereaved individuals in Korea, potentially exacerbating their experience of loneliness. Consistent with the findings of caregiving as predominantly female domains, sociocultural contexts may amplify the effects of gender and caregiving on the association between time to spousal loss and loneliness. In the lack of formal caregiving support in Korea, informal caregiving remains female’s domains ( Park et al., 2020 ). Moreover, gender role socialization continues to portray distinct caregiving expectations, emphasizing women’s roles as primary caregivers. Paik (2015) noted that engagement in spousal caregiving diminishes the “fear of being left alone” among older women. On the other hand, Confucian values establish role expectations as household heads and primary breadwinners among men ( Lee, 2015 ). In the absence of deceased women as “kin-keepers” for emotional ties among the family members ( Rosenthal, 1985 ), surviving men are likely to become emotionally lonely ( Hwang &amp; Hong, 2021 ; Kim, 2023 ). These challenges of adaptation are often exacerbated by constrained socioeconomic resources, as caregiving duties can impede men’s ability to fulfill their expected breadwinner role ( Lee et al., 2020 ). Despite the importance of these issues, a significant research gap persists concerning the experiences of male caregivers within the Korean cultural context ( Park et al., 2020 ). This study examines the temporal patterns of emotional loneliness among widowed individuals in Korea, with particular attention to gender differences and caregiving roles. Drawing on nine waves of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), a nationally representative longitudinal survey, this research explores the effects of spousal loss among adults aged 45 and older. To address potential confounding factors, the analysis employs standard fixed effects models, which control both observed and unobserved individual-level heterogeneity in the association between spousal loss and loneliness. The study adopts subgroup analyses to reflect the heterogeneous nature of bereavement experiences based on the gender of surviving spouses. Moreover, it investigates how pre-loss caregiving involvement may moderate the trajectories of loneliness following spousal loss. This methodological approach allows for a nuanced understanding of how gender and caregiving roles simultaneously shape the trajectories of loneliness among bereaved individuals in the Korean context. Data and Methods Data The present analysis draws on data from the KLoSA, a nationally representative study designed to examine various aspects of aging among Korean adults aged 45 and older. Established in 2006, KLoSA conducts biennial surveys to document socioeconomic, psychological, and health-related patterns, providing essential data for policy decisions affecting middle-aged and older Korean adults. The sampling framework utilizes enumeration districts from the Population and Housing Census of Korea Statistics, implementing a multistage stratification process based on housing type (apartment or ordinary housing) and geographical location (urban or rural). All participants provided informed consent, and the data were deidentified prior to public release, exempting this secondary analysis from additional ethical review requirements. The analytical sample spans a 16-year period from 2006 (Wave 1) to 2022 (Wave 9), with systematic sample refinement proceeding through several stages ( Supplementary Figure S1 ). From an initial sample of 6,988 individuals who participated with their partner at least once during the study period, the analysis excluded 19 individuals with missing information on time to spousal loss. Additionally, 57 individuals who died in the same year as their spouses were excluded because this made it impossible to determine the sequence of events. Among the remaining 6,912 individuals, an additional 34 cases were removed due to missing values on key analytical variables, including loneliness assessment, caregiving status, sociodemographic characteristics, and health-related variables. Considering the negligible size of missing data (1.5% of observations), which did not threaten the study’s statistical power, listwise deletion was employed for handling the missing data. The final analytical sample thus comprised 6,878 individuals, consisting of 1,329 individuals who experienced spousal loss and 5,549 who did not. Those who did not experience the death of a spouse were included in the analysis to enable robust estimation of time-varying control variables. Measures Dependent variable The dependent variable, loneliness, was measured using a single item. Some studies examined the effect of spousal loss on loneliness using the identical questionnaire from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression scale (CES-D) ( Fried et al., 2015 ; Robinaugh et al., 2014 ; Vedder et al., 2021 ). Likewise, the respondents were asked to answer the following question, “Did you feel lonely during the past week?” Responses were recorded on a 4-point scale: 1 (rarely or none of the time [less than 1 day]), 2 (some or a little of the time [1–2 days]), 3 (occasionally or a moderate amount of time [3–4 days]), and 4 (most or all of the time [5–7 days]). Based on the previous evidence of using dichotomized measure ( Fried et al., 2015 ; Kim &amp; Hwang, 2024b ; van Borkulo et al., 2014 ), this measure was dichotomized, with respondents reporting loneliness for one or more days per week (scores of 2–4) classified as experiencing loneliness, whereas those reporting less than one day (score of 1) were classified as not experiencing loneliness. Independent variable The primary independent variable measures the temporal distance from spousal loss, calculated as the difference between the interview date and the date of spouse’s death: the first year (months 0 to 11), second year (months 12 to 23), third year (months 24 to 35), and fourth and subsequent years (months ≥36) after the loss, as well as the last year (months −12 to −1), second year (months −13 to −24), third year (months −25 to −36), and fourth and subsequent years (months ≤ −37) before the loss of a spouse. For individuals who did not experience spousal loss during the observation period, this variable remained constant at zero in our fixed effects models. This lack of within-subject variation means these respondents do not contribute to estimating the coefficient for time to spousal loss. However, their inclusion is crucial for accurately identifying the effects of other control variables and avoiding potential sample selection bias. Control variable The analysis incorporated both time-constant and time-varying control variables to account for potential confounding factors. The time-constant variables included gender (male/female) and spousal caregiving status, with the latter assessed based on provision of assistance with activities of daily living (ADLs) or instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs) during the 3 months preceding spousal loss. Time-varying controls were organized into four categories: demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, living arrangements, and health status. Demographic variables included age (grand mean-centered with a squared term to account for nonlinear effects), marital status (married vs single/widowed/divorced/separated), and place of residence (large cities, small cities, and rural areas). Socioeconomic status was measured through log-transformed household income, homeownership status (owner-occupier vs nonowner), and economic activity (engagement in paid employment). Living arrangements were captured through household size and number of living children. Health status was assessed through three measures: the number of chronic diseases (sum of doctor-diagnosed conditions including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cancer/malignant tumors, chronic lung disease, cerebrovascular disease, arthritis/rheumatoid arthritis, psychological disorders, liver disease, and prostatic disease), ADLs measuring assistance needed with seven basic activities (dressing, face washing, bathing, eating, room mobility, toilet use, and continence management), and IADLs categorized by the number of activities requiring assistance (0, 1, 2, 3+) from among grooming, household chores, cooking, laundry, outdoor mobility, transportation use, shopping, financial management, telephone use, and medication management. Statistical Analysis The analysis employs fixed effects regression models to estimate temporal changes in loneliness before and after spousal loss. Comparing loneliness levels between bereaved and nonbereaved spouses may lead to biased results due to underlying compositional differences. This bias arises because bereaved and nonbereaved individuals may differ in both observed and unobserved characteristics, potentially influencing the observed differences in emotional loneliness. To address this limitation, the fixed effects approach focuses on within-person temporal changes while controlling for all time-invariant individual heterogeneity, including both observable and unobservable factors that could confound the relationship between spousal loss and loneliness. This method enhances the robustness of the analysis by isolating the effect of bereavement from other stable individual traits. The trajectory of loneliness is examined across multiple time points, spanning from 4 years before to 4 years after bereavement. This longitudinal approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of both pre- and post-loss effects on loneliness. The fixed effects model can be formally expressed as: y i t = α + β 1 ( − 3 r d y e a r ) i t + β 2 ( − 2 n d y e a r ) i t + β 3 ( − 1 s t y e a r ) i t + β 4 ( + 1 s t y e a r ) i t + β 5 ( + 2 n d y e a r ) i t + β 6 ( + 3 r d y e a r ) i t + β 7 ( + 4 t h a n d s u b s e q u e n t y e a r s ) + Z i t δ + v i + ε i t ( 1 ) y i t is the dependent variable, the level of loneliness of individual i at time t . The β coefficients represent the level of loneliness in comparison to the level observed 4 years prior to the bereavement. The vector Z i t comprises a set of time-varying control variables, whereas all time-invariant variables are automatically excluded, as they are absorbed by v i , representing individual fixed effects. Robust standard errors are denoted by ε i t . To examine potential heterogeneous effects in the trajectory of changes in loneliness surrounding spousal loss, we conduct a stratified analysis. This approach allows us to determine whether there are differences in the estimated coefficients of the spousal loss time variable across groups based on the gender of the respondents. Then, stratification analyses further estimated whether the trajectories of loneliness differ by gender and caregiving. Results Table 1 presents comprehensive baseline characteristics of the analytical sample from Wave 1, stratified by experience of spousal loss during the study period. The total sample ( N = 6,878) is presented in Column 1, whereas Columns 2 and 3 display characteristics for those who did not experience spousal loss ( n = 5,549) and those who did ( n = 1,329), respectively. In the total sample, 22.7% of respondents reported experiencing loneliness, with an approximately equal gender distribution (50.0% female). The mean age was 60.31 years, and 96.4% of participants reported no impairment in ADLs. Socioeconomic indicators revealed that 80.1% were homeowners, and 42.5% were engaged in paid employment. The average household comprised 3.06 members, with respondents having an average of 2.98 children. Among those who experienced spousal loss during the study period (Column 3), several distinctive characteristics emerged. This group reported higher levels of loneliness (30.4%) and showed a notably different gender composition, with women comprising 78.6% of bereaved individuals. The bereaved group was also older on average (65.19 years) and 4.4% of respondents reported ADL limitations. Regarding caregiving status, there was an almost even split among the bereaved group, with 49.6% having provided caregiving to their spouse before death. These characteristics greatly differ depending on whether individuals have lost a spouse (Column 2 vs Column 3). The temporal distribution of observations relative to spousal loss presents that the 1,329 individuals experienced bereavement during the study period, yielding 8,941 person-wave observations ( Supplementary Table S1 ). Based on previous studies This study delineates the distribution of observations across eight time periods (four pre-loss and four post-loss). The balanced distribution across middle periods provides robust support for analyzing the timing effect of spousal loss, which is central to the study’s primary research questions regarding trajectories of loneliness. Table 2 presents the results of fixed effects models examining the relationship between time since spousal loss and loneliness for the total, female, and male samples. The variable representing temporal distance from spousal loss indicates changes in loneliness relative to the reference period (4 or more years before loss), with all models controlling for age-related changes and time-varying confounders. The analysis of the total sample (Column 1) reveals a significant immediate increase in loneliness during the first year following spousal loss ( b = 0.164, CI = 0.097–0.230). This effect appears to be temporary, as loneliness levels return to the pre-loss baseline in subsequent years. The gender-stratified analyses ( Table 2 , Columns 2 and 3) reveal distinct patterns in the relationship between spousal loss and loneliness across genders. For female survivors (Column 2), results indicate a significant increase in loneliness during the first year following spousal loss ( b = 0.183, CI = 0.106–0.260), followed by a return to baseline levels in subsequent years. In contrast, male survivors (Column 3) exhibited no statistically significant changes in loneliness at any time point relative to the loss, despite coefficients of large magnitude during the first year post-loss. Table 3 presents analyses examining the intersection of gender and caregiving status in shaping loneliness trajectories following spousal loss. Among female survivors, caregiving status did not significantly moderate the relationship between spousal loss and loneliness. Both noncaregiving (Column 1, b = 0.162) and caregiving women (Column 2, b = 0.180) experienced similar immediate increases in loneliness during the first year post-loss, with effects dissipating in subsequent years. In contrast, male survivors demonstrated more complex patterns that varied by caregiving status. This sustained elevation in loneliness among male caregivers stands in marked contrast to both female patterns and to male noncaregivers, for whom the temporal pattern of loneliness was less distinct. Discussion This study utilized data from nine waves of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) to investigate the trajectories of loneliness among bereaved individuals in Korea, with a focus on gender and caregiving status. The analysis revealed several key findings. First, spousal loss was associated with an immediate increase in loneliness during the first year following the loss. Second, gender emerged as a significant moderator, with female survivors experiencing temporary increases in loneliness, whereas male survivors exhibited no statistically significant changes. Third, female survivors experienced temporary increases in loneliness regardless of caregiving status. Conversely, male caregivers faced more pronounced and prolonged elevations in loneliness, persisting through the second year after the loss. The study revealed a distinct temporal pattern in the relationship between spousal loss and loneliness. This pattern was characterized by a significant increase in loneliness during the first year following bereavement. Notably, pre-loss effects were observed in the periods preceding the loss, suggesting that the emotional reaction manifests primarily after the actual loss occurs. Consistent with previous research ( van Baarsen, 2002 ), the present study documented not only the immediate effect of spousal loss on loneliness but also the relatively rapid recovery trajectory, highlighting the resilience of bereaved individuals in navigating this significant life transition. Moreover, the transient nature of elevated loneliness may reflect the distinct characteristics of loneliness as a temporal emotional state, potentially leading to other prolonged psychological symptoms. In light of this, previous studies have suggested the role of loneliness as predictors of depression and anxiety ( Wolters et al., 2023 ). The present study highlighted that trajectories of loneliness following spousal loss differ by gender. Consistent with previous research ( Olawa et al., 2021 ), the findings suggest that spousal loss has a more pronounced negative effect on loneliness in women. Women often maintain deeper emotional connections within marriages and are more sensitive to relational disruptions ( Gyasi &amp; Phillips, 2020 ; Sasson &amp; Umberson, 2014 ). This heightened emotional interdependence, coupled with a tendency toward loss-oriented coping, may contribute to women’s intense initial loneliness after bereavement. However, this immediate emotional intensity should not be misconstrued as maladaptive. According to the dual process model, effective grief processing involves both engaging with loss and moving toward adaptation, with the timing and intensity of these processes naturally varying across individuals ( Stroebe &amp; Schut, 1999 ; Stroebe et al., 2001 ). This pattern of heightened loneliness may represent a critical phase of the grief process, particularly in processing the loss of significant emotional bonds. The temporary nature of these elevated loneliness levels may, in fact, reflect a healthy oscillation between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping over time, demonstrating women’s strong emotional engagement in the grieving process. The analysis revealed that both female caregivers and noncaregivers exhibited similar trajectories of temporary increases in loneliness following spousal loss, with no significant interaction effects of caregiving status among women. Considering several methodological limitations of this study, these findings may be attributed to the lack of detailed information on caregiving. The caregiving measure used in this study primarily captured short-term effects, potentially overlooking longer-term influences identified in previous research. For example, Van Hout et al. (2020) found that bereaved spouses who provided care for an average of 2 years experienced persistent loneliness both before and after their loss. Additionally, crucial variables such as caregiving intensity and the specific types of caregiving activities were not included in the analysis. The omission of these factors may have obscured distinct trajectories of emotional loneliness between female caregivers and noncaregivers. Given that previous research has demonstrated strong associations between intensive caregiving and adverse mental health outcomes ( Kolodziej et al., 2022 ), understanding the detailed context of caregiving experiences is essential. To provide a more accurate estimation of the heterogeneity in bereavement adjustment patterns among female partners, future studies should consider key caregiving characteristics, including duration, intensity, and the nature of caregiving tasks. On the other hand, the present study pointed out the different trajectories of loneliness among surviving male partners based on their prior caregiving status. Despite the lack of previous findings on the bereavement experience of male spousal caregivers, the present study contributes additional evidence highlighting the negative effect of caregiving on their loneliness. Male spousal caregivers experience an increase in loneliness only the first year following the death of a spouse, with this prolonged loneliness extending into the second year. This extended period of loneliness may stem from cultural expectations that traditionally emphasize men’s roles as primary breadwinners. Accordingly, male spouses typically seek support primarily from their children, especially daughters ( Hwang &amp; Hong, 2021 ). These sociocultural factors may exacerbate the vulnerability of male caregivers following the death of a spouse. As a result of their caregiving role, limited social networks may further amplify their emotional loneliness. However, there is a lack of research to explain the detailed mechanism among the male caregivers. A notable limitation of the present study is the limited information available on key variables. The use of a single-item measure for loneliness may have oversimplified the trajectories of loneliness, potentially obscuring complex patterns associated with spousal loss. Additionally, the study gave minimal attention to the qualitative aspects of the relationship with the deceased spouse, which could influence bereavement outcomes. Another limitation is the lack of data on whether the death was anticipated, as a significant portion of responses related to the causes of spousal death were missing. This gap in information prevented a deeper exploration of how the anticipation of death versus sudden loss might differently affect the surviving spouse’s well-being. Future studies should address these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of loss. Finally, the study focused exclusively on legally married heterosexual couples, excluding cohabiting couples. Limited information in the data set, along with potential stigma surrounding cohabitation, made it challenging to accurately identify cohabiting partners and analyze their experiences ( Lee, 2015 ). Persistent negative stereotypes about cohabitation suggest that further research is needed to better understand the bereavement experiences of cohabiting couples. The findings of this study have significant implications for policy development in Korea, particularly with regard to support services for bereaved adults. The identification of distinct temporal patterns in post-loss loneliness underscores the necessity for targeted interventions during the first year of bereavement, when individuals are most vulnerable to experiencing loneliness. For instance, community-based programs that provide immediate emotional support and social engagement opportunities could help mitigate the initial impact of spousal loss ( Walsh, 2007 ). Additionally, the gender differences observed in loneliness trajectories suggest the need for gender-specific support services that consider the distinct ways in which men and women experience and cope with loss in the Korean cultural context. Finally, the findings suggest that spousal caregiving responsibilities intersect with gender norms to shape bereavement experiences, highlighting the need for targeted policy interventions. Given that male caregivers may face challenges in maintaining social networks during caregiving, support programs should focus on strengthening their social connections both during and after caregiving.
