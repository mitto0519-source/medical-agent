ABSTRACT Aims This study aimed to explore the relationship between PA domains, PA intensity, sedentary time, cardiometabolic factors, inflammatory markers, and risk of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in adults. It also examined potential moderators of the relationship between PA and DKD including age, race and ethnicity. Materials and Methods This cross‐sectional study used data from the 2017–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and included participants aged ≥ 18 years. The survey subjectively measured PA using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire. After conducting multiple imputations to address missing data, we performed hierarchical logistic regression to identify factors significantly associated with DKD diagnosis and to examine moderating effects. Results DKD prevalence was 13.32%. Non‐Hispanic Black participants showed higher odds of DKD than non‐Hispanic White participants. Age, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, fasting glucose levels, haemoglobin A1C, high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein, sedentary time, and occupation‐related PA were significant predictors of DKD. Although age, race and ethnicity were significant predictors of DKD, we observed no moderating effects of these variables on the PA‐DKD risk relationship. Conclusions Cardiometabolic markers, inflammatory markers, and sedentary time significantly increase DKD risk, whereas occupation‐related PA serves as a protective factor. This study highlights the need for interventions promoting a more active lifestyle in diabetic populations to mitigate their DKD risk. Introduction Diabetes mellitus is showing a dramatic rise in incidence and prevalence worldwide that is largely driven by an increase in type 2 diabetes mellitus cases [ 1 ]. In 2021, diabetes afflicted about 537 million people globally, or 11% of the world population, and this value is expected to reach 783 million, or 12%, by 2045 [ 1 ]. During their lifetime, nearly half of individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus and one‐third of those with type 1 diabetes mellitus are at risk of developing diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [ 1 ]. Among the long‐term complications associated with diabetes mellitus, DKD is one of the most common and severe [ 2 ]. DKD accounts for approximately 50% of such cases worldwide, severely impacts patients' quality of life, and is also linked with high rates of cardiovascular disease and mortality [ 1 , 3 ]. Chronic kidney disease affects around 700 million people, or 9% of the global population, with nearly four million requiring kidney replacement therapy [ 4 ]. Although chronic kidney disease is more prevalent in women, men with chronic kidney disease are more likely to progress to end‐stage kidney disease, thus requiring kidney replacement therapy [ 5 ]. These statistics highlight the urgent need to deepen our understanding of DKD in order to alleviate the significant global health burden imposed by this disease [ 2 ]. Analysis of Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) data from 1999 through 2020 showed 528,430 deaths among American adults with DKD aged 25 and older, with mortality rates rising over time [ 3 ]. Males with DKD had consistently higher age‐adjusted mortality rates than females [ 3 ]. Among ethnic groups, non‐Hispanic American Indian and Alaska Native individuals showed the highest mortality rates, followed by non‐Hispanic Blacks, Hispanics, non‐Hispanic Asians. The CDC study highlighted significant disparities in DKD mortality rates across demographic groups, with Non‐Hispanic American Indians and Alaska Natives, males, and elderly individuals particularly affected [ 3 ]. The pathophysiology of DKD involves complex interactions between metabolic, haemodynamic, and inflammatory pathways, all of which can be influenced by lifestyle factors. Among these factors, physical activity (PA) has emerged as a potentially modifiable factor that could play a critical role in mitigating the risk of DKD [ 6 ]. Regular PA is known to improve glycaemic control, reduce blood pressure, and improve lipid profiles, all of which are beneficial in preventing DKD onset and progression [ 2 , 7 ]. Furthermore, PA has been shown to positively affect inflammatory markers and insulin sensitivity, thus providing additional protection against DKD [ 6 , 8 ]. However, the direct association between PA—particularly the different domains of PA (occupational, transportation, leisure time)—and DKD remains understudied. Furthermore, the interplay between PA and key metabolic factors, such as blood pressure, lipid profile, and inflammatory biomarkers, has not been adequately addressed [ 9 ]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the associations between PA, including its various domains, and DKD while controlling for key metabolic factors. Specifically, our first aim was to examine the relationship between total PA and DKD. Second, we aimed to explore the impact of the different PA domains on DKD. Finally, our third aim was to evaluate whether age or race‐ethnicity moderates the relationship between total PA and DKD. 2. Methods 2.1. Study Design and Subject Eligibility This study is a cross‐sectional analysis of publicly accessible data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) for the 2017–2018 cycle. This nationwide survey programme implemented by the CDC aims to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The survey employs a multi‐stage probability sampling design to represent the United States population, utilising individual data, strata, clusters, and year weights [ 10 ]. All survey participants provided informed consent. Standardised in‐home interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory assessments were performed at mobile examination centres to evaluate the participants' nutritional status and physical health. The survey protocol was approved by the National Centre for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, thereby eliminating the need for additional ethical review of the present study. The NHANES 2017–18 cycle included 9254 participants, with 4697 males and 4557 females. As the present study focused on adults, 3398 participants under the age of 18 were excluded. No additional exclusion criteria were applied. 2.2. Measures 2.2.1. Outcome Variable Participants were classified as having DKD if they met any of the criteria for a diabetes diagnosis [ 9 ]. These criteria included (1) prior diabetes diagnosis, (2) taking diabetes medication to control plasma glucose (in the form of insulin or pills to lower blood glucose), (3) exhibiting fasting plasma glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or (4) exhibiting glycosylated haemoglobin or haemoglobin A1C levels ≥ 6.5% [ 9 ]. Additionally, kidney function had to meet one of the following criteria: (1) an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) &lt; 60 mL/min/1.73 m 2 or (2) an albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/g [ 11 ]. Calculations for eGFR adhered to National Kidney Foundation Laboratory Participation Workgroup guidelines for implementing race‐free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 equation [ 12 ]. The DKD outcome for each participant was operationalised as a binary variable (no/yes). 2.2.2. Physical Activity The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire was used to collect PA data from participants, as it had been validated and shown to be reliable [ 13 ]. The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire provides respondent‐level interview data on PA through 15 questions that classify PA into three domains: occupation physical activity (OPA), transportation physical activity (TPA), and leisure‐time physical activity (LTPA). These questions capture the activities' frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per session). PA intensities, namely vigorous and moderate activities, must be differentiated to calculate OPA and LTPA. Vigorous PA typically involves activities that produce significant increases in heart rate and/or large increases in breathing and entail high physical loads (e.g., lifting or carrying heavy objects, excavation, construction work, running, or football) [ 9 ]. Moderate PA is characterised by activities with lighter physical loads that mildly affect heart rate variability and/or cause small increases in breathing (e.g., working with lighter objects, brisk walking, cycling, swimming, or volleyball) [ 9 ]. Meanwhile, TPA involves activities involving walking or bicycling for transportation. Applying metabolic equivalents (METs) values to activity levels allowed us to calculate the total METs of OPA, TPA, and LTPA during a typical week. The time spent on each PA domain was calculated in minutes per week (min/week) and energy expenditure was calculated as MET‐min/week. Energy expenditure for moderate and vigorous PA was calculated by multiplying weekly PA time (min/week) by 4 METs and 8 METs, respectively. A person's caloric consumption is four times as high when they are moderately active and eight times as high when they are vigorously active [ 14 ]. Total energy expenditure, expressed in MET‐min/week, was calculated by summing the energy expenditure from both moderate and vigorous PAs. Total energy expenditure was then categorised using recommended cut‐off points. Following the World Health Organisation recommendations for PA, we set the first cut‐point at 600 METs‐min/week [ 14 ]. The second cut‐off point was set at 1100 METs‐min/week based on the optimal dose of PA identified by the American Diabetes Association to improve glycaemic control [ 15 , 16 ]. To estimate the population's mean PA duration, we calculated the weekly duration of OPA and LTPA (in hr) as follows: [(number of days with vigorous PA sessions per week × duration of each vigorous session (hr) × 2) + (number of days of moderate‐intensity PA sessions per week × duration of each moderate session (hr))]/60 [ 9 , 14 ]. Also, we calculated the duration of TPA (in hr) as follows: [number of days with continuous walks or bicycle rides of at least 10 min to get to and from places × duration of each session (hr)]/60 [ 9 , 14 ]. 2.2.3. Sedentary Time This sedentary time was measured using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, excluding sleep duration. It covers activities such as sitting or reclining at work, at home, during travel, or socialising with friends. Specific examples include sitting at a desk, travelling by car or bus, reading, playing cards, or watching television. It does not include time spent sleeping. Participants provided their responses in hours and minutes. 2.2.4. Lifestyle Behaviours Based on responses to a self‐report instrument, healthy diet status was categorised into five levels (excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor). Additionally, smoking history was assessed based on whether an individual had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime (no/yes), and alcohol consumption was assessed by asking the individual if there ever was a time or times in their life when they drank 4/5 or more drinks of any kind of alcoholic beverage almost every day (no/yes). 2.2.5. Cardiometabolic Markers Cardiometabolic markers consist of a cluster of abnormalities with insulin resistance and obesity as predisposing features [ 17 ]. The following parameters were used to classify cardiometabolic markers: (1) body mass index (BMI) in kg/m 2 , (2) hip circumference in cm, and (3) waist circumference in cm. These parameters were presented as continuous variables and reported as mean and standard error. (4) systolic blood pressure was dichotomised into &lt; 130 or ≥ 130 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure into &lt; 80 or ≥ 80 mm Hg; (5) total cholesterol levels were categorised as desirable (&lt; 5.2 mmol/L), borderline (≥ 5.2–6.2 mmol/L), and high (&gt; 6.2 mmol/L); and (6) fasting plasma glucose levels were categorised as low (&lt; 7 mmol/L) and high (≥ 7 mmol/L) [ 18 , 19 ]. (7) Haemoglobin A1C levels were categorised as normal (&lt; 5.7%), prediabetes (≥ 5.7% to &lt; 6.5%), diabetes (≥ 6.5%) [ 20 ]. 2.2.6. Inflammation Markers High sensitivity C‐Reactive Protein (hs‐CRP) is a marker of inflammation that can promote atherosclerosis and predict cardiovascular events [ 21 ]. According to the hs‐CRP cut‐off points recommended by the CDC and the American Heart Association, concentrations were categorised as low (&lt; 1 mg/L), moderate (1–3 mg/L), and high (&gt; 3 mg/L). Higher baseline hs‐CRP levels have shown significant associations with cardiovascular risk and heart disease [ 12 , 21 ]. 2.2.7. Covariates This study included covariates that could impact the relationship between PA and DKD. Demographic parameters included age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, marital status, and the poverty income ratio. Age was categorised into six groups (18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, and 70–80 years), and individuals 80 and over are topcoded at 80 years of age [ 22 , 23 ]. Sex was classified as male or female. Race and ethnicity categories included Mexican American, Other Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, non‐Hispanic Asian, and ‘other’ ethnicities. Education was categorised into below high school, high school, some college, and college graduate/above. Marital status categories were never married, married/living with a partner, and widowed/divorced/separated. The poverty income ratio was categorised into four levels: poor (&lt; 1), low (1–1.99), middle (2–3.99), and high (≥ 4). NHANES uses a ratio of family income to the Department of Health and Human Services poverty guidelines. A poverty income ratio less than one indicates an income less than the poverty level, and a ratio greater than one indicates an income higher than the poverty level. 2.3. Statistical Analysis Data collection for NHANES involved various complex sampling techniques. For data analysis, masked variance pseudo‐stratum and masked variance pseudo‐primary sampling unit cluster variables were employed in all procedures. Multiple imputation with chained equations was used to handle missing data, assuming that the data were missing at random [ 24 ]. Descriptive statistics included weighted frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard errors for continuous variables. The chi‐square test for categorical variables and the weighted Student's t ‐test for continuous variables were utilised to compare differences in baseline characteristics between the non‐DKD and DKD groups. Hierarchical logistic regression analysis was used to examine the association between blocks of independent variables and DKD diagnosis. Block 1 included fundamental demographic data, namely age, race and ethnicity, sex, education level, marital status, and the ratio of family income to poverty; these variables served as baseline factors potentially associated with DKD. Block 2 included lifestyle behaviour factors such as diet quality, smoking history, and alcohol consumption; these variables were selected to capture behavioural characteristics that might influence DKD. Block 3 consisted of eight cardiometabolic markers, namely BMI, hip circumference, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, fasting glucose, and haemoglobin A1C. Block 4 included the inflammatory marker hs‐CRP. Block 5 included sedentary time. Block 6 included total MET levels of PA intensity. Finally, interaction terms (age × total METs and race‐ethnicity × total METS) were added in Block 7. We performed a second hierarchical regression analysis to examine the effect of the three PA domains on DKD diagnosis. The first five blocks are the same as the first hierarchical model. Occupation‐related PA, leisure‐time PA, and transportation‐related PA were introduced into the model in Blocks 6–8, respectively. The significance of each model was then assessed using the Wald test. This stepwise inclusion of variables allowed for the evaluation of each block's unique contribution to the prediction of DKD. The hierarchical approach was used to identify the most relevant variables in order to provide a nuanced understanding of the complex relationships between the sets of various factors and DKD prevalence. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp, Texas, USA). 3. Results 3.1. Description of Sample Characteristics of the 5856 participants included in the analysis are presented in Table 1 . Among these participants, 13.32% showed DKD status. The average age of the total sample was 49.89 ± 18.78 years, and about 51.50% of the sample were women. In the total sample, 34.70% self‐identified as non‐Hispanic White, 22.93% as non‐Hispanic black, and 14.50% as non‐Hispanic Asian. Notable differences were observed between the non‐DKD and DKD groups across various metabolic and PA markers. In terms of BMI, the average BMI for the overall group was 29.66 kg/m 2 . For patients with DKD, the average BMI was 31.54 kg/m 2 , while patients without DKD had an average BMI of 29.38 kg/m 2 . The mean for hip circumference was 106.88 cm overall, with patients with DKD averaging 110.26 cm (males: 107.82 cm, females: 112.95 cm) and patients without DKD averaging 106.37 cm (males: 104.10 cm, females: 108.45 cm). For waist circumference, the overall mean was 100.32 cm, with patients with DKD averaging 107.79 cm (males: 109.40 cm, females: 106.00 cm) and patients without DKD averaging 99.19 cm (males: 101.07 cm, females: 97.47 cm). Systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mm Hg was observed in 61.32% of the DKD group versus 30.63% in the non‐DKD group, while diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mm Hg was present in 28.45% of the DKD group compared to 24.54% in the non‐DKD group. Fasting plasma glucose levels ≥ 7 mmol/L were found in 41.86% of the DKD group compared with 10.78% of the non‐DKD group. For Haemoglobin A1C levels, 42.26% of DKD patients had levels ≥ 6.5%, compared to 9.58% in the non‐DKD group. Elevated hs‐CRP &gt; 3 mg/L was seen in 46.70% of the DKD group versus 34.55% of the non‐DKD group. For sedentary time, the overall mean was 331.53 min per day, with patients with DKD averaging 358.64 min per day and patients without DKD averaging 327.43 min per day. Regarding PA, 58.51% of the DKD group had total METs &lt; 600 METs min/week compared to 40.67% in the non‐DKD group. Additionally, the DKD group reported lower engagement than the non‐DKD group in OPA (6.95 h/week vs. 13.97 h/week), TPA (0.91 h/week vs. 1.27 h/week), and LTPA (1.40 h/week vs. 2.68 h/week). Between the two groups, significant differences ( p &lt; 0.05) were observed in age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, marital status, ratio of family income to poverty, lifetime smoking of at least 100 cigarettes, BMI, hip circumference, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose, haemoglobin A1C, hs‐CRP, sedentary time, and total METs of PA and PA domains. 3.2. Associations of Physical Activity Intensity With DKD Table 2 presents the results of the first hierarchical regression modelling. The selection of Model 5 (which included the first 5 blocks of variables) was based on the results of the Wald test (Pr &gt; F &lt; 0.05). Age, race and ethnicity, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose, haemoglobin A1C, hs‐CRP, and sedentary time were found to be significant risk factors for DKD. The results showed that DKD diagnosis increased with age. When compared to those aged 18–29 years, those aged 50–59 years had 2.51 times greater odds ( p &lt; 0.01) of being diagnosed with DKD, those aged 60–69 years had 4.32 times greater odds ( p &lt; 0.001), and those aged 70–80 years had 9.60 times greater odds ( p &lt; 0.001). As for race and ethnicity, the odds of DKD diagnosis were higher for non‐Hispanic Blacks, who showed 1.31 times greater odds ( p &lt; 0.05) than non‐Hispanic Whites. For each unit increase in waist circumference, the risk of developing DKD increases by 2% ( p &lt; 0.01). Having an SBP ≥ 130 mmHg was associated with higher odds of having DKD (odds ratio [OR] 1.65, p &lt; 0.001). Similarly, elevated fasting plasma glucose (≥ 7 mmol/L) was associated with higher odds of having DKD (OR 1.64, p &lt; 0.001). When compared to participants with haemoglobin A1C levels &lt; 5.7%, those with haemoglobin A1C levels ≥ 5.7% to &lt; 6.5% had 7.35 times greater odds of having DKD ( p &lt; 0.001), those with haemoglobin A1C levels ≥ 6.5% had 10.65 times greater odds of having DKD ( p &lt; 0.001). Meanwhile, individuals with total cholesterol levels between 5.2 and 6.2 mmol/L showed 27% lower odds of having DKD compared to those with levels below 5.2 mmol/L ( p &lt; 0.01). For the inflammatory marker, participants with hs‐CRP levels greater than 3 mg/L had 1.47 times greater odds ( p &lt; 0.01) of being diagnosed with DKD than those with levels less than 1 mg/L. For each additional minute of sedentary time per day, the odds of being diagnosed with DKD increased by 0.05% ( p &lt; 0.05), equating to a 3% increase for every hour of daily sedentary behaviour. Physical activity intensity was not significantly associated with DKD after controlling for sedentary behaviour. 3.3. Associations of PA Domains With DKD Table 3 presents the results of the hierarchical logistic regression analysis examining the associations between the three different PA domains and DKD. Model 6, which included most variables except for LTPA and TPA, had the best fit (Pr &gt; F = 0.02). For every 1‐h per week increase in OPA, the odds of having DKD decreased by 1% ( p = 0.02) even after controlling for sedentary time. 4. Discussion The findings from this study support the protective role of PA, specifically occupation of PA, against developing DKD. The role of OPA emerged as a protective factor against DKD risk in this study, aligning with previous research [ 25 , 26 ]. This is likely because OPA represents a consistent activity pattern in daily life, incorporating elements such as volume, intensity, frequency, posture, and the nature of tasks (e.g., lifting, carrying, walking, or handling items while standing) [ 25 , 26 ]. This study also suggests that while engaging in PA beyond the recommended PA guidelines is generally beneficial for health [ 15 , 16 ], its protective effect against DKD diminishes when sedentary time is taken into account. In other words, simply meeting or exceeding PA guidelines does not necessarily reduce DKD risk if individuals also spend prolonged periods being sedentary. This highlights the independent and potentially dominant role of sedentary behaviour in influencing DKD risk, reinforcing the need to focus not only on increasing PA but also on reducing sedentary time to achieve meaningful health benefits [ 27 ]. Additionally, this study provides a detailed analysis of the risk factors associated with DKD. Utilising a large, diverse sample, we confirmed that advancing age significantly increases DKD risk, aligning with the established trend of age‐related kidney function decline. Moreover, we uncovered notable racial and ethnic differences in DKD risk, with non‐Hispanic Black individuals having higher odds of DKD diagnosis than non‐Hispanic Whites. The study also sheds light on the complex interplay of cardiometabolic and lifestyle factors, including waist circumference, elevated systolic blood pressure, hyperglycemia, and sedentary time in DKD development. Overall, we believe that our research contributes valuable insights into DKD risk factors that can inform future preventive and therapeutic strategies. 4.1. Sedentary Time, PA Total METS, and PA Domains Engaging in total physical activity exceeding 1100 MET‐min/week, which equates to 275 min/week of moderate PA or 137.5 min/week of vigorous PA, was not significantly associated with a reduced risk of DKD in this study. Furthermore, among the three PA domains, only OPA was significantly associated with DKD risk. In contrast, He et al. [ 9 ] reported no significant association between DKD prevalence and PA duration across all domains. Our study found that increased sedentary time was positively associated with an elevated risk of DKD. Similar to the 2019 results of Bukht and colleagues [ 28 ], who reported a correlation between DKD and inactivity and low levels of PA. The discrepancies among recent findings may be attributable to differences in researchers' methods of handling missing data. Additional research is needed to further examine the relationship between PA duration and intensity and DKD risk. Engaging in appropriate patterns and durations of PA is widely believed to serve as a significant protective factor against DKD. The protective effects of PA are well documented, especially in the context of chronic diseases [ 6 , 29 ]. PA promotes better metabolic control in individuals with diabetes [ 16 ]. In previous studies [ 16 ] integrating regular exercise at 1100 MET‐min/week with various durations of different PA types, muscle contraction stimulated improvements in insulin sensitivity. These improvements were associated with increases in AMP‐activated protein kinase activity, which deactivated TCB1D1 protein, promoted Insulin‐responsive glucose transporter (GLUT4) translocation to the cell membrane, and thereby increased glucose uptake [ 30 , 31 ]. These findings suggested that individuals with diabetes who engage in sufficient PA can manage their blood glucose levels more effectively, reducing the stress on the kidneys that results from high blood sugar levels [ 15 , 16 ]. Additionally, a recent study showed that PA affected lipid metabolism and had a positive effect on insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscles [ 7 ]. The researchers concluded that PA improves the body's ability to utilise insulin more efficiently, which can reduce inflammation, thereby enhancing insulin sensitivity, decreasing the levels of biologically active lipids as well as blood pressure, and reducing the risk of cardiovascular events [ 7 ]. 4.2. Risk of Developing DKD Our study demonstrated a strong association between age and risk of DKD, as odds of DKD diagnosis significantly increased in older age groups. These findings are consistent with previous research identifying age as a critical factor in DKD development, as evidenced by a slow, progressive eGFR decline in the general population after approximately age 40 [ 32 ]. As individuals age, they are more likely to experience a decline in kidney function due to a combination of factors such as prolonged hyperglycemia, hypertension, and the cumulative effects of oxidative stress and inflammation on the renal system [ 32 , 33 ]. In addition to age, our results revealed racial and ethnic differences in DKD diagnosis. Specifically, non‐Hispanic Black participants had greater odds of being diagnosed with DKD than non‐Hispanic White participants. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that non‐Hispanic Black individuals face higher DKD risk due to factors such as genetic predisposition, health behaviours in terms of dietary patterns, and social disparities that limit access to healthcare [ 34 ]. Our study also revealed significant associations between various cardiometabolic and inflammatory factors and the risk of developing DKD, shedding light on its underlying pathophysiological processes [ 30 , 35 ]. Waist circumference is a key measure of obesity, particularly reflecting visceral fat, which is linked to increased risk of DKD through mechanisms like insulin resistance and inflammation [ 36 ]. While waist circumference is associated with a higher risk of DKD, its predictive value weakens over time and is less effective at forecasting long‐term DKD outcomes compared with other obesity indices [ 36 ]. Chronic hyperglycemia, defined as a fasting glucose level of ≥ 7 mmol/L, is linked to the diagnostic criteria for diabetes, with an HbA1c of 6.5% or higher being a standard marker [ 2 , 30 ]. This condition is also associated with an increased risk of developing DKD [ 30 ]. As hyperglycemia contributes to oxidative stress and the formation of glycation end‐products that damage kidney tissues and promote inflammation and fibrosis [ 2 , 35 ]. Unexpectedly, individuals with total cholesterol levels between 5.2 and 6.2 mmol/L exhibited lower odds of DKD than those with lower total cholesterol levels. This finding may be related to the complex role of lipid metabolism, but the link between cholesterol and renal function requires further investigation [ 30 , 35 ]. Elevated systolic blood pressure (≥ 130 mmHg) was linked to increased DKD risk; this can be attributed to increased glomerular pressure and damage to renal microvasculature caused by hypertension, leading to glomerulosclerosis and progressive kidney function decline [ 35 ]. Lastly, we found that hs‐CRP levels &gt; 3 mg/L were significantly associated with the presence of DKD. As a critical component of the pathophysiological processes underlying DKD, inflammation contributes to endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and eventually kidney damage. Elevated hs‐CRP levels indicate a heightened inflammatory state that may exacerbate the progression of kidney disease by promoting fibrosis and scarring of kidney tissues [ 30 , 35 ]. The chronic inflammatory state reflected by high hs‐CRP levels could thus serve as both a marker and a mediator of DKD progression. 4.3. Practical Implications This study provides important insights into potential means of managing and preventing DKD in patients with and without the condition. For patients with DKD, the findings highlight the importance of managing excessive waist circumference, high blood pressure, elevated fasting glucose levels, high total cholesterol levels, and extended sedentary time. Additionally, elevated hs‐CRP levels (&gt; 3 mg/L) in participants with DKD suggest that inflammation management could play a role in slowing disease progression. For individuals without DKD, the study results emphasise the importance of preventive measures such as maintaining a healthy weight, controlling blood pressure, and keeping glucose levels in check. In addition, routine assessment of waist circumference, blood pressure, blood glucose, inflammatory markers (such as hs‐CRP), and sedentary behaviour is crucial for early DKD detection and intervention. In particular, we observed that non‐Hispanic Black individuals are at higher risk for DKD, indicating the need for targeted monitoring and intervention in this population. Furthermore, because age is a significant risk factor for DKD, especially in those over 40, focused preventive efforts are necessary for middle‐aged and older adults. Overall, to achieve better health outcomes for patients with and without DKD, our findings illustrate the importance of addressing modifiable risk factors such as PA, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, hs‐CRP, and sedentary time to reduce the risk and impact of the disease. 4.4. Strengths The primary strengths of our study include our use of a nationally representative and multi‐ethnic sample, making our findings applicable across diverse population groups. A second strength is the ability to determine organ functional level and undiagnosed disease with laboratory values, such as eGFR and DKD. Our eligibility criteria were methodically designed to control for factors influencing DKD risk, thus providing accurate and reliable results. Another significant advantage of our study is the use of hierarchical logistic regression for data analysis. This robust statistical method offers several benefits such as allowing for the examination of complex relationships between multiple variables and accounting for potential interactions and dependencies within the data. Our application of hierarchical analysis was particularly advantageous in terms of controlling for confounding variables, reducing bias, and improving the precision of our estimates. It also facilitated the stepwise inclusion of variables, enabling us to assess the incremental value of each predictor and understand its hierarchical significance in influencing DKD. On the whole, this study not only advances the scientific understanding of DKD risk factors but also has practical implications for the inclusion of PA in targeted interventions and personalised healthcare approaches. 4.5. Limitations and Recommendations for Future Research While our study provides valuable information on the associations between PA and DKD, it also has limitations that should be addressed in future research. One key limitation is our reliance on self‐reported PA data, which may be susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias. Another limitation is the potential for confounding variables that were not fully accounted for in our analysis. For example, co‐morbidities, including mental health conditions such as depression, could significantly impact PA levels and dietary habits, thereby influencing DKD risk. Finally, our study's cross‐sectional design limits our ability to infer causality. Considering these limitations, future researchers should employ objective measures of PA, such as accelerometers or pedometers, to obtain more reliable data. Additionally, controlling for a larger group of potential confounders would provide a clearer picture of the relationship between lifestyle factors and DKD. For instance, accounting for the roles of mental health problems and other comorbid conditions in the context of DKD could yield important insights for the development of comprehensive disease management strategies. Moreover, the use of longitudinal research designs would provide a better understanding of how PA patterns and DKD risk evolve over time, the causal links between PA and DKD development, and the effectiveness of targeted interventions designed to prevent and mitigate DKD. 4.6. Conclusion In conclusion, this study identified a significant association between sedentary time and the risk of DKD. Conversely, OPA showed a protective effect against DKD. Several cardiometabolic markers were found to be associated with the risk of DKD. These findings underscore the importance of targeted public health interventions that promote the management of waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, and blood glucose as preventive measures against DKD. Promoting an active lifestyle that incorporates sufficient levels of PA has emerged as a key strategy for mitigating the risk of DKD. Future research should employ objective PA measures, account for additional potential confounders such as co‐morbidities, and utilise longitudinal study designs to further validate the associations identified.
