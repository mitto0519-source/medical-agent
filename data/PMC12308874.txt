ABSTRACT The objectives of the scoping review were to: (1) conduct a systematic search for published literature focused on American Indian/Alaskan Native/Indigenous (AI/AN/I) youth, (2) identify current interventions that are focused on reducing sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB) or increased water intake (3) draw an understanding of who is leading these programmes, and (4) identify the lenses being used in developing and implementing the interventions. High consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) among youth is associated with numerous health problems, such as obesity, tooth decay, type 2 diabetes, and heart disease. Unless addressed early, many of these problems extend into, and/or present with additional complications in, adulthood. A comprehensive search was conducted across 5 electronic databases for peer-reviewed articles published until 20 March 2024. Additionally, manual searches were performed in 10 AI/AN/I-focused health journals. Data extraction was performed by 4 reviewers. Data management and analysis were performed using DistillerSR Inc. software, with screening and extraction conducted at all stages. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus among reviewers. The protocol for this scoping review was registered in the Open Science Framework ( https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/MFZ8X ). AI/AN/I youth, prenatal to 17 years of age, caregivers, and educators. The search yielded 13 studies that met the eligibility criteria. Interventions were predominantly implemented through school, community, and school-community approaches. Individuals with a range of skills and training levels delivered the interventions. Out of the 13 studies, only 2 studies in this scoping review comprise all 4 aspects of the cultural/spiritual, emotional, mental, and physical domains and characteristics of engaging Indigenous communities when conducting research. Interventions with AI/AN/I communities with strong community and Native Nation support have a greater chance of success regardless of community, home, or school settings. Importantly, AI/AN/I communities have distinct definitions of health, underscoring the importance of identifying these meanings and implementing them as appropriate within research designs. KEYWORDS child infant adolescent community-based school-based American Indian or Alaska Native Indigenous Canadians Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander sugar-sweetened beverages drinking water Selection of source evidence Eligibility was initially verified by 4 independent reviewers (R.G-H, A.M.M., R.A., and C.W.) through title and abstract screening using DistillerSR Inc. software. At each level of review, citations needed consensus by at least two reviewers to be included or excluded. Citations meeting the eligibility criteria were procured for full-text article review. All full-text articles were obtained from the University of Arizona Health Sciences Library. The team again used DistillerSR Inc. software to facilitate full-text article review and confirm the fit of this scoping review. Each article was reviewed for eligibility and was prepared for data extraction. Data charting process Thirteen articles were identified as eligible for inclusion in the scoping review and were included in data extraction. At this level, the team used an adapted version of the Cochrane Public Health Group Data Extraction and Assessment Template [ 55 ]. All 4 reviewers independently extracted data into the DistillerSR Inc. software. Duplicate records were then removed with conversations throughout the process. After data extraction was complete, any extraction conflicts were discussed during regular team meetings. Data items Data collected from articles included general characteristics (e.g. date of publication, geography, funder), contextual factors (e.g. place and type of intervention activity, place of intervention, use of theoretical frameworks to inform intervention), outcome measures (e.g. type of outcome, time points measured/reported), results (e.g. increase/decrease beverage consumption), and use of an Indigenous lens (outcomes in all four areas, cultural/spiritual, emotional, mental, and physical) and level of community engagement (outreach, consulting, involving, collaborating and shared leadership) [ 9 , 44 ]. Synthesis of results The researchers grouped the studies by intervention setting (e.g. community, home, school) and summarised intervention characteristics (e.g. focus such as water or SSB-related or both, providers). The team also summarised all articles on the characteristics of SSB (e.g. definitions and types) and whether the intervention included any Indigenous definitions (e.g. health). Results The purpose of this scoping review was to identify and summarise evidence-based interventions that increase water consumption or decrease SSB intake (or both) in AI/AN/I youth; 13 studies met the inclusion criteria ( Table 3 ). Figure 2 indicates that, of those 13 studies, only 2 had a statistically significant ( p &lt; 0.05) increase in water intake [ 56 , 57 ] and 6 demonstrated a statistically significant ( p &lt; 0.05) decrease in SSB consumption [ 56 , 63 , 58–60 , 64 ]. Two articles did not report statistical significance in changes to water or SSB consumption [ 61 , 65 ] due to differences in their approaches to measuring intake over time. One study used qualitative methods to explore youth perspectives regarding water [ 66 ], providing an important cultural context for better understanding environmental contamination and its impact on water consumption. The research was based on a variety of locations, spanning from participants’ homes, schools, and communities. Seven of the studies were based in the local community in combination with either the participants’ home ( n = 2) [ 56 , 66 ] or school ( n = 5) [ 60 , 61 , 64 , 65 , 67 ] The remainder of the studies were described as home-based ( n = 3) [ 58 , 59 , 62 ], or based on their local community ( n = 3) [ 57 , 63 , 68 ] ( Figure 3 ). Those centred around the community typically used multiple modes to reach participants, ranging from media campaigns, outreach and education in clinics or health care centres, point-of-sale in retail outlets, informational mailers, and press releases in addition to direct contact within the home and school. The interventions were delivered by individuals with a range of skills and training levels. Many studies utilised multiple types of interventionists. Trained programme staff were the most frequently used interventionists ( n = 9) [ 63 , 66 , 60–62 , 64 , 65 , 67 , 68 ] followed by teachers who were engaged in delivering school-based interventions ( n = 6) [ 60 , 61 , 64 , 65 , 67 , 68 ] and community health workers and paraprofessionals, who delivered interventions that were based in the home ( n = 3) [ 56 , 58 , 59 ] ( Figure 4 ). Interventions that took place in healthcare settings worked with clinical staff [ 57 , 67 , 68 ], public health nurses, and other healthcare providers [ 65 , 68 ] to assist with assessment and education ( n = 2). Finally, two studies ( n = 2) [ 63 , 68 ] engaged cultural leaders, community members, and knowledge-keepers. Interventions that included nutrition education ( n = 9) reported statistically significant results ( n = 5), notably an increase in water intake and/or a decrease in SSB consumption ( Figure 5 ). Four of those 5 studies reported significant results for decreasing SSB intake [ 58–60 , 64 ] and 1 study [ 57 ] reported significant results for increased water consumption. Of the 5 statistically significant studies utilising nutrition education, 3 were based in community settings [ 57 , 60 , 64 ] and 2 were in the home [ 58 , 59 ]. In addition to nutrition education, other statistically significant interventions utilised increased time spent doing more physical activity [ 60 , 63 ], setting goals for exercise and nutrition [ 56 , 59 ], and following a culturally based curriculum [ 58 , 59 , 63 , 64 ]. There is an overlap across these approaches, as most studies use multiple strategies [ 63 , 66 , 57–61 , 64 , 65 , 67 , 68 ] to achieve their desired outcomes, with only one study [ 56 ] utilising a single strategy. Because there is not a specific independent variable being tested, and all of the studies’ except for one, primary outcomes were not water or SSB in particular. We are reporting which studies reported a significant change in increasing water consumption or decreasing SSB consumption (or both). Although less frequently employed, the strategy of making changes to the environment ( Figure 5 ) by removing SSBs from school vending machines, making water more accessible at points-of-sale in stores, and replacing SSBs from schools and household beverages with water or zero-calorie drinks was noted to be effective [ 57 , 60 ], however, this strategy did not always produce statistically significant differences in the research outcomes of interest [ 61 , 66 ]. Finally, promoting breastfeeding in combination with cultural and nutritional education for pregnant people, parents and caregivers of infants is noted to be an effective approach for increasing water intake and decreasing SSB consumption among parents and caregivers [ 58 ]. In conducting this scoping review, the team was particularly interested in evaluating the integration of AI/AN/I perspectives in research. Only 3 of the studies [ 63 , 66 , 68 ] provided an AI/AN/I-based definition or description of health. In reference to Indigenous health outcomes utilised by each study, of the 13 studies included in the review most ( n = 7) utilised only physical outcomes (e.g. weight status or accelerometry data) [ 58–60 , 64 , 61 , 67 , 68 ]. Four utilised both mental outcomes (e.g. health behaviour(s), nutrition or diabetes knowledge, and/or TV viewing time) and physical outcomes [ 56 , 57 , 62 , 65 ]. Two exceptions stand out: Lewis et al [ 63 ] and Jennings et al [ 66 ] articles. Both articles included both an AI/AN/I definition of health behaviour and utilised outcomes in all four areas of AI/AN/I understandings of health. Community engagement was defined in 5 levels guided by the Task Force on Community engagement [ 49 ] as well as Harding and Oetzel's [ 44 ] findings on implementation effectiveness for interventions for non-communicable diseases in Indigenous communities. The levels included outreach, consult, involve, collaborate, and shared leadership. Reviewers decided the levels based only on what was reported in the text of the article reporting the results of the intervention (i.e. if there was a separate article on study design, it was not included). If multiple communities were included in the same study, then the reported engagement process was assumed to happen with all communities included in each study unless otherwise stated. Five studies included some level of consultation (consult) [ 58 , 59 , 65 , 67 , 68 ] with the community, often going to the community for permission and feedback ( Table 4 ). Two studies included outreach to the community(ies) (outreach) [ 57 , 64 ] to inform them of their intervention. Two studies involved the community (involve) [ 56 , 60 ] at multiple stages of the intervention. Two studies collaborated with the community (collaborate) [ 61 , 62 ] in each stage of the intervention. Finally, two studies had shared leadership (shared leadership) [ 63 , 66 ] of the project with the community, often repeatedly returning to the community for engagement and feedback to strengthen the intervention and increase community leadership. Finally, the team investigated the key aims of the studies included in this review. Obesity prevention was the most common goal ( n = 7) [ 57 , 58 , 60 , 66 , 62 , 67 , 68 ] followed by diabetes prevention ( n = 3) [ 59 , 61 , 65 ] ( Figure 6 ). Two studies focused on the promotion of healthy lifestyles ( n = 2) [ 56 , 63 ] and one study [ 64 ] focused on SSB reduction. Discussion Community engagement All of the included studies engaged the community on some level. The level of community engagement differed vastly amongst studies spanning from simply informing the community of the intervention [ 57 , 64 ][], to recurrent conversations with tribal leadership and advisors to guide all levels of the intervention [ 63 , 66 ]. While many studies claimed to engage the community, over half ( n = 7) [ 57–59 , 64 , 65 , 67 , 68 ] did not engage with the community beyond approval (consult) or answer-seeking (outreach) ( Table 4 ). This finding engages with and builds off the work of Harding and Oetzel [ 44 ] who noted the specific importance of community engagement and culturally centred approaches for successful interventions of non-communicable diseases in Indigenous communities. Specifically, they argue that implementation science for AI/AN/I communities should be grounded in Indigenous knowledge, participatory approaches, and systems thinking, and includes 4 elements: culture-centred approach, community engagement (levels of community engagement aforementioned in results section), systems thinking, and integrated knowledge translation. Harding and Oetzel [ 44 ] reiterate the importance of engaging with AI/AN/I communities throughout all stages of a study. Although the studies included in the review reported community engagement, more explicit integration of elements of community engagement needs to be incorporated and reported in future studies. The inclusion of how researchers engaged the community in the article not only demonstrates researchers’ commitment to AI/AN/I work but also strengthens their work with Indigenous Peoples. Indigenous lens The review of the literature included an examination of how the researchers integrated an Indigenous model of health in the research process. As described previously, Indigenous health is a balance among the interrelated aspects of cultural/spiritual, emotional, mental, and physical that function as an interconnected whole [ 2 , 48 , 66 , 69 ]. Although every study included in this review was conducted exclusively with AI/AN/I populations, only 2 studies [ 63 , 66 ] comprise all 4 aspects of the cultural/spiritual, emotional, mental, and physical domains and included AI/AN/I health perspectives. Only the Jennings and Lewis studies approached the cultural content of their research with a nuanced understanding of the AI/AN/I ontological and epistemological conflicts with standard Western science and provided a holistic understanding of health via cultural/spiritual, emotional, mental, and physical aspects and an AI/AN/I model or definition of health. Unfortunately, the reliance on Western definitions of health prioritising government agency health standards are mismatched with the cultures and priorities. As examples, the studies in this scoping review used biomedical measures such as body mass indices (BMI) and anthropometrics to gauge health status ( n = 10) [ 56 , 63 , 58–60 , 61 , 62 , 65 , 67 , 68 ]. In future studies, researchers might consider collaborative research designs to develop metrics that are oriented to Indigenous concepts of health and wellness that are centred on community priorities such as language, land, culture, and relationships. Enhancing AI/AN/I Indigenous health research entails expanding definitions beyond the mere absence of a disease. When communities and Indigenous organisations are given the opportunity to make their own measures related to health, they often look different from external collaborators (e.g. funders, government) who provide or demand specific measures be used [ 63 ,, 70 ]. Cultural components are important in developing interventions for Native communities. For example, AI/AN/I People’s relationship to water includes a sacred responsibility and understanding of the conceptualisation of water that differs from settler colonial society [ 47 ]. Examples of this responsibility can be seen through the legal agency of land and water established in Tribal Law through environmental personhood. This scoping review revealed the limited attention to the complex socio-economic and cultural elements that intertwine water insecurity and Indigenous health. This complexity includes a need to discuss pervasive systematic issues when interventions encourage water consumption, such as quality or access to drinking water [ 71 ]. Participants in Jennings et al’s 3 study on health perceptions of food and water had mixed perceptions of water. Participants knew that water was generally good for them and healthy but did not trust their water sources to be clean and safe [ 66 , 72 ]. In a scoping review of correlates for consuming SSBs in Indigenous communities worldwide, researchers observed an increase in SSB consumption in places with poor water quality [ 22 ]. There were no studies that the team found that tied interventions to improve water quality to behavioural changes, such as drinking more water or less SSB within AI/AN/I communities. By reframing health issues utilising an Indigenous lens, reclaims the way issues are defined and solutions developed [ 73 ] that can only be identified by prioritising community engagement and strengthening those relationships. Examination of regional and tribal water policy would have provided additional insight but is beyond the scope of this review. Weight stigmatization and primary measures Of the articles included in this review, 10 of the 13 included the status of body size and obesity in their study as a primary or secondary measure [ 56 , 63 , 58–62 , 65 , 67 , 68 ]. This is concerning, especially when considering how framing obesity interventions as a primary measure can increase weight stigma rooted in deficit. Additionally, it renders research extractive from communities with little progress towards solutions. Further, Hall and Kahan [ 74 ] in a scoping review looking into weight-loss interventions, indicated that 80% of people who were reviewed from 29 long-term weight-loss studies had regained their weight within 5 years, indicating the unsustainability of weight-loss interventions. As well, the language of obesity has been shown to increase shame and embarrassment in adolescents when they are called “obese” or “fat” by a parent or healthcare provider [ 75 ]. This is especially interesting when parents have a large role in the introduction of SSBs even before the child is born. Promoting healthy habits absent the risk of weight stigmatisation appears more beneficial in a youth context [ 76 ]. Weight stigmatisation increases chronic diseases, bullying, and suicidality amongst AI/AN/I youth [ 75 , 77 ] amongst being obesogenic in and of itself. Additionally, stigma and discrimination due to weight are additive when considering the intersection of other marginalised identities. From an AI/AN/I research perspective, it is imperative that researchers develop culturally sensitive and contextually relevant interventions that effectively address health and social issues. The degree and importance of focus on weight and waistline is indicative of how methods are reflexive of the research team and how much the research team engages with communities. This is not to say that no Native communities are interested in weight status but is rather a comment on the quantity and quality of engagement with weight status in communities. It is not what the researchers are studying, but how they are studying it. If the methods researchers use create stigma in, around, or about communities, there is little reason for communities to want to engage in the research process [ 78 ]. Context is important The majority of studies which reported significant results in decreasing SSBs or increasing water consumption (or both) utilised an educational intervention with a nutrition-focused curriculum. The studies often combined other strategies with nutrition education. Wahi et al [ 79 ] systematic review mentions the use of multiple nutrition education programmes to achieve health outcomes. However, the number of nutrition education interventions that the team found in the current review is more than Wright et al scoping review [ 71 ] about the reduction of SSB consumption among Indigenous Australian and Torres Strait Islander communities. Although this is the case, future studies would need to evaluate further benefits of the integration with strategies Wright identified for transferability across continents and communities. Wright et al [ 80 ] found greater reporting of significant results in environmental changes, utilisation of community health workers, and exploring the role of tribal leaders in helping reduce the availability of SSBs than educational interventions. This speaks to the importance of contextual factors in intervention implementation and that interventions are not a one-size-fits-all solution for every Indigenous community. However, the results of our review show a preponderance of studies that utilise nutrition education interventions to reduce SSB consumption or increase water consumption (or both). Future research and interventions should incorporate traditional AI/AN/I knowledge, food sovereignty, AI/AN/I pedagogies, and community contexts into nutrition education to increase the success of beverage consumption interventions. Environment Many of the studies included in the review utilised many approaches within their intervention in multiple different locations including the school, home, and community. This parallels other research that highlights the importance of environmentally contextual and varied approaches. In a school-based intervention study aimed at reducing obesity and type 2 diabetes, Yoshida and Simoes [ 81 ] highlighted the necessity for interventions tailored to age, gender, language, and involvement of families and local communities. Considering the significant time children spend at home and within their communities, interventions ought to incorporate more parents and caregivers. Kasman et al [ 82 ] noted that reducing the availability of SSBs at home – where children spend the greatest amount of time – resulted in the largest consumption decrease. This decrease achieved at home has the potential to be extended to other environments, such as at school. Furthermore, in a systematic review, Vercammen et al [ 32 ] noted that some overarching strategies for successfully reducing SSB consumption include training for children and healthcare providers, distributing pamphlets (passive education), and altering the environment to limit children’s physical access to beverages. Limited access to beverages can be a unified effort involving schools, homes, and stores, as highlighted in the study conducted by Wright et al [ 80 ] within an Australian Aboriginal community. Another study supporting this position found that removing SSBs from the home environment significantly reduced consumption by up to 60% [ 83 ]. This evidence underscores the importance of holistic interventions that involve varied approaches and locations. Limitations This scoping review has some limitations. A noteworthy challenge was identifying the appropriate key terms to ensure the comprehensive retrieval of relevant articles across multiple databases. A second limitation is the restriction of studies to the United States and Canada. The authors deliberately chose to focus on the two nations due to shared patterns of cultural diversity and integration stemming from their colonial history. However, this geographical constraint does not preclude future scoping/systematic reviews examining the role of SSBs in health issues related to Indigenous Peoples. During our search process, we identified studies that examined interventions with Indigenous communities across Oceania, which could potentially add more insight to the review for future reviews. Furthermore, prior studies [ 27 , 80 ] to this review on SSB consumption have taken a more localised approach, focusing on specific tribes or regions. It is important to acknowledge that while the current review focused on North America it provided valuable insights, although significant differences exist among the tribes within this region. Future research would benefit from the inclusion of more countries, taking into consideration the unique challenges faced by Indigenous Peoples globally. This broader approach in tandem with intervention research, could facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the nutritional landscape and illuminate ways multinational entities such as SSB corporations interact with AI/AN/I communities on an international scale, regardless of their country of origin [ 22 ]. Finally, governments worldwide, including Native Nations, the U.S., and Canada, have implemented policies and taxes related to sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). The Healthy Diné Nations Act [ 84 ] of 2014 and the Lower Sioux Indian Community’s Honoring Little Crow with the Healthy and Indigenous Foods Initiative [ 85 ] of 2016 passed statutes promoting healthier food initiatives, signifying the reclamation of sovereignty through policy action. In this scoping review there was only one article, the Children’s Healthy Living Program that addressed policy shifts, emphasising local government advocacy and community asset utilisation [ 68 ]. The lack of the utilisation of a search string that includes policy-related terms weakens the evidence of intervention success. In the U.S., various restrictions on SSB sales, excise taxes, and service limitations have been enacted [ 86 ]. Similarly, Canada has implemented measures prohibiting the sale of certain SSBs in public schools [ 87 ]. Finally, the team did not differentiate between interventions in urban and rural areas. This is important because of the differences in engagement in rural communities compared with urban communities in areas such as transportation, housing, and food access. There is often an assumption of ruralism for AI/AN/I communities, but it needs to be recognised that AI/AN/I communities and people exist in urban, rural, suburban, and everywhere in between. Conclusion In conclusion, community engagement at all levels of programme intervention is crucial for success in working with Native communities. Regardless of where the interventions are implemented, whether at home or in school, the involvement of the community should not be underestimated. Programme staff and teachers are essential when implementing programmes as they play a vital role in facilitating engagement and promoting adherence to interventions. AI/AN/I communities have distinct definitions of health, underscoring the importance of identifying these meanings and implementing them as appropriate within their research designs. Moreover, the reduction of sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) availability requires a unified effort from schools, families, and community leaders to modify the environment and increase the accessibility of safe and clean drinking water. Neglecting to address these components helps to promote the imbalance of healthy food marketing and affordable options by SSB companies targeting marginalised populations []. Future research endeavours in AI/AN/I communities should take a holistic approach to health considered by the local communities and employ Indigenous research approaches that promote more community engagement. Given this position, it is imperative that research conducted with communities results in actionable knowledge that empowers them to utilise the data to enhance their resilience, health and well-being. Without research implications being shared with communities in a meaningful manner, the research process itself is rendered extractive.
