ABSTRACT Problem Suicide rates among adolescents continue to rise globally. Frequent changes in residency can be traumatic for adolescents, increasing their risk of suicidal behaviors. However, the impact of residential mobility on mental health remains understudied. While numerous studies have linked childhood residential mobility to suicidal tendencies, limited research explores the relationship between residence changes and suicide attempts among adolescents. This study examines the relationship between residential mobility and suicide attempts among adolescents. Methods This cross‐sectional retrospective study uses data from California emergency department visits for deliberate self‐harm. Adolescents aged 10–19 were included, with exposure defined as suicide attempts in 2010 and the outcome as no history of zip code changes or at least one change (2006–2009). Logistic regression models adjusted for demographic characteristics (gender, age, race, and insurance status) were used to assess the association between residential mobility and suicide attempts. Stratified analyses explored variations across demographic subgroups. Findings Among 496,268 participants, adolescents who attempted suicide were more likely to have one or more residence changes (AOR = 1.71, 95% CI = 1.58–1.85). This association showed higher odds among females (AOR = 1.51), older adolescents (15–19 years, AOR = 1.42), nonwhite racial groups (e.g., Black = 1.55; Hispanic = 1.71; Other = 1.70), and those with Medicaid (AOR = 1.87), Self‐pay (AOR = 1.47), or Other insurance (AOR = 2.20) compared to their respective reference groups. Conclusions Adolescents with at least one history of relocation are at risk of suicide attempts. Targeted interventions should focus on individuals who have made frequent moves during their adolescent years. Clinicians should consider relocation histories in suicide risk evaluations. Introduction Suicide is one of the leading causes of death among adolescents (World Health Organization 2021 ). Globally, more than 700,000 adolescents take their own lives every year (World Health Organization 2021 ). The burden is particularly prevalent in the United States, where suicide ranks as the second leading cause of death among youth aged 12 to 24 years (World Health Organization 2021 ; Heron 2018 ). This alarming data emphasizes the need for comprehensive approaches to adolescent mental health that address both individual and systemic risk factors. Frequent residential mobility is a common yet understudied factor that may worsen adolescents' mental health. Approximately 50% of children experience at least one relocation before their 10th birthday, which often results in psychological distress or traumatic events (Engemann et al. 2019 ; Carballo et al. 2020 ). Frequent relocations can disrupt an adolescent's social relationships, interfere with educational goals, and increase exposure to adverse childhood experiences (Perreault et al. 2020 ). As a result, these disruptions can lead to poor mental health outcomes and increased suicidal behaviors (Perreault et al. 2020 ). Addressing how residential mobility may increase adolescent suicide is critical to informing effective interventions and public health strategies. Frequent changes in residency can be a burden to children and have long‐term psychological consequences (Carballo et al. 2020 ). Residential mobility is associated with an increase in suicide attempt rates among adolescents, poor mental health, and behavioral problems that often lead to substance abuse (Hagedoorn and Helbich 2022 ). The stress of moving, feelings of isolation, and loss of stability can destroy coping mechanisms among adolescents whose brains are still developing the capacity to manage complex emotions (Simsek et al. 2021 ). Children often experience difficulties adjusting to change, which can impact their mental, social, and emotional well‐being (Tseliou et al. 2016 ; Simsek et al. 2021 ). Existing literature has established a correlation between childhood residential mobility and adverse mental health outcomes, including increased rates of suicide attempts (Bramson et al. 2016 ; Webb et al. 2016 ; Zhang et al. 2021 ). For example, Bramson et al. ( 2016 ) examined the association between childhood relocations and various adverse outcomes, including suicide attempts, severe mental illness, substance abuse, and low academic achievement (Bramson et al. 2016 ). The researchers used Cox regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HR) for adverse outcomes and found that childhood relocations were associated with suicide attempts (HR = 1.19; 95% CI, 1.19–1.20). Additionally, Webb et al. ( 2016 ) assessed the association between childhood residential mobility and several adverse outcomes (e.g., suicide attempt, violent criminality, psychiatric illness, substance misuse, and premature mortality). They used a national cohort data from Denmark and tracked individuals born between 1971 and 1997 (Webb et al. 2016 ). The participants were followed from their adolescent years to early middle age. Webb et al. ( 2016 ) revealed that individuals who had a single residential move during adolescence had a 60% higher risk of attempted suicide compared to those who did not move (Webb et al. 2016 ). Those who had a history of multiple relocations in a year during early or mid‐adolescence showed significantly higher risks, with attempted suicide IRRs reaching up to three compared to those who did not move (Webb et al. 2016 ). Webb et al. ( 2016 ) found that the risk for any psychiatric disorder increased gradually across childhood with single moves (IRRs = 1.10–1.20) and was elevated for multiple moves (IRRs = 1.50–1.80). These findings align with broader research associating unstable living environments with heightened vulnerability to mental health issues. While many studies have found that childhood residential mobility is related to suicidal behaviors (Bramson et al. 2016 ; Tseliou et al. 2016 ; Webb et al. 2016 ; Simsek et al. 2021 ), there has been limited research on the histories of residence changes and their associations with suicide attempts. Understanding the association is important for designing targeted interventions that mitigate the mental health impacts of frequent relocations among adolescents. This study fills the gap by examining the association between the history of zip code changes and suicide attempts among adolescents. The study hopes to provide evidence to guide clinical practices and health policy development. The findings will provide crucial context for clinicians, educators, and policymakers, underscoring the importance of addressing residential mobility as a social determinant of adolescent mental health. Additionally, the findings will highlight the potential for interdisciplinary collaboration in addressing this complex issue, drawing on insights from psychology, sociology, public health, clinical expertise, and education to develop comprehensive solutions for suicide among adolescents. 2. Materials and Methods This study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross‐sectional observational research. 2.1. Study Design We conducted a stratified cross‐sectional analysis to examine the relationship between residential mobility and suicide attempts. Stratifying the data by demographic characteristics allowed for a more nuanced analysis of these associations. 2.2. Data Source and Ethical Considerations The data set was provided by the Health Care Access and Information, formerly known as the Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development (OSHPD). The data provided by OSHPD is fully deidentified and contains no personally identifiable information. The study is exempt from IRB review under the provisions for research involving existing data that is publicly available or deidentified, as outlined in 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4). The data set contains records for all California emergency department visits in 2010 that were for deliberate self‐harm. Emergency departments (EDs) are essential for identifying individuals at high suicide risk, especially among adolescents. The International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM) includes a deliberate self‐harm visit as a medical procedure for billing purposes. According to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, a suicide attempt is coded using the ICD‐9‐CM Diagnosis Codes Suicide and Self‐Inflicted Injury (E950‐959). Since 1990, California has required the reporting of all external‐cause‐of‐injury codes (Abellera et al. 2005 ). 2.3. Study Population Our study population included adolescents aged 10–19 who were admitted to the emergency department (ED) as patients in 2010 for suicidal behaviors. Adolescents were identified as individuals who had attempted suicide during the study period (2006‐2009). The study population must locate a zip code as their place of residence. 2.4. Variables Exposure variable: Residential mobility was categorized as having no history of zip code changes (2006–2009) or having at least one zip code change (2006–2009). Outcome variable: Suicide attempts among adolescents (aged 10–19) in 2010. Confounding variables: Confounding variables were selected to be controlled to ensure that other factors did not influence the relationship between the exposure and outcome variables. Gender, race, and insurance status were included to control for their potential influence on the relationship between residential mobility and suicidal behaviors. The variables were categorized as follows: (1) Gender: Male and female, (2) Race: White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Other, and (3) Insurance Status: Private, Medicaid, Self‐pay, and Other. 2.5. Statistical Analysis Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 16. The stratified data included gender, race, and insurance status. We modeled logistic regressions to estimate odds ratios for the association between residential mobility and suicide attempts. Confounders were controlled to ensure that external factors did not bias the observed associations. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore variations in the impact of residential mobility across demographic subgroups. 3. Results After the analyses, a total of n = 496,268 participants were included in this study. There were 48.4% males and 51.6% females. Regarding race, 34.6% were identified as White, 12.3% as Black, 41.9% as Hispanic, 4.0% as Asian, and 7.2% as “Other.” For insurance status, the largest group of participants had Private insurance (41.9%), followed by Medicaid (41.6%), Self‐pay (12.9%), and Other (3.6%). See Table 1 for the detailed breakdown of demographic characteristics. Adolescents who attempted suicide (15.85%) in California had higher rates of were a history of at least one zip code change compared to those with no history of zip code change (9.42%). Those who experienced one or more zip code changes from 2006 to 2009 had significantly higher odds of reporting a suicide attempt in 2010 compared to those with no history of zip code changes (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: [1.58, 1.85]). See Table 2 for the stratification of the history of zip codes. In the unadjusted model, females (OR = 1.52, 95% CI [1.44–1.61]) had higher odds than males of reporting a suicide attempt. Adolescents aged 15–19 years (OR = 1.65, 95% CI [1.51–1.79]) had significantly higher odds compared to those aged 10–14 years. Compared to White participants, minority groups had lower odds of suicide attempt: Black (OR = 0.67), Hispanic (OR = 0.67), Asian (OR = 0.79), and Other (OR = 0.93). Participants with Medicaid (OR = 1.01) showed similar odds compared to those with Private insurance, while Self‐Pay (OR = 0.77) and Other (OR = 0.93) had lower odds of reporting a suicide attempt compared to Private insurance. See Table 3 for odds ratios and confidence intervals from the unadjusted analyses. After controlling for gender, females had 1.51 times higher odds (AOR = 1.51, 95% CI = [1.37, 1.67]) of reporting a suicide attempt compared to males. Adolescents aged 15–19 years also showed higher odds (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI = [1.23, 1.64]) compared to those aged 10–14 years. Regarding race, compared to White adolescents, Black (AOR = 1.55, 95% CI = [1.26, 1.91]), Hispanic (AOR = 1.71, 95% CI = [1.48, 1.96]), and Other (AOR = 1.70, 95% CI = [1.26, 2.31]) racial groups had significantly higher odds of reporting a suicide attempt. The odds for Asian adolescents were higher (AOR = 1.22, 95% CI = [0.58, 2.57]) but not statistically significant. Insurance status was also associated with suicide attempts. Compared to adolescents with Private insurance, those with Medicaid (AOR = 1.87, 95% CI = [1.68, 2.07]), Self‐Pay (AOR = 1.47, 95% CI = [1.14, 1.89]), and Other (AOR = 2.20, 95% CI = [1.47, 3.30]) had higher odds of reporting a suicide attempt. Adolescents with at least one zip code change between 2006 and 2009 had higher odds of attempting suicide in 2010 compared to those with no zip code change. Adjusted stratified analyses highlighted notable demographic differences in this relationship. Females and adolescents aged 15‐19 had higher odds of suicide attempts compared to males and those aged 10‐14. Similarly, adolescents who identify as Black, Hispanic, and Other racial groups demonstrated higher odds than Whites, and adolescents with ‘Other’ forms of insurance had higher odds of suicide attempts compared to those with Private insurance. Our findings align with previous research suggesting that frequent residential changes during formative years can increase susceptibility to mental health challenges and behavioral problems. For example, Cotton et al. ( 2017 ) investigated the impact of residential instability on adolescent delinquent behaviors. They found that adolescents with three or more residential moves during middle childhood (ages 6–10) had an odds ratio (OR) of 1.55 (95% CI: 1.05–2.25) for committing crimes at age 11. Similarly, frequent moves during early childhood (ages 1 month–5 years) were associated with an OR of 1.67 (95% CI: 1.17–2.37) for school delinquency, while moves during adolescence increased the likelihood of tobacco use by age 16 (OR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.07–3.61). Additionally, Fowler et al. ( 2015 ) examined the impact of housing instability on the outcomes of young adults using data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health. Their research demonstrated that frequent residential moves significantly increased the odds of depression (OR = 1.10), arrest (OR = 1.08), and regular smoking (OR = 1.12). These findings highlight the need to address housing instability to mitigate its long‐term impact on adolescent mental health and behavioral outcomes. This study contributes to existing knowledge by examining how gender and insurance status may affect adolescents' mental health. While previous studies have focused on socioeconomic mobility broadly, this study highlights the unique vulnerabilities faced by adolescents with Medicaid or other non‐private insurance. These insights deepened our understanding of the intricate relationship between social determinants and mental health outcomes. Building on this foundation, this study is among the first to explore the nuanced relationship between residential mobility and adolescent suicide risk within a large and diverse population. Additionally, the findings underscore the importance of incorporating residential history into suicide risk assessments to enable clinicians to identify overlooked risk factors, such as frequent moves during adolescence. The elevated risk observed among adolescents with ‘Other’ insurance status (OR = 2.20) may reflect a concentration of vulnerable individuals with inconsistent or limited access to health coverage. This category could include underinsured populations, such as those with temporary health plans or families facing job insecurity. These insights highlight the importance of housing stability initiatives and targeted interventions in mitigating the mental health consequences of frequent relocations during the early stages of an individual's life. While this study has added knowledge to the current literature revolving around adolescent suicide and residential mobility, it is essential to acknowledge the study's limitations. First, the reliance on emergency department data may underrepresent adolescents who do not seek medical care for suicide attempts, which may potentially skew the results. This limitation could exclude cases where suicide attempts go unreported, especially among populations with barriers to healthcare access or a stigma surrounding mental health. Additionally, the data set lacked detailed information about specific reasons for residential mobility, such as economic hardship, parental job changes, or family instability. These details could provide crucial context for interpreting the findings. Furthermore, the classification of race as “Other” limited the detail of analysis for underrepresented groups, such as American Indian and Alaska Native adolescents. This lack of specificity may overlook unique risk factors or protective elements in these populations. Lastly, unmeasured confounders such as family instability or economic hardship, which may influence both residential mobility and suicide risk, were not captured in the data set and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Our findings suggest several directions for future research. Data collection efforts could incorporate survey questions or structured interviews to capture the underlying motivations for residential moves. This would allow researchers to differentiate between voluntary moves (e.g., job promotions) and involuntary moves (e.g., evictions or job loss), which may have vastly different impacts on adolescent mental health. Additionally, qualitative research methods could be employed to explore the lived experiences underlying residential mobility. These narratives could identify patterns and contextual factors, such as social support disruptions or financial strain, that may mediate the relationship between mobility and mental health outcomes. A more diverse sample that includes underrepresented racial groups can enhance generalizability. From a practical standpoint, clinicians and educators should incorporate relocation history into suicide risk assessments and develop interventions tailored to the unique needs of this population. Finally, policymakers should prioritize housing stability initiatives as a preventative measure to reduce mental health disparities among adolescents. 5. Conclusions Our findings showed a significant association between residential mobility and suicide attempts among adolescents in California. Adolescents who experienced at least one zip code change between 2006 and 2009 are more likely to attempt suicide compared to those who did not experience such changes. This study stresses the need for targeted interventions that address the unique challenges faced by adolescents who frequently relocate. Clinicians should incorporate residential history into suicide risk assessments, especially in emergency department settings where at‐risk adolescents may first present. Schools and community‐based programs should be equipped to identify students who frequently move and offer them tailored counseling. Health policymakers should recognize residential instability as a determinant of health and implement policies that promote housing stability, particularly for low‐income families and those with limited access to insurance coverage. Future research should aim to include a more diverse sample to elucidate the complex relationship between residential mobility and adolescent mental health. Longitudinal studies could also investigate the long‐term effects of relocation and inform the development of evidence‐based prevention strategies.
