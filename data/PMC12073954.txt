Abstract Background: Chronotype is trait-like construct reflecting long-term morningness–eveningness preferences. Higher eveningness scores are linked to sleep disturbances, night-eating, and addictive behaviors but remain understudied in subjects with bipolar disorder (BD). Aim: This study aimed to explore the clinical and behavioral factors associated with chronotype in a euthymic cohort with BD. Methods: Adult individuals (18–45 years) with DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5 th Edition) diagnosis of BD in clinical remission (≥3 months) were enrolled, if they did not have current psychiatric or substance use disorder as per Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST score &lt; 27). Participants were assessed using the validated Hindi version of Composite Scale of Morningness-Eveningness (CSM), the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)-Life Chart Method, IGLSI (International Group for Study of Lithium) Scale for typical/atypical features, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, Cognitive complaints in BD Rating Assessment, Seasonal Pattern Assessment Questionnaire, Bipolar Eating Disorder Scale, Internet Disorder Scale, and Functioning Assessment Short Test. After data normalization, exploratory factor analysis was conducted using principal component analysis with varimax rotation on the study variables excluding the CSM. Subsequently, regression analysis was performed with CSM as the dependent variable. Results: The mean age of the sample was 32.43 ± 7.68 years (n = 100; 35% females). Factor analysis revealed a latent structure with three factors: Factor 1 (global functioning), Factor 2 (disordered biological functions and rhythms), and Factor 3 (dysfunction due to sleep and internet use). The regression model was found to be statistically significant [F (3,96) = 5.520, P = 0.002; R 2 = 0.147, adjusted R 2 = 0.120). Factor-2 was a significant predictor of CSM score (B = -2.201, SE: 0.606, P &lt; 0.001). For every unit increase in Factor 2 score, the CSM total score reduced on average by 2.2 units. Conclusion: This study highlights the role of disrupted sleep, eating behaviors, and seasonality in influencing the chronotype and their interconnectedness in individuals with BD. INTRODUCTION Rhythm disturbances are the hallmarks of bipolar disorder (BD) and have been documented in drug-naive individuals with BD.[ 1 , 2 ] Eveningness is over-represented in individuals with BD compared to healthy control samples and has been considered as a bipolarity-related marker in individuals with depression.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Chronotype refers to an individual’s long-term preference for morningness–eveningness or wakefulness, and timing of daily activities if the choice is left to the individuals themselves, rather than externally enforced schedules.[ 4 , 5 ] The chronotype is a trait-like construct that is relatively stable from adolescence onward up until middle adulthood, though it varies at extremes of age.[ 5 , 6 ] It determines the physiological functions like body temperature as well as behaviors such as sleep, food intake, physical activity, and peak cognitive performance during the day.[ 7 , 8 , 9 ] About one half of the inter-individual variability in diurnal preferences is explained by genetic effects.[ 10 ] Though chronotype has been categorized into morning or evening types, it lies on a continuum of morningness–eveningness preferences.[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] Eveningness is considered as a proxy marker for the circadian dysfunction, leading to a chronic conflict of internal circadian rhythms and signals that entrain them, such as societal routines.[ 12 ] This social jetlag has been linked to adverse physical and mental health outcomes in general population samples, university students, and shift workers.[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] Those with higher eveningness scores had a higher body fat percentage,[ 16 ] reduced sleep quality,[ 17 , 18 ] poor daytime functioning,[ 17 ] increased electronic media,[ 18 ] and alcohol use.[ 17 , 19 ] A pilot factor analytic study of patients with major depression identified that the latent structure of evening chronotype and bipolar features were linked to each other, independent of sleep quality and depressive symptoms.[ 3 ] Available studies specific to BD show that higher eveningness scores are linked to an adverse course of bipolar illness, including rapid cycling, more episodes, early age of onset, suicidality, and poor functioning in patient samples.[ 20 , 21 ] Remitted bipolar patients with an evening circadian propensity were more likely to live in an unsuitable sleeping environment and engage in disordered eating behavior and nocturnal eating before bed.[ 21 , 22 ] A meta-analytic review found that evening chronotype status was linked to an emotional eating score that was 1.52 points higher overall (95% CI: 0.89–2.14).[ 23 ] Eveningness was associated with greater sleep–wake disturbances, more unhealthy dietary habits, worse quality of life, more impaired interpersonal relationships, and more dysfunctional sleep-related cognitions and behaviors, controlling for age, gender, and years of education.[ 24 ] In the Indian context, Kuppuli and colleagues[ 25 ] in their study from Puducherry reported a higher rate of disturbances in sleep and biological rhythms in euthymic cases with BD (n = 50) compared to healthy controls. No study from India has examined factors associated with circadian preference or chronotype in BD. The chronotype disruptions have been linked to adverse outcomes pertaining to poorer illness course, daily functioning, and maladaptive behaviors, with potential clinical and treatment implications. Though chronotype and its associated factors have been well studied in nonpsychiatric samples, the studies pertaining to clinical samples with BD are still limited. The current study aimed to explore the clinical and psychobehavioral determinants of chronotype in a euthymic cohort with BD. Given the diverse yet interrelated study variables—such as sleep, functioning, seasonality, and eating behaviors—exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was employed as a data-driven approach to identify underlying latent constructs. Prior research has not established a well-defined factor structure linking these domains in individuals with BD. Psychobehavioral variables in mental health research often exhibit complex interrelationships that benefit from data-driven exploration. Additionally, multiple scales may have overlapping subdomains. EFA can help to illustrate the conceptual overlap, ensuring that extracted factors represent distinct psychological dimensions for exploring their association with chronotype. In line with the study’s aim to explore and elucidate these relationships, EFA was utilized to reduce dimensionality and uncover meaningful patterns, without being constrained by a priori assumptions. MATERIALS AND METHOD This was an observational study on patients with BD seeking treatment at a tertiary care teaching institute in India. The study sample included long-term follow-up patients, with reliable clinical records to minimize recall bias. Institute Ethics Committee (IEC) approval was obtained prior to start of the study (Ref no: 653/25.08.2022). We used the STROBE case-control checklist when writing our report. The study data were collected between May 2023 and January 2024. Written informed consent was taken from all participants. Adult individuals, aged 18 to 45 years, visiting the outpatient psychiatry department and fulfilling the predefined selection criteria were enrolled in the study. Participants were included if they had DSM-5[ 26 ] diagnosis of BD and were currently euthymic and in clinical remission for 3 or more months. Those with current psychiatric comorbidity or substance use disorder (score ≥27 on ASSIST; Alcohol, smoking and substance involvement screening test),[ 27 ] apart from nicotine, were excluded. Those with any uncontrolled or significant medical illness that may interfere with study assessments were excluded. Study tools All participants were assessed using a demographic sheet and semistructured clinical proforma to examine the duration, past episodes details, treatment details, and course of illness. Chronotype scores were assessed using the Composite Scale of Morningness-Eveningness (CSM) validated Hindi version.[ 11 , 28 , 29 ] The CSM is a self-report questionnaire designed to determine an individual’s chronotype—their natural preference for activity and alertness at different times of the day. The CSM evaluates the preferred sleeping and waking times of individuals if the choice would be left up to them, besides assessing the ease of waking up, peak performance times, and subjective alertness levels. The possible scores on the scale range from 13 to 55, with greater scores indicating greater preference for morningness. The CSM Hindi version has been validated in the Indian population and was used after obtaining the requisite permission from lead authors.[ 29 ] The illness characteristics were assessed using National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)-Life Chart Method (LCM)[ 30 ] and IGLSI (International Group for Study of Lithium) Scale for typical/atypical features.[ 31 ] The NIMH LCM charts the retrospective course of affective illness, including detailed depiction of each episode, its severity, symptoms, treatment, and hospitalizations, with guidance outlined in the accompanying manual. The IGLSI Scale for typical/atypical features evaluates four core features of the affective illness, with scoring based on responses to lifetime comorbidity (absence/presence), psychotic features (mood congruent only/mood incongruent), interepisodic intervals (full remission/residual symptoms), and polarity of lifetime episodes (greater number of depressive episodes/greater number of manic episodes). Relevant psychological and behavioral variables were assessed using standardized scales for sleep efficacy (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, PSQI),[ 32 ] cognitive complaints (Cognitive Complaints in BD Rating Assessment, COBRA),[ 33 ] seasonal variations of mood and behaviors (Seasonal Pattern Assessment Questionnaire, SPAQ),[ 34 , 35 ] eating dysregulation (Bipolar Eating Disorder Scale, BEDS,[ 36 ] and severity of internet addiction (Internet Disorder Scale, IDS-15),[ 37 ] and daily functioning (Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST)).[ 38 ] A brief description for each of these tools is provided here. The PSQI is a standardized measure with 19 items that assess the quality and pattern of sleep across seven domains, that is, subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction over the last month. Lower scoring in PSQI total score reflects better sleep quality. The COBRA is a 16-item, Likert-scaled (0–3), self-report instrument that was primarily developed to assess the subjective cognitive complaints experienced by patients with BD. The items cover areas indicative of executive function, processing speed, working memory, verbal learning and memory, attention/concentration, and mental tracking. The SPAQ carries questions to assess seasonality in a retrospective manner, asking for degree to which the sleep length, social activity, mood, weight, appetite, and energy levels change with the seasons. Each of these is scored on a Likert scale (0–4), with a higher score indicating a greater seasonal variation. The BEDS is a 10-item, self-administered tool that evaluates both the intensity and frequency of eating dysfunctions in patients with BD. The scale items ask for night-time eating, difficulty in controlling eating, eating to feel better, eating between meals, and so on, with possible scores from 0 to 30, indicating the severity of dysregulated eating behaviors. The IDS-15 is designed to assess symptoms and severity of problematic Internet use based on the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for Internet Gaming Disorder. It consists of 15 items, each scored on a 1–5 Likert scale, covering four key dimensions: escapism and dysfunctional emotional coping, withdrawal symptoms, impaired self-regulation, and dysfunctional Internet-related self-control. The FAST is an interview-administered instrument consisting of 24 brief items, each scored on a 0–3 Likert scale. It covers six domains of functioning—autonomy, occupational, cognitive, financial, interpersonal, and leisure—with higher scores indicating greater dysfunction. Statistical analysis The data analysis was done using a licensed SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, SPSS Inc., Chicago).[ 39 ] Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean (SD), median [interquartile range, IQR (Q 1 , Q 3 )], and frequencies (%) for the demographic and clinical profiles. Shapiro–Wilk’s test for normality indicated a non-normal distribution for all study variables except for age, body mass index, and CSM. A nonparametric Spearman’s rank correlation was used to explore the association between the quantitative study variables. The EFA was performed to identify underlying factors using data from quantitative clinical variables, namely, age, total episodes, time since remission, IGSLI atypical score, PSQI domain scores, COBRA, SPAQ, BEDS, IDS factors, and FAST domains. This approach enabled a data-driven identification of clusters of interrelated variables, providing an empirical framework for understanding these complex interactions. The integration of different scale domains was based on their conceptual interrelatedness as they may represent broader latent constructs contributing to the overall phenotype. To ensure the validity of factor extraction, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was used to assess sampling adequacy, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was performed to confirm that the correlation matrix was factorable. A KMO value above 0.8 and a significant Bartlett’s test ( P &lt; 0.001) indicated that the data were suitable for factor analysis, ensuring robust extraction of meaningful latent constructs. Data normalization was performed using the maximum-minimum approach while preprocessing it for EFA. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed as the extraction method to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset. Initial eigenvalues suggested multiple components. The criteria for deciding the number of factors were based on eigen value &gt;1, leveling of the steep curve on Scree Plot (‘elbow point’), and the total variance explained, besides the balance between simplicity or interpretability and explanatory power. Varimax rotation with Kaiser normalization was applied to achieve a clearer factor structure. A two-step approach involving the EFA, following data normalization, and regression analysis was employed in view of non-normal distribution of the study variables. This approach also aimed to identify a parsimonious set of latent factors.[ 40 , 41 ] Bivariate correlations between factor scores and the CSM total score were examined. Subsequently, regression analysis was performed with CSM as the dependent variable and the extracted factor scores as independent variables to assess the predictive power of each factor. P &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. RESULTS A total of 167 patients with BD were screened from the outpatient clinic in the Department of Psychiatry in a tertiary care teaching hospital. Of these, 55 did not meet the selection criteria and 12 did not provide informed consent to participate. A final sample of 100 participants was enrolled in the study. Except for one participant with bipolar II disorder, 99% of the sample was diagnosed with bipolar I disorder according to DSM-5 criteria, established through clinical interview and evaluation by a psychiatrist. Demographic profile and clinical variables The mean age of the participants was 32.43 years (SD 7.68). Approximately one-third of the sample was female (35%) The majority were married (57%). The average number of years of education was 12.14 (SD3.26). Most participants lived in nuclear families (86%) and resided in the National Capital Territory of Delhi or surrounding satellite cities (74%) [ Table 1 ]. The mean age of onset for bipolar illness was 22.08 years (SD 6.31) with an average illness duration of 10.40 ± 6.30 years. The mean time since remission was 16.81 ± 15.94 months. About 35% of participants had a history of hospitalization. About 11% of participants had comorbid hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and/or hypothyroidism that were well controlled. A family history suggestive of psychiatric illness was noted in 22% of participants, while 16% had family history suggestive of BD in first- or second-degree relatives. The mean score of CSM was 37.74 (SD: 6.43). The CSM has a score range from 13 to 55, with higher scores indicative of morningness. The mean (SD) and median (IQR) scores for PSQI, COBRA, SPAQ, BEDS, IDS, and FAST are shown in Table 1 . Univariate analysis CSM score had a significant correlation with time since remission (r s = 0.28; P = 0.005), PSQI global (r s = -0.29; P = 0.003), PSQI latency (r s = -0.27; P = 0.006), PSQI sleep efficiency (r s = -0.21; P = 0.038), FAST occupational functioning (r s = -0.20; P = 0.047), and FAST leisure time (r s = -0.226; P = 0.024) domains [ Table 2 ]. A higher CSM score indicative of morningness was positively associated with a longer time of remission, better sleep parameters, and better functioning in occupation and leisure time. Multivariable analysis The EFA was conducted with resultant factors taken as independent variables for regression analysis with CSM total score as the dependent variable. Figure 1 depicts the scree plot of the EFA, where the X-axis represents the principal components extracted during the EFA, while the Y-axis represents the Eigenvalues associated with each component. The three-component solution accounted for nearly half of the total variance. Table 3 shows the rotated component matrix with factor loadings across three components. Factor 1 (Global functioning) has loadings on FAST Interpersonal Relationships (0.933), FAST Cognitive Functioning (0.901), FAST Autonomy (0.875), FAST Financial Issues (0.849), FAST Leisure Time (0.825), FAST Occupational Functioning (0.798), COBRA total (0.499), total episodes (0.477), and age (0.438). Factor 2 (disordered biological functions and rhythms) has loadings on PSQI Sleep efficiency (0.622), PSQI Overall Sleep Quality (0.574), PSQI Need medications to sleep (0.552), SPAQ Global Seasonality Score (0.515), PSQI Sleep duration (0.494), PSQI Sleep latency (0.410), BEDS Score (0.469), and time since remission (-0.494). Factor 3 (Dysfunction due to sleep and Internet use) has loadings on PSQI Sleep Disturbance-Frequency (.883), PSQI Day Dysfunction from Sleepiness (0.757), and IDS Internet Disorder Scale (.411). Table 4 shows the best-fit model for regression. The regression model was statistically significant [F (3,96) = 5.520, P = 0.002; R 2 = 0.147, adjusted R 2 = 0.120], with 12% of the variance explained by three factors in chronotype scores. For every unit increase in Factor 2 score, the CSM total score reduced on average by 2.2 units. DISCUSSION Findings from the paper provide a framework for understanding the self-reported chronotype in a euthymic cohort of BD, exploring the interconnectedness of multiple dimensions of functioning, clinical, and psychobehavioral variables with each other and with the individual’s chronotype. The findings from correlational analysis highlight the associations between CSM scores, with higher scores indicative of a morningness, and various clinical and behavioral domains in euthymic individuals with BD. The significant positive correlation between CSM score and time since remission suggests that those with a morning chronotype may experience longer periods of symptom stability. Morningness has been consistently reported to be a protective factor with good outcomes in BD.[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] Furthermore, individuals with higher morningness reported better overall sleep quality, shorter sleep latency, and higher sleep efficiency in the current study. These findings align with prior research[ 24 ] indicating that a morning chronotype is often associated with healthier sleep patterns. This is crucial in BD management as poor sleep quality is frequently linked with mood dysregulation. Additionally, the CSM scores correlated with occupational and leisure time functioning. Morningness preferences were related to a better engagement in work-related and recreational activities. This functional advantage may be partly due to societal norms that align work and social expectations with morning-oriented schedules, as well as the improved sleep stability and regularity of daily routines that support social and occupational roles. The factor analysis yielded underlying latent structure of the study variables, identifying three distinct factors. Factor 1 constituted the problems faced in the interpersonal, cognitive, social, and occupational functioning, leisure, and autonomy indicative of an underlying construct of overall functionality pertaining to an individual’s day-to-day life. The FAST scale is validated to measure multiple dimensions of functioning, supporting the grouping of all those domains under a single factor.[ 38 ] This validation also underscores the robustness of the Factor 1 (Global functioning). Age and total illness episodes had positive loadings on this factor indicating their contribution to impairment, implying that the overall functionality decline with more illness episodes that is congruent with existing literature.[ 42 ] Factor 2 reflects the interconnectedness of sleep, eating behavior, and seasonality, pointing toward a disrupted pattern of biological functions and rhythms. Previous studies on subjects with BD report that night eating was linked to poor illness course,[ 43 ] poor sleep quality,[ 43 , 44 ] and seasonality,[ 44 ] providing support to their grouping under this factor. Seasonality was found to have a direct effect on night eating symptoms.[ 44 ] Similar reports from university student samples indicate relationship between night eating symptoms and global seasonality scores.[ 45 ] The occupational functioning had some loading on Factor 2 but was grouped with Factor 1 due to a stronger loading with components of overall functioning. Factor 2 (disordered biological functions and rhythms) had negative loadings for time since remission, implying that longer remissions are associated with better sleep efficiency, quality, and less disturbance in biological rhythms. Factor 3 (dysfunction due to sleep and internet use) encompasses the frequency and impact of sleep disruptions and problematic Internet behaviors. The PSQI items in the Factor 3 are indicative of frequency of sleep disturbance and disruptive nature of poor sleep on the day’s activities and its association with excessive Internet use. The IDS, that is based on modified criteria for DSM-5 Internet Gaming Disorder, also reflects the growing concern about the impact of Internet use on sleep and daily functioning.[ 37 ] Overall, the identified factors are in broad conceptual alignment and show an agreement with what is known from existing international literature.[ 12 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ] The morningness–eveningness preference is influenced by various factors, including genetic propensity, temperamental traits, and environmental influences, which contribute to the chronotype.[ 10 , 46 , 47 ] Given these complexities, the variance explained by the current study variables, though modest, is meaningful for understanding the construct. The regression model with CSM as the dependent variable was statistically significant, with the model explaining 12% variance in chronotype scores (R 2 = 0.147; Adjusted R 2 = 0.120). The three-component solution explained a meaningful proportion of variance, confirming an interpretable latent structure. The factor loadings demonstrated a conceptually coherent and interpretable clustering of variables. Given the exploratory nature of our study, EFA was chosen to allow the data to reveal the underlying structure without being constrained by a predefined model. As study variables were non-normally distributed, the use of a two-step approach was better suited with the EFA, combined with normalization techniques, to preprocess the data, followed by subsequent regression. Regression analysis indicated that the score for Factor 2 (disordered biological functions and rhythms) emerged as the significant predictor of CSM score. Essentially, Factor 2 reflects the disruptions in sleep, disordered eating behaviors, and seasonality. For every one unit increase in score of Factor 2, the CSM total score reduced by 2.2. It means that more disruptions in biological functions and seasonal rhythms predicted a lower CSM score, that is, lower morningness preference. Findings are broadly comparable to the limited existing studies on factors associated with chronotype in BD.[ 1 , 5 , 22 ] The circadian disruptions have been previously associated with the disordered eating and unhealthy dietary behaviors in BD patients.[ 22 ] BD patients with nighttime eating were more likely to have evening chronotype.[ 43 ] In a prior study by Romo-Nava and colleagues, variables such as age, suicide attempt, and co-occurring substance use disorder were associated with evening chronotype (Odds ratio range of 0.97 to 1.59).[ 1 ] The findings from the study have clinical implications. The study highlights that morningness in individuals with BD in remission is associated with longer periods of stability, better sleep quality, and improved occupational and leisure functioning. Disruptions in biological rhythms, such as irregular sleep, eating behaviors, and seasonal variations, significantly predict a lower preference for morningness. These findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions like chronotherapeutic or lifestyle modifications, structured routines, and other behavioral strategies. These could include work adjustments that align better with an individual’s chronotype in order to reduce the social jetlag. Furthermore, behavioral measures can reduce the adverse impact of disrupted circadian rhythms on the physical and psychological health. Literature suggests the role of modifiable factors such as time spent outdoors, physical activity, and meals in levels of morning alertness that can be targeted to enhance overall wellbeing and quality of life.[ 48 , 49 , 50 ] There is also a role for personalized chronobiological interventions that support overall wellbeing and improve treatment outcomes in BD.[ 46 , 48 , 49 ] Some individuals with such disruptions might benefit from pharmacological, psychosocial, and light therapy geared at circadian rhythm disturbances and seasonal mood variations. Implementing comprehensive treatment plans that consider these interrelated factors can enhance patients’ overall wellbeing and quality of life. The study findings must be interpreted in light of certain limitations. The current sample was derived from the patients who were on psychotropic medications that could not be withheld due to ethical reasons. The study has a modest sample size, though it provides a sufficient power for correlational statistical analysis with medium effect size and is comparable with existing studies in the literature.[ 21 , 22 , 24 ] Outpatient clinics being in the first half of the day may have resulted in an underrepresentation of individuals with eveningness or extreme eveningness preferences. The average CSM score of the study sample is positioned on the higher side of the morningness spectrum (mean CSM score: 37.74; SD: 6.43), which could have potentially influenced the study findings by including participants with better functioning and a likely better prognosis. The inclusion of dim-light melatonin onset could have strengthened the study assessment but was limited by practical considerations such as multiple, hourly blood samples from late evening onward till night time. A longitudinal study design could help to identify the causal relationships and trajectories and interrelationships of study variables over time. Finally, while EFA facilitated the data-driven identification of latent factors, it is important to acknowledge that this exploratory study requires validation in larger samples to establish robustness. Additionally, as with all factor analytic approaches, the extracted factors are influenced by the variables included in the model, and future studies should examine whether similar factor structures emerge in independent BD cohorts. To conclude, the study investigates the broader impact of various clinical and psychobehavioral factors on chronotype, offering insight on how these factors may predict the circadian functioning in BD. This study highlights the role of disrupted sleep, eating behaviors, and seasonality in influencing the chronotype and their interconnectedness in individuals with BD. The findings also contribute to understanding the phenotype associated with disruptions in biological rhythms and behavioral patterns.
