See: http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/ . Abstract Background A key target of the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals is to eliminate preventable deaths in newborns and children under 5. This study aimed to estimate the effect of time of birth on early neonatal mortality (ENM) and low Apgar scores at 5 min (LA5) in newborns. Methods A retrospective cohort study was conducted using vital statistics data on live births, maternal morbidity, congenital defects and perinatal mortality in Cauca-Colombia (2017–2021) excluding out-of-hospital, multiple and major defect cases. A directed acyclic graph was constructed to define the confounder adjustment set. Multivariable logistic, linear and propensity score models evaluated the effect of birth timing on neonatal outcomes, estimating crude and adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRa). Results We assessed 65 182 live births, finding similar baseline characteristics for daytime and night-time births. ENM was 0.2% (95% CI 0.19% to 0.26%) at 7 days of follow-up, absolute mortality difference 0.1% (95% CI –0.01% to 0.12%). Night-time births increased the incidence of ENM in the primary analysis IRRa 1.27 (95% CI 0.90 to 1.82), in the secondary IRRa 1.45 (95% CI 0.94 to 2.20), and in the primary and secondary sensitivity analysis, respectively, IRRa 1.48 (95% CI 1.06 to 2.07) and 1.70 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.59). LA5 was present in 0.7% (95% CI 0.60% to 0.72%) of birth, with absolute LA5 difference 0.1% (95% CI –0.02% to 0.22%). Night-time births increased the incidence of LA5 in the primary analysis IRRa 1.31 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.49), in the secondary IRRa 1.44 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.83), and in the primary and secondary sensitivity analysis, respectively, IRRa 1.31 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.59) and IRRa 1.54 (95% CI 1.23 to 1.92). Conclusions Birth at night-time is associated with worse neonatal outcomes, ENM and low Apgar scores in Colombia’s diverse population, highlighting the need for optimised prenatal care, revised work schedules and improved referral systems in maternal health. WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS This study quantified the impact of night-time births on adverse perinatal outcomes in a diverse Colombian population. It found consistent evidence that early and late night births increase the risk of low Apgar scores at 5 min by 31%–54% and early neonatal mortality by 48%–70%. Using real-world data, a predefined statistical approach and a directed acyclic diagram to control for confounders, the study provides robust insights into the association between night-time births and neonatal mortality. HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY Night-time births face systemic gaps in care, highlighting the need for equitable reforms to ensure timely, high-quality maternal and neonatal healthcare for all. Introduction Reducing infant and neonatal mortality is one of the sustainable development goals for improving maternal and child health worldwide. 1 During the last few years, the number of deaths in children younger than 5 years of age has decreased from more than 12 million in 1990 to 5 million in 2021. However, this reduction was inferior in the neonatal population, where mortality went from 5 million in 1990 to 2.3 million in 2021. 1 , 3 The neonatal period is considered a critical period in infant mortality. In 2021, of the total deaths in children under 5 years of age, 46% occurred in the first month of life and 75% of this percentage corresponded to early neonatal mortality (ENM) occurring during the first week of life. 2 4 Among the leading causes of neonatal mortality are preterm births, intrapartum events, sepsis and congenital defects. Despite the success in implementing several interventions that have substantially reduced neonatal mortality in the last decades, still 5% of neonatal deaths are due to unclear causes and specific factors such as sociodemographic particularities, the level of healthcare and some individual characteristics such as the time of birth. 4 5 Likewise, in addition to the mortality data, there are reports on perinatal morbidity, with the presence of low Apgar at birth being relevant as it is related to worse outcomes in neurodevelopment or other long-term processes. 5 The effect of time of birth on the occurrence of adverse perinatal events has been a topic of research since the 1970s; studies have focused on investigating perinatal outcomes considering the day of the week, time of year and time of day. Several publications report higher odds of early neonatal death when birth occurs at night. 5 , 10 Some of the factors that may be related to these worse perinatal outcomes at night include lack of specialised personnel, health personnel fatigue and institutional response capacity for emergency pregnancy. Given that there is uncertainty in the interpretation of the outcomes. 11 , 14 This study aimed to estimate the effect of time of birth on ENM and low Apgar in newborns in the Department of Cauca, Colombia. Methods Design We conducted a retrospective cohort study by using data from administrative sources. This manuscript is reported by following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Initiative 15 and the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data statement. 16 Data sources For this study, we considered all live births in the Department of Cauca, in Colombia. The department of Cauca is 1 of the 42 territorial entities in the country. It is located in southwestern Colombia and has boundaries with the Amazon rainforest in the south and with the Pacific Ocean in the northwest. It has a population of 1 464 488, with indigenous and black ethnic groups representing an important part of the population. 17 The data were taken from the registers of the live birth certificate (LBC) of the Departamento Administrativo Nacional de Estadísticas (DANE), and the databases perinatal and late neonatal mortality (code event 560), 18 congenital defects (code event 215), 19 and extreme maternal morbidity (code event 549), 20 provided by the Sistema Nacional de Vigilancia en Salud Pública of the Instituto Nacional de Salud, Colombia following a strict deidentification process ( online supplemental appendix 1 ). We used this population as broadly representative of all Colombian children, including those living in remote and rural areas and socioeconomic and ethnic diversity. 21 Eligibility criteria We included all live newborns with a DANE who had LBC including information from January 2017 to December 2021. We excluded children delivered out-of-hospital, from multiple gestations and with major congenital defects defined as those that represent a vital risk and require medical or surgical treatment. Since LBC did not provide all the required information for our analyses, we used three other source databases: (1) perinatal and late neonatal mortality database, (2) congenital defects database and (3) extreme maternal morbidity database to identify the live births with major congenital defects, children born to mothers with extreme maternal morbidity and newborns who died in the first 7 days. Measures Time of birth In Colombia, the time of birth is registered by the general practitioners or the attending obstetrician based on direct information and recorded in LBC. Considering the Colombian working day times in healthcare services, we dichotomised the time classifying time of birth into day (07:00–18:59 hours, Monday–Saturdays) and night (19:00–06:59 hours Monday to Saturday+Sundays+public holidays). Perinatal mortality The primary outcome measure of interest was perinatal mortality, defined as early neonatal death within 7 days postdelivery. Vital status and cause of death data are recorded by general practitioners or attending physicians and registered, and the information is filed in the perinatal and late neonatal mortality databases. Databases and information were matched by a process based on maternal identification. Neonatal morbidity The secondary outcome measure was a low Apgar, defined as an Apgar score of &lt;7 at 5 min. Apgar score data are recorded by general practitioners or attending physicians during birth and calculated based on direct clinical examination of the newborn. This information is also recorded in the LBC as part of the usual clinical record. Directed acyclic graph development and selection of confounders Potential confounders were selected by prespecifying a directed acyclic graph (DAG). 22 DAGs provide a transparent way for observational research to identify and demonstrate knowledge, theories and assumptions about the causal relationships between variables. 23 24 To construct the DAG, we extensively searched and reviewed the published literature of variables directly or indirectly influencing the exposure (time of birth) and, separately, the literature of variables influencing the outcome (ENM) ( online supplemental appendix 2 ). We identified additional variables to include on the DAG and determined the directionality of arrows connecting variables based on the existing scientific literature, theoretical expectations and our own subject matter expertise. Subsequently, we constructed a dependency graph of the relationships between these variables aiming to include all plausible confounding variables (ie, that may possibly cause both the exposure and the outcome), regardless of whether direct measurements were available or possible. 23 Furthermore, external knowledge and hypothesised variables related to the exposure, outcome or other variables were contributed by the entire research team and an independent expert professor in gynaecology and obstetrics in several meetings. These inputs were incorporated to achieve DAG saturation, with careful consideration given to the fact that removing an arrow from the DAG represents a stronger assumption in causal inference. 23 Therefore, we do not base decisions on statistical associations observed in our data. We used the DAGitty platform ( http://www.dagitty.net ). 22 Included DAGitty code is available in online supplemental appendix 2 . Primary statistical analyses The effect of interest was defined as the reduction in the absolute risk of undesirable outcomes, specifically neonatal morbidity (low Apgar scores at 5 min, LA5) and ENM, in newborns born at night (exposed group) compared with those born during the day (control group). First, we used descriptive statistics to describe the cohort, stratified by day vs night birth and performed a primary analysis using a dichotomous day/night exposure variable. We used multivariable logistic regression adjusted for the identified set of potential confounders (sufficient adjustment set) to assess the risk of time of birth and low Apgar and mortality. In addition, we used Quasi-Poisson and negative binomial regression models to address overdispersed data ( online supplemental appendix 3 ). 25 26 Estimating the effect Marginal structural models are a relatively new class of causal models in which the parameters are estimated through inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting (IPTW). We used marginal structural models to account for confounding. 27 Mathematical details of the modelling are described in the following subsections. In brief, we first estimated the population-average risk of poor outcomes in newborns born at night based on the observed proportion of poor outcomes among participants. We then estimated the population-average risk of poor outcomes in eligible families under the hypothetical intervention (birth at daytime) using a marginal structural logistic model, 28 assuming a linear relationship of the outcome ENM and low Apgar on the logistic scale conditional on baseline confounders. We expressed the effect of the intervention in both the absolute and relative scales respectively: The absolute risk difference was calculated as the difference in the estimated population-average risks in newborns under birth at night and daytime (average causal effect), whereas relative risk was the ratio of the estimated population-average risks in newborns under birth at night and daytime. We estimated the incidence rate ratios (IRR) and 95% CIs using Quasi-Poisson regression models. 25 26 Secondary statistical analyses Considering some publications reporting adverse outcomes at different times of the night, 6 7 9 we planned a secondary analysis using an exposure variable that classified time of birth into day (07:00–18:59 hours), night (whole night, 19:00–06:59 hours) and also night was divided into early night (19:00–23:59 hours) and late night (00:00–06:59 hours) from Monday to Sunday. In this secondary analysis, we also performed multivariable logistic regression, Quasi-Poisson and negative binomial regression models for overdispersed data for the association between time of birth and perinatal morbidity (low Apgar) and mortality ( online supplemental appendix 4 ). Sensitivity analysis We performed a sensitivity analysis with complete, trimming and imputed data to assess the consistency and robustness of results. We adjusted IPTW methods, and we estimated the IRRs and 95% CIs using Quasi-Poisson regression models ( onlinesupplemental appendices 3 4 ). Multiple imputation To reduce the risk of bias, we performed multiple imputation by chained equations. Results from multiple imputation analyses are shown for the primary, secondary and sensitivity analysis. Coding for imputation and analysis models is presented in onlinesupplemental appendices 3 4 . All statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.3.0. Coding for imputation and analysis models is presented in onlinesupplemental appendices 3 4 . 29 Patient and public involvement None. Results The source dataset consisted of 75 649 live newborns registered for the Cauca Department in the LBC of the DANE from 1 January 2017 to 31 December 2021. We excluded 26 live births with major congenital defects, 942 multiple gestations, 7415 delivered out-of-hospital and 22 duplicate observations. We included 67 244 births in the analysis ( figure 1 ). We found 2062 (3%) observations with missing data and thus, we ended up assessing 65 182 (97%) newborns with complete data. Births during the day and night had similar frequencies and baseline characteristics. 16% (n=10 434) of the population was indigenous and 8% (n=5280) was Afro-Colombian. Mothers had secondary schooling in 60% (n=39 269) and 81% (n=52 873) did not have the ability to pay, so they had access to health services through a subsidised health system offered by the government. Births were spontaneous in 64.4% of cases (n=41 986), the median gestational age was 39 weeks (IQR 38–40) and 6.4% (n=4173) of the newborns had low birth weight ( table 1 ). ENM of the entire cohort was 0.2% (95% CI 0.19% to 0.26%) at 7 days of follow-up; ENM for children born at night was 0.3% (95% CI 0.20% to 0.31%) versus children born during daytime 0.2% (95% CI 0.15% to 0.25%), an absolute mortality difference of 0.1% (95% CI −0.01% to 0.12%). LA5 were present in 0.7% (95% CI 0.60% to 0.72%). A low Apgar was reported in 0.7% (95% CI 0.62% to 0.80%) of infants born at night vs 0.6% (95% CI 0.52% to 0.70%) in infants born during daytime (95% CI 0.52% to 0.70%), an absolute low Apgar difference of 0.1% (95% CI −0.02% to 0.22%) ( table 1 ). The DAG ( online supplemental appendix 5 ) provides a visual description of the hypothetical aetiological context of birth time and ENM. The minimum adjustment set of variables identified and that we were able to analyse from our DAG included: socioeconomic status, birthplace (hospital attention level), mother’s education, mother’s age, stable partner, type of delivery, newborn sex, gestational age, prenatal care, low birth weight, extreme maternal morbidity and area of birth. Three variables identified in the DAG could not be retrieved from the available databases (onset of labour, previous abortion and patient preference). In the primary analysis, we found that the birth at night (19:00–06:59 hours) increased the adjusted IRR (IRRa) for ENM between 1.24 (95% CI 0.90 to 0.71) and 1.27 (95% CI 0.90 to 1.82). Sensitivity analysis showed similar results for mortality, IRRa between 1.24 (95% CI 0.90 to 1.71) and 1.48 (95% CI 1.06 to 2.07) ( table 2 ). Moreover, night-time births increased the incidence of LA5, IRRa 1.20 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.45) and 1.31 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.49). Sensitivity analysis also showed similar results for this outcome, IRRa 1.20 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.46) and 1.31 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.59) ( table 2 ). The secondary analysis showed that late night births had the highest risk of ENM with IRRa between 1.42 (95% CI 0.95 to 2.11) and 1.45 (95% CI 0.94 to 2.20). Sensitivity analysis showed that late night births consistently increased the incidence of ENM, IRRa between 1.42 (95% CI 0.97 to 2.08) and 1.70 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.59). In addition, late night births increased the incidence of LA5, IRRa between 1.40 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.75) and 1.44 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.83). Sensitivity analysis showed that late-night births consistently increased the incidence of LA5, IRRa between 1.40 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.76) and 1.54 (95% CI 1.23 to 1.92) ( tables3 4 ). These results were consistent among the five models estimated in each of the primary and secondary analyses and the sensitivity analysis. Discussion We quantified the effect of night and daytime births in ENM and LA5 in a Colombian population characterised by demographic, socioeconomic and ethnic diversity. We defined the effect of interest and identified confounding factors using a DAG. We found evidence that births during the early and late night increased between 31% and 54% the risk of presenting a LA5 and between 48% and 70% the risk of ENM; these results were consistent in the primary and secondary analysis through different statistical models. We evaluated neonatal morbidity using the LA5, which is associated with several neonatal complications and is included as part of the pragmatic criteria of the ‘neonatal near miss’ (NNM), concept defined as a neonate who had severe morbidity but survived this condition within the first 28 days of life. We hypothesised that NNM is also part of the spectrum of stillbirth-neonatal death, thus it is proposed to use this concept as a tool to increase the opportunities for evaluation of maternal and perinatal care. 30 31 The magnitude of the effect we found of the night-time birth on neonatal mortality is similar to most of the previous studies performed in both high and low-income and middle-income countries. 5 6 9 31 However, variations across studies in inclusion and exclusion criteria, time ranges and outcome measurement methods make it challenging to perform a detailed direct comparison of our results with previous studies. For instance, some studies did not measure the LA5, while others used a composite adverse perinatal outcome measure (eg, mortality, low Apgar Score, severe birth trauma, low cord pH, admission to a neonatal intensive care unit). 7 9 10 14 32 33 These results are a source of indirect evidence of neonatal morbidity and mortality, and other studies have also reported adverse maternal outcomes at night (eg, duration of caesarean section, prolonged postoperative hospitalisation and maternal mortality). 34 , 36 These adverse neonatal and maternal outcomes during the night suggest the plausibility of this association. Interpreting results from previous years, despite their consistency with our findings, is challenging due to the limited ability to identify causal pathways. This limitation arises from the constraints of observational study designs and multivariable analyses, where confounder selection was driven primarily by mathematical algorithms with minimal input from investigators and context. 5 , 1014 32 33 In response to this methodological challenge, we prespecified a set of potential confounders using a DAG constructed based on the investigator’s a priori knowledge. 23 The effect of time of birth at night seems to be a multifactorial factor for negative outcomes. Some causes that may contribute to its occurrence are the lower or slowed access to diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, lower availability of healthcare personnel qualified for critical maternal and neonatal situations which sometimes depend on the level of care provided by the hospital, staff fatigue among others. 9 10 36 The Cauca department in Colombia is a region with a high percentage of rurality and high multidimensional poverty index. About 80% of the population receives subsidies to access health services, and care by gynaecologists, anaesthesiologists, paediatricians and neonatologists is centralised in only two of the 42 municipalities. Also, there are reports of more births of children with very low birth weight in municipalities with less state presence and more exposed to the armed conflict, 37 and it remains one of the Colombian departments that exceeds the national perinatal mortality rate. The adverse perinatal outcomes presented in this study (increased the risk of presenting a LA5 and the risk of ENM in night-time births) are plausible considering the context of the vulnerable conditions of the population analysed. Our study has some methodological strengths to highlight. First, the use of population-based obligatory notification registries in a multiethnic region exposed to significant territorial and economic deprivation allows these results to have external validity to be considered in regions of the world with similar characteristics to ours. Second, we selected a minimal set of confounders through a transparent and explicit process using a DAG. 23 Third, the sources we used had a low proportion of missing data (3%). Among the limitations of this work, we should mention that our data comes from secondary sources and its quality may be questionable. The reliability of these records depends on the accurate completion of data by the professionals certifying the evaluated variables. This may introduce a potential misclassification bias for the time of birth and the morbidity outcomes, but it is considered a non-differential information bias. Another limitation is that despite having used a DAG to guide our analyses, it was not possible to have some variables mentioned in the minimum adjustment set, which generates probable residual or unmeasured confounding and limits a strong causal interpretation. Our findings can be used as academic contributions for organisational management in favour of a ‘high-quality health system’, distributed equitably throughout the population. 38 39 Optimising prenatal care programmes, counselling on emergency care when a pregnant woman is considered vulnerable, restructuring the work schedules of personnel qualified for maternal and perinatal care, providing solidity to the processes of emergency referral to higher levels of care and responding to the changing needs of the population. Lastly, we think further research may be needed to understand the causes behind the higher risk for negative outcomes of newborns born during early and late night in our context. This will help in implementing strategies to reduce this risk in future births. In conclusion, the evidence suggests that birth at night-time is associated with worse neonatal outcomes.
