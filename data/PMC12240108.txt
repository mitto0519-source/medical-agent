Objective Autistic adolescents and young adults (AYAs) are more likely to be obese than nonautistic peers. They are also more likely to be on psychiatric medications that can influence weight. The transition to adult health care is a challenge for autistic AYAs. The extent to which medications and transfer to adult care influence the weight of autistic youth is not well described. Study design We assessed changes in body mass index (BMI) over time among a cohort of 216 autistic AYAs who had transferred from a pediatric hospital to an adult primary care clinic for autistic AYAs. We compared the mean BMI before and after transfer and used linear regression with main effects and age-related interaction terms to evaluate the contribution of transfer and psychiatric medications on BMI over time. Results We found that mean BMI increased over time and that age and transfer to adult care both appeared to be drivers of BMI increases. We also found that psychiatric medications had complex effects on weight over time. For example, medications for attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder were associated with a lower BMI at age 16, but showed interaction with age such that each year of increased age beyond 16 mitigated the BMI decrease at age 16. Conclusions These findings highlight the importance of evaluating for changes in side effects of medicines over time, particularly during the transition to adult care and support other calls to address healthy eating and activity habits with autistic AYAs in adolescence. The transition and transfer to adult health care is associated with a number of challenges for autistic AYAs, such as disruption of support services, 4 an increase in unmet health care needs, 5 and increased use of the emergency department. 5 The extent to which these challenges influence weight is less well known. Another potential factor influencing weight trajectory in autistic AYAs are their medications. The relationship between antipsychotics and weight gain has been demonstrated. 3 Other psychiatric medications may also influence weight. For example, certain antidepressant medications, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) may also be associated with weight gain, 6 but the data on the impact of these medications on the weight of autistic youth are limited. Additionally, stimulants are generally associated with weight loss, 7 but their effect on weight in autistic AYAs is less well understood. In this study, we sought to explore the change in BMI among a cohort of autistic AYAs before and after the transfer to an adult provider and evaluate the impact of age, transfer to adult care, and psychiatric medications on BMI in a cohort of autistic AYAs. Methods Patient Population The patients included in this study had to meet the following eligibility criteria: (1) they had been seen at least once at the Center for Autism Services and Transition (CAST), a primary care–based program established to provide primary care to autistic adults in a way, that is, adapted to the unique needs of this population and with particular attention to their transition to adult care 8 , 9 and (2) they had at least one encounter at Nationwide Children's Hospital (NCH) prior to their first CAST appointment. NCH and CAST are located less than 15 miles apart, so many CAST patients have been referred to CAST by an NCH clinician. Thus, the CAST patients are a group well-suited for studying the transition and transfer to adult health care. Patients also had to have one BMI measurement recorded, which could be at NCH or CAST. Ethics Considerations This study was approved by the NCH Institutional Review Board with a waiver of consent due to the retrospective nature and minimal risk of the research. Data Set Formation To acquire the data set, the patients in CAST were cross-referenced with the historic patients at NCH, matching by name and date-of-birth by an honest broker (a data expert who was not part of the study team) who then provided a deidentified data set to the study team. As shown in Figure 1 , out of the 834 patients seen at CAST, 244 could be matched. For all matched patients, data from the electronic medical record from both institutions for the dates January 2011-May 2020 were pulled and linked, so that analyses could be performed across both institutions over time. Analyses were completed using this linked data set. Variables The variables used in this analysis were: ■ BMI: in children, “underweight” is defined as a BMI &lt;5th percentile for age and sex, normal BMI is a BMI measurement between the 5th and 85th percentile for age and sex, “overweight” is defined as having a BMI between the 85th and 95th percentile for age and sex, and “obese” is defined as having a BMI &gt;95th percentile for age and sex. In adults, “underweight” is defined as a BMI &lt;18.5, “normal” BMI is defined as BMI between 18.5 and 24.9, “overweight” is defined as having a BMI from 25.0 to 29.9, and “obese” is defined as having a BMI of 30 or more. 10 We limited analyses to those 16 and older and used the adult cutoffs for BMI to define underweight, normal, overweight, and obese. ■ Transfer to adult care: the first appointment in the CAST clinic was used to define the transfer to adult care. We included a dichotomous variable to indicate whether a patient had transferred to adult care or not. ■ Age: we documented age at the time of the first CAST appointment for the purpose of generating descriptive statistics. For the linear regression, where all data came from age 16 or older, we normalized age such that the age input into the regression was the number of years above age 16 for each patient at the time of each BMI measurement in the linear regression. For example, if someone had a BMI taken when they were 20 years old, then the normalized age for the linear regression would be 4 because 20 minus 16 is 4. ■ Psychiatric medications of interest: our data set contained all prescriptions sent for all eligible patients over the study period. As we have noted previously, 11 two of the study authors (C.H. and L.H.) worked together to classify all prescribed medications into 32 higher level groupings to ensure that the groupings were salient to autistic people. This was done in lieu of standard medication taxonomies because certain medications are used in off-label ways among autistic people, and we wanted to capture that in our medication classifications. In this particular analysis, we used the following groupings from the list to focus on psychiatric medications: 1) SSRI/SNRI included medications such as fluoxetine, citalopram, and venlafaxine; 2) Antipsychotic medications included both typical antipsychotics (such as haloperidol) and atypical antipsychotics (such as risperidone); 3) attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) medications included both stimulant treatments for ADHD (such as methylphenidate) and nonstimulant medications for ADHD (such as atomoxetine); and 4) other psychiatric medications included a wide array of medications, such as benzodiazepines, hydroxyzine, and naltrexone (used to help manage disruptive behavior in autistic people). 12 , 13 In addition, the following patient characteristics were determined in order to report the demographic information of the study population. We determined the race as reported in the electronic medical record and report that data. We also determined what type(s) of insurance each person had and classified them as public or private. Any given patient could be covered under more than one insurance type. None of the included participants had missing data for these variables. For medication groups, we categorized medications as prescribed or not. Statistical Analysis We assessed the relationship between changes in BMI and the transfer to adult care in 2 ways. First, among those with BMI data available both at NCH (pediatric data) and CAST (adult data) after the age of 16, we classified their last BMI measurement at NCH as being underweight, normal, overweight, or obese based on adult definitions. We then classified their first adult CAST BMI using the same categories to evaluate what movement among these categories was occurring during the transfer to adult health care. We also conducted an adjusted linear regression assessing the relationship between BMI (outcome variable) and transfer to adult care, as well as psychiatric medications prescribed, and age. This analysis was limited to BMI measurements completed at age 16 and over to reduce confounding from expected increase in BMI with growth in the adolescent years. Each individual BMI measurement was included, so we added a patient-specific intercept to account for the correlation among BMI measurements for each patient. To account for variation of medication prescriptions over time, we used varying time windows, meaning that the medication had to be prescribed within that time window to be considered as a medication that the patient was taking. The time windows we used were 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years. We then ran the regression at each time window. Initial analyses suggested a strong contribution from age, and so in our final analyses, we included interaction terms for age with all of the other independent variables to allow the effects to change over time with age. This allowed us to separately consider the absolute effect of each independent variable at age 16 and trends associated with each variable after the age of 16. Thus, the final adjusted linear regression equation is: BMI = Post _ transfer _ to _ adult _ care + Age + AntiPsy + ADHD + OtherPsy + SSRI / SNRI + Post _ transfer _ to _ adult _ care ∗ Age + AntiPsy ∗ Age + ADHD ∗ Age + OtherPsy ∗ Age + SSRI / SNRI ∗ Age + Patient _ Id Where Post_transfer_to_adult_care is the binary variable indicating if the patient had an adult encounter at CAST already at the time of the BMI measurement; Age is age in years since age 16; AntiPsy, ADHD, OtherPsy, and SSRI/SNRI are the medication binary variables; and Patient_Id is a patient specific intercept value. We also ran a sensitivity analysis, where we only included BMI measurements of those who had BMI data available both pre- and post-transfer to adult care, hereafter referred to as the paired data analysis. All regressions were run in R. Results Demographics As noted in Figure 1 , out of the 244 patients with matched data, 216 met the eligibility criteria, and 158 of those patients had BMI measurements both before and after their first adult care visit. The patients were mostly male (82.7%), mostly White (68.4%), and had a mean age of 19.8 at the time of the first CAST appointment ( Table I ). There was a total of 216 patients with at least one BMI measurement, and 158 of those patients had BMI measurements both before and after their first adult care visit. BMI Changes around the Time of Transfer As shown in Figure 2 , among those with BMI data both at NCH and CAST (n = 158), most either stayed in the same weight category during the transfer to adult care or went up a category. Linear Regression Results Table II shows the regression analysis for the 6-month time window that included all BMI data from age 16 onward. In that analysis, both transfer to adult care and increasing age after age 16 were again associated with an increase in BMI, and these associations were statistically significant. On average, BMI increased by 0.48 kg/m 2 ( P &lt; .001) for each 1-year increase in age before transfer and increased by 0.73 kg/m 2 ( P = .001) at the time of transfer. The interaction term for transfer and age was associated with a BMI decrease of 0.07 per year, which means that BMI only increased by 0.41 kg/m 2 ( P &lt; .001) for each 1-year increase in age after transfer. The annual rate of BMI increase after transfer was significantly different from the rate before transfer ( P &lt; .05). Antipsychotic and ADHD medications had a significant influence on BMI at age 16 and also had a significant influence on the annual increase in BMI after age 16 in the adjusted analyses. The 2 effects were not always in the same direction, highlighting the complex and dynamic way in which these factors appear to influence BMI. For example, for the antipsychotic medications, the effect of the medications at age 16 was to increase BMI by 0.84 kg/m 2 ( P &lt; .001), but this effect was mitigated as age increased because the annual change in BMI was a reduced by 0.18 kg/m 2 ( P &lt; .001) for these medications after age 16. ADHD medications were the opposite. For ADHD medications, the effect of the medication at age 16 was to decrease BMI by 1.63 kg/m 2 ( P &lt; .001), but this effect was mitigated as age increased because the annual change in BMI was increased by 0.38 kg/m 2 ( P &lt; .001) for these medications. The other psychiatric and SSRI/SNRI medication groups did not have a statistically significant effect on BMI at age 16 or with increases in age. The findings from the adjusted paired data analysis can also be found in Table II . The findings in the paired analysis were similar to those of the analysis including all BMI data. Results for the adjusted 1-month, 1-year, and 2-year windows can be found in the Appendix. Overall, the 1- and 2-year window results were similar to the 6-month window results. The 1-month results had similar directions of effect for all the included variables but smaller magnitudes of effect and fewer significant results. Discussion Our data suggest that, among autistic youth, BMI increases with age from the adolescent to young adult years Most patients for whom paired data were available stayed in the same weight category during the transition period to adult care, but the trend over time is concerning as it suggests that autistic AYAs who are currently at normal weight may move into the overweight or obese categories as they move further into adulthood. The linear regression results show that both age and transfer to adult care were also significantly associated with an increase in BMI, suggesting that both long-term habits and the disruptions that are occurring around the time of transfer may play a role in increasing BMI in this population. The linear regression results also suggested that the relationships between BMI and medications are complex and show variation with age. Antipsychotic medications and ADHD medications had 1 effect at age 16, but this was mitigated over time, as signified by interaction terms indicating that increases in BMI per year moved in the opposite direction. The findings here suggest that more effort is needed in the adolescent years (ages 13-18) to address healthy behaviors among autistic adolescents in order to try to prevent the steady increase in BMI during the years of late adolescence and early adulthood (ages 16-25) that was seen in this study. One meta-analysis found that autistic adolescents are less active than their typically developing peers. 14 That lower activity could explain our data which demonstrates excess weight gain. Providers caring for autistic adolescents should assess health behaviors of their patients. As our paired data show, autistic AYA may not move between BMI categories at the time of transition to adult care but helping young people establish healthy habits regarding food and exercise may mitigate the continued increase in BMI with increasing age noted in this study. Transfer to adult care was associated with an increase in BMI independent of the effect of age. This suggests that the known disruptions in support and health services that autistic AYAs experience 4 , 5 or other unknown factors may be leading to decreases in overall health for this population. We were not able to explore possible mechanisms for this association in this data set. Further work to understand the impacts on autistic AYAs of service disruption and other changes at the time of transfer to adult care is needed. At the same time, the rate of BMI increase was significantly lower after transfer to adult health care compared to the rate of BMI increase before transfer to adult health care, as indicated by the negative direction of the transfer∗age interaction term in the analysis, though the difference was small. There are a number of possible reasons for this. One possible reason is that patients are moving back to their pretransfer baseline over the 7-10 years after the transfer to adult care. In other words, the jump in BMI associated with transfer is being offset over time via a lowered BMI trajectory after transfer without any appreciable change in behavior. We also feel it is important to acknowledge that it is possible that the autistic AYAs or their families reached a point after transfer where they wanted to change their eating patterns or activity levels. While we know that autistic children and adults exhibit less healthy eating and exercise habits compared to nonautistic peers, 14 , 15 less is known about how the habits of autistic people themselves change over time. More data are needed to explore this. It is also possible that comorbid conditions associated with obesity, such as prediabetes, diabetes, hypertension, or joint pain, became more common and the autistic AYAs in the study opted to work on addressing these in part by losing weight or at least gaining less weight. We did not have a large enough sample size to fully explore this possibility. Nonetheless, this difference in rate of weight gain suggests that autistic AYAs can change the trajectory of their weight gain, and clinicians caring for autistic AYAs may want to explore ways they can support those efforts. The findings also show that medications play a complex role in weight changes among autistic AYAs. For example, ADHD medications did show an association with lower BMI at age 16, but that effect that appears to be partially mitigated with age, given the positive direction of the ADHD medications∗age interaction term. ADHD medications have known side effects of appetite suppression and concerns for weight loss in children, 7 and our findings of an association of ADHD medications and lower BMI at age 16 is consistent with this. Typically, in clinical care, if an ADHD medication is severely affecting appetite or growth, the medication is stopped. So, patients taking ADHD medications in this cohort may be skewed toward those who can tolerate the medication without those side effects, thus the relationship between ADHD medications and decreases in weight becomes less strong as the cohort moves into adulthood. Antipsychotic medications demonstrated a similar phenomenon, but in the opposite direction, where the medications were associated with an increased BMI at age 16, but this relationship to increased BMI was mitigated with increasing age after age 16. This is a somewhat unexpected finding, as antipsychotics have known associations with increased weight. 16 It is possible that the decreases noted are the result of stopping the medications in those who gained significant weight on the medicines. However, the varying time windows used to determine if someone was taking the medication reduces the chances of this. It is also possible that weight stabilizes and even resets back to a previous baseline in some people who are prescribed antipsychotics. Other studies have shown significant variability in response to antipsychotics in the treatment of schizophrenia. 17 Further analysis to assess for and understand the extent of such variability among autistic people is needed. Autistic individuals tend to have increased number of medications and polypharmacy as their age increases. 18 It may be tempting to assume that medication side effects will offset each other, but our data suggest that this may not be the case. Because the effect with increasing age is different from the primary effect of the medications at age 16, the relationship between the medications and weight is complex and likely difficult to fully predict in an individual. The current study focused on a limited number of factors that may contribute to BMI trends over time, namely mental health medications, transfer to adult care, age, and the interactions among these factors. A multitude of factors may contribute to BMI changes with age. 19 , 20 Additional research is needed to understand how these other factors contribute to BMI trends in autistic people and where trends for autistic people may be similar or different from neurotypical peers. This study has limitations. It represents a population of autistic AYAs transferring from one specific pediatric hospital to one specific primary care program, and so may not generalize to other populations. We also did not have sufficient sample sizes to distinguish between stimulant and nonstimulant treatments for ADHD. Lastly, the majority (90%) of the BMI data were collected prior to age 25, and so the model is specific to the age range of 16-25 and may not be applicable to ages outside this range. Conclusions This paper showed that both age and transfer to adult care are associated with increases in BMI among autistic AYAs as they transition to adult health care. It also identified a complex relationship between certain groups of psychiatric medications and BMI, with variations by age and differences between the primary effect of the medication at age 16 and the effect of the medication on the increase in BMI with increasing age. This study supports other calls to address physical activity and healthy eating with autistic AYAs to ensure that they have established the habits for health that will help them to mitigate the effects of other factors on weight over time.
