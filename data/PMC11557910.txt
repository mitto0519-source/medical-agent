Abstract Delayed marriage and childbirth often result in individuals becoming grandparents in middle and older age, coinciding with significant physical transitions. This study examines the relationship between the intensity of grandparental childcare, measured in terms of time-commitment over the last year, and relative handgrip strength among South Korea adults aged 45–84. Data from 3,742 participants with grandchildren were analyzed using the 2008–2010 waves of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging. After applying propensity score matching, a final sample of 286 participants was analyzed with multivariate logistic regression model. Results showed that grandparents who provided childcare for an average of less than 40 h per week (Odds Ratio [OR] = 3.62) were more likely to have lower handgrip strength compared to those without caregiving experience. Additionally, those over the age of 65 showed a similar trend (OR = 2.76). Conversely, grandparents with positive self-rated health were less likely to exhibit lower handgrip strength than those rating their health as moderate (OR = 0.28). Grandparents who provided care for more than half the year were also less likely to have lower handgrip strength than those who provided less frequent care (OR = 0.26). This study highlights grandparental childcare as both a vital family support mechanism and a factor influencing physical health. Findings on caregiving intensity, particularly the role of moderate weekly childcare hours and longer terms involvement, underscore the importance of aligning caregiving commitments with grandparents’ health perceptions and capacities. Developing guidelines for optimal childcare duration and recommending physical activities tailored to support grandparents’ well-being would further promote healthy aging within a family-centered caregiving system. Introduction Socioeconomic changes, such as women’s increased participation in the workforce, the growth of single-parent households, and extended working hours, have amplified the importance of grandparents’ role in childcare 1 , 2 . Although the intensity and nature of grandparental childcare vary significantly across cultures and nations, it is a universal phenomenon 3 . In the United States and Europe, 40–60% of grandparents provide childcare to support working parents or assume custody due to parental incapacity 4 . In East Asian countries, however, grandparental childcare is often regarded as an obligation rooted in Confucian cultural norms that emphasize family support and filial piety 5 , 6 . Recent demographic shifts, such as delayed childbirth and rising life expectancy, mean that grandparents often begin caregiving later in life. For example, between 1985 and 2011, the average age at which individuals in Canada first became grandparents increased by 10 years 2 . In Germany, this age has also risen, averaging an increase of three months per year from prewar to postwar cohorts 7 . As grandparents are beginning their caregiving roles at an older age, they are more likely to encounter age-associated health challenges 8 . In South Korea, several socioeconomic factors contribute to the growing reliance on grandparents for childcare. The discrepancy between the long working hours typical of dual-income households and the limited operating hours of daycare centers has created a gap in childcare coverage that grandparents frequently fill 9 . South Korean employees, often work longer hours than their counterparts in many other developed nations, making it challenging for parents to balance work and family responsibilities 10 . Grandparents step in to provide essential childcare support, helping families manage the demands of modern work schedules and facilitating work-life balance 11 . High educational demand in South Korea further increases reliance on grandparents for childcare. The highly competitive educational environment places early pressure on children to succeed, leading many parents to enroll them in after-school programs and enrichment activities even during their preschool and elementary years 12 , 13 . Grandparents often help bridge this gap by transporting children to and from these activities, overseeing homework, and providing additional learning support 11 . This involvement allows parents to maintain their work schedules while ensuring their children’s participation in academic and developmental activities 9 , 11 . Thus, grandparents in South Korea are not only essential caregivers but also trusted figures who provide stability and support in their grandchildren’s educational journeys 9 . Additionally, South Korea’s declining birth rate and rapidly aging population have shifted family dynamics, resulting in a larger pool of grandparents who are available to provide care 14 , 15 . However, these demographic changes also mean that grandparents are increasingly taking on caregiving responsibilities at older ages, when they may face age-related health challenges 8 , 16 . These socioeconomic and cultural factors place grandparents in a central caregiving role, sometimes with limited resources or preparation for the physical and psychological demands involved 17 . Given these factors, understanding the health implication of grandparental childcare is essential. For many grandparents, caregiving is not merely a responsibility but also a meaningful aspect of their identity, offering them purpose and a renewed perspective on aging 16 . Health impacts can be explained by two primary theories: role enhancement and role strain 3 . The role enhancement theory suggests that assuming diverse social roles, such as caregiving, could positively affect health by stimulating physical activity, enhancing familial bonds, and improving life satisfaction 18 , 19 . Conversely, the role strain theory posits that managing multiple responsibilities, especially with limited resources, could negatively impact self-rated health status 20 . Furthermore, such responsibilities may encroach on time for personal health-promoting behaviors, such as regular exercise and social interactions 21 . In the context of Korean culture, where caregiving is often viewed as an expected familial duty, the role strain may be compounded if grandparents prioritize caregiving over their own health needs 11 , 22 . This sense of duty, influenced by Confucian values, may lead grandparents to take on caregiving tasks regardless of their own health limitations, potentially intensifying the physical demand on their aging bodies. Studies on the relationship between the type of care grandparents give and their health have demonstrated mixed results. Psychological well-being has been found to be influenced by grandparental childcare and contingent on sociodemographic factors 23 . In addition, custodial grandparents face an increased risk of depression 24 . Conversely, moderate involvement in caregiving has been shown to result in enhanced well-being, with the optimal level of involvement being influenced by sociocultural context 3 . However, a significant knowledge gap remains regarding the association between childcare intensity, particularly the time commitment required, and physical health outcomes. Previous studies have primarily focused on caregiving status or self-reported physical conditions, yielding inconclusive results 6 , 21 . Handgrip strength, an objective measure of muscle strength, is an important indicator of physical health in older adults. Sarcopenia, an age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength, poses a significant challenge for older adults and is influenced by factors such as genetic predisposition, health conditions, and exercise habits 25 . The influence of caregiving responsibilities on muscle strength remains underexplored, yet caregiving may place an additional burden on muscle strength beyond these typical aging-related factors 26 . Caregiving responsibilities often require physical exertion, adding strain to older adults who may already experience age-related muscle loss 27 , 28 . Conversely, caregiving may provide opportunities to maintain or even strengthen muscle through physically demanding tasks, such as lifting, preparing meals, and accompanying children to activities 29 . Korean grandparents may experience a particularly high intensity of caregiving due to cultural expectations to fill these roles within the family, unlike in some Western countries where external childcare options may be more readily available. This study, therefore, examines how these caregiving demands may uniquely relate to handgrip strength in South Korean grandparents, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of caregiving’s health impacts. This study addresses a critical gap by exploring the relationship between grandparental caregiving and handgrip strength among middle-aged and older adults in South Korea. Specifically, we sought to determine whether and to what extent grandparental caregiving relates to handgrip strength. By focusing on this relationship in the South Korean context, where grandparents are integral to family structures due to unique cultural and socioeconomic factors, this research contributes a nuanced understanding of the health effects of grandparental childcare. Our findings aim to inform the development of age-friendly policies that support grandparents in their caregiving roles, thereby promoting healthier aging within family-centered caregiving systems. Results Participants’ general characteristics Table 1 summarizes the participants’ baseline characteristics. In total, 3,599 participants had no grandparental childcare experience (NE), while 143 had grandparental childcare experience (WE). After applying 1:1: propensity score matching, adjusting for sex, age, and the number of chronic illnesses to control for baseline health differences, 143 individuals were selected for the NE group. The majority of WE group reported no depression (88.8%), were socially active (83.9%), rated their health as moderate or better (81.8%), were nonsmokers (76.9%), exercised less than once per week (66.4%), were grandmothers (60.1%), were younger than 65 years old (58.7%), were nondrinkers (55.2%), and had more than nine years of education (51.7%). They typically lived in households with an average of three members and had more household assets than the NE group. In terms of the characteristics of grandparental childcare, the mean and standard deviation of average weekly hours spent on caregiving in the last year were 43.3 ± 38.3 h (part-time (&lt; 40 h/day), 57.3%; full-time (≥ 40 h/day), 42.7%), and the total weeks spent on caregiving in the last year were 19.6 ± 22.7 weeks (less than a year: 65.7%; entire year: 34.3%). The distribution of average weekly hours and total weeks spent caregiving in the last year, stratified by low and normal relative handgrip strength, are illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2 . In Fig. 1 , most participants with both normal and low relative handgrip strength spent less than 40 h per week on caregiving. However, a notable proportion of participants with low relative handgrip strength spent more than 40 h per week caring for grandchildren. In Fig. 2 , both the normal and the low handgrip strength group displayed two main caregiving patterns. A substantial proportion of participants in both groups provided care throughout the year, as indicated by the peak at 52 weeks. Additionally, some participants in both groups spent less than 20 weeks per year on caregiving. Association between grandparental childcare experience and relative handgrip strength Table 2 presents the association between having grandparental childcare experience and relative handgrip status. Although there was no statistical significance, respondents with grandparental childcare experience had higher odds of having a lower relative handgrip strength than those without childcare experience (odds ratio [OR]: 1.19, p = .694). However, those who had a positive self-rated health status (OR:0.28, p = .039) were less likely to exhibit lower handgrip strength. Conversely, individuals aged over 65 years (OR: 3.12, p = .011), were more likely to have higher odds of exhibiting lower handgrip strength. Association between the intensity of the most recent grandparental childcare and relative handgrip strength Table 3 presents the results of the association between the intensity of the most recent grandparental childcare and relative handgrip status. Part-time caregivers were significantly more likely to have lower handgrip strength compared to those with no caregiving experience (OR: 3.62, p = .032). In contrast, participants who provided caregiving for more than half year were significantly less likely to exhibit lower handgrip strength than those who provided care for less than a year (OR: 0.26, p-value = 0.031). Additionally, participants aged 65 and older had higher odds of having lower handgrip strength (OR: 2.76, p-value = 0.024). Conversely, individuals with a positive self-rated health status had significantly lower odds of having lower handgrip strength compared to those who assessed their health as moderate (OR: 0.28, p-value = 0.043). Discussion Using data from a representative national sample, this study provides novel insights into the relationship between grandparental childcare experience—including caregiving intensity based on time commitments—and relative handgrip strength among middle-aged and older adults in South Korea. Our findings suggest that the intensity of grandparental childcare, rather than caregiving alone, is associated with variations in handgrip strength, highlighting the significance of caregiving contexts. This study adds to the limited body of research on physical health outcomes linked to grandparental childcare by focusing on handgrip strength. Specifically, our findings reveal that participants who spent less than 40 h per week on childcare were more likely to exhibit weaker handgrip strength than those with no caregiving experience, supporting the role strain theory, which underscores the potential downsides of added responsibility 18 , 20 . Although caregiving may involve physical activity, the level of engagement seen in moderate weekly caregiving may not offset the cumulative strain and fatigue, especially for older adults more susceptible to muscle loss. Furthermore, these hours may limit opportunities for personal health-promoting activities like exercise or recovery, contributing to muscle weakness over time. Given our study’s focus on grandparents caring for grandchildren under the age of 10, a role likely to impose high physical demands even with shorter caregiving hours 6 . These results suggest a dual challenge for older grandparents: balancing caregiving roles with their own health management 18 . Future research should investigate the optimal balance in caregiving duration and intensity that maximizes health benefits while minimizing role strain, providing data to guide healthier caregiving practices. Conversely, those who provided childcare for more than half the year were less likely to have lower handgrip strength compared to those who provided care for a shorter duration. This finding implies that extended caregiving may offer adaptive benefits, potentially acting as routine physical activity. Tasks like lifting, carrying, and playing with young children can serve as light, consistent physical exertion, which may support muscle strength maintenance, especially among individuals who might otherwise lead more sedentary lives 17 , 30 . This aligns with research suggesting that regular, low-intensity physical activity helps preserve muscle function 30 . Additionally, longer-term caregiving might allow individuals to adapt to the physical demands over time, potentially providing a form of ongoing, low-intensity strength training that can help maintain handgrip strength 29 . Therefore, while moderate weekly caregiving hours may elevate the risk of muscle weakness, long-term caregiving may contribute to muscle maintenance, potentially through adaptive strengthening. Future research should investigate the specific types of caregiving tasks that may foster strength retention in older adults. Our findings also reveal that individuals with a positive self-rated health status are significantly less likely to have lower handgrip strength. Positive self-rated health is associated with greater physical and functional capacity, often reflecting an individual’s engagement in health-promoting behaviors like regular physical activity and balanced nutrition 1 , 31 . This finding aligns evidence that a positive health outlook enhances motivation for an active lifestyle, which may contribute to better physical health and resilience 29 . The sense of control linked to positive self-perception may act as a protective factor, reducing stress and fostering behaviors that support muscle health 32 . An individual’s sense of control has been studied as both a crucial precursor and outcome of the aging process 33 and a higher sense of control is associated with a lower risk of physical limitations, improved physical activity, and better self-rated health status 29 . Future research could further explore self-rated health as a mediator in the relationship between caregiving and physical strength, as well as specific behaviors that contribute to improved handgrip strength. This study has several limitations. First, our data lacked detail on specific caregiving activities, which may vary significantly in physical demands. Future research should identify particular activities that influence grandparents’ physical health. Second, although our study analyzed grandparental childcare experience across all participants, future studies should consider sex-specific analyses, as caregiving contexts and impacts may differ between grandfathers and grandmothers due to biological and sociocultural factors. Third, reliance on self-reported data for childcare involvement may introduce reporting bias. Fourth, while we used a nationally representative sample, the relatively small sample size suggests that further research with larger sample is warranted. Fifth, though our findings are grounded in the South Korean context, they may not be generalizable to other cultural setting with different caregiving norms. Lastly, the cross-sectional design of this study identifies association but no causation. Future longitudinal studies using fixed-effects models to track changes in caregiving patterns and their impact on handgrip strength would provide stronger evidence for causality. Regardless of these limitations, this study is one of the first to explore how caregiving intensity and duration specifically relate to handgrip strength in older Korean adults, providing a culturally grounded perspective on the health impacts of grandparental caregiving. Our focus on handgrip strength as an objective, clinically relevant indicator adds a new dimension to the understanding of physical health outcomes in older caregivers, with implications for developing culturally sensitive guidelines and age-friendly policies. Conclusions This study underscores the role of grandparental childcare as both a critical family support mechanism and a factor influencing grandparents’ physical health. Our findings, particularly those related to caregiving intensity, such as providing moderate weekly childcare hours or longer-term involvement, highlight the importance of aligning caregiving commitments with grandparents’ physical capacities to enhance their health. This study also emphasizes the need to moderate the intensity of grandparental childcare to prevent health challenges, such as physical strain and exhaustion, which may arise from overextension. With an aging population and increasing the reliance of grandparents for childcare, these findings hold implications for health policy and practice. Policymakers are encouraged to promote a balanced level of caregiving engagement that protects grandparents from excessive caregiving demands. Developing guidelines that specify optimal childcare duration and recommending physical activities tailored to improve grandparents’ well-being would further support healthy aging within a family-centered caregiving system. Methods Data source We used data from the 2008 to 2010 waves of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), a publicly available and accessible nationwide longitudinal survey dataset. The KLoSA tracks data on the demographic characteristics, physical and mental health status, health-related behaviors, familial structures, and economic activities of individuals aged 45 and older. It was launched in 2006, with a baseline sample of 10,254 participants selected through a multistage, stratified probability sampling design to ensure national representation. Participants From the 2006 wave onward, biennial follow-ups were conducted using computer-assisted personal interviews, which gathered detailed information regarding the hours that respondents spent on grandparental childcare per week. Of the 7,920 respondents in the 2010 wave, we excluded those without grandchildren ( n = 2,408), aged over 85 years ( n = 246), with a disability ( n = 66), and with lower handgrip strength, defined as the lowest 20% based on handgrip strength adjusted for body mass index and sex, at the 2008 wave ( n = 584). The rationale for excluding those with low handgrip strength was to reduce potential reverse causality and isolate the effects of grandparental childcare experience on handgrip strength. We also excluded individuals not followed up from the 2008 wave to the 2010 wave ( n = 654) and those with missing data ( n = 220). The final sample comprised 3,742 participants. Participants who reported having grandparental caregiving experience in the previous year in the 2010 survey were included in the case group. The control group consisted of participants who did not provide care for their grandchildren in the previous year. To investigate the relationship between grandparental caregiving and handgrip strength, we employed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM). This method matched case and control participants with similar propensity scores 34 , helping to minimize selection bias. We included sex, age, and the number of chronic illnesses in the propensity score model to control for baseline health differences. As a result, from the 3,599 individuals with no caregiving experience, 143 individuals were selected for the control group. In total, 286 individuals were included in the final analysis (143 caregivers and 143 non-caregivers). Measures Relative handgrip strength Previous studies have underscored the significance of handgrip strength as an indicator of overall muscular strength and fitness given its association with factors such as frailty, physical activity, and mortality 27 , 35 . We used handgrip strength data obtained via a handgrip dynamometer (model number: No. 6103, manufacturer: TANITA, Japan). Each participant sat with their elbow flexed at 90° and squeezed as hard as possible. If they had a hand injury or illness, no measurements were recorded. The mean value of two measurements from each hand was used. Relative handgrip strength accounts for the confounding impact of body mass on strength measurements and considers sex differences 36 . It aligns with laboratory-based methodologies and potentially enhances the prognostic value of handgrip strength 35 . Accordingly, we computed participants’ relative handgrip strength, adjusted for body mass index and sex 37 . Baseline handgrip strength was assessed in the 2008 wave to select participants with normal grip strength, and survey data from the 2010 wave were used to assess changes. Following the methodology of a previous study 38 , we divided body mass index into quartiles. The lowest 20% and the remaining 80% in each quartile were designated as low and normal handgrip strength, respectively. Ultimately, participants were grouped into either the normal or low grip strength categories. Grandparental childcare We assessed the influence of grandparental childcare from two perspectives: experience of grandparental childcare and the intensity of the most recent grandparental childcare. Grandparental childcare experience In the KLoSA, grandparental childcare experience was assessed using a single question: “ Did you care for any grandchildren under the age of 10 in the last year? ”. The status of having experience in grandparental childcare was categorized into two groups (yes/ no). We also categorized respondents dichotomously depending on whether they cared for more than one grandchild (yes/no). Intensity of the most recent grandparental childcare We determined the intensity of the most recent grandparental childcare undertaken by participants using two survey questions “On average, how many hours did you spend caring for each grandchild per week in the last year?” and “How many weeks have you spent caring for grandchildren in the last year?”. Based on this question, we assessed grandparental childcare intensity by calculating the daily and yearly time participants spent on childcare. For respondents caring for multiple grandchildren, we considered the maximum reported average weekly childcare hours. The intensity of respondents’ involvement was categorized based on the reported daily hours they spent caregiving per week into (1) a part-time caregiving group (1–39 h) and (2) a full-time caregiving group (≥ 40 h). We established a threshold of 40 h per week, corresponding to standard working hours 24 . In addition, caregivers were classified into two categories based on the duration of their caregiving in the last year: those who provided care for (1) less than 26 weeks (less than half of the year) and (2) more than 26 weeks (more than half of the year). This distinction was made as more than half of the caregivers reported providing grandparental childcare for a full year in the preceding year. Covariates Covariates encompassed demographic, health behavior, and health-related characteristics. Demographic details included sex (male/female), age (&lt; 65/≥65 years), educational level (&lt; 9/≥9 years), number of household occupants, and household assets, which were log-transformed and calculated as the total annual household assets divided by the square root of the number of household members. Health behavior aspects included smoking or drinking history (former or current experience/no experience) and frequency of regular exercise (&lt; 1/≥1 time per week). Social interaction levels were evaluated based on the frequency of social activities, such as religious events, gatherings with friends, or volunteer work (&lt; 1/≥1 time per week). Regarding health-related characteristics, we considered the total number of chronic illnesses diagnosed, such as hypertension, diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, chronic lung, liver, and prostate diseases. Psychological health status was assessed via the short form of the Korean version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. The scores ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A score of 4 was used as the threshold to indicate significant depressive symptoms, which demonstrated a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 92% 39 . Self-rated health status was categorized into two groups: good (ranging from fair to very good) and bad (ranging from bad to very bad). Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the general characteristics of the participants and to assess differences between the caregiving and non-caregiving groups. Analysis of variance and t-tests were employed for categorical and continuous variables, respectively, to compare characteristics based on grandparental childcare experience. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between grandparental childcare experience and lower handgrip strength. First, we investigated whether providing caregiving in the last year was related to lower handgrip strength after adjusting for all covariates. Additionally, we analyzed the relationship between the intensity of caregiving in the last year and handgrip strength, again adjusting for covariates. Statistical significance was defined as p -values of &lt; 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA (Statistics and Data) software Version 16.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, Texas, USA, URL: https://www.stata.com/stata-news/news35-1/updates-to-stata16/ ).
