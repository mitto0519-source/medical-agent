Background Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) is characterized by the presence of diffuse ground glass opacities and/or consolidation on chest CT. Emphysema has been reported occasionally. Nevertheless, the pathogenesis, incidence, imaging subtypes, and clinical impact of emphysema in patients with DAH are still unclear. Methods This was a retrospective clinical study. Children with DAH who were admitted to our hospital between January 2013 and December 2019 were included. All chest CT images of the patients were analyzed by two board-certified thoracic radiologists. The clinical features of the patients with diffuse emphysema were further analyzed. A matched case-control study was performed to explore the risk factors for developing diffuse emphysema. Results Ninety-four patients were included. Chest CT scans revealed emphysema in 28 patients (29.8%). Paraseptal emphysema ( n = 24) was the most common imaging subtype. The median interval between the onset and development of emphysema was 11.0 months ( n = 18, range: 2–113 months). Nine cases developed diffuse emphysema, in which six cases had persistent dyspnea and exercise intolerance, nine cases had persistent ground-glass opacities and 8 cases had a delay in initial treatment of 12 or more months. Compared to t hose in the matched control group, the interval between the onset and start of regular therapy with glucocorticoids was significantly longer in the cases with diffuse emphysema (median interval 13.5 vs. 1.5 months, P &lt; 0.01). Conclusions In cases with DAH, emphysema is a relatively common but ignored radiologic sign and diffuse emphysema seems to be a sign of irreversible lung function impairment. A delay in initial treatment is a risk factor for developing diffuse emphysema. A variety of etiologies, including immune and nonimmune disorders, can cause DAH [ 2 ]. Hemorrhage originates in the pulmonary microvasculature [ 1 ] and is pathologically characterized by intra-alveolar accumulation of hemosiderin-laden macrophages [ 3 , 4 ]. On chest computed tomography (CT), it usually presents as diffuse ground glass opacities and / or consolidation bilaterally [ 3 , 4 ]. Recently, Stainer A et al. reported the development of emphysema in three cases with DAH [ 5 ], which has received little attention in patients with DAH. Whereas, the incidence, imaging subtypes, and clinical impact of emphysema on a background of DAH are still unclear. Here, we performed a retrospective study to analyze emphysema in children with DAH and explored the clinical features and risk factors for diffuse emphysema. Emphysema is defined as abnormal airspace enlargement distal to the terminal bronchioles and accompanied by destruction of the alveolar walls [ 6 ]. It is being increasingly recognized in other interstitial lung diseases (ILDs), such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and ILD related to rheumatic diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis and ANCA-associated vasculitis) [ 5 , 7 ]. In this study, chest CT scans revealed emphysema in 29.8% of the cases with DAH, which demonstrated that emphysema was not uncommon in the children with DAH. In addition, we noticed that there were five cases who developed emphysema within a short period of time (≤ 3 months) in this study, unlike the cases reported by Stainer A et al. in which all three cases developed emphysema many years after onset [ 5 ]. This demonstrated that destruction of the alveolar walls can develop in the early course of DAH. According to the site at which the lung acinus is destroyed, emphysema can be classified as centrolobular, paraseptal, and panlobular [ 8 ]. Centrolobular emphysema is characterized by the destruction of respiratory bronchiole walls and surrounding alveolar structures; paraseptal emphysema is characterized by the destruction of the distal part of the lung acinus, and panlobular emphysema is classically described as diffuse uniform destruction of the alveolar and respiratory bronchiolar walls [ 6 ]. Usually, distinct subtypes of emphysema can be distinguished on chest CT [ 8 ]. In this study, various subtypes of emphysema were found in DAH cases, and paraseptal emphysema was the most common subtype. Interestingly, the majority of the cases developed one subtype of emphysema, demonstrating that the site at which the lung acinus was destroyed appeared to be the site of injury in the majority of cases with DAH. However, the underlying mechanism remains to be explored. Methods Diagnostic criteria for DAH DAH was diagnosed as the presence of respiratory symptoms (including dyspnea, cough, or hemoptysis), iron deficiency anemia, diffuse pulmonary infiltrates on chest CT, and hemosiderin-laden macrophages in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and / or lung tissue specimens [ 2 ]. Radiology methods Emphysema severity was independently assessed by two board-certified thoracic radiologists using a six-lobe visual scoring system. Lobar extents of emphysema were summed and divided by 6 to obtain a lung percentage of emphysema. The low-density shadows, reduced CT values, thinning of peripheral blood vessels, as well as thickened and enlarged hilar vessels are evaluated. CT protocols standardized acquisition parameters: 100–120 kVp, 70–120 mA, helical pitch (1.0-1.5), and slice thickness (1.0–1.25 mm). Images were reconstructed using iterative algorithms and analyzed at lung window settings (window width:1500 WW; window level:-450 WL). For the purposes of this study, a patient was defined as having emphysema if they had any related features on a CT. To assess interobserver agreement, ninety-four subjects underwent blinded duplicate evaluations using semi-quantitative scoring criteria. This dual-reading protocol enabled quantification of measurement variability through comparative analysis of their respective emphysema extent classifications. Study population and data collection Children with DAH who were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between January 2013 and December 2019 were included in this study. Patients aged &gt; 16 years on admission or without chest CT were excluded. All included chest CT scans underwent blinded, independent evaluation by two board-certified thoracic radiologists to: Confirm emphysema presence using standardized diagnostic criteria. Classify morphological subtypes (panlobular/centrilobular/paraseptal). The demographic data, clinical information, laboratory test results and lung biopsy findings of the patients with emphysema were collected. The clinical features of the patients with diffuse emphysema were further analyzed. A matched case-control study with matching for the following factors was performed to explore the risk factors for the development of diffuse emphysema in patients with DAH: age, sex and the interval between onset and the last chest CT assessment. Statistical analysis All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0). Continuous variables are reported as medians. Qualitative variables were reported as percentages. The Mann‒Whitney test was used for continuous variables. Results The incidence and imaging subtypes of emphysema in children with DAH A total of ninety-four cases were included in this study. The median age at onset was 44.5 months (range: 2–161 months). Thirty-six cases were males and fifty-eight cases were females (M: F ratio = 0.62). The median interval between disease onset and the last chest CT assessment was 33.0 months (range: 1–142 months). Chest CT scans revealed emphysema in 28 cases (29.8%). Among these cases with emphysema, the median age at onset was 37.5 months (range: 12–161 months). Sixteen cases were males and twelve were females (M: F ratio = 1.33). The median interval between disease onset and the last chest CT assessment was 51.0 months (range: 3–142 months). Nine cases had diffuse emphysema and nineteen cases had non-diffuse emphysema. Various imaging subtypes of emphysema, including centrolobular, paraseptal, and panlobular emphysema were found in DAH patients, among which paraseptal emphysema was the most common type and was found in twenty-four cases. Twenty-three cases developed one subtype of emphysema, including centrolobular emphysema in three cases, paraseptal emphysema in nineteen cases, and panlobular emphysema in one case. However, the remaining five cases had both centrolobular and paraseptal emphysema. The median interval between the onset of emphysema and the development of emphysema on chest CT was 11.0 months ( n = 18, range: 2 to 113 months), and five patients developed emphysema within a short period (≤ 3 months) after onset. These data were detailed in Table 1 ; Fig. 1 . Clinical features of the cases with diffuse emphysema In cases with diffuse emphysema, the median age at onset was 48.0 months (range: 17–99 months). Etiological diagnoses were established in three cases: systemic lupus erythematosus ( n = 1), isolated pulmonary capillaritis ( n = 1), and suspected juvenile idiopathic arthritis ( n = 1). Six cases demonstrated persistent dyspnea and exercise intolerance even in the absence of alveolar hemorrhage. In addition to diffuse emphysema, persistent ground-glass opacities were revealed on chest CT in all cases (Fig. 2 ). In the four cases that had undergone spirometry after developing diffuse emphysema, one case had small airway dysfunction (FEF25 81.1%, FEF50 57.3%, FEF75 29.0%, and MMEF 47.2%), and the remaining three cases had restrictive ventilatory dysfunction. Additionally, three cases had detected diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and lung volume were measured in three cases using the single breath (SB) method after they had developed diffuse emphysema. Decreased DLCO was found in all the cases, and elevated RV/TLC ratio (174.65%) was found in one case. Pulmonary hypertension (PH) was found in one case by echocardiography. Among the nine diffuse emphysema cases, 88.9% ( n = 8) demonstrated treatment initiation delays ≥ 12 months. Compared to the in the matched control group, the interval between the onset and start of regular therapy with glucocorticoids was significantly longer in the cases with diffuse emphysema (median interval 13.5 vs. 1.5 months; P &lt; 0.01). Pathological features of the cases with emphysema Two emphysema cases underwent thoracoscopic lung biopsy, with one demonstrating diffuse patterns and one non-diffuse morphology. Histopathological confirmation of emphysema was obtained in both cases (Fig. 3 ). The case with diffuse emphysema had inflammatory cell infiltration in the alveolar septa and was definitively diagnosed with pulmonary capillaritis (Fig. 3 ), whereas the case with non-diffuse emphysema had no features of capillaritis or vasculitis and was diagnosed with idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (IPH). Complementary transbronchial biopsy in the diffuse emphysema cohort ( n = 1). Pathologic diagnosis of pulmonary capillaritis was suspected due to the presence of inflammatory cell infiltration in the alveolar septa (Fig. 3 ). In addition, significant accumulation of hemosiderin-laden macrophages within alveolar lumens was found in both cases with diffuse emphysema (Fig. 3 ). Discussion DAH is a progressive pulmonary disorder characterized by alveolar space and interstitial involvement, frequently complicating with emphysema development and restrictive ventilatory impairment. In this study, Histopathological confirmation was otained in three emphysema-associated DAH cases. Among the three cases that underwent lung biopsy, two cases had inflammatory cell infiltration in the alveolar septa. Of these, one case was definitively diagnosed with pulmonary capillaritis, and another one was diagnosed with suspected pulmonary capillaritis. However, the third case had no features of capillaritis or vasculitis, which supported the diagnosis of IPH. Additionally, the lung biopsy findings revealed no features of capillaritis or vasculitis in one case of the three cases having been reported by Stainer A et al. [ 5 ]. Thus, in addition to the inflammatory reaction within capillaries and/or small vessels, other mechanisms likely contribute to emphysema development in DAH. Emphysema has been well described in IPF, a typical progressive fibrotic ILD in adults. This is a description of a proposed distinct entity named the combination of pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE) [ 9 , 10 ]. In IPF, a reduction in DLCO is related to a reduction in lung volume [ 11 ]. When CPFE occurs, a marked reduction in DLCO, but with normal or near normal lung volumes, can be revealed by pulmonary function testing (PFT) [ 11 , 12 ]. However, it seems to be unavailable for DAH, as the reduction in DLCO in patients with DAH may be mainly due to alveolar hemorrhage rather than a reduction in lung volume. Air trapping, a characteristic of emphysema, is usually characterized by an increased RV/TLC ratio [ 12 ], which may help clinicians recognize the emphysema, especially in patients with diffuse emphysema. We further analyzed the PFT results of the cases with diffuse emphysema in this study. Only three cases had detectable lung volumes after developing diffuse emphysema, in which one case had an increased RV/TLC. In addition, four cases underwent spirometry after developing diffuse emphysema. Although none of them had obstructive ventilatory dysfunction, one case had small airway dysfunction, which might reflect the development of diffuse emphysema. Recently, the prognosis of patients with DAH has improved significantly [ 13 , 14 ]. However, the long-term prognosis of patients with DAH is still difficult to assess due to the lack of prospective studies or large registries. The development of diffuse emphysema seems to be a sign of late-stage DAH [ 15 ]. Our findings were consistent with this observation. In this study, patients with diffuse emphysema had a high incidence of persistent respiratory symptoms of dyspnea as well as exercise intolerance, and all the cases had little or no improvement on chest CT over time due to the presence of persistent ground-glass opacities. Patients with diffuse emphysema were more likely to have irreversible lung function impairment, a sign of late-stage DAH [ 16 ]. Notably, lung biopsy revealed inflammatory cell infiltration as well as mild fibrosis in the alveolar septa and intra-alveolar accumulation of hemosiderin-laden macrophages in two cases with diffuse emphysema. The persistent ground-glass opacities on the chest CT may correlate with alveolar interstitial abnormalities and/or unabsorbed intra-alveolar hemosiderin-laden macrophages. In this study, we found that a delay in initial treatment was common in cases with diffuse emphysema, and the duration of initial treatment delay was longer in cases with diffuse emphysema than in cases in the matched control group, which demonstrated that a delay in initial treatment might be a risk factor for developing diffuse emphysema or irreversible lung function impairment in cases with DAH. Timely and appropriate treatment may be required to prevent disease progression. However, two key limitations warrant emphasis in interpreting these findings: Firstly, the limited cohort size reflects the orphan disease status of DAH-associated emphysema. Secondly, as a retrospective study, some clinical data were incomplete or missing in this study. Consequently, prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate these observations.
