Methods The diet of 200 volunteers was evaluated using a Food Frequency Questionnaire. The patients were further allocated to mild, moderate, and severe OSA groups. The relationships of OSA with diet were evaluated using multivariate linear regression analysis and the relationships of the energy supply ratio for three major nutrients and demand with sleep parameters were assessed using correlation analysis. Results The analysis of nutrient intake showed a reduced energy intake in non-OSA patients (11953.98 ± 7578.11 kJ, 95% CI 9677.27–14230.70 kJ) as compared to severe-OSA patients (15153.43 ± 6541.89 kJ, 95% CI 13678.46–16628.40 kJ) ( p &lt; 0.05). Similarly, lower intake of carbohydrates was observed in non-OSA (249.94 ± 134.96 g/day, 95% CI 209.39–290.48 g/day) as compared to severe OSA patients (348.57 ± 112.34 g/day, 95% CI 323.24–373.90 g/day) ( p &lt; 0.05). Compared with participants without OSA, we found that those with OSA had significantly poorer diets, with higher contents of rice (β = 0.195, p = 0.006), wheat (β = 0.236, p = 0.001), stuffed food items (β = 0.278, p &lt; 0.001), fried food (β = 0.193, p = 0.006), dairy products (β = 0.198, p = 0.005), and sweet beverages (β = 0.154, p = 0.030). After adjustment for age, sex, the presence of diabetes, and waist-hip ratio, these relationships remained. Furthermore, a higher proportion of energy intake in the form of carbohydrate was associated with more severe OSA. Conclusion The severity of OSA is related to the level of carbohydrate consumption. Poor dietary habits predispose toward body mass gain and a worsening of sleep-related parameters, which may accelerate the pathogenesis of OSA. Introduction Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a condition characterized by intermittent upper airway obstruction during sleep, which can cause sleep fragmentation and intermittent hypoxemia. Approximately one billion individuals are estimated to have OSA worldwide, and the number of patients with OSA in China has already reached 176 million [ 1 , 2 ]. According to the latest published review in JAMA by Gottlieb et al., the prevalence of symptomatic OSA is 15–50% in the general population [ 1 ]. It is now recognized to be a substantial problem, because of its close associations with obesity, cardiometabolic diseases, and mortality [ 3 – 5 ]. The pathogenesis of OSA is multifactorial and involves a complex interaction between anatomy, genetics, and cardiometabolic abnormalities, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance [ 6 – 9 ]. Given that obesity is a critical modifiable risk factor for OSA, the lifestyle of patients may affect its incidence and pathogenesis [ 3 ]. Patients with OSA have been reported to have frequent cravings for carbohydrates and to consume a larger amount of energy in the form of protein and fat [ 10 ]. However, there are few published data regarding the effects of dietary composition and quality on OSA, and there is a limited amount of evidence to suggest that diet influences apnea hypopnea index (AHI), and therefore the severity of OSA. Previous studies have shown that moderate-to-severe OSA is negatively associated with whole-grain intake and positively associated with a red/processed meat-rich diet [ 11 ]. According to Du et al., a higher frequency of consumption of fried food, salted food, offal, carbonate, and non-carbonated beverages is associated with a higher risk of OSA [ 12 ]. The mechanism whereby diet interacts with OSA is currently being investigated. Obesity, abnormal glucose homeostasis, and OSA have been reported to be linked [ 13 , 14 ]. Fat accumulation in the neck, tongue, and pharyngeal tissue may contribute to airway compromise in obesity [ 15 ], and excess body mass can also directly affect pulmonary dynamics [ 16 ]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and leptin resistance appear to play individual parts in the intricate cross-talk between metabolism and OSA [ 17 – 19 ]. Some previous studies have shown that changes in the central nervous system responses to food stimuli and the dysregulation of appetite hormones might be linked to sleep disorders, independent of obesity [ 11 , 20 ]. In addition, consuming foods that contain high concentrations of advanced glycation end-products may prompt the development of OSA via oxidative stress and inflammation [ 21 ]. The growing evidence for complex effects of diet on health (e.g. articles by Ismail, A. M. A. et al.) [ 22 – 25 ]. OSA involves an interaction between genetic and environmental factors, including dietary factors, but environmental factors are more likely to be manipulable for the prevention and treatment of OSA. Therefore, we aimed to analyze the relationship between diet and the severity of OSA in a group of patients at risk of OSA. Further investigation of the relationship between disease, patient and dietary factors may assist in the development of multidisciplinary treatments for OSA. Materials and methods Study design and participants Two hundred and twenty-five patients underwent a diagnostic investigation for OSA for the first time at the Sleep Disorders Center of Nanjing First Hospital from June to December 2020 and were recruited for this study. They had symptoms compatible with OSA, such as snoring, apnea during sleep, a high level of daytime sleepiness, and morning weariness; consumed a standard diet during the assessment period, and were willing to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were age &lt; 18 years or &gt; 80 years, a history of cancer, the presence of severe liver or kidney disease, pregnancy, lactation, active drug or alcohol abuse, and limited legal competence. After carefully checking every data by the exclusion criteria, 25 patients were excluded. The participants provided their written informed consent and the Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital, Nanjing Medical University, approved the study (KY20200409-15). Assessment of anthropometric parameters and polysomnography Anthropometric parameters, including body mass, height, neck circumference, waist circumference, and hip circumference were measured to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm. The values obtained were used to calculate the waist-hip ratio and body mass index (BMI). Overweight and obesity are defined as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that poses a health risk. BMI of more than 25 is considered overweight, and BMI of more than 30 is considered obese. A polysomnography (PSG) System (Philips Respironics, Murrayville, PA, USA) and Alice Sleepware Software (Philips Respironics, Murrayville, PA, USA) were used to diagnose OSA in Laboratory. PSG data with one night were analyzed manually by respiratory physiologists using the Irish Sleep Society/American Association of Sleep Medicine guidelines [ 26 ]. Obstructive apnea was defined as a reduction in air-flow to 90% of the baseline value for ≥ 10 s, measured using an oronasal sensor. Hypopnea was defined as a decline in air-flow of 30% from baseline, measured using a nasal cannula, for ≥ 10 s, accompanied by a 3% loss of oxygen saturation or arousal. The PSG parameters measured were AHI, minimal arterial oxygen saturation (MiniSaO 2 ), oxygen desaturation index (ODI), and the amount of time spent below 90% oxygen saturation (TS90%). AHI represents the total number of apnea and hypopnea episodes relative to the total sleep time. After PSG, the severity of OSA was also categorized using AHI (no OSA: &lt;5 events per hour, mild: 5–15 events per hour, moderate: 16–30 events per hour, and severe: &gt;30.0 events per hour). Nutritional assessment The participants completed a detailed multiple-choice Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) that evaluated their regular dietary intake over the preceding year. The FFQ provides valid and reproducible estimates of dietary intakes for various food groups among Chinese Healthy Adults according to the article by Qiumin Huang et al. [ 27 ]. The FFQ was used to assess the frequency of consumption of 22 common food groups: rice and its products (congee, rice noodles, etc.), wheat and its products (noodles, pancakes, bread, steamed buns, etc.), stuffed food items (stuffed food items are classic Chinese street food, featuring fluffy steamed buns filled with savory meat and vegetable fillings, such as steamed stuffed buns, wontons, dumplings, etc.), fried food (fried dough sticks, fried cakes, French fries, etc.), coarse food grains (brown rice, millet, corn, barley, oats, buckwheat, etc.), starch-containing tubers (sweet potatoes, potatoes, taro, yams, konjac, etc.), dairy products (milk, yogurt, powdered milk, etc.), eggs (chicken, duck, and goose eggs, etc.), red meat (beef, lamb, pork, etc.), poultry meat (chicken, duck, goose, etc.), processed meat (sausage, bacon, luncheon meat, ham, etc.), river fish (black carp, perch, silver carp, river prawns, etc.), marine products (hairtail, pomfret, yellow croaker, sea shrimp, etc.), legumes (soybeans, tofu, dried bean curd, soy milk, etc.), nuts (watermelon seeds, sunflower seeds, walnuts, peanuts, pistachios, etc.), dark-colored vegetables (green vegetables, spinach, water spinach, tomatoes, peppers, carrots, etc.), pale-colored vegetables (cabbage, radishes, cucumber), mushrooms (shitake, cap fungus, etc.), fruits (apples, pears, peaches, bananas, grapes, oranges, tangerines, watermelon, etc.), sweet beverages (cola, lemonade, iced black tea, sports drinks etc.), desserts (snacks, cakes, sweet bread, etc.), and alcohol (beer, rice wine, distilled spirits, red wine, etc.). Each participant was shown a series of photos of food items to help them recall their food intake. Nine defined options for the frequency of intake of each item were provided in advance: “never eat,” “&lt;1 time per month”, “1 to 3 times per month”, “1 to 2 times per week”, “3 to 4 times per week”, “5 to 6 times per week”, “1 time per day”, “2 times per day”, and “3 times per day”. Six defined options for the amount of each item consumed at one time were also provided in advance: “≤50 g”, “100 g”, “150 g”, “200 g”, “≥250 g”, and “not applicable”, for certain items, such as drinks, which were expressed as bottles. The amounts of selected nutrients (expressed in g) and total energy consumed were then estimated using Chinese Food Composition List. Statistical analysis Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 software (IBM, Inc, Armonk, NY, USA). Frequencies and percentages are used to summarize categorical data. The distribution of continuous data was tested using the one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and data are presented as mean with standard error and 95% confidence interval (95% CI), or median and interquartile ranges. Continuous datasets were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test, depending on whether they were normally distributed or not. Post-hoc comparisons were made using the Least Significant Difference test following one-way ANOVA and the Nemenyi test following the Kruskal-Wallis test. The relationship of OSA with total dietary intake was characterized using multivariate linear regression analysis after adjustments for factors including age and sex. The effect of the presence of diabetes was also accounted for, because this is linked to OSA and can affect dietary preferences and physical activity. Waist-hip ratio was also included in a further analysis to investigate the links between sleep apnea, diet, and obesity. The relationships of the energy contents and energy supply ratios for the three major nutrients and their demand with sleep parameters were also characterized using correlation analysis. A two-tailed p -value of less than 0.05 was regarded as representing statistical significance. Results We identified a total of 200 patients who matched the selection criteria, and of these, 173 were diagnosed as having OSA. Table 1 shows the demographic and biological characteristics of the participants. The 27 patients with an AHI of &lt; 5 were allocated to the OSA-negative group. As expected, there were significant differences in the AHI, MiniSaO 2 , and ODI of the two groups (all p &lt; 0.001, Table 2 ). The participants with normal AHI were younger than those with mild or moderate OSA ( p = 0.026, Table 1 ). Individuals with severe OSA were more likely to be male than those without OSA ( p = 0.014, Table 1 ). Participants with moderate or severe OSA had higher BMIs than those without OSA ( p &lt; 0.001, Table 1 ). Individuals with severe OSA had higher BMIs and greater neck, abdominal, and hip circumferences than those without severe OSA (all p &lt; 0.001, Table 1 ). However, there was no difference in the waist-hip ratios of the two groups ( p = 0.206, Table 1 ). Finally, the prevalences of hypertension in the moderate and severe OSA groups were significantly higher than that of the OSA-negative group ( p &lt; 0.001, Table 1 ). The Dietary Reference Intakes for Chinese Residents (2023 Edition) recommend that the energy ratio of carbohydrate to adults is 50-65%, the energy ratio of protein is 10-20%, and the energy ratio of fat is 20-30% [ 28 ]. The results showed that almost all participants reported nutrient energy ratio within the suggested valid ranges. However, analysis of nutrient intake showed a reduced energy intake in non-OSA patients (11953.98 ± 7578.11 kJ, 95% CI 9677.27–14230.70 kJ) as compared to severe-OSA patients (15153.43 ± 6541.89 kJ, 95% CI 13678.46–16628.40 kJ) ( p &lt; 0.05, Table 2 ). Similarly, lower intake of carbohydrates was observed in non-OSA (249.94 ± 134.96 g/day, 95% CI 209.39–290.48 g/day) as compared to severe OSA patients (348.57 ± 112.34 g/day, 95% CI 323.24–373.90 g/day) ( p &lt; 0.05, Table 2 ). Likewise, non-OSA patients had significantly lower intake of protein (133.68 ± 92.55 g/day, 95% CI 105.88–161.48 g/day) and fat (144.13 ± 107.38 g/day, 95% CI 111.87–176.39 g/day) as compared to severe-OSA patients (161.44 ± 85.58 g/day, 95% CI 142.15–180.74 g/day; 171.06 ± 97.73 g/day, 95% CI 149.02–193.09 g/day), respectively (both p &lt; 0.05, Table 2 ). There were little differences in fiber intake among groups ( p = 0.609 , Table 2 ). Unadjusted analyses showed that OSA was associated with higher intakes of rice (β = 0.195, p = 0.006), wheat (β = 0.236, p = 0.001), stuffed food items (β = 0.278, p &lt; 0.001), fried food (β = 0.193, p = 0.006), dairy products (β = 0.198, p = 0.005), and sweet beverages (β = 0.154, p = 0.030) (Table 3 ). In addition, analysis of the nutrient intake of the participants revealed that OSA was associated with higher carbohydrate intake (β = 0.197, p = 0.005) (Table 3 ). This association persisted when the data were adjusted for age, sex, and the presence or absence of diabetes. Further adjustment for waist-hip ratio did not affect the association of high carbohydrate intake with OSA (β = 0.238, p = 0.016, Table 3 ). Assessment of the relationships of energy and energy supply ratio for the three major nutrients with sleep parameters revealed significant relationships of the energy supply ratio for carbohydrate, extrapolated from the FFQ, with AHI ( p &lt; 0.001, r = 0.338), ODI ( p &lt; 0.001, r = 0.339), MiniSaO 2 ( p = 0.001, r = − 0.257) and TS90 ( p &lt; 0.001, r = 0.319) (Table 4 ). However, there were no significant relationships between the absolute energy values obtained from the questionnaire and sleep parameters. Discussion In the present study we have analyzed the relationships between dietary factors and the severity of OSA, described using AHI category. We found that compared with adults without OSA, those with OSA had a significantly poorer diet, characterized by greater consumption of rice, wheat, and their products; stuffed food items; fried food; and sweet beverages. These associations persisted after adjustment for age, sex, the presence or absence of diabetes, and waist-hip ratio. Furthermore, a high proportion of energy supplied in the form of carbohydrate was associated with more severe OSA. The participants with OSA had significantly higher BMIs than those without in the present study, and those with severe OSA had the highest BMIs, which suggests that patients with OSA are more likely to have obesity, as shown previously. Weight gain in Asian people appears to put them at greater risk of OSA than a similar gain in people of other ethnicities [ 3 ]. In addition, the mean AHI has been shown to increase by 19.7% in Chinese people, by 11.6% in white and black people, and by 10.5% in Hispanic people with sleep apnea for each 1 kg/m 2 increase in BMI [ 29 ]. Obesity has both genetic and environmental components to its etiology, and the consumption of a poor diet, featuring high energy and high fat contents, increases the prevalences of overweight and obesity, which are important risk factors for OSA. To date, there have been few studies regarding the diets of patients with OSA, despite this population requiring dietary guidance for the control of obesity. Therefore, we analyzed the dietary habits and composition of the diets consumed by a group of adults at risk of OSA, and found that individuals with OSA had mean daily energy intakes and intakes of carbohydrate, fat, and protein that were significantly higher than those of individuals without OSA. Further multifactorial analysis suggested that the poor diets of these individuals principally involved the excess consumption of sources of carbohydrate, such as rice, wheat products, stuffed food items, and sweet beverages. Therefore, a poor diet may increase the risk of OSA. According to Kechribari et al., OSA is associated with cereal grain intake, and high refined cereal intake may be a risk factor for more severe OSA [ 30 ]. In Western and Asian populations, refined carbohydrates, which are one of the primary sources of carbohydrate in a traditional Asian diet, have been linked to a higher risk of type 2 diabetes [ 31 ]. However, few studies have evaluated the relationships between specific sources of carbohydrate and the risk of OSA in Asian populations. In recent years, a new model of obesity has been proposed: the Carbohydrate-Insulin Model of obesity [ 32 ]. In this model, the consumption of a large quantity of carbohydrates causes an increase in insulin secretion, which promotes fat synthesis and storage, and may also induce hunger and therefore excess food consumption. A high-carbohydrate diet usually contains a large amount of refined starchy foods and added sugar. The resulting increase in blood glucose concentration induces postprandial hyperinsulinemia, which promotes the deposition of fat and increases body mass [ 33 ]. In addition, the long-term consumption of beverages containing a large amount of sugar promotes fat regeneration by increasing substrate utilization lipase activity [ 34 ]. Previous studies have suggested that links exist between OSA, obesity, and glucose dyshomeostasis [ 14 ]. It has been shown that a high carbohydrate/low fat diet is related to poor sleep quality compared to a balanced diet. Consuming meals with high glycemic index four hours before bedtime causes a shift in sleep onset and shortens the period before going to sleep, which is the reason behind the tryptophan surge after carbohydrate consumption [ 35 ]. In addition, insulin resistance, sympathetic tone, and systemic inflammation are upregulated during OSA-related intermittent hypoxia episodes, and diabetic neuropathy and hyperglycemia render the carotid body less susceptible to hypoxic stimulation, which predisposes toward OSA [ 36 ]. In the present study, we have provided further evidence of an association between sleep apnea and the consumption of carbohydrates, and specifically that substantial carbohydrate consumption represents a risk factor for more severe OSA. We found that OSA (AHI ⩾5 events/hour) was associated with greater consumption of fried food and dairy products. Fried foods are rich in fats and carbohydrate. Frying changes the nutritional composition of foods and the frying medium, with an accumulation of trans-fats and a decrease in the content of unsaturated fats [ 37 ]. High concentrations of saturated and trans-fatty acids are associated with increases in the expression and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 [ 38 ]. Consistent with the present findings, it was previously reported that the consumption of fried food more than once per week is associated with a higher risk of OSA [ 12 ]. Several studies have also shown positive associations between the consumption of fried food and waist circumference, weight gain, and obesity [ 39 – 41 ], all of which are risk factors for OSA. Frying also causes the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products in foods, which may also predispose toward OSA by inducing oxidative stress and inflammation [ 21 ]. There is little information available regarding the consumption of dairy products by patients with OSA [ 42 ]. Dairy products are rich in protein and fats. However, the various types of dairy products may have differing effects on cardiometabolic risk through contrasting effects on adiposity and lipid metabolism [ 43 ]. Thus, further epidemiological studies regarding the relationships between the consumption of specific types of dairy products and OSA are warranted. The adjustment of the present findings for waist-hip ratio did not affect the identified association between high carbohydrate intake and OSA, which implies that diet is linked to sleep apnea, independent of obesity. A large body of literature has linked sleep quality and duration with diet [ 44 ]. For instance, individuals who report poor sleep quality eat fewer vegetables, less fish, and more confectionery, sugar-sweetened beverages, energy drinks, and carbohydrates than those who report good sleep quality [ 45 , 46 ]. A previous study also showed that a single high-carbohydrate meal in the evening is associated with greater carbohydrate oxidation and less fat oxidation during sleep, and less slow-wave sleep than a single high-fat meal [ 47 ]. According to Reid et al., OSA and an unhealthy diet may also be linked through a reduction in N3 sleep [ 11 ]. Hedonic and hormonal mechanisms may at least in part mediate the relationship between insufficient sleep and diet [ 20 ], because a period of sleep restriction has been shown to enhance the processing of hedonic stimuli in the brain [ 48 ]. In addition, the hunger and satiety hormones ghrelin and leptin, which are linked to a greater propensity to consume food, have also been shown to be affected by poor sleep quality and a short duration of sleep [ 49 – 52 ]. The present study adds to the limited literature regarding this topic, but several limitations should be considered. First, because this was a cross-sectional study, inferences regarding causality cannot be made. Second, we cannot rule out the possibility that unmeasured or inaccurately measured confounding factors contributed to the associations identified. Third, the use of a predefined list of foods, varying perceptions of portion sizes, and the cognitive challenge involved in estimating the frequency of consumption of specific food items are the principal limitations associated with the use of FFQs. Furthermore, this is a study of a relatively small sample size conducted in a single center. Thus, we are willing to further investigate the dietary habits of patients with OSA in a multicenter-based study and will update our analysis result in a separate paper. We are also considering adding polysomnography data about body position, as these data can influence the results and conclusions [ 53 ]. Conclusion In the present study, we evaluated the dietary habits of patients with OSA, and found that they tended to consume a large amount of carbohydrates, including sweet beverages. OSA was also found to be associated with high levels of consumption of fried food and dairy products. A high proportion of energy consumption in the form of carbohydrates may predispose toward more severe OSA. These findings suggest that dietary behavior modification may represent a strategy for the prevention and/or treatment of OSA and its complications. In addition, dietary modification helps the control of obesity, which would have similar effects. Author contributions Ying Shi and Jun Xu contributed equally to this work.
