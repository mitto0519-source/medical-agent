Background Individuals with chronic kidney disease are at markedly increased risk of incident cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and premature death. The impact of social isolation on adverse outcomes in chronic kidney disease remained understudied. Methods This prospective cohort study included 13 090 individuals with chronic kidney disease from the UK Biobank. The exposure was social isolation categorized into 3 levels: least, moderately, and most isolated. Using accelerated failure time and restricted mean survival time analyses. Results Compared with being least isolated, moderate and most isolation are associated with the acceleration of all‐cause death with a time ratio of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.82–0.93; P &lt;0.001) and 0.76 (95% CI, 0.70–0.82; P &lt;0.001), respectively, and restricted mean survival time differences of 49.1 and 71.6 days within 10 years. We observed marginal association of moderate (time ratio, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.87–0.99]; P =0.039) and most isolation (time ratio, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.79–0.96]; P =0.005) with CVD. But the association was significant only for stroke, not for coronary artery disease. Based on pathways revealed by a directed acyclic graph constructed using the Bayesian network, we conducted inverse odds ratio–weighted mediation to decompose the total effect of social isolation on death, and found that CVD and sarcopenia mediated 21.8% (95% CI, 7.1–43.0) of the total effect of social isolation on death. Conclusions Social isolation is associated with shortened survival and accelerates the onset of CVD in individuals with chronic kidney disease. Assessment of Social Isolation For the current study, the identification of social isolation was based on self‐reported the questionnaires in UK Biobank, including 3 domains corresponding to 3 questions: (1) “Including yourself, how many people are living together in your household?” (data field 709; 1 point for living alone, and 0 for otherwise); (2) “How often do you visit friends or family or have them visit you?” (data field 1031; 1 point for &lt;1 friend or family visit per month, and 0 for otherwise); and (3) “Which of the following (sports club or gym, pub or social club, religious group, adult education class, other group activity, and none) do you attend once a week or more often?” (data field 6160; 1 point for not participating in any social activities at least weekly, and 0 for otherwise). This was reported to be similar to other indices of social isolation, including the Berkman–Syme social network index. 12 , 13 In line with previous studies evaluating the impact of social isolation, the sum of the responses to these 3 questions resulted in a score ranging from 0 to 3. Individuals who scored 0 were defined as being least isolated, who scored 1 as moderately isolated, and who scored 2 or 3 as most isolated. 12 , 13 , 14 Ascertainment of Health Outcomes The primary outcomes were all‐cause death and incident CVD. All‐cause death was identified through linkage to the death register. The identification of incident CVD was based on diagnosis or procedure codes indicating coronary artery disease and stroke, via linkage with hospital inpatient data and death registry, and self‐reported medical conditions (Table 1 ). 15 The secondary outcomes were cause‐specific death and incident coronary artery disease and stroke. We defined the incident coronary artery disease as having a diagnosis of myocardial infarction, presence of coronary bypass graft or coronary angioplasty implant and graft, or receiving procedures involving coronary revascularization (codes for International Classification of Diseases , Tenth Revision [ ICD‐10 ] and operative procedures were listed in Table 1 ). 15 We defined stroke as with diagnosis or procedures indicating the presence, evaluation or interventions of subarachnoid hemorrhage, intracerebral hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, specified cerebrovascular diseases, or the diagnosis of stroke per se (Table 1 ). 15 We identified the following cause‐specific mortality categories using the ICD‐10 : diseases of the circulatory system (I05–I89), neoplasms (C00–D48), diseases of the respiratory system (J09–J99), diseases and the digestive system (K20–K93); and other causes (except the aforementioned causes). The end of follow‐up was recorded as the date of health outcomes; the date of death; or November 30, 2022 (by which time the status of death was last updated for this study), whichever came first. Covariates and Construction of Directed Acyclic Graph We obtained demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and health‐related information collected at baseline. The detailed definitions for the potential covariates were listed in Table S1 . Briefly, covariates included age (continuous), sex (male or female), race or ethnicity (White or others (Due to the extremely limited number of individuals from other racial/ethnic groups in this study, the analysis only distinguished between the 'White' category and a combined 'Other Races/Ethnicities' group)), household income (&lt;₤18 000, ₤18 000 to ₤51 999, or ≥₤52 000), educational levels (college/university degree or others), employment status (employed or unemployed), Townsend deprivation index (upper, medium, or lower tertile), physical activity (low, moderate, and high level according to the International Physical Activity Questionnaire group defined by the UK Biobank), time spent on sedentary behaviors (using computer, watch TV, or driving; ≥5 hours per day or &lt;5 hours per day), diet (ideal or unhealthy for cardiac protection 15 ), smoking (current, previous, or never), alcohol consumption (excessive, moderate, or none 15 ), body mass index (overweight: ≥25 kg/m 2 , or ideal: &lt;25 kg/m 2 ), urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (≥3 or &lt;3 mg/g creatinine), and eGFR calculated from serum creatinine using Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (≥60 mL/min per 1.73 m 2 , 30 to &lt;60 mL/min per 1.73 m 2 , 15 to &lt;30 mL/min per 1.73 m 2 , or &lt;15 mL/min per 1.73 m 2 ). Hypertension was identified by inpatient diagnoses, self‐reported medical conditions and medications, and increased baseline systolic or diastolic blood pressure. Dyslipidemia was identified by increased level of total cholesterol (≥6.2 mmol/L) or low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (≥4.1 mmol/L) 16 or self‐report use of lipid‐reducing medication. Diabetes cases were identified by participants' diagnoses and self‐reported medical conditions and medications. Probable or confirmed sarcopenia was identified on the basis of decreased hand grip and low muscle mass. 17 Depression and diseases listed in the Charlson comorbidity index, including liver disease, peptic ulcer disease, pulmonary disease, and rheumatic disease, were identified via linkage to inpatient diagnoses (HIV and hemiplegia or paraplegia were not included due to the small number of cases). All missing values of the covariates were coded to an “unknown” category. To avoid incomplete adjustment or overadjustment for confounders, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) for the choice of a minimally sufficient set of confounders. A DAG uses the “backdoor criterion” to determine which variables should be controlled for when estimating the effect of one on another, and thus, can be viewed as a tool for identifying appropriate adjustment strategies in epidemiology analysis. 18 In the current study, the DAG was constructed by using the Bayesian network, a graphical model that allows representation of the probabilistic dependencies between a given set of variables (Data S1 ). 19 We used the R package “bnlearn” (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to estimate the Bayesian network using the hill‐climbing algorithm. 20 Applying nonparametric bootstrap (n=2000), we estimated the strength of the probabilistic relationships expressed by the arcs between covariates, and only those with strength confidence ≥0.7 and direction confidence ≥0.5 were kept. The DAG was plotted using the web application DAGitty ( https://dagitty.net/ ) (Figure S1 ). 21 Finally, for analyses of death, we adjusted for confounders including age, sex, race and ethnicity, educational level, depression, employment status, eGFR stage and Townsend deprivation index, while for analyses on CVD, age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, GFR stage, and smoking status were identified as confounders to be controlled for (Data S1 ). Statistical Analysis Primary Analyses Baseline characteristics were expressed as medium and interquartile range for continuous variables or percentage for categorical variables, and compared by Kruskal–Wallis tests or χ 2 tests, as appropriate, according to the status of social isolation. The adjusted cumulative incidence was graphically estimated by adjusted Kaplan–Meier curve using inverse probability weights. The weights were calculated from a multinomial logistic regression model with the status of social isolation as an outcome using the minimally sufficient set of confounders revealed by DAG as inputs. Considering prevention and delaying the onset of adverse outcomes is among the primary targets of CKD management, which underscores the time to adverse complications as an important, substitute outcome, 22 we conducted an accelerated failure time model to assess the potential impact of social isolation in terms of time ratio. Log‐logistic, exponential, and gompertz distributions were tested, and the log‐logistic accelerated failure time model was chosen on the basis of the minimum Akaike information criterion value. A variable with a time ratio &lt;1 is an accelerator factor, which is associated with the acceleration of the onset of events. In addition, based on the approach of the restricted mean survival time (RMST) analyses, which reflect the average duration of event‐free survival over a prespecified follow‐up period calculated as the area under the survival curve, 23 we compared the difference of RMST within a 10‐year time frame between moderate isolation, as well as most isolation, with least isolation. Schoenfeld's global test indicated the assumption of proportional hazards for Cox proportional hazard regression was not met; thus, Cox proportional hazard regression was not performed. Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses Subgroup analyses were conducted according to age (≥65 years and &lt;65 years), sex (male and female), the presence of diabetes, the presence of hypertension, and the level of GFR (normal and decreased GFR). We performed likelihood ratio tests to compare the model fit of models with and without an interaction term of sex, aged &gt;65 years, and the presence of diabetes, hypertension, and decreased GFR. We also performed the sensitivity analyses by excluding health outcomes occurring within 2 and 5 years of the baseline date to further avoid reserve causality, and conducting accelerated failure time model analyses adjusting all the potential covariates and the status of loneliness (defined as a positive answer to the question “Do you often feel lonely?” [data field: 2020]). Considering the potential bias that might arise from the missing‐indicator method, 24 we used multiple imputation to deal with missing data and used the subset of complete cases without missing information for analysis to assess the influence of missing data. Additionally, for the analysis of death as an outcome, we conducted the sensivitity analysis by not excluding individuals with CVD at baseline. For the analysis of CVD as an outcome, we performed competing risk survival analysis using death as a competing risk. Mediation Analyses For all‐cause death, based on the pathways identified by the DAG, we performed causal mediation analyses to decompose the total effect of social isolation into natural direct and indirect effects using the inverse odds ratio–weighted approach with the CMAverse R package. 25 The confounders considered in the mediation analyses included exposure–mediator, exposure–outcome, and mediator–outcome confounders identified in the DAG, all of which were not affected by the status of isolation in the current study (Data S1 ). 26 The weights were calculated with respect to the status of social isolation by performing multinomial logistic regression on mediators and confounders, and the outcome was regressed on social isolation and confounders using log‐logistic accelerated failure time model. The point estimate of each effect was obtained by imputing counterfactuals directly, and 95% CIs were obtained by bootstrap estimation with 5000 boots. All analyses were performed with the R software version 4.1.2. A 2‐sided P &lt;0.05 was considered statistically significant. 27 , 28 Results Of the 13 090 participants, the median age was 65 (interquartile range, 61–68) years, and 57% were women. Decreased eGFR was presented in 62% of participants. Among the study participants, 5896 (45%) were defined as the least isolated, 5497 (42%) were moderately isolated, and 1697 (13%) were the most isolated at baseline. The demographic characteristics and profiles of potential confounders stratified by the status of isolation are shown in Table 2 . Compared with individuals in the least isolated group, those being moderately or most isolated were less willing to report their educational qualifications and less likely to have a college degree; were less likely to be employed, to have a high household income, or to be physically active; and were more likely to have long sedentary hours and to be current or former smokers. They also had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, such as diabetes, depression, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, peptic ulcer, and liver diseases. Over a median follow‐up period of 12.6 years, a total of 2684 deaths and 3056 cases of incident CVD were observed, corresponding to an incidence of 16.3 and 19.0 per 1000 person‐years, respectively. The adjusted incidence rate of these adverse outcomes is presented in Figure 2 . Association Between Social Isolation and All‐Cause Death in Patients With CKD We first evaluated the association between individual domains of social isolation with all‐cause death. Living alone, lack of frequent visitors, and no social activities was associated with shortened survival at the time ratio of 0.82, 0.89, and 0.88, respectively (Table S2 ). When evaluated together, the accelerated failure time model generally revealed dose‐dependent association between the statuses of social isolation with all‐cause death. Compared with individuals being least isolated, the association between most isolation and adverse outcomes tended to be more pronounced than that of moderate isolation. Specifically, moderate and most isolation accelerated death by a factor of 0.86 (95% CI, 0.81–0.92; P &lt;0.001) and 0.74 (95% CI, 0.68–0.81; P &lt;0.001), respectively, indicating that social isolation may shorten the survival period by 14% to 26% on average (Figure 3 ). When evaluated in cause‐specific death, being moderate and most isolated was also associated with acceleration of deaths, except moderate isolation was not associated with the risk of death caused by digestive diseases and other diseases (Table 3 ). To assess the absolute decrease in event‐free time within a 10‐year window, we conducted an RMST analysis and found that, compared with individuals being least isolated, the most isolated group was associated with a decrease in survival by 72 days (95% CI, 34–109; P &lt;0.001). Moderate isolation, to a lesser extent, was associated with a decrease in survival by 49 days (95% CI, 26–73; P &lt;0.001; Table 4 ). Association Between Social Isolation and CVD in Patients With CKD When evaluated individually, only living alone was associated with shortened CVD‐free time, by a time ratio of 0.87; while lack of visitors and lack of social activities seemed not to influence the time of onset of CVD (Table S3 ). When evaluated together, as depicted in Figure 3 , we observed a marginal yet significant association of moderate (time ratio, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.85–0.98]; P =0.015) and most isolation (time ratio, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.76–0.93]; P =0.001) with incident CVD. In addition, we noticed that social isolation was associated with accelerated onset of stroke but not coronary artery disease (Table 3 ). RMST analysis showed that being most isolated was associated with a decrease in CVD‐free time by 78 days (95% CI, 21–108; P &lt;0.001), and being moderately isolated with a decrease in CVD‐free time by 55 days (95% CI, 27–82; P &lt;0.001; Table 4 ). Subgroup Analyses and Sensitivity Analyses We conducted subgroup analyses to explore the potential moderating factors that might influence the association between social isolation and adverse outcomes in patients with CKD. For death, as shown in Figure 3 , the time ratio for isolation was smaller in patients aged &lt;65 years, without hypertension, and with decreased eGFR compared with their corresponding counterparts, indicating a more profound effect of social isolation on accelerating deaths in these groups of patients (all with P for interaction &lt;0.01). It is noteworthy that, despite a lack of statistical significance for the interaction of sex in the assessment of time ratio, the negative effect of social isolation seemed weakened in women in terms of the absolute decrease in survival evaluated by RMST analysis. Regarding CVD, the time ratio was significant for most isolation in most subgroups (except those aged ≥65 years), while moderate isolation was significant only in participants aged &lt;65 years, men, or those with normal eGFR (Figure 3 ). All interactions between strata of these subgroups and isolation status did not reach statistical significance. When evaluated using the RMST model, social isolation was associated with reduced survival and CVD‐free time in most subgroup analyses (Table 4 ). Sensitivity analyses revealed similar results (Table S3 ), with both moderate and severe levels of social isolation being accelerating factors for all‐cause death. For CVD, severe social isolation consistently emerged as a significant risk factor across most sensitivity analyses. Moderate social isolation, however, showed a marginal association with incident CVD only in specific analyses: those using multiple imputation to handle missing data and those based on complete case data sets. Mediation Analyses We conducted inverse odds ratio–weighted mediation to further explore the pathways linking social isolation to all‐cause death. Through DAG (Figure S1 ), sarcopenia and CVD were identified as 2 potential mediators. When evaluated separately, CVD and sarcopenia individually mediated 16.0% (95% CI, 3.1–33.6; P =0.01) and 12.3% (95% CI, 3.7–24.7; P =0.01) of the total effect of social isolation on death, respectively (Table S4 ). DISCUSSION Patients with CKD are at a significantly increased risk for cardiovascular events and premature deaths, but less is known about the influence of social support on these adverse outcomes. Our study on 13 090 individuals with CKD revealed that social isolation is associated with shortened survival time in this population. A high isolated status may also accelerate the onset of CVD in patients with CKD. Additionally, through DAG derived from Bayesian network analysis, we identified sarcopenia and CVD as 2 potential mediation pathways linking social isolation to increased mortality risk. These findings hold crucial implications for the comprehensive management of patients with CKD, emphasizing the need to address modifiable factors from the perspective of social support, which may be potential targets for the prevention of adverse outcomes in individuals with CKD. Our findings highlight the negative impact of being isolated on the well‐being in CKD, a group of population with confirmed abnormalities in kidney structure or function, and usually with other comorbidities. These findings on the association between social isolation with adverse outcomes are generally consistent with previous studies, while previous studies were mainly conducted in the general population or older adults, and some in individuals diagnosed with cancer or CVD. For instance, a meta‐analysis of 90 cohort studies showed that social isolation was significantly associated with an increased risk of all‐cause death, but with substantial heterogeneity observed among the original studies. 5 In addition, social isolation, as evaluated by 3 aspects, including living alone, lack of social contact, and lack of participation in social activities, was reported to be associated with the risk of acute kidney injury. 29 Consistent with this, a study using the aforementioned 3 criteria, along with marital status and rural residence, identified a significant association between social isolation and the risk of rapid eGFR decline and the onset of CKD. 10 However, null findings have also been reported. A study based on 2 large UK prospective studies that included 938 559 participants found that social isolation was not associated with hospital admission for a first coronary heart disease or stroke event. 13 Similarly, the English Longitudinal Study of Aging, which measured social isolation using 6 indicators—including living alone, unemployment, lack of contact with children and other relatives outside the household, absence of friendships, and no involvement in organizations, religious groups, or committees—reported no association between social isolation and cardiovascular events. 30 In addition, a study with Korean participants found that social disengagement, quantified by the level of participation in various social activities, was not associated with death. 31 As mentioned above, the measurement tools of social isolation may differ among studies. However, generally, social isolation refers to an objective lack or limitation of social contact and a reduced social network size. In the current study based on the UK Biobank, we assessed the status of social isolation on the basis of 3 aspects, namely, living alone, lack of social contact, and lack of participation in social activities. This index has been used in previous studies and is similar to other indices of social isolation, including the Berkman–Syme social network index. 12 , 13 , 29 Our study extends the previous knowledge by several ways. First, to the best of our knowledge, we were among the first to indicate that social isolation still stands out as a risk factor for adverse outcomes, especially all‐cause and a range of cause‐specific death, in patients with CKD, who are already at significantly increased risk for premature death. Second, we explored the impact of individual domains of social isolation on the adverse outcomes, and found that living alone and lack of social activities may exert more profound influences on the all‐cause death, with living alone also showing a stronger influence on CVD. Third, we used the accelerated failure time model and RMST model to directly model the effect of social isolation on individuals' event‐free time, aligning with the goal of tertiary prevention of poor outcomes in CKD. We found that both moderate and most isolation accelerated the onset of all‐cause death and shortened the survival time by 14% and 26%, respectively. Of note, we observed that sex may be a moderating factor on the association between social isolation with death. The findings indicate that while isolation was not associated of shortened survival in women, being most isolated was associated with a difference of 147 days in survival in a 10‐year window when compared with individuals being least isolated in men with CKD. This is consistent with a previous study conducted in Korean population. 32 The phenomenon might be attributed to sex differences in patterns of social isolation over the life course 33 and the physical adaptation to stress, 34 as well as self‐care abilities. 35 The clinical implications of these findings for the management of patients with CKD are substantial. Health care professionals should be aware of the potential impact of social isolation. Assessment of social supports in routine evaluations and interventions aiming at reducing social isolation can be considered as part of comprehensive CKD care to delay or prevent these severe complications. The underlying mechanisms for the association between social isolation with all‐cause death and CVD remain inconclusive. Social support can serve as a stress buffer, and stress is thought to influence health by promoting detrimental behaviors and by prolonged activation of physiological systems such as the sympathetic nervous system and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal cortical axis. 35 Sam Hodgson and colleagues synthesized literature and proposed a conceptual framework for the link between social isolation and CVD and death. The contributing factors included physiological (eg, inflammation, neuroendocrine axis), psychological (eg, cognitive decline, suicide risk), behavioral (eg, dietary choices, smoking), and sociological (eg, health literacy, social roles) factors. 8 In a recent study conducted in 160 patients with end‐stage kidney disease receiving maintenance hemodialysis, a higher level of perceived social support was correlated with a lower level of fatigue, depressive symptoms, and better sleep quality. 36 These may also influence the prognosis of patients, as psychological and physical well‐being is closely interlinked. Other possible explanations include underexpression of genes to exacerbate inflammation, 37 oxidative stress, 38 increased blood pressure, 39 and higher vascular resistance. 40 Health‐promoting behavior such as appropriate health service use and medical adherence was also proposed as a potential pathway. 41 In the current study, we found that the magnitude of impact of living alone was more profound than lack of social contact or social activities. The finding was consistent with previous reports that living alone may be associated with increased risk of falls 42 and untimely resuscitation during emergencies, such as cardiac arrest. 43 In addition, except for CVD, the current study identified sarcopenia as an additional mediator for the link between social isolation and death in patients with CKD. The major strength of our study included the use of data from the UK Biobank, which collected detailed data on social contact, social support, and other lifestyle factors; prospectively followed up the participants of &gt;10 years; and provided health outcomes obtained through linkage to hospital inpatient records and death registry. In addition, a DAG was derived on the basis of the Bayesian network, so that we could adjust for confounders appropriately, neither over‐ nor underadjustment, in survival analyses. Furthermore, by applying DAG, we were also able to identify potential pathways linking social isolation to all‐cause death. There are also limitations of the study. First, the evaluation of social isolation was based on a self‐reported questionnaire at baseline. The information on the duration of social isolation, as well as the change of status of social isolation, which might influence the health outcomes, 44 cannot be obtained. Despite the fact that previous studies indicated that loneliness remained essentially stable from adolescence to oldest old age, 45 and the percentage of persistent and incident social isolation was higher than transient isolation, 44 future studies on the effect of change in social isolation on subsequent adverse outcomes in patients with CKD are warranted. Passive sensing techniques and ecological momentary assessment for the detection and measurement of social isolation may facilitate this type of research. 46 , 47 Second, despite the fact that the UK Biobank collected extensive data on health and lifestyle factors, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. This might influence the construction of DAG and controlling for confounders in survival analyses. Here, we used the accelerated failure time model, which is more robust to covariate omission than Cox proportional hazard regression. 48 Third, observational studies do not suffice to support causality. In addition, the mechanisms of how social isolation affects the health of individuals are complex and multidimensional. More evidence of this causal relationship and related explanations is needed. Fourth, regarding external generalizability, our study included participants with CKD, aged 40 to 71 years, from the UK Biobank. Given that the UK Biobank sampling population is volunteer based and not representative of the broader population, this may influence the generalizability of our findings. Also, we defined CVD outcomes on the basis of coronary artery disease and stroke, aligning with several previous studies. The impact of social isolation on other CVDs merits further investigation. Fifth, we noticed that the percentage of participants with missing values for confounders was not balanced across isolation statuses. Thus, in addition to using the missing‐indicator method in the primary analyses, as used in some studies leveraging UK Biobank data, we also performed multiple imputation and complete‐case analyses. Despite these efforts, the potential for bias could not be entirely excluded. In conclusion, based on the prospective cohort of 13 090 participants, being socially isolated is associated with shortened survival in individuals with CKD. Additionally, a high level of social isolation accelerates the onset of CVD in this population. Sarcopenia and CVD are potential mediators for the link between social isolation and increased risk of death. Such findings underscore the necessity for the assessment of isolation status and the intervention to improve social support in the comprehensive management of CKD.
