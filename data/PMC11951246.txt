ABSTRACT Background Evidence‐based practice (EBP) is an approach that integrates the best evidence from research. It combines four concepts: patient preferences and values, clinical expertise, available resources, and the best evidence. Models exist and allow for its implementation, and the mentor appears as a facilitator. Objectives To explore the mentor's role and its impact on nursing staff. Methods Inclusion criteria included sources that report clinical setting, EBP context, nurse, mentor, and related terms. We searched for published and unpublished studies and reports without any language and date limit. The search was conducted on January 25, 2024. Databases searched included MEDLINE, CINAHL, COCHRANE Library, Embase, Emcare, Google Scholar, Web of science, JBI EBP Database, and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses. The search strategy identified a total of 3195 studies and reports. The relevant data were extracted by two reviewers as recommended by the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis. Results Seventy studies and reports were included in this scoping review. The mentors are mostly advanced practice nurses (APNs) who have completed EBP training. Mentorship programs aim to instill a spirit of inquiry and improve EBP skills of future mentors. As interprofessionality appears as a facilitator, a lack of time and resources is the main barrier. Mentors help teams improve their research skills in different ways to develop a research culture in a sustainable way and significantly improve beliefs and implementation. Linking Evidence to Action This study demonstrated the interest of the mentor l working in interprofessionality while establishing a close link between the clinical environment and the university to combine the skills and resources of the two fields. The implementation of a mentoring program appears to be a necessity to train nurses in EBP daily. An investment in mentoring makes it possible to improve the quality of care, but also the quality of the work environment. APNs and managers must take on this leadership role to drive this culture of research and practice improvement. Managers must free up time for mentors in order to be able to support and carry out EBP projects. As mentors, they are expected to communicate about ongoing research projects, whether through conferences, congresses, informal, or formal meetings. champion clinical setting evidence‐based practice mentor nurse Any disagreement between reviewers (JA and JM) at each stage of the selection process was resolved by discussion with a senior researcher (ACA). 2.5 Data Extraction Data extraction was performed by two independent reviewers (JA and JM) using two tables. There were no disagreements between the two reviewers. 3 Results 3.1 Inclusion of Studies The database search was conducted on January 25, 2024, identifying 491 articles from MEDLINE (Ovid), 831 articles from CINAHL (EBSCOhost), 1444 articles from EMBASE, 77 articles from COCHRANE Library, 1066 articles from Web of Science, 19 articles from Google Scholar, 156 theses and dissertations from ProQuest Dissertations and Theses, 68 articles from JBI EBP Database (Ovid), and 776 articles from Emcare (Ovid), totaling 4928 articles. A total of 1733 duplicates were excluded, leaving 3195 articles to be screened by title and abstract. Three thousand and seventy‐seven articles were excluded based on title and abstract. Of the 118 full‐text articles, 70 met the selection criteria and were included in the final analysis. The selection details and results were presented in the flow diagram (Figure 1 ). 3.2 Characteristics of Selected Studies The characteristics of included studies were recorded in Table 1 . The included sources were published between 2001 and 2023 in 11 different countries (Table 3 ), with the majority of studies in 2020 (10/70). Of the 70 selected articles, 78.6% were conducted in the United States (55/70), 4.3% in Canada (3/70), 2.9% in Finland (2/70), 2.9% in Australia (2/70), 2.9% in Singapore (2/70), 1.4% in Switzerland (1/70), 1.4% in Iran (1/70), 1.4% in Turkey (1/70), 1.4% in Korea (1/70), 1.4% in China (1/70), and 1.4% in Ireland (1/70). All the included studies were written in English (70/70, 100%). Most articles were quantitative studies (33.3%, 23/70), followed by qualitative studies (27.5%, 19/70), mixed‐method studies (20.3%, 14/70), editorials (5.8%, 4/70), theses (4.3%, 3/70), reports (4.3%, 3/70), expert opinions (4.3%, 3/70) and pilot study (1,4%, 1/70). Upon analysis, 4 distinct themes emerged from the data: (1) Work, (2) Training, (3) Environment, and (4) Benefits. A comprehensive overview of these themes, the categories, and subcategories has been realized and can be seen in Figure 3 . In addition, a figure of which countries/settings accounted for which themes has been created (Figure 4 ). The results were categorized according to the different themes found during the extraction of the data from each article. 3.3 Population The populations targeted in the articles were primarily nurses, mentioned in 90% of articles (63/70), healthcare leaders (10.1%, 7/70), advanced practice nurses (APNs) (8.6%, 6/70), nursing instructors (4.3%, 3/70), and faculty nurses. 3.4 Context All included studies or articles were conducted in hospitals or healthcare organization settings (100%, 70/70), and some articles were associated with universities (8.6%, 6/70). 3.5 Nursing Profession and Specialties of the EBP Mentor Professionals who most commonly assume the role of EBP mentor are nurses with EBP experience (50%, 35/70) and APNs (41.4%, 29/70). Other roles include nurse managers (11.4%, 8/70), nurse educators (11.4%, 8/70), faculty nurses (7.1%, 5/70), doctorally prepared researchers (7.1%, 5/70), nurse scientists (7.1%, 5/70), administrative coordinators (1.4%, 1/70), and nurse researchers (1.4%, 1/70). The study showed that the mentor role was more often assumed by APNs. This can be explained by their deeper clinical expertise (an average of 15 years), leadership, and the research skills they developed through EBP mentorship programs (Ylimäki et al. 2022 ). The role of mentor requires experience and research skills that novice nurses do not have. The academic environment has specialized nurses who are able to take on the role of guide in the research process. These nurses typically have completed training that includes programs on research, EBP, and leadership (Wang et al. 2021 ). 3.6 Role and Activities of the EBP Mentor The majority of selected studies highlight training (62.8%, 44/70), support (33.3%, 23/70), guidance (33.3%, 23/70), and mentorship (29%, 20/70) as central activities of the EBP mentor. The mentor trains nurses in EBP, typically through coaching sessions, workshops, monthly journal clubs, participation in mentoring programs, and sharing EBP experiences (Aitken et al. 2011 ; Alves 2021b ; Breckenridge‐Sproat et al. 2015 ; Chan et al. 2020 ; Ecoff 2009 ; Ferren et al. 2022 ; Melnyk et al. 2022 ). Three studies principally highlighted that support is an integral part of the EBP mentor's activities, providing necessary resources for EBP practice and using research as a facilitator for practice (Levin et al. 2011 ; Shrout and Gallagher‐Ford 2021 ; Sweetapple 2015 ). Mentors save time on EBP processes and assist in completing ongoing projects while managing conflicts between research evidence and clinical practice (Farokhzadian et al. 2015 ; Friesen et al. 2017 ; Innis et al. 2015 ; Jeffers et al. 2008 ; Lunden et al. 2021 ; Newhouse et al. 2005 ; Oh and Yoo 2020 ). Thus, the mentor contributes to EBP project implementation and evaluation. Their research activities allow them to identify the facilitators and barriers to implementing EBP and work with teams on guidelines and best practices culture (Melnyk, Bullock, et al. 2010 ; Ryder et al. 2020 ; Schuler et al. 2020 ; White 2011 ). To implement evaluations, strategies such as audits or regular monitoring of practices are used (Oh and Yoo 2020 ; Ploeg et al. 2010 ). Some articles (14.2%, 10/70) highlight the leadership role the EBP mentor assumes, particularly in clinical practice and within interdisciplinary workgroups around EBP project implementation (Levin et al. 2011 ; Ryder et al. 2020 ; Smith et al. 2009 ; White 2011 ). The mentor emphasizes communication about EBP initiatives, project results, successes, encountered obstacles, and possible identified solutions. They foster communication with peers and across professions to disseminate information and the importance of EBP. Participation in conferences, congresses, or even meetings is essential to promote the EBP culture and share project results through scientific posters or professional summaries. Promoting EBP project outcomes also highlights participants' acquired skills and direct patient outcomes (Debourgh 2001 ; Ferren et al. 2022 ; Magers 2014 ; Schuler et al. 2020 ). Mentors have significant knowledge of the organization's functioning. They promote an EBP organizational culture to facilitate its implementation by adapting guidelines emerging from evidence to the organization's specific needs (Green et al. 2014 ; Kim et al. 2017b ). 3.7 Training and Programs of EBP Mentors Most studies addressing mentor training report the interest in a mentoring program (28.6%, 20/70) taking place over several months. The structure of the program varies, often starting with workshops and courses on EBP, its process, and steps. This is usually followed by the creation of a mentor‐mentee pair, where both partners work together on an EBP project and implement it. The program ends with the presentation, dissemination, and evaluation of the results (Balakas et al. 2009 ; Breckenridge‐Sproat et al. 2015 ; Chan et al. 2020 ; Dos Santos et al. 2022 ; Friesen et al. 2017 ; Hole et al. 2023 ; Jeffers et al. 2008 ; Lott et al. 2020 ; Melnyk et al. 2017 , 2018 , 2020 , 2021 , 2022 ; Melnyk et al. 2010 ; Mudderman et al. 2020 ; Oh and Yoo 2020 ; Schuler et al. 2020 , 2021 ; Wallen et al. 2010 ). Some programs are based on the use of implementation models such as the ARCC model intervention program. These programs evaluate the effects of mentoring on the patient and care teams through specific outcomes: job satisfaction, team cohesion, attrition/turnover, EBP implementation, and beliefs (Levin et al. 2011 ). One study presents an e‐mentoring program, based on online tutorials and video conferencing guidance. This highlights the benefits of distance mentoring, particularly in addressing the insufficient number of mentors in organizations while significantly improving beliefs in EBP. Online workshops also exist and can be conducted during personal time to address the issue of work priority (Hooge et al. 2022 ). Similar support programs for EBP are called “Fellowship programs.” Five studies, among those selected, highlight this type of program (7.1%, 5/70). These programs spread over a period of 9 to 18 months, comparable to mentoring programs, and consist of courses, workshops, and an EBP project. The support is more or less formal, depending on the exchange, which takes place at least once a month, face‐to‐face or remotely, and according to the participants' preferences. The university, supported by clinical organizations, provides EBP experts to support the mentor‐mentee pair in the implementation and monitoring of the project until the dissemination of the results (Alves 2021a ; Kim et al. 2016 , 2017a , 2017b ; Laibhen‐Parkes 2014 ; Wentland and Hinderer 2020 ). 3.8 Facilitators and Support of Mentorship Support from management, administration, and the organization appears to be an important facilitator according to authors (15.7%, 11/70). It facilitates the professional development of teams by providing resources related to EBP, while allowing the mentor time to support nurses in the EBP processes or projects (Caramanica and Spiva 2018 ; Crawford et al. 2023 ; Farokhzadian et al. 2015 ; Kilicli et al. 2019 ; Kim et al. 2017b ; Li et al. 2018 ; Lott et al. 2020 ; Lunden et al. 2021 ; Roe and Whyte‐Marshall 2012 ; Royer et al. 2018 ; Ryder et al. 2020 ; Wang et al. 2021 ). Information technology or electronic resources are described as essential by several studies (10%, 7/70) to obtain more effective access to information (Aitken et al. 2011 ; Green et al. 2014 ; Hooge et al. 2022 ; Innis et al. 2015 ; Laibhen‐Parkes 2014 ; O'Leary et al. 2022 ; Shifaza et al. 2013 ). The time available to carry out EBP projects or conduct research is a major facilitator of mentorship (12.8%, 9/70) (Aitken et al. 2011 ; Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2004 ; Innis et al. 2015 ; Kim et al. 2016 , 2017b ; Melnyk et al. 2021 ; O'Leary et al. 2022 ; Royer et al. 2018 ; Smith et al. 2009 ; Wang et al. 2021 ). This is often coupled with the need for a significant number of mentors (12.8%, 9/70) to support each nurse in developing EBP skills and project management (Alves 2021a ; Hole et al. 2023 ; Melnyk 2007 , 2014 ; Melnyk, Bullock, et al. 2010 ; Melnyk et al. 2017 , 2020 , 2021 ; Ploeg et al. 2010 ). To assess the effectiveness of mentorship programs or to gauge the status of various EBP attributes within the organization, the use of specific scales is recommended (15.7%, 11/70). Several exist; the most used are the Organizational Culture and Readiness for System‐Wide Integration of Evidence‐Based Practice Survey (OCRSIEP), the EBP Beliefs scale, and the EBP Implementation scale, which respectively assess organizational preparedness for EBP, EBP beliefs, and EBP implementation. Other scales exist to evaluate EBP skills, job satisfaction, group cohesion, intention to stay, or EBP engagement (Friesen et al. 2017 ; Gorsuch et al. 2020 ; Levin et al. 2011 ; Melnyk et al. 2018 , 2020 , 2021 ; Morgan 2012 ; Mudderman et al. 2020 ; O'Neill 2023 ; Roe and Whyte‐Marshall 2012 ). A scale developed by Melnyk et al. ( 2022 ) helps analyze, using a questionnaire, the availability of EBP mentors, allowing organizations to adjust their activities (Melnyk et al. 2022 ). EBP implementation models (7.1%, 5/70), such as the ARCC model or the Clinical Scholar model, foster mentorship implementation by identifying organizational culture, facilitators, and barriers to overcome (Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2005 ; Levin et al. 2011 ; Melnyk 2007 ; Melnyk et al. 2017 ; O'Neill 2023 ). Collaboration with librarians (8.6%, 6/70) is necessary and allows nurses to access additional bibliographic resources and improve their scientific literature search skills (Aitken et al. 2011 ; Breckenridge‐Sproat et al. 2015 ; DiGaudio Mariano et al. 2009 ; Friesen et al. 2017 ; Gorsuch et al. 2020 ; Majid et al. 2011 ). Interprofessional collaboration (10%, 7/70) with medical, paramedical, and academic professionals is a significant facilitator, as is continuous education (5.7%, 4/70) and collaboration with universities (5.7%, 4/70), all contributing to the professional development of EBP skills (Ferren et al. 2022 ; Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2004 ; Krugman 2012 ; Long et al. 2009 ; Magers 2014 ; Melnyk, Bullock, et al. 2010 ; Melnyk et al. 2022 ; Ploeg et al. 2010 ; Roe and Whyte‐Marshall 2012 ). As a final mentorship facilitator, regular feedback allows staff to visualize the success of their projects and the impact they have on practices (2.9%, 2/70) (Magers 2014 ; Schuler et al. 2020 ). 3.9 Barriers and Challenges Faced by EBP Mentors Of the articles that discussed barriers of EBP mentoring, lack of time is one of the most cited barriers (17.1%, 12/70), often linked to a high workload or other priorities (Kim et al. 2016 ). Nurses lack time to read scientific journals, gray literature, or implement EBP‐related projects. Management does not have enough time to fully support teams and mentors, and the EBP mentor lacks time to complete mentoring duties and manage EBP projects within the organization and care units (Ecoff 2009 ; Farokhzadian et al. 2015 ; Ferren et al. 2022 ; Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2004 ; Friesen et al. 2017 ; Gorsuch et al. 2020 ; Green et al. 2014 ; Hooge et al. 2022 ; Krugman 2012 ; O'Neill 2023 ; Wang et al. 2021 ). The second identified barrier is the insufficient number of EBP mentors (15.7%, 11/70) due to their limited availability, a result of time constraints between EBP projects (Dos Santos et al. 2022 ; Ecoff 2009 ; Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2005 ; Melnyk, Bullock, et al. 2010 ; Melnyk et al. 2004 , 2018 , 2021 , 2022 ; Melnyk et al. 2010 ; Schuler et al. 2021 ; Wang et al. 2021 ). Then comes the lack of support and organizational culture (5.8%, 5/70) (Alves 2021b ; Ferren et al. 2022 ; Melnyk et al. 2017 ; Oh and Yoo 2020 ), insufficient resources (access to a computer, the internet, database, staff resources, high‐quality materials) (2.9%, 2/70) (Farokhzadian et al. 2015 ; Melnyk et al. 2010 ), limited budget (1.4%, 1/70) (Debourgh 2001 ), and high turnover (1.4%, 1/70) (Breckenridge‐Sproat et al. 2015 ). The challenges identified by the authors support the importance of sustaining EBP (Debourgh 2001 ). It is necessary to analyze the cost of implementing mentorship, as well as the benefits it could bring to clinical practice and within care teams (Ferren et al. 2022 ; Friesen et al. 2017 ). Understanding the role and responsibilities of a mentor within an organization is essential to sustain an EBP culture (Oh and Yoo 2020 ). Implementing a standardized national mentorship program would enable better management of mentors and EBP projects (Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2005 ). 3.10 Benefices and Impact of EBP Mentorship The impact of a mentor or EBP mentorship programs was measured and grouped into an initial table, then synthesized in Figure 3 for better clarity. The results come primarily from evaluations using scales specific to the outcomes studied, but the results and expert opinions were also included in the synthesis. The authors predominantly identified that mentorship programs had a positive impact on EBP implementation (48.5%, 34/70) and EBP beliefs (35.7%, 25/70). An improvement in knowledge (28.6%, 20/70) and EBP skills (25.7%, 18/70) followed. Studies highlights that mentorship also positively impacts nursing staff with an improvement in job satisfaction (12.8%, 9/70), organizational readiness for EBP (11.4%, 8/70), EBP culture (10%, 7/70), EBP engagement (10%, 7/70), and group cohesion (7.1%, 5/70). Several authors have demonstrated the direct impact on reducing nurse turnover and therefore retention (8.5%, 6/70). These results are partly explained by improved quality of care, caregivers' increased self‐confidence, and an environment conducive to change (Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2005 ), all thanks to mentorship and mentoring programs (Levin et al. 2011 ; Smith et al. 2009 ; Spiva et al. 2017 ). Some studies demonstrated that the positive effects of EBP mentorship lasted several months after the program ended (8,6%, 6/70) (Crawford et al. 2023 ; Gorsuch et al. 2020 ; Kim et al. 2016 ; Levin et al. 2011 ; Melnyk 2007 ; Melnyk et al. 2010 ). 4 Discussion The aim of this scoping review was to explore the role of the mentor in the clinical setting and their use in the implementation of EBP. The definitions of mentor and EBP champion vary from one article to another. Several studies clearly differentiate the terms and roles of each, but the majority merge them under the common term of mentorship or guidance (Aitken et al. 2011 ; Melnyk et al. 2010b , 2020 ). For studies that separate the two terms, the champion's role focuses more on helping to implement specific evidence at a given time, while the mentor guides and supports nurses in the long‐term acquisition of skills and EBP project implementation (Aitken et al. 2011 ). Many studies focus on the role of the champion but do not specify in which area; whether they are champions in research, change, or clinical practice, they generally have specific skills, roles, or missions (Santos et al. 2022 ). This study chose to explore the role of the expert, whether they are an EBP mentor or an EBP champion in clinical settings as a whole, in order to provide a comprehensive analysis of their activities and impact on practices. The use of a mentor would help integrate the necessary knowledge for EBP implementation while supporting teams in practice changes at each stage of the process (Crawford et al. 2023 ). Nurse participation in mentorship programs appears to be essential. These programs are all based on mentor‐mentee pair systems that allow both parties to build their skills, whether in pedagogy or EBP (Oh and Yoo 2020 ). However, it was difficult to distinguish between mentorship programs and “fellowship” programs, as both have many similarities (Alves 2021a ). These programs are generally led by a hospital or university. They have specific names, such as the Evidence Equal Excellence program (Balakas et al. 2009 ) or the Spirit of Inquiry, Expanding EBP Knowledge program (Hole et al. 2023 ), and they also include nursing students in the mentorship system to support their development in EBP use during training. It is important to structure and standardize EBP mentoring programs to support nurses in the application and acquisition of EBP knowledge. As the research focused primarily on nurses in clinical settings, this limited the number of articles addressing students and, notably, the university environment. However, the importance of collaboration between clinical and academic settings is evident and would provide additional resources, particularly in terms of pedagogical skills, research, and access to university librarians (Birkhoff et al. 2020 ). The university mentor can dedicate their time to preparing the organization for EBP and free up time for mentors in the clinical setting. One of the keys to mentoring is the integration of EBP from the initial training stage by strengthening collaboration between universities and the clinical setting to best prepare students for the use of EBP. During the initial training, mentoring programs can be set up directly to start developing their autonomy and critical thinking skills in EBP at an early age. Teachers must be mobilized to train future clinical and academic mentors (Jeffers et al. 2008 ). The ARCC model and the Clinical Scholar model are two models cited by the selected articles and are the main EBP implementation models centered on mentorship (Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2005 ). These two models are similar in their purpose, with Melnyk et al.'s (2002) model and Schultz ( 2005 ) model both concluding with the dissemination of results after an evaluation of outcome indicators and their analysis (Melnyk and Fineout‐Overholt 2002 ; Schultz 2005 ). Despite their differences, implementation models seem to be key to effectively preparing an organization and its culture for mentorship implementation and success (Dusin et al. 2023 ; Melnyk et al. 2022 ). However, it is necessary to anticipate, before implementation, the choice of model and the strategies to be used, all depending, of course, on the organizational context and environment. Implementation sciences provide the different steps the organization must go through in order to safely implement mentoring (McNett et al. 2021 , 2022 ). In low‐ and middle‐income countries, geographical, political, economic, and social factors hinder the effective application of EBP. Among the major obstacles are lack of funding, lack of human resources, insufficient lecturer trained in EBP, lack of specific policies and lack of access to the internet. It is essential that healthcare professionals develop the skills to research, evaluate and integrate new knowledge in order to effectively implement EBP. However, in many developing countries, the infrastructure and expertise for adequate EBP training is lacking. In addition, there is little research on the barriers and levers to EBP in these regions, leaving a gap in the scientific literature. It is crucial to set up training the trainer programs to strengthen skills and ensure effective knowledge dissemination (Nalweyiso et al. 2024 ). Thus, implementation strategies like mentorship must also be anticipated. Therefore, it is necessary to identify facilitators and barriers as an essential step in the implementation models (Fontaine et al. 2024 ). Identifying them is part of the EBP implementation strategies defined and compiled by Powell and colleagues in 2012 and 2015 (Powell et al. 2015 ). The most important facilitator identified is managerial and administrative support, provided that management can be trained in research and EBP beforehand to better understand the related issues (Caramanica and Spiva 2018 ). On the other hand, the main barriers, namely the lack of time and mentors, will be difficult to overcome. The lack of time is linked to excessive workloads and higher priorities, especially patient care and administrative tasks (Hooge et al. 2022 ). The number of mentors can be increased or addressed through regular mentorship programs or e‐mentoring. However, this requires that nurses are willing to train in EBP and that management can free up time for training, for example by incorporating it into a continuing education program in addition to initial training. It is therefore necessary to find solutions to compensate for the lack of time and human and material resources (digital tools, databases). It is necessary to develop the infrastructure and have easy access to the various digital tools to facilitate project management in EBP (Fineout‐Overholt et al. 2004 ; Saunders and Vehviläinen‐Julkunen 2017 ; Soukup and McCleish 2008 ). As initially found during the state of knowledge, mentorship has a positive impact on EBP beliefs and implementation (Green et al. 2014 ). The study confirms its benefit on job satisfaction, group cohesion, and turnover reduction. Teams have a strong desire to improve the quality of care and mentoring allows them to do so. This explains these benefits on their quality of life at work. This is an important point that could provide future solutions to address staffing shortages in healthcare systems (Alves 2021b ; Melnyk et al. 2021 ). Although not evaluated in this scoping review, mentorship also positively impacts patient outcomes and the quality of care (Ryder et al. 2020 ). It is interesting to note that patient outcomes improve indirectly through mentorship, thanks to its positive impact on care teams (Ferren et al. 2022 ). Further research in this direction is needed to more accurately assess patient outcomes and caregivers' satisfaction with the quality of care provided. By investing in mentorship, nurses are investing in the future of nursing. 4.1 Strengths and Limitations The distinction between the terms mentor and champion is not necessarily clarified in most articles. It is possible that some terms or situations may have been misinterpreted. A concept analysis would be useful to clarify the use of these different terms. 5 Linking Evidence to Action Tools exist to evaluate the mentor's implementation and availability. Healthcare organizational leaders and managers should not hesitate to use them; they are a powerful facilitator for analyzing the impact of mentoring. APNs are the professionals best suited to take on the role of mentor in EBP, thanks to their research and clinical skills. Managers must free up time for them to be able to support and carry out EBP projects. Collaboration between universities and the clinical setting is essential to obtain the skills and resources of both fields. Mentorship should be conducted by both nurses in clinical settings and faculty nurses because their research skills are much more developed. Mentors are expected to communicate about ongoing research projects, whether through conferences, congresses, informal meetings, or formal meetings. Information is a key element in the development of EBP and must be supported by the organization. Organizations must provide, in the continuing education of nurses, mentoring programs to increase their skills in EBP but also to improve the support from the mentors. The research unit of health establishments can use these programs to develop most of its EBP projects. 6 Conclusion EBP mentorship is a key component in the implementation and acquisition of EBP skills among care teams. The mentor in a clinical setting has a role of support, accompaniment in EBP projects, leadership, guiding clinical reasoning, disseminating information related to research and EBP, and participating in mentoring programs. Facilitators identified are mainly the support of management and organization, digital and material resources necessary for the management of EBP projects, time dedicated to mentoring, and the use of specific scales to measure mentor effectiveness. The most common barriers encountered are the lack of time and mentors in EBP. The benefits of mentoring in clinical settings on nursing teams are the increase of EBP beliefs and implementation. It also impacts job satisfaction, reducing turnover and promoting team cohesion. There is now an urgent need to invest in and work on the implementation of EBP mentorship in clinical settings to better train and support nurses in research in implementing EBP and ultimately improve the quality of care.
