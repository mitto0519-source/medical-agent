Objective To evaluate surgical strategies and clinical outcomes for atlantoaxial instability/dislocation (AAI/AAD) in Down syndrome (DS) patients. Methods A retrospective review was conducted on 12 DS patients with AAI/AAD treated between March 2018 and June 2024. Surgical plans were tailored based on reducibility: reducible cases underwent posterior atlantoaxial/occipitocervical fusion ( n = 8), while irreducible cases received transoral anterior release combined with posterior fixation ( n = 4). Outcomes were assessed through complications, radiographic parameters (anterior atlantodental interval, ADI), and neurological status (JOA score). Results The cohort (5 males, 7 females) aged 5–28 years (mean 11.3 ± 6.4) completed 1–6 year follow-up. Clinical presentations included myelopathy (75.0%, 9/12) and neck pain (25.0%, 3/12). Radiographic anomalies included os odontoideum (66.7%, 8/12) and odontoid fracture (8.3%, 1/12). Postoperative ADI significantly decreased from 8.95 ± 3.19 mm to 3.40 ± 0.81 mm ( P &lt; 0.05), with JOA scores improving from 10.92 ± 4.40 to 15.50 ± 2.43 ( P &lt; 0.05). Complications occurred in 25.0% (3/12): one surgical site infection managed by debridement, one dural tear repaired intraoperatively, and one hardware displacement requiring revision. Median fusion time was 6 months (range 4.5–16). All patients demonstrated neurological improvement except one with residual lower limb weakness. Conclusion DS patients with AAI/AAD frequently present with os odontoideum and spinal cord compromise, necessitating surgical intervention. Reducible dislocations may be effectively managed with posterior fusion alone, whereas irreducible cases require combined anterior–posterior approaches. Early surgical intervention is recommended for DS patients exhibiting os odontoideum with AAI/AAD due to elevated risk of progressive myelopathy. Characteristic features include short stature, hypotonia, intellectual disability, and congenital heart defects [ 2 ]. Ligamentous laxity and joint hypermobility in these patients predispose them to atlantoaxial instability (AAI), which may progress to atlantoaxial dislocation (AAD) [ 3 ]. Defined as a pathological displacement of the atlantoaxial joint secondary to progressive AAI, AAD constitutes a severe disabling condition in spinal surgery. Diagnosis is primarily established by lateral cervical radiography demonstrating an anterior atlantodental interval (ADI) exceeding 5 mm.While early studies estimated AAD prevalence in DS populations at 10–20% [ 4 ], recent U.S. data (2016–2020) from 213,095 patients with primary AAI/AAD diagnoses revealed DS comorbidity in 7.2% of cases [ 5 ]. Current guidelines recommend conservative management with regular surveillance for asymptomatic patients [ 6 ]. However, DS children face elevated risks of acute AAD-induced catastrophic spinal cord injury from minor trauma. Although cervical orthoses and external immobilization remain mainstays of nonoperative care, these approaches demonstrate limited efficacy and substantial complication rates [ 7 ]. Notably, clinical evidence supporting surgical fixation strategies remains insufficient, with no consensus guidelines existing for AAI/AAD management in DS patients [ 8 ]. This study analyzes surgical outcomes in 12 symptomatic DS-AAD patients to inform clinical decision-making for this vulnerable population. Study design and methods Patient cohort This retrospective study enrolled 12 consecutive DS patients with AAD treated at our institution between March 2018 and June 2024. The cohort comprised 5 males (41.7%) and 7 females (58.3%), aged 5 ~ 28 years (mean 11.3 ± 6.4 years). Median symptom duration was 4.5 months (range: 2 days—~ 20 years). Clinical presentations Neurological manifestations included myelopathy (75.0%, 9/12) presenting as limb weakness/numbness and gait disturbance, with 6 cases developing myelopathy following low-energy trauma (including 1 quadriplegic patient with bladder-bowel dysfunction). Cervical pain was the primary complaint in 25.0% (3/12). JOA scores ranged from 6 to 11 (mean 8.2 ± 2.3). Comorbidities included surgically corrected congenital heart defects (16.7%, 2/12) and basilar invagination (8.3%, 1/12). Radiographic evaluation Bony anomalies were identified as follows: Os odontoideum (OO): 66.7% (8/12) Odontoid fracture: 8.3% (1/12) Isolated transverse ligament laxity (ADI &gt; 5 mm): 25.0% (3/12) Preoperative neutral-position ADI averaged (8.95 ± 3.19) mm Surgical indications Operative criteria included: ADI &gt; 5.0 mm with significant myelopathy ADI &gt; 5.0 mm accompanied by intractable neck pain or functional impairment Classification and surgical protocols Reducibility was determined through dynamic radiographs and intraoperative traction under fluoroscopy: Reducible AAD (66.7%, 8/12): Underwent posterior atlantoaxial/occipitocervical fixation and fusion Irreducible AAD (33.3%, 4/12): Received combined transoral anterior release with posterior instrumentation Outcome measures Neurological improvement was quantified using Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores (see Table 1 for demographic details). Surgical protocol Preoperative traction All patients underwent skull traction with initial weight set at 1/10 body weight (2.5 ~ 5 kg). Traction load was progressively increased to 1/6 body weight (4.0 ~ 8.0 kg) based on serial fluoroscopic reduction assessment. Surgical techniques Posterior Fixation (Reducible AAD). Positioning &amp; Exposure: Under general anesthesia, patients were placed prone with midline posterior exposure of C1-C2 segments. C2 Pedicle Screw Placement: The C2 nerve root and venous plexus were carefully retracted to expose the superior aspect of the C2 pedicle isthmus. Screw entry points were established at the center of C2 inferior articular processes. Bilateral C1 lateral mass screws were inserted after creating pilot holes with fine drills. Reduction &amp; Fixation: Pre-contoured titanium rods were connected and locked after compression reduction. Autologous iliac cancellous bone grafts were placed in lateral mass and interlaminar spaces. For cases with challenging C1 screw placement, occipitocervical fixation was performed using six short occipital screws connected to C2 pedicle screws through contoured rods. Combined Anterior–Posterior Approach (Irreducible AAD). Anterior Release: Transoral release was performed in supine position, involving anterior longitudinal ligament resection, longus colli muscle detachment, and scar tissue debridement around atlantoaxial joints. Anatomic reduction was achieved using curettes to mobilize os odontoideum. Posterior Stabilization: Patients were repositioned prone for standard posterior instrumentation as described above. Postoperative care Mobilization: Early ambulation with cervicothoracic orthosis commenced 24 h postoperatively. Bracing was maintained for 3 ~ 4 months until radiographic fusion, considering DS patients' ligamentous laxity and limited compliance. Medical Management: Protocol included 48-h broad-spectrum antibiotics, multimodal analgesia, and neurotrophic agents. Imaging Follow-up: Drains were removed when output &lt; 50 mL/day. Post-discharge evaluations included X-ray/CT for reduction verification and MRI for spinal cord decompression assessment. Outcome parameters Primary endpoints included: Radiographic ADI changes on neutral lateral radiographs JOA score improvement Fusion status confirmed by dynamic radiographs during follow-up Statistical analysis Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD or median (range) for skewed distributions. Categorical data were presented as frequencies/percentages. Pre-post comparisons used paired t-tests, with P &lt; 0.05 considered statistically significant. Delayed infection (1 case): Presented 16 months postoperatively with sinus tract formation, successfully treated by implant removal and debridement. Functional recovery: 91.7% (11/12) demonstrated improved ambulatory capacity, with 1 case retaining mild gait instability (Table 2 ). Discussion Controversies and consensus in surgical indications Current literature supports surgical intervention for DS patients with AAI/AAD manifesting spinal cord/neurological symptoms, while prophylactic stabilization in asymptomatic cases remains contentious [ 9 , 10 ]. The 2005 Radiographic Guidelines established critical thresholds: Absolute surgical indication: ①Atlanto-dens interval (ADI) &gt; 9.9 mm; ②Relative indication: ADI 4.5–10 mm with MRI-confirmed cord compression [ 11 ]. Notably, these guidelines mandate activity restrictions for high-risk DS children (ADI &gt; 4.5 mm) even without neurological deficits,recognizing their vulnerability to spinal cord injury (SCI) from minor trauma.AAI constitutes the primary risk factor for cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI) in children with DS, with OO serving as a critical predisposing factor for AAI development [ 3 ]. 1%-2% of patients with Down syndrome (DS) develop symptomatic spinal cord compression, manifesting as neck pain, gait disturbances, loss of sphincter control, and potentially paralysis or death [ 5 ].Among the 12 patients in this cohort, 8 cases (66.67%) were complicated with os odontoideum (OO), 9 cases (75.00%) exhibited severe spinal cord dysfunction, and 6 cases (50.00%) sustained spinal cord injuries from low-energy trauma. These findings indicate a high prevalence of OO and elevated spinal cord injury risk in children with Down syndrome (DS) accompanied by atlantoaxial dislocation (AAD). Follow-up demonstrated poor postoperative recovery in trauma-associated spinal cord injuries. Based on our cohort data and clinical experience, we conclude that cognitive impairment and ligamentous laxity in DS children predispose them to cervical vulnerability. When imaging reveals concurrent os odontoideum and atlantoaxial instability, the risk of delayed spinal cord injury increases significantly, warranting aggressive surgical intervention. Screening and intervention strategies AAD in DS patients predominantly manifests during childhood/adolescence. This study included one adult case (28-year-old female) with 20-year progressive myelopathic symptoms, indicating disease onset in early childhood. Given the 1% ~ 2% symptomatic spinal compression rate in DS [ 5 ] and 50.0% (6/12) trauma-triggered injuries in our cohort, we recommend: Staged screening: Cervical lateral radiographs and MRI at 5–10 years and 10–15 years [ 12 ];Parental education: Avoid cervical hypermobility activities (e.g., somersaults, contact sports). Surgical indications (Institutional Criteria) ADI &gt; 5.0 mm PLUS any of: ① Progressive myelopathy; ② Anatomical anomalies (OO, basilar invagination); ③ Refractory cervical pain. Evidence for prophylactic surgery Takeoka et al. [ 8 ] 's long-term follow-up of 20 cases of DS combined with AAD showed that even if asymptomatic, early surgery for those with ADI &gt; 5 mm could reduce the risk of delayed paralysis.Our data confirm that DS-related cognitive impairment and ligamentous laxity exacerbate atlantoaxial vulnerability. Structural abnormalities (OO and ADI &gt; 5 mm) warrant aggressive stabilization given elevated delayed spinal injury risks. However, standardized protocols remain lacking due to disease rarity and insufficient evidence, necessitating prospective studies to validate long-term surgical outcomes. Surgical technique advancements and safety Advancements in imaging navigation, high-precision screw placement techniques, and multidisciplinary perioperative management systems have significantly enhanced the safety of posterior rod-screw systems for DS-associated AAD. Literature demonstrates:Ito et al. [ 13 ] achieved 100% spinal cord function improvement and 100% fusion rate using atlantoaxial screw fixation in 2 DS-AAI cases;Yang et al. [ 14 ] reported 33.3% delayed fusion (4/12 cases) but no severe neurovascular complications in posterior fixation for DS-AAD;Li et al. [ 7 ] reported 5 pediatric cases: 1 intraoperative dural tear repaired surgically, and 1 implant fracture requiring revision surgery (screw trajectory adjustment with augmented bone grafting) achieving secondary fusion. Among our 12 cases, 3 developed postoperative complications:①Intraoperative dural injury with postoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, managed by prolonged bed rest, extended antibiotic administration, and aggressive fluid therapy, discharged uneventfully;②Implant loosening due to postoperative infection, treated with debridement, implant removal, and sinus tract excision at 16 months postoperatively, alongside extended antibiotics, achieving satisfactory recovery;③Implant loosening with cephalad migration detected at 6-month follow-up, managed by implant removal despite maintained atlantoaxial fusion alignment, resulting in favorable outcomes (JOA score: 17/17). Surgical strategy and technique selection All 12 patients underwent intraoperative skull traction under general anesthesia (initial weight: 1/10 body weight, maximum ≤ 1/6 body weight). C-arm fluoroscopy assessed atlantoaxial reduction status: Reducible dislocations (8 cases): Direct posterior atlantoaxial pedicle screw fixation Irreducible dislocations (4 cases): Combined anterior transoral release for adequate reduction Posterior fixation efficacy and risk mitigation Posterior atlantoaxial screw fixation achieved 100% fusion rate (12/12) with a median fusion time of 6 months (range: 4.5–16 months). Despite literature-reported 2% vertebral artery injury risk [ 15 ], preoperative 3D CT angiography (CTA) mapping of vertebral artery trajectories minimized vascular complications. Anatomical challenges and adaptive strategies: personalization and diversification Among 12 DS combined with AAD cases (11 adolescents/children), anatomical variations included hypoplastic atlas, posterior arch deficiency, and low bone density, complicating screw placement.Therefore, among the 8 patients with recoverable dislocation, 7 cases chose simple posterior atlantoaxial fixation and bone graft fusion, and 1 case chose posterior unilateral atlantoaxial fixation combined with contralateral occipital axis fixation. Among the 4 patients with refractory dislocation, 3 cases chose anterior transoral atlantoaxial joint release combined with posterior atlantoaxial fixation and bone graft fusion, and 1 case chose anterior transoral atlantoaxial joint release, posterior unilateral atlantoaxial reduction and fixation,and contralateral occipital axis fixation.Postoperative cervicothoracic orthosis was maintained ≥ 3 months to compensate for bone-screw interface insufficiency, achieving stable fusion. Surgical approaches and fixation levels were tailored to individual anatomical development and intraoperative technical feasibility. Indications for occipitocervical fixation (Based on Cohort Experience) Neuroplasticity value of postoperative rehabilitation Systematic rehabilitation interventions promote neural recovery by activating motor cortex plasticity [ 16 , 17 ], particularly beneficial for patients with severe preoperative spinal cord dysfunction. Functional MRI (fMRI) evidence demonstrates positive correlation between motor-related brain activation intensity and functional improvement (r ≥ 0.84, P ≤ 0.16) [ 18 ]. A representative case in our cohort (preoperative JOA 6/17, incomplete tetraplegia) underwent combined hyperbaric oxygen therapy, neuromuscular electrical stimulation, and progressive motor training, achieving JOA 13/17 (ΔJOA = 7) with restored sphincter control at 3 months postoperatively. Imaging confirmed stable fixation (ADI 4.26 mm). Study limitations Retrospective design with potential temporal bias in data collection.Heterogeneous fixation strategies (occipitocervical vs. atlantoaxial fusion) confounding complication/fusion time analyses.Small sample size ( n = 12) and pediatric self-reporting inaccuracies. Future Directions:Prospective cohort studies with standardized follow-up intervals to optimize management protocols. Conclusions DS associated AAD primarily manifests in childhood with high os odontoideum (OO) comorbidity, severe myelopathy, and poor spontaneous recovery. Posterior or combined anterior–posterior screw-rod fixation achieves:Atlantoaxial reduction and stabilization;Spinal cord decompression; Symptomatic cervical pain relief. Early postoperative rehabilitation (hyperbaric oxygen, neuromuscular stimulation, progressive motor training) enhances functional recovery, with stable fusion typically achieved within 6 months. Proactive surgical intervention is warranted for DS patients exhibiting os odontoideum with atlantoaxial instability, given their elevated risk of delayed spinal cord injury.
