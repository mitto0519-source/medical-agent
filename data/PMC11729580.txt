ABSTRACT Introduction Paranoid ideation, a crucial component of psychotic‐like experiences, tends to increase between early and late adolescence, even in the general population, and it negatively affects overall mental health and social adjustment. Japanese adolescents experience high levels of interpersonal stress. However, few studies have examined the relationship between paranoid ideation and interpersonal stress. This study investigated the effects of maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and interpersonal stress on paranoid ideation to provide suggestions for preventive education and early intervention for paranoid ideations among Japanese university students. Methods An online questionnaire survey involving 400 Japanese university students, aged between 18 and 22 years, was conducted. The questionnaires used were the Paranoia Checklist, the Metacognitive Questionnaire‐30, and the Interpersonal Stressor Scale. The Interpersonal Stressor Scale was developed specifically for Japanese adolescents. Results A multiple regression analysis was performed, which showed that the regression coefficients of the interaction between maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and interpersonal stress were significant for paranoid ideation. The results of the simple slope analysis regarding the scales on which interactions were found also indicated that the influence of maladaptive metacognitive beliefs on paranoid ideation was greater when interpersonal stress levels were higher. Conclusion The combination of interpersonal stress and maladaptive metacognitive beliefs exacerbates paranoid ideation among Japanese university students. It is important to modify metacognitive and cognitive biases in preventive education and early intervention strategies. differences in culture interpersonal stress Japanese university students metacognitive beliefs paranoid ideation JSPS KAKENHI 22K03096 4 Discussion Descriptive statistics, alpha coefficients and correlation coefficients for the PCL, MCQ‐30 and ISS were each as high as or higher than those reported in previous studies. The results of the correlation analysis showed that the Pearson correlation coefficients between the three scales were moderate, and the partial correlation coefficients were also significant. Therefore, this study also confirmed that paranoid ideation, interpersonal stress and maladaptive metacognitive beliefs are significantly linked. However, because all the study participants were university students at the time of study, the majority of participants with paranoid ideation in this study should be considered subclinical. The results of the multiple regression analysis showed that, overall, interpersonal stress and maladaptive metacognitive beliefs affected paranoid ideation. The findings were significant because they clarified the relationship between the two factors affecting paranoid ideation. The fact that the PCL total score and the results of the three subscales were nearly identical could be a consequence of the strong correlation between them. Furthermore, a comparison of regression coefficients through a simple slope analysis showed that maladaptive metacognitive beliefs influenced paranoid ideation regardless of whether interpersonal stress was strong or weak, but that the influence was greater when interpersonal stress was stronger. Although these analyses were based only on the results of a cross‐sectional survey and could not establish a clear causal relationship, the results suggest that maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and interpersonal stress significantly influence paranoid ideation, at least among Japanese university students. Few previous studies have incorporated daily interpersonal stressors into models of paranoid ideation. Similar to Hashimoto et al. ( 2012 ), Lee et al. ( 2023 ) also argued that Japanese university students were more likely to experience interpersonal stress than their Western counterparts. This study investigated culture‐specific types of stress to which the study participants were imagined to be exposed on a daily basis. In this respect, we believe that we have at least confirmed the validity of the model that suggests the relationship among delusional ideation, maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and interpersonal stress regarding Japanese university students. However, it is unclear why gender differences, which are often reported in interpersonal behaviour, were not observed in this study. 4.1 Clinical Implications Attempting to modify maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and encouraging the acquisition of coping strategies for interpersonal stress are of primary importance for psychological interventions aimed at addressing paranoid ideation. However, there is increased interest in interventions aimed at addressing interpersonal stress among non‐clinical groups of young people and university students in many countries. In many counselling sessions for university students, the aim is to alleviate depressive symptoms and problem behaviours, such as self‐harm, by reducing the level of interpersonal stress (e.g., Coiro, Bettis, and Compas 2017 ; Smith‐Adcock and Kerpelman 2022 ). Similarly, many intervention studies have been conducted on young adults with depression in Japan. However, less attention has been paid to PLEs, such as paranoid ideation, as evidenced by Sullivan et al. ( 2020 ) cohort study in which PLEs, including paranoid ideation, increased between the ages of 13 and 24 years, which includes university age groups. Therefore, when dealing with the psychological problems affecting young people in non‐clinical groups who are not at the level of requiring clinical psychiatric services, the existence of subclinical paranoid ideation should not be forgotten, and it is necessary to learn techniques and build support systems aimed at enabling those in charge to respond appropriately. Giusti et al. ( 2018 ) investigated the effects of group intervention methods among young patients suffering from anxiety (cognitive‐behavioural therapy for anxiety and some modules of metacognitive training). The results showed that cognitive biases reduced, as did interpersonal sensitivity, anxiety and paranoid ideation. Their intervention method comprised the modification of anxiety‐producing cognitions and behaviours, as well as the awareness and control of their cognitive biases. The findings of this study that metacognitive beliefs and interpersonal stress interacted to influence the levels of paranoid ideation were thought to strengthen the validity of their intervention methods and encouraging its development. 4.2 Limitations and Directions for Future Studies Sellers et al. ( 2018 ) proposed a model investigating and discussing the effects of the combination of paranoid ideation and maladaptive metacognitive beliefs on the development of anxiety symptoms, but if their model is correct, maladaptive metacognitive beliefs influence both the formation of paranoid ideation and the subsequent negative affect. Therefore, a more comprehensive model that incorporates emotions as a factor and the methods of intervention based on such a model needs to be tested. 5 Conclusion This study suggests that maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and interpersonal stress are interrelated and influence paranoid ideation among Japanese university students. Therefore, in preventive education, early intervention strategies are necessary to not only help such individuals acquire stress coping skills at a behavioural level, but also provide them with programmes aimed at increasing their awareness of maladaptive metacognition.
