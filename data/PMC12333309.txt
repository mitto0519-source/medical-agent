Abstract Background Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a common disease in preschool children and seriously affects their quality of life. Defining the risk factors of AR can help early diagnosis and prevention. The aim of this meta-analysis was to identify and summarizes the risk factors associated with allergic rhinitis in preschool children. Methods A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library, Web of Science, built up to 20 January 2025 was performed. Studies were included if they reported risk factors for AR in preschool children. A random-effects model was used to calculate the combined odd ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Results A total of 10 cohort studies involving 94,774 preschool children were included in this meta-analysis. The results suggested that several factors may associated with higher odds of allergic rhinitis in preschool children. These factors include parental allergic rhinitis (OR = 2.4, 95% CI: 2.01–2.87), parental eczema (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.48–1.99), parental asthma (OR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.53–3.93), male (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.37–1.72), caesarean birth (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.08–1.61), and dampness at home (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.08–1.42). On the other hand, the presence of pets at home was found to be protective, with a reduced risk of allergic rhinitis (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.41–0.78). Conclusion This study suggests that factors such as parental history of allergy, boys, caesarean section and damp environment at home may increase the risk of allergic rhinitis in preschool children, whereas pets may have a protective role. These findings emphasize the influence of genetic and environmental factors on allergic rhinitis in children. Background Allergic Rhinitis (AR) is an inflammation of the nasal mucosa caused by allergens and is one of the most common allergic diseases among preschool children [ 1 , 2 ]. Symptoms of allergic rhinitis include nasal congestion, runny nose, sneezing and itchy nose, which in severe cases may affect children’s quality of life, ability to learn, and overall health [ 3 ]. Allergic rhinitis has become a global public health problem as environmental factors change, and the prevalence of allergic diseases increases every year [ 4 ]. Preschool children are particularly susceptible to the onset and development of allergic rhinitis because their immune system is not yet fully developed, and their physiology is still in the developmental stage [ 5 ]. The development of allergic rhinitis is usually closely related to a variety of factors such as genetic susceptibility and environmental exposure. Studies have shown that the pathogenesis of allergic rhinitis involves a complex immune response, including IgE-mediated allergic reactions and abnormalities in local immune responses [ 6 ]. Factors such as types of allergens and degree of exposure, environmental pollutants, climate change, and lifestyle may affect children’s sensitivity to allergens to varying degrees [ 7 ]. Despite the increasing number of studies on allergic rhinitis in children in recent years, there is still a lack of uniform conclusions regarding the specific risk factors for allergic rhinitis in preschool children [ 8 , 9 ]. Some studies have found that a family history of allergic disease is a high-risk factor for allergic rhinitis in preschool children, and that children with allergic parents have a significantly increased risk of developing allergic rhinitis [ 10 , 11 ]. In addition, air pollution and exposure to indoor and outdoor allergens (dust mites, moulds, pets’ dander) have been identified as important environmental factors for allergic rhinitis in preschool children. However, the results of studies on these risk factors are still somewhat controversial and may vary between regions and populations [ 12 ]. Some studies suggest that children living in urban areas are more likely to develop allergic rhinitis than those in rural areas, possibly due to higher concentrations of air pollution and allergens in urban environments; however, other studies have found that rural children have a lower prevalence, likely due to less exposure to allergens [ 13 , 14 ]. In addition, factors such as climate change, indoor air quality, and exposure to smoking may also have different effects in different regions. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the risk factors for allergic rhinitis in preschool children, this study systematically evaluated the relevant research data in the existing literature by means of Meta-analysis, a statistical method for combining the results of multiple studies, which can provide more persuasive conclusions than those of a single study by combining the data from different studies. Therefore, this study aims to summarizes the main risk factors for allergic rhinitis in preschool children through Meta-analysis, and to explore the interrelationships between different factors and their effects in different populations, to provide a theoretical basis for the early diagnosis, intervention and prevention of allergic rhinitis in preschool children in clinical practice. Methods This systematic evaluation and meta-analysis will strictly follow the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [ 15 ], and it is registered in Prospero with registration number CRD420251067459. Literature retrieval A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library, Web of Science, built up to 20 January 2025 was performed. The search was performed using mesh and free words with the search terms Allergic Rhinitis, Child, risk factors, and the specific search strategy is described in Supplementary Material Table S1. Criteria for inclusion and exclusion Inclusion criteria The subjects included in the study were pre-school children (age range between 3 and 6 years), and there were no restrictions such as gender and ethnicity. Inclusion in the study required clear criteria for the diagnosis of allergic rhinitis, including clinical diagnosis and laboratory tests. Included studies need to provide adequate statistical data including, but not limited to, the odds ratio (OR), hazard ratio (HR), or relative risk (RR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). When studies did not provide sufficient statistical data, they could be considered for inclusion if the required data could be obtained by contacting the authors. Included studies had to be observational, including cohort studies. Exclusion criteria The following types of studies were excluded from this study: studies with non-school-aged children (in some of the included studies, the age range was broader. Only data related to the 3- to 6-year-old age group were extracted for analysis. If the data were not clearly separated, age-adjusted estimates or subgroup analyses were used where possible to ensure consistency with the 3- to 6-year-old range. This approach minimized the impact of any potential age differences in the studies); studies without a clear diagnosis of allergic rhinitis or with unclear diagnostic criteria; studies that did not provide relevant data on the risk factors for allergic rhinitis or did not report sufficient statistical data (ratio ratios, relative risks, and their 95% confidence intervals); duplicate published studies, or the same dataset in multiple publications; unpublished literature (conference abstracts, preprints), and non-English literature that could not be translated. These exclusion criteria were used to ensure that the included studies were of sufficient quality and reliability to improve the scientific validity and accuracy of the Meta-analysis. Data extractions In this research study, Endnote21 software was used to screen the literature of the search results according to the inclusion criteria, and the two authors extracted data from the included studies using Excel data tables, so that if the two authors disputed the data, they could discuss it or invite a third person to adjudicate. The extracted data included basic characteristics of the studies (first author, year of publication, type of study), basic characteristics of the population (sample size, number of AR occurrences, mean age), and regression models. Quality evaluation This study assessed the quality of the included studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [ 16 ]. The NOS scale assesses study quality through three dimensions: selection bias, comparison bias, and outcome assessment. First, the selection dimension assesses whether the case and control groups in the study were selected in a representative manner, whether clear selection criteria were followed, and whether the similarity of baseline characteristics of the samples was controlled. Second, the comparison dimension assesses whether potential confounders, such as age, gender, and lifestyle, were effectively controlled for and whether appropriate statistical methods were used to reduce confounding bias. Finally, the outcome dimension assessed whether the study outcomes were clearly defined and accurately measured and examined the completeness of the follow-up data and whether lost visits or missing data were reasonably addressed. Each dimension was scored as 0 to 4 (selection), 0 to 2 (comparison), and 0 to 3 (outcome), with a total score of 9. Higher scores indicated better study quality. To ensure the scientific validity and reliability of the Meta-analysis, we will weigh the studies in the assessment process, with higher quality studies carrying more weight, and ensure the objectivity and consistency of the assessment through independent reviewer scoring. Statistical analysis In this study, the odd ratio (OR) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) of each included study were combined using Stata 15 software. First, for each study, we extracted the corresponding effect size OR and its 95% confidence interval. To combine these ORs, we pooled them using a random effects model, which can account for heterogeneity between studies, variability in effect sizes across studies. ORs and 95% CIs were calculated for each study and combined into an overall effect size. Heterogeneity of the model was assessed by the I 2 statistic; if the I 2 was greater than 50%, it was considered that there was a high degree of heterogeneity and that the sources of heterogeneity needed to be further explored. For high heterogeneity, we may conduct sensitivity analyses to identify potential factors that may affect the combined effect sizes. In addition, funnel plots and Egger’s test were used to assess the likelihood of publication bias. If bias exists, it may have an impact on the interpretation of the results. The combined effect sizes will be reported as ORs and their 95% CIs to allow for interpretation of results and statistical inference. Discussion Main finding To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that risk factors for allergic rhinitis in preschool children have been explored. This study included 94,774 preschool children through Meta-analysis of data from 10 cohort studies and identified a variety of factors that may increase the risk of developing allergic rhinitis, including parental allergic rhinitis, parental eczema, parental asthma, being a boy, being born by caesarean section, and having a humid environment at home. In addition, the presence of pets may reduce the occurrence of allergic rhinitis. These findings have important clinical implications and can provide guidance for early intervention and prevention of allergic rhinitis. Distinctive features of allergic rhinitis in young children There are significant differences between young children and adults in terms of physiology, immune system development and environmental exposures, and these differences make the young child population unique in terms of allergic rhinitis presentation and risk factors. Firstly, young children’s immune systems are not fully developed, and their immune responses are different from those of adults, so they may exhibit stronger or different responses to allergens [ 27 ]. Secondly, young children live in different environments than adults, and the sources of allergens usually come mainly from the home environment, such as dust mites and pets, whereas adults may be exposed to more external pollutants and allergens from the work environment. In addition, genetic factors have a greater influence on allergic rhinitis in young children, especially family history, whereas in adults, the immune system may have been affected by many years of environmental exposure, and genetic factors play a relatively minor role [ 28 ]. During the physiological developmental stage, young children have an immature upper airway structure and function and therefore have a more pronounced response to allergens and may have a more insidious presentation of symptoms, manifesting as behavioral changes rather than obvious self-reported symptoms. Allergic rhinitis may be an early manifestation of allergic disease in early childhood, and early intervention can help prevent the development of other allergic diseases such as asthma in the future [ 29 ]. Finally, treatment regimens for young children require consideration of drug dosage, safety of use, and side effects, whereas treatment for adults is based on their more mature physiology. Therefore, an independent study of allergic rhinitis in young children could help to identify risk factors specific to them and thus provide personalized strategies for early screening, prevention and treatment. Parental allergies and child rhinitis risk In this study, parental allergic rhinitis may increase the risk of developing allergic rhinitis in preschool children. However, the presence of heterogeneity reminds us that there may be differences in certain factors between studies, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Therefore, sensitivity analyses were performed for this indicator, and the results suggest that despite some heterogeneity, the effect of parental allergic rhinitis on allergic rhinitis in preschool children has a high level of confidence. The genetic background of parental allergic rhinitis may influence the risk of allergic rhinitis in children through abnormal immune system responses [ 12 ]. Parents carry susceptibility genes, such as IL-4, IL-13, and other immune-related genes, which may be passed on to the child during inheritance, leading to an overreaction of the immune system to external allergens (dust mites, pollen) [ 30 ]. In addition, a parent’s history of allergy may alter the concentration of allergens in the home environment, such as dust mites, pets hair, which may also exacerbate the child’s immune response, further increasing the risk of developing allergic rhinitis [ 31 ]. Therefore, taking preventive measures is crucial. Parents should conduct early screening for children of family members with a history of allergy to identify potential allergy risks as early as possible and take effective environmental control measures such as reducing allergen exposure and encouraging breastfeeding to enhance immune protection. Parents may also consider genetic counselling to understand the transmission of susceptibility genes for prevention. These measures can help reduce the incidence of allergic rhinitis and improve the health of preschool children. Parental eczema, as a genetic factor in allergic diseases, may affect the development of the child’s immune system, causing an allergic reaction to allergens [ 32 ]. Genes associated with parental eczema and the immune system, may influence the immune response of the child, resulting in greater sensitivity to common allergens in the environment. In addition, the concentration of allergens may be higher in the family with eczema than in other families, thus exacerbating the child’s immune response [ 33 ]. This could further explain the results of the current study. In response to this risk factor, parents can take the following preventive measures: firstly, parents should screen their children early, especially in infancy, to recognize early signs of allergic diseases; secondly, reduce possible allergen exposure in the home, by regular cleaning of the living environment, and reduction of mold growth. Parental asthma may influence the development of a child’s immune system through genetic susceptibility, resulting in a greater susceptibility to allergic reactions, and a history of parental asthma may result in a higher concentration of allergens in the home environment, which may exacerbate the child’s immune response and further increase his or her risk of developing allergic rhinitis [ 34 , 35 ]. Therefore, early screening for parents with a history of asthma and attention to the presence of allergic symptoms in children is indicated. Pets reduced risk of allergic rhinitis in preschool children In the present study, it was shown that pets may reduce the risk of allergic rhinitis in preschool children, on the one hand, and on the other hand, early exposure to pets may stimulate the development of the immune system through exposure to pet dander, hair, saliva, etc., thus contributing to the development of a more diversified immune response and a reduction in allergic reactions in the child’s immune system [ 36 ]. This phenomenon is known as ‘immunomodulation’, which is explained by the ‘hygiene hypothesis’, moderate exposure to microorganisms contributes to the maturation of the immune system and reduces the risk of allergic diseases [ 37 ]. In addition, pets may alter the microbial community in the home environment, providing a ‘natural immune stimulus’ that reduces the risk of allergic rhinitis in children. A similar study was conducted by Ojwang V (2020) [ 38 ], which analyzed the effect of pet’s exposure on allergic rhinitis in children. The study found that having a dog or cat in the family at the age of one may protect against childhood asthma and allergies. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of this study and further supports the potential role of pets in the prevention of allergic rhinitis. Gender differences in allergic rhinitis risk The results of the present study indicate that male children have a significantly higher risk of developing allergic rhinitis compared to female children. The higher risk of allergic rhinitis in male children may be related to gender differences in the immune system. Studies [ 39 ] have shown that males and females have certain physiological differences in their immune systems, with males typically having a stronger immune response that may be more likely to trigger an allergic reaction. Specifically, the male immune system may be more likely to activate a Th2 cell response when confronted with an allergen, which is strongly associated with the development of allergic disease. In addition, the anatomy and physiology of the airway in male children may make them more susceptible to nasal inflammation when exposed to allergens, which is one of the reasons why males are more susceptible to allergic rhinitis [ 40 ]. Therefore, clinicians should pay special attention to the early signs of allergic rhinitis in male children when dealing with them, especially if there is a family history. Through early screening and timely intervention, the impact of allergic rhinitis on children’s health can be effectively reduced, and appropriate preventive measures, such as allergen management and immunotherapy, can be taken to reduce the incidence of allergic rhinitis. Caesarean section and increased risk of allergic rhinitis The mechanism by which caesarean section may increase the risk of developing allergic rhinitis in children is related to the effects of immune system development. Studies [ 41 , 42 ] have shown that babies born by caesarean section are born without access to the mother’s vagina and birth canal through the natural route of delivery, which may result in babies being born without access to the normal microbial community from their mothers. This lack of microbiota may affect the development of the infant’s immune system, leading to reduced immune tolerance, which in turn increases the risk of allergic reactions. In addition, caesarean section may lead to over-activation of the child’s immune response to external allergens, which is strongly associated with the development of allergic rhinitis [ 43 ]. Babies born by caesarean section are more likely to suffer from incomplete immune system development and the risk of allergic diseases than those born by normal delivery. Therefore, early screening for allergic diseases should be performed in clinical practice, especially for children born by caesarean section, to detect allergic reactions in time and take appropriate interventions. At the same time, immune system support and microbiota repair for children born by caesarean section are important strategies to reduce the risk of allergic rhinitis. Impact of damp environments on allergic rhinitis risk Moist environments are conducive to the growth of molds spores are one of the known strong allergens. Prolonged exposure to mold spores in children may lead to an excessive immune response by the immune system, which in turn may lead to allergic diseases such as allergic rhinitis [ 44 ]. Damp home environments are usually accompanied by poor air quality, which may lead to higher concentrations of allergens (dust mites, pollen), thereby increasing the risk of allergic rhinitis in children [ 45 ]. Therefore, in clinical practice, doctors should advise parents to take measures to reduce children’s exposure to humid environments, especially for children with a family history of allergy [ 46 ]. In addition, regular monitoring and improvement of humidity levels in the home environment and keeping the room dry and ventilated can help reduce the incidence of allergic rhinitis and the negative impact of allergic diseases on children’s health. In our analysis, Egger’s test indicated that five risk factors (parental allergic rhinitis, parental eczema, parental asthma, pets, gender, cesarean section, and dampness in the home) may be subject to publication bias, suggesting that the results of studies on these factors may not fully reflect the true effects of all completed studies. Publication bias typically occurs when negative or non-significant study results are less likely to be published, leading to an overestimation of positive effects. Specifically, for the factors of parental asthma ( P = 0.003) and pets ( P = 0.035), the presence of bias may result in an overestimation of the effects of these factors. Therefore, we recommend that future studies focus on reducing the impact of publication bias to further validate the true influence of these factors. Strengths and limitations This study has the following strengths: this study integrates a large amount of empirical data through the aggregation and Meta-analysis of several related literatures, which enhances the breadth and representativeness of the findings. Such an approach can better reveal the risk factors of allergic rhinitis with high reliability. Heterogeneity tests and sensitivity analyses were used in the analysis process to ensure the robustness of the study results. These methods are effective in identifying and explaining the differences between different studies, improving the accuracy and credibility of the results. This study not only focused on genetic factors (parental history of allergy) but also considered environmental factors (dampness in the home, pets, caesarean section), which enabled the results to reflect more comprehensively the mechanisms of allergic rhinitis and influencing factors in preschool children. The current study has the following limitations: first: despite the use of strict literature screening criteria, there may still be a problem of literature bias. Some high-quality studies may not have been included, resulting in results that are not fully representative of all relevant data. Second: Although heterogeneity was analyzed and adjusted for in this study, differences in design and data collection methods among the included studies may still affect the interpretation of the results. For example, individual studies may have lacked rigorous control variables, resulting in certain potential confounders not being adequately considered. Third: As this study was a retrospective Meta-analysis, which was mainly based on available data from cross-sectional or cohort studies, the causal relationship between the factors and allergic rhinitis could not be clearly established, and only correlations could be speculated. Conclusion This study suggests that factors such as parental history of allergy, boys, caesarean section and damp environment at home may increase the risk of allergic rhinitis in preschool children, whereas pets may have a protective role. These findings emphasize the influence of genetic and environmental factors on allergic rhinitis in children. However, due to the large heterogeneity of studies, more high quality, multi-center, large sample studies are needed to support our conclusions. Supplementary Information Supplementary Material 1.
