Background Tobacco use is closely linked to social and health inequalities, including economic vulnerability, morbidity, and premature death. Young adults with disabilities experience significant social and material hardships, which may be exacerbated by tobacco use. Limited research exists on smoking and e-cigarette use in this population. This study examines the prevalence of disability among young adults in Ireland, compares smoking and e-cigarette use between those with and without disabilities, identifies protective and risk factors, explores shared risk factors, and evaluates disability as an independent risk factor for smoking and e-cigarette use. Methods We analysed weighted data from 4,729 20-year-olds in the Growing Up in Ireland Cohort ’98 study who were present in Waves 1 (2008), 3 (2016), and 4 (2019). Current smoking, e-cigarette use, disability (excluding mental ill-health) and all other variables were assessed at Wave 4, while peer smoking data were drawn from Wave 3. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27. Results 18.1% of participants reported a disability, which was associated with significantly higher smoking (41.8% vs. 36.7%) and e-cigarette use (16.1% vs. 12.9%). Protective factors against both behaviours included being female (OR 0.87 for smoking, OR 0.57 for e-cigarettes), later smoking initiation (OR 0.35, OR 0.62), living in two-parent families (OR 0.83, OR 0.70), and physical activity (smoking only). Risk factors included having peers who smoked (OR 3.67 for smoking; OR 2.36 for e-cigarette use) and caregivers who smoked (OR 1.48, OR 1.48), being employed at age 20 (OR 1.58, OR 1.48), and social media engagement (smoking only). Young adults with disabilities were significantly more likely to experience risk factors (e.g., earlier smoking initiation, caregivers who smoked, one-parent families, employment) but were less likely to have peers who smoked or engage with social media. Disability was independently associated with a higher likelihood of smoking (by 54%) and e-cigarette use (by 36%) after adjusting for protective and risk factors. Conclusion Higher smoking and e-cigarette use in 20-year-olds with disabilities adds further inequality to their lives. Increased awareness, targeted surveys and focused prevention and therapeutic interventions are required to reduce inequalities in this population and hasten the tobacco endgame. We understand disabilities to encompass a broad range of physical, intellectual, sensory or mental impairments [ 2 ]. Depending on environmental and cultural factors, these impairments may lead to further disability, manifesting as functional limitations and reduced social participation. In Ireland, the number of individuals reporting a disability or disabling condition increased significantly between Census 2016 [ 3 ] and Census 2022 [ 4 ] from 643,131 (13% of the population) to 1,109,557 (22%). The prevalence of long-lasting conditions rises dramatically with age. For example, only 4% of children under 4 years old were reported to have a disability in 2022, compared with 76% of those aged 85 and older [ 4 ]. People with disabilities face a much higher risk of poverty and deprivation than those without disabilities [ 5 ]. In 2022, 13.1% of the general Irish population was at risk of poverty, but this figure rose to 35.2% for people who were unable to work due to a long-term health problem or disability. Furthermore, 40% of these individuals live in enforced deprivation, and 19.7% experience consistent poverty, defined as being both at risk of poverty and facing enforced deprivation [ 6 ]. People with disabilities also report poorer health, with 25% describing their health as “bad” or “very bad,” compared to just 4% of the general population. Additionally, 43% report suffering from depression, compared to 14% of the overall population [ 7 ]. The standardised mortality rate for people with disabilities is 4.1 times higher than for those without disabilities, with a higher rate for females (4.5 times) than for males (3.9 times) [ 8 ]. Disparities in health outcomes are often linked to unmet healthcare needs, unhealthy lifestyle behaviours, chronic conditions, and social determinants of health [ 9 ]. There is evidence suggesting higher tobacco and substance use among some groups of people with disabilities, particularly at certain ages. A review of U.S. data found higher rates of tobacco use—across cigarettes, e-cigarettes, pipes, hookah, and smokeless tobacco—among adults with disabilities [ 10 ]. Rahmander and Gribben [ 11 ], highlight the long history of the tobacco industry in promoting health disparities through targeted marketing campaigns aimed at vulnerable populations. Studies indicate that teens with disabilities are more likely to smoke than their peers without disabilities [ 12 ], and this trend persists into adulthood [ 13 ]. The same pattern has been observed with e-cigarette use, with teens with disabilities using e-cigarettes at higher rates than those without disabilities [ 14 ]. Among people with disabilities, tobacco use is particularly prevalent in those with mental health conditions [ 15 – 17 ] including anxiety and depression [ 18 ]. For this reason, and to avoid inaccurately or unwittingly conflating tobacco use in the overall population with disabilities, we do not include those with disabilities related to mental health in this study. Tobacco use is strongly linked to social and health inequalities, contributing to economic vulnerability, poor health, and premature death [ 19 , 20 ]. Despite its significance, little is known about tobacco and especially about e-cigarette use among young adults with disabilities. People who are deaf or who have severe hearing impairments, or who are blind or have serious vision impairments, represent minority disability groups experiencing health inequalities. While some studies suggest that Deaf communities may generally exhibit healthier lifestyles, with lower smoking rates in the UK [ 21 ] and the US [ 22 ], hearing loss itself is associated with higher rates of substance use disorder, including smoking [ 23 , 24 ] and other nicotine use [ 10 ]. A recent systematic review found that physical and mental health outcomes for Deaf populations are generally worse than those in the general population [ 25 ] and we identified one qualitative study that reported low e-cigarette usage in Deaf and hard of hearing students [ 26 ]. Some limited evidence points to higher smoking rates among those who are blind or vision impaired [ 27 ] and higher e-cigarette use in this population [ 28 ], but there is little research on smoking and e-cigarette use in this group, particularly among young adults. Nagarajan and Okoli’s [ 12 ] systematic review of adolescents with physical disabilities found them, in nationally representative samples, to be significantly more likely to use tobacco compared to their peers without physical disabilities. Concerns have also been raised about tobacco and alcohol use among individuals with mild-to-moderate intellectual disabilities, with attention being drawn to the increase in people with mild/moderate intellectual disabilities living in the community, leading less restricted lives and being exposed to social and environmental pressures that encourage them to adopt behaviours that impact negatively on their health [ 29 – 31 ]. Few studies have examined smoking prevalence in young people with intellectual disabilities compared to those without. Huxley’s review [ 32 ] found that prevalence rates of substance use in individuals with intellectual disabilities were highly variable, ranging from 0 to 62.9%. The literature comparing smoking prevalence between young people with and without intellectual disabilities is inconsistent, with some studies reporting higher rates [ 33 ], lower rates [ 34 ], and others showing no significant difference [ 35 ]. To date, only one study has compared e-cigarette use in young people with and without intellectual disabilities, finding higher use among female teenagers with intellectual disabilities [ 35 ]. Children, adolescents, and young adults with special needs are often excluded from surveys that collect data on tobacco and nicotine use, often systematically so. For instance, the ESPAD (European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs) survey [ 36 ] in 40 European countries does not include this population if they are not in mainstream schools, resulting in a lack of data on tobacco use among individuals with disabilities, despite their vulnerability to health, educational, and economic inequalities. This gap in data may exacerbate disability-related health disparities and hinder the development of effective health interventions. Our study utilises data from the Growing Up in Ireland (GUI) National Longitudinal Study of Children, specifically the Child Cohort ’98 Wave 4 (2019), when participants were 20 years old. We aim to answer previously unexplored questions about smoking and e-cigarette use in young people with disabilities, comparing them to those without disabilities. The GUI dataset includes extensive information on children, adolescents, and young adults with disabilities, as well as their parents/caregivers. It includes questions about participants’ long-lasting conditions, including mental health, physical, sensory, learning, intellectual, and mobility impairments, across several waves. Wave 4 includes data on both smoking and e-cigarette use. We will also analyse socio-demographic, familial, and individual behavioural data that have been identified as protective and risk factors for tobacco and e-cigarette use [ 36 – 43 ]. These variables include the young person’s sex, activity levels, employment status, age at smoking initiation, and social media use. Familial variables include family wealth, social class, parental education, family structure, household composition, and caregiver smoking behaviour. Peer smoking data from Wave 3, a key predictor of substance use in youth [ 44 ], will also be considered. Additionally, we will examine the role of social media use in influencing tobacco and e-cigarette use, particularly given the significant role of social media marketing in promoting nicotine products among youth [ 45 ]. In this study, we set out to augment the existing scarce data on smoking and e-cigarette prevalence among young adults with disabilities other than disabilities due to mental ill-health. The aims of our study are to: Establish the prevalence of disability in young adults in Ireland. Determine the prevalence of smoking and e-cigarette use in this population. Compare smoking and e-cigarette use rates between young adults with and without disabilities. Examine protective and risk factors for smoking and e-cigarette use in this young adult population. Determine whether young adults with disabilities are as likely as their peers without disabilities to have specific protective or risk factors for smoking/e-cigarette use. Estimate if having a disability is an independent risk factor for smoking and e-cigarette use in young adults. Consider health practice and policy implications. Methods Data source The data we use are from the Cohort ’98 (formerly Child Cohort) of the Growing Up in Ireland (GUI) National Longitudinal Study of Children. This nationally representative cohort study of children living in the Republic of Ireland commenced in 2008 when the children were aged 9 years (Wave 1). For Wave 1 in 2008, a two-stage clustered sampling method was employed to recruit 8,568 children born between November 1997 and October 1998 from the national primary school system, along with their caregivers (82% response rate). These children and their caregivers were surveyed subsequently approximately every four years: Wave 2 in 2012 at age 13 (89% follow-up rate, n = 7,525), Wave 3 in 2016 at age 17/18 (81% follow-up rate, n = 6,216), and Wave 4 in 2019 at age 20 (55% follow up rate, n = 5,190). Representativeness of the analytic sample Our analytic sample comprises weighted data from 4,729 participants who were present in three waves of the GUI study: Waves 4, 3 and 1 (participants in all Waves were also present in Wave 1). Reports on smoking, e-cigarette use, disability and all other variables were drawn from Wave 4, but we derived the variable on peer smoking from Wave 3 as information on peer smoking was only available in that Wave. This combined weighted dataset (Waves 4, 3 and 1) accounts for the difference in numbers between our analytic sample ( n = 4,729) and the Wave 4 only sample ( n = 5,190). The sample weight generated by the GUI study team was applied to ensure the sample was representative of the population in the Republic of Ireland. This adjustment allowed for differential response rates and inter-wave attrition [ 46 ]. Attrition was explained by factors such as refusal to participate, emigration, change of address, and unavailability at the time of surveying, and has been discussed elsewhere in more detail [ 43 , 47 , 48 ]. The young adults and their parents in our analytic sample were comparable to the baseline cohort based on the distribution of background characteristics, such as household income and sex (shown in Supplemental File Table S1 ). Measures The main variables were extracted from the Wave 4 Young Adult Main Questionnaire (chronic health conditions of young adults) and the Wave 4 Young Adult Self-Complete Questionnaire (their smoking and e-cigarette use), but we also used Wave 3 Young Adult responses to establish peer smoking, which was only available from Wave 3. Young adult disabilities Young adult disabilities were measured by whether respondents answered yes or no to the question: “Do you have any of the following long-lasting conditions or difficulties?”. The conditions are: Blindness or a serious vision impairment; Deafness or a serious hearing impairment; A difficulty with basic physical activities such as walking, climbing stairs, reaching, lifting or carrying; An intellectual disability; A difficulty with learning, remembering or concentrating; A difficulty with pain or breathing or any other chronic illness or condition. Answers were initially coded as yes or no to establish the prevalence of each condition (shown in Table 1 below) and subsequently re-coded to having “1 or more conditions” (shown in Table 2 below), providing a composite measure of disability. This composite measure of disability excluded mental ill-health conditions such as anxiety and depression which are reported in full elsewhere [ 18 ]. Young adult smoking and e-cigarette use We examine current smoking and current e-cigarette use rather than ever-smoking and ever e-cigarette use. Current smoking: Those who responded yes to ever smoking a cigarette were asked: “Which of the following best describes you?”, response options: “Only ever tried smoking once or twice”, “Used to smoke but not now”, “Smoke occasionally”, “Smoke daily”. Respondents indicating that they smoked occasionally or daily were classified as “current smokers” while all other responses were classified as not being “current smokers”. Current e-cigarette use: Respondents were asked: “How often, if at all, do you currently use an electronic cigarette?” with response options “Daily”, “Less than daily, but at least once a week”, “Less than weekly, but at least once a month”, “Less than monthly”, “Not at all”. Respondents indicating that they used e-cigarettes less than monthly or not at all were classified as not being “current e-cigarette users” while all other responses were classified as “current e-cigarette users”. Demographic, behavioural, family and peer characteristics associated with young adult smoking Demographic variables were extracted from Wave 4 Young Adult Main Questionnaire: sex (male, female); family income quintile (equivalized to account for household size and composition using the modified Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development equivalence scale and presented in quintiles); household type (one parent, one child; one parent, two or more children; two parents, one child; two parents, two or more children, recoded into “one-parent families”, “two-parent families”). Young adult paid employment status was determined by asking: “Are you currently in paid employment? – do not include the term-time employment or apprenticeship job you told us about already.” Response Options were “Yes”, “No”. Physical Activity was assessed by asking: “How many times in the last 14 days have you done at least 30 mins of moderate-intensity activity, that is activity that causes a small increase in your heart rate and breathing (this includes brisk walking, cycling, swimming and active travel/transport on a daily basis)?” Response options were “None”, “1 to 3 days”, “4 to 6 days”, “7 to 9 days”, “10 to 13 days”, “Everyday”; recoded into a dichotomous variable (“None/1–6 days”, and “7–13 days/Everyday”). Age of the young adult when they first smoked was recorded and coded into a dichotomous variable “16 years or older”, “15 years or younger”. Parental smoking data was extracted from the Parent/Guardian Wave 4 self-complete questionnaire. We include peer use of cigarettes at Wave 3 (when the study participants were 17 years old) as this variable was not available in Wave 4. Peer smoking was measured by asking respondents how many of their regular friends smoke or have ever smoked cigarettes (“None”/”A few”/”Some”/”Most/All”, recoded into “Yes” vs. “No” to “Most or all of my friends smoke”). Social media use in Wave 4 was assessed by asking respondents, “Which of these apps do you use daily/almost daily?” The options provided were Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, LinkedIn, Pinterest, and Google+ (G+), with response options of “Yes” or “No”. Snapchat and Instagram are commonly used platforms for e-cigarette messaging [ 49 , 50 ]. Of these, Snapchat has been identified as the preferred platform [ 49 ] and was selected for the current analysis due to its high usage rates among individuals with and without disabilities. GUI survey A full description of the GUI survey, including the design, instruments and data collection procedures, has been provided elsewhere [ 47 , 48 , 51 , 52 ]. The data, in the form of a Researcher Microdata File (RMF), are archived in the Central Statistics Office and are available to researchers on request and following a formal application, training, and approval process. Ethical approval Ethical approval for the GUI project was granted by the Irish Health Research Board’s Research Ethics Committee. We confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) (e.g., https://www.growingup.gov.ie/pubs/9_Year_CC_Parent_Leaflet.pdf ). Statistical analysis We used descriptive statistics to present sample characteristics. We applied Pearson’s chi-square tests to test for associations between smoking and e-cigarette use in young adults with and without disabilities, and to assess protective and risk factors for smoking and e-cigarette use in young adults with and without disabilities, allowing us to compare departures from the expected distributions. This was followed by logistic regression models to examine likelihood of smoking and e-cigarette use in young adults with and without disabilities, adjusting for demographic, familial, peer, and behavioural variables. In the latter, all associations are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and a P &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Weighting, a minimum information loss algorithm developed by GUI, was used to adjust for differences between the GUI sample and the population at age 9, 13, 17/18 and 20. These weights were applied in all analyses to enable inferences to be made about the entire population from which the study participants were selected. Further details regarding weighting in GUI can be found here: www.gui.com/guide-to-datasets/ . All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27. Results Disabilities among 20-year-olds Table 1 describes the prevalence of disabilities or long-lasting conditions in the study participants. Learning difficulties were the most common disability reported, affecting 10.2% of participants, followed by 5.7% who reported experiencing pain or other chronic conditions and 4.5% who reported intellectual disabilities. Some 1.6% reported blindness or serious vision impairment while 0.9% reported deafness or a serious hearing impairment. Mobility difficulties were reported by 1.5% of respondents. Overall, 18.1% ( n = 855) of participants reported having a disability (Table 2 ). This composite measure included all those who reported having at least one of the long-lasting conditions or difficulties above but did not include respondents who reported psychological/emotional difficulties or mental ill-health. Young adults with disabilities differed significantly from young adults without disabilities on many, but not all, of the protective and risk factors for smoking and e-cigarette use that we assessed in our study (Table 2 ). They were significantly more likely to be in households in the lowest two income quintiles rather than the upper three income quintiles; more likely to have parents who left school earlier, with the Junior Certificate reported as their highest educational attainment; less likely to be in families where parents had professional/managerial occupations; and less likely to be in two-parent households. They were also significantly more likely to have started smoking earlier (at age 15 years or younger) and to report “stress” and “enjoyment” as their main reasons for smoking. A higher number reported being in paid employment and having primary caregivers who smoked but neither of these findings was statistically significant. They were less likely to engage in physical activity and significantly less likely to report daily or almost daily Snapchat use. They were significantly less likely to report peer influence as a reason for smoking and also significantly less likely to report that most or all of their peers had smoked when they were 17 years old. Smoking and e-cigarette use in young adults Table 3 , shows that, among the ( n = 4,729) population of 20-year-olds in our study, 37.6% of young adults currently smoke and 13.5% currently use e-cigarettes. Almost a third (31.3%) were in paid employment, nearly half (47.8%) were moderately or intensively active on many or most days, most (91.7%) were daily Snapchat users, and 29.4% had been 15 years or younger when they smoked their first cigarette. Over a fifth (22.5%) reported having a primary caregiver who smoked (98% of primary caregivers were mothers), and four in every five respondents (80.3%) reported having peers who smoked. Almost a quarter (23.2%) were in one-parent families. Table 3 also shows protective and risk factors for smoking and/or e-cigarette use in 20-year-olds. Protective factors against smoking and e-cigarette use included being female, later age of smoking initiation, and family structure. Partaking in more frequent physical exercise was found to be protective against smoking but not e-cigarette use. Compared with boys, girls were significantly less likely to be current smokers (39.2% vs. 36%) and current e-cigarette users (16.6% vs. 10.2%). Compared with males, females were 13% less likely to smoke and 43% less likely to use e-cigarettes (Table S2 ). Starting to smoke at a later age (16 years or older vs. 15 years or younger) is protective for both smoking (43.9% vs. 69.4%) and e-cigarette use (15.7% vs. 22.9%). Those with delayed smoking initiation were 65% less likely to smoke at age 20 and 38% less likely to use e-cigarettes (Table S2 ). Respondents in two-parent households were 17% less likely to smoke and 30% less likely to use e-cigarettes at age 20 than were those in one-parent households (Table S2 ). Participants who reported engaging in more physical activity were significantly less likely to smoke at age 20 (35.8% vs. 39.5%) but there was no significant difference in e-cigarette use (14.0% vs. 13.00%) (Table 3 ). Risk factors for smoking and e-cigarette use include having caregivers who smoke (44.8% vs. 35.4% smoking; 17.4% vs. 12.5% e-cigarette use) and peers who smoke (42.9% vs. 17.0%; 14.9% vs. 7.0%). Logistic regression analysis in the whole sample estimated the probability of current smoking as OR 3.67 (CI: 3.06–4.42) and current e-cigarette use as OR 2.36 (CI: 1.80–3.09) for young adults with peers who smoked. Those who were in paid employment at age 20 years were also significantly more likely to be smokers (45.1% vs. 34.2%) and e-cigarette users (16.8% vs. 12.0%). Social media engagement (Snapchat use daily or almost daily) was associated with higher smoking (39.4% vs. 31.2%) and e-cigarette use (14.1% vs. 11.3%) but only smoking was statistically significant. Logistic regression analysis revealed increased odds of current smoking among young adults in the highest family income quintile (OR 1.27, Table S2 ) and those with parents holding the highest educational qualifications (OR 1.37). Crosstab analyses (Table 3 ) similarly indicated a higher-than-expected prevalence of current smoking within these groups. However, in these analyses, associations between current smoking/e-cigarette use and socio-economic status variables - household income quintile, household social class, or parental education level - did not reach statistical significance (Table 3 ). Associations between disability and smoking and e-cigarette use Disability was associated with both smoking and e-cigarette use, with significantly higher prevalence in young adults who reported having at least one condition compared with those without any (Table 3 ). Current smoking prevalence was 41.8% in those with a disability compared with 36.7% in those without. Current e-cigarette use was 16.1% compared with 12.9%. In terms of the likelihood of being a smoker, our logistic regression analyses (Table S2 ) show that 20-year-olds with a disability are 24% more likely to be current smokers and 30% more likely to be current e-cigarette users. In multivariable logistic regression having a disability remained significantly associated with cigarette smoking (AOR (95% CI) = 1.54 (1.25, 1.91)) and with e-cigarette use (1.36 (1.08, 1.72)) after adjusting for demographic, familial, peer and behavioural factors (Table S4 ). In summary, young adults with disabilities account for 18.1% of the population of 20-year-olds, but 20% of current smokers, and 22% of current e-cigarette users in this age group. These young adults are disproportionately burdened with several risk factors for smoking and/or e-cigarette use including being more likely to be in one-parent households, and to have started smoking earlier. Moreover, after adjusting for known risk factors, we find that having a disability remains an independent risk factor. Discussion This study provides the first account comparing smoking and e-cigarette use in young adults (20-year-olds) with and without disabilities in Ireland. Young adults with disabilities accounted for 18.1% of all young adults in our study and our findings show a significantly higher prevalence of both smoking and e-cigarette use in this population compared to young adults without disabilities. This is concerning, especially given the health implications of tobacco use for a population already at risk of health inequalities. Young adults with disabilities are more likely than their peers without disabilities to have certain known risk factors associated with smoking and e-cigarette use, while also being less likely to have certain protective factors. Even after adjusting for these protective and risk factors, having a disability remains an additional risk factor for both smoking and e-cigarette use. Specifically, compared to those without disabilities, the odds of being a smoker were 54% higher, and the odds of using e-cigarettes were 36% higher among young adults with disabilities. These higher odds were more pronounced in our adjusted model than in our bivariate analyses, suggesting that nicotine use is more likely in young adults with disabilities, even when accounting for other risk factors. We found that both smoking and e-cigarette use are associated with similar protective and risk factors. However, some characteristics were specifically linked to smoking but not to e-cigarette use. Key factors identified in our study include protective factors (e.g., being female, later smoking initiation, physical activity, and family structure) and risk factors (e.g., having peers or caregivers who smoke, being in paid employment, and social media engagement). Young men with disabilities are particularly vulnerable to both smoking and e-cigarette use. This likely reflects the generally higher smoking prevalence in males [ 43 , 53 ] and suggests the need for gender-sensitive interventions. Previous studies have shown that adolescent males have higher e-cigarette use than do females, though e-cigarette use among adolescent females has been rising at a faster rate [ 44 ]. In the present study, our multivariable model showed that 20-year-old males have significantly higher prevalence of smoking and e-cigarette use than females. However, we note that gender may not be homogeneously associated with smoking and e-cigarette use across different types of disability. For instance, we have previously found higher rates of ever and current smoking among teenage girls with intellectual disabilities compared to their male peers with intellectual disabilities [ 54 ]. Our findings underscore the negative implications of early smoking initiation, as those who started smoking at age 16 or older have significantly lower odds of being a current smoker at age 20. Specifically, not smoking before age 16 is associated with being 65% less likely to be a smoker at age 20. The association between later smoking initiation and e-cigarette use is also evident but less pronounced (38% less likely). Nonetheless, delaying smoking initiation is likely to have positive outcomes for reducing both smoking and e-cigarette use, particularly for young adults with disabilities, who are significantly more likely to start smoking at a younger age than their peers without disabilities. Poverty is a well-established risk factor for smoking, with population smoking prevalence disproportionately higher among low-income individuals. This contributes to a cycle of poverty, as tobacco use diverts household resources away from basic needs and exacerbates poverty-related morbidity [ 5 , 55 ]. Our study found that young adults with disabilities are more likely to reside in low-income households, have parents with lower educational attainment, and belong to households outside the professional or managerial social classes. Surprisingly, there was some evidence indicating that current smoking was more prevalent among young adults from the wealthiest and most well-educated families shown in logistic regression analysis that revealed odds for current smoking among young adults in the highest family income quintile at OR 1.27 (Table S2 ) and among those with parents holding the highest educational qualifications at OR 1.37 (Table S2 ). Crosstab analyses (Table 3 ) similarly showed a higher-than-expected prevalence of current smoking within these groups. The risk for smoking pertained only to the highest income quintile group while there were no significant differences between young adults in the first to fourth income quintiles. who While the most well-off young adults may have access to additional financial resources for smoking, the importance of other unidentified factors cannot be excluded. Being in a two-parent family was associated with lower prevalence of both smoking and e-cigarette use. This aligns with previous studies showing that family structure plays a significant role in health and welfare outcomes [ 43 , 44 , 56 ]. In Ireland, 17% ( n = 220,000) of family households were headed by a single parent in 2022, which may increase the vulnerability of children to unhealthy behaviours in adulthood [ 6 ]. We echo calls for additional supports for children in single-parent families, particularly to reduce risks of unhealthy behaviours [ 56 ]. Young adults with disabilities in our study are more likely to be in single-parent families and therefore have greater needs in this regard. While we did not measure parental monitoring in our study, this factor has been found to mediate the effects of family structure, with higher parental monitoring linked to lower risks of substance use, in all household types [ 57 ], including risk of e-cigarette use [ 44 ]. Caregiver smoking is a significant risk factor for both smoking and e-cigarette use among young adults [ 43 ]. Our study also confirms that those with primary caregivers who smoke have significantly higher rates of smoking and e-cigarette use. Caregiver smoking was higher in those with disabilities in our study, although not statistically significant. Notably, when adjusting for other variables in our multivariable regression model, we found that the influence of parental smoking diminished as individuals transitioned into early adulthood, supporting the social learning theory that suggests parental behaviours may be less influential in adulthood than in adolescence [ 58 ]. Peer smoking is another well-established risk factor for both smoking and e-cigarette use, and our study confirmed these strong associations. Young adults whose peers predominantly smoked at age 17 are nearly four times more likely to smoke and over twice as likely to use e-cigarettes by the age of 20 compared to those with fewer peers who smoked at 17. Young adults with disabilities were significantly less likely to have had peers who smoked at age 17 and were therefore somewhat less at risk in this regard. Nonetheless, peer smoking remains one of the most important risk factors and interventions targeting peer influence are likely to be beneficial for this group as for their peers without disabilities. Paid employment is another known risk factor for teenager [ 59 ] and young adult [ 60 , 61 ] smoking, likely due to increased access to financial resources for purchasing tobacco products. Our study found that being in paid employment at age 20 is a risk factor for both smoking and e-cigarette use. Previous research has suggested that these associations may be mediated by social class, wealth, and education [ 60 ]. Young adults who are in paid employment at age 20 years are less likely to be in higher education and, correspondingly, less likely to benefit from the educational, occupational and social benefits associated with higher education participation. In light of these findings we suggest targeted interventions in the workplace focused on education, prevention, and cessation. We also explored the role of social media in influencing e-cigarette use. Given that social media marketing plays a central role in promoting e-cigarette use [ 45 ], we hypothesized that social media use could predict e-cigarette use. Our analysis of daily or almost daily Snapchat use found a significant association with smoking but not with e-cigarette use. This discrepancy may be due to mixed messaging on social media, where both promotional content for e-cigarettes and health-related prevention messages are prevalent [ 62 ]. Social media platforms do offer unique opportunities for targeted health communication advertisements and one study shows that Snapchat advertisements most commonly used to improve tobacco prevention included a focus on health consequences and on quitting, with resources for cessation [ 62 ]. That study also reported a focus on adverse financial or legal implications of substance use and industry tactics [ 62 ]. As we found that young adults have near universal social media use, it is likely that some influences occur and we recommend more in-depth research on industry strategies for product promotion as well as the use of social media platforms for health promotion. Such approaches could benefit young adults with disabilities whose social media engagement, although lower than that of their peers without disabilities, is still very high. Data on smoking and e-cigarette use were self-reported, which may be subject to recall and reporting bias, potentially leading to underreporting or overreporting of behaviours or conditions. Additionally, participants’ self-reports of doctor-diagnosed conditions were used to classify chronic conditions, which could result in misclassification if participants misinterpreted the question, failed to disclose a condition, or were unaware of their diagnosis. Furthermore, residual confounding may influence the observed associations and an important limitation is that the available list of protective/risk factors in our dataset is not exhaustive. Despite controlling for several behavioural and sociodemographic variables in our study, therefore, the results may still be affected by unmeasured confounders. We recognize that biological sex, which we measured, may not allow respondents to adequately express their gender identities, which would have required more nuanced response options for the variable labelled ‘respondent’s gender’. Despite these limitations, this first study of smoking and e-cigarette use in young adults with disabilities in Ireland, using a large sample from the nationally representative GUI Child Cohort ‘98, provides valuable insights into the relationship between disability, smoking, and e-cigarette use among young adults. These findings can inform future research and public health interventions. The study participants and their parents were comparable to the Wave 1 baseline cohort in terms of background characteristics, such as household income, sex, and parents’ level of education (see Table S1 ). All results were weighted to account for attrition, ensuring that the findings are generalizable to young adults born in 1998 who are still residing in Ireland at age 20. We note the urgency for sensitive and targeted prevention and cessation supports for people with disabilities who at present may be expected to avail of the extant one-size-fits-all supports. This would include identifying and developing disability-specific supports, accommodations and interventions. For example, limited understanding has been found in deaf and hard-of-hearing students’ awareness of the health risks associated with tobacco and nicotine use, but alongside strong interest in learning more [ 26 ]. These young people face significant barriers in accessing health information and prevention materials, particularly in a format that is accessible to them (e.g., sign language interpreters in clinical settings). There is a significant gap in data collection on tobacco and e-cigarette use among young people with disabilities. Many surveys that include young adults with disabilities do not include questions on smoking/e-cigarette use, and many population surveys of smoking and e-cigarette use do not specifically include young adults with disabilities, making the link between disability and tobacco use impossible. In some surveys, young people with disabilities are systematically excluded – for example, if they are not attending mainstream schools (e.g., ESPAD [ 36 ]). If they are included, data on tobacco use may be collected but disability status may not be identified. We suggest that this population must be better represented in research so that tobacco-related and other health vulnerabilities can be identified and addressed. These interventions would primarily benefit this population while also accelerating progress toward a tobacco-free society—an achievement that is only possible if no group in society is left behind. Conclusion Young adults with disabilities face heightened health risks and financial challenges, which are exacerbated by smoking and e-cigarette use. This study reveals that smoking and e-cigarette use are significantly more prevalent among young adults with disabilities compared to their peers without disabilities. It also highlights modifiable risk factors and emphasizes the need for urgent action from caregivers, educators, policymakers, and specialised tobacco cessation health services. Such measures would reduce inequalities and accelerate progress toward achieving the tobacco endgame. Electronic supplementary material Below is the link to the electronic supplementary material.
