Abstract Objective The Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) provides access to nutritional resources to low-income children starting at birth through enrollment in kindergarten or turning five years old. The objective of this study was to quantify the effect of aging out of WIC prior to kindergarten on a child's weight status in kindergarten. Methods This study used WIC certification data merged with a state-wide body mass index (BMI) dataset among kindergarteners measured between 2019 and 2023 in Arkansas and included children who remained enrolled in WIC until at least 30 days before their fifth birthday ( N = 14,045). We used a multivariable linear regression to estimate the association between a child's WIC gap (i.e., the time in days between when a child aged out of WIC at age five and the child's kindergarten start date) and the child's BMI/BMI 95 in kindergarten. Results Among kindergarteners, the mean BMI/BMI 95 was 0.915 and the average WIC gap was 0.56 years. The fully adjusted multivariable regression found no association between the duration of aging out of WIC and BMI/BMI 95 in kindergarten (β = −0.002, 95 %CI = −0.012, 0.008). The association between the duration of aging out of WIC and BMI/BMI 95 in kindergarten remained insignificant even after the population was subset to kindergartners with a free (β = −0.003, 95 %CI = −0.013, 0.007) or paid (β = −0.001, 95 %CI = −0.017, 0.015) meal status. Conclusions Aging out WIC is not associated with a kindergartener's weight status. Additional analyses are needed to better understand the potential implications of extending WIC until a child's sixth birthday. Introduction The Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) is designed to support the health of low-income women, infants, and children under the age of five who are at risk of poor nutrition ( U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024 ). Among participating children, WIC is frequently recognized for its role in improving diets ( Au et al., 2018 ; Chiasson et al., 2013 ; Melo Herrera et al., 2023 ; Zimmer et al., 2022 ). These improvements include increased fruit, vegetable, and whole grain consumption ( Chiasson et al., 2013 ; Zimmer and Vernarelli, 2020 ), a decrease in sugar-sweetened beverages ( Anderson et al., 2022 ) and lower intake of energy-dense foods ( Zimmer and Vernarelli, 2017 ). When children turn five years old and age out of WIC, many will transition to school-based child nutrition programs, the most prominent of which are the National School Lunch Program and School Breakfast Program, which provide free or reduced-price meals to children from lower-income families. However, most children are not yet in school on their fifth birthday when WIC ends. This results in a “gap” in nutrition support until the time the child is eligible to enter kindergarten. Age requirements to enter kindergarten, while varying by state, often require that a child attain the age of five years by July 31 of the year of kindergarten entry. A child that just misses this cutoff with an August 1 birthday would face a gap of 12 months between the time WIC benefits ending and the opportunity to participate in school nutrition programs. Previous studies have shown that aging out of WIC can lead to negative outcomes, including increases in food insecurity ( Arteaga et al., 2016 ; Cho, 2022 ), reductions in consumption of whole grains ( Frisvold et al., 2020 ), and lower overall diet quality as measured by the healthy eating index ( Smith and Valizadeh, 2023 ). There is also evidence that families increase use of charitable food resources when WIC benefits end ( Si and Leonard, 2020 ) and that mothers offset the loss of WIC assistance by lowering their own caloric consumption as children age out ( Bitler et al., 2023 ). Given the potential negative effects of aging out of WIC, Congress is considering extending WIC until the child's sixth birthday or until the child enters kindergarten ( WIC Act of, 2023a ; WIC Act of, 2023b ). WIC provides healthy foods, including whole grains, low-fat diary, lean sources of seafood and plant protein, fruits, and vegetables. Thus, households inclined to consume healthier foods may be more likely to self-select into the program. Self-selection into WIC becomes more problematic as children age. Over 64 % of eligible one-year-olds participate in WIC, but just under 25 % of eligible four-year-olds participate ( Neuberger et al., 2025 ). Disentangling the effect of WIC participation on nutritional outcomes is a research challenge, given preferences for healthier foods that may drive selection into the program ( Fox et al., 2004 ). The WIC gap provides a plausibly exogenous source of variation in program exposure that can enable quasi-experimental designs for causal inference about loss of program access on diet and health. For example, recent work has used regression discontinuity designs to evaluate aging out of WIC on program diet, health, and food procurement outcomes but none have assessed the effect of aging out of WIC on the child's weight status ( Arteaga et al., 2016 ; Smith and Valizadeh, 2023 ; Si and Leonard, 2020 ; Bitler et al., 2023 ). The objective of this study was to estimate the effect of WIC gap (days between aging out of WIC and starting kindergarten) on body mass index (BMI) among kindergartners. The present study links administrative data from the Arkansas WIC program with a unique state-wide database containing BMI screenings in kindergarten to evaluate whether, and to what degree, the WIC gap contributes to changes in BMI. 2. Methods This study was determined to be non-human subjects research by the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences Institutional Review Board (#297196). 2.1. Data sources and study subjects Individual BMI measurements of kindergartners collected from 2019 to 2023 were obtained from the Arkansas BMI panel, which is the result of a legislatively-mandated BMI screening program. Arkansas has the nation's longest running BMI screening program reaching nearly all public schools and covering the majority of children enrolled in kindergarten ( Gee, 2015 ). Primary reasons for not having a BMI assessment include being absent on the day of BMI measurement followed by parent or child refusal to participate in the screenings ( Arkansas Center for Health Improvement, 2023 ). During the assessments, a school nurse with the help of another adult recorder conducted height and weight measurements using a stadiometer and a digital scale. A complete description of protocols and equipment used to assess children's heights and weights is documented in the Arkansas Center for Health Improvement training document entitled BMI Screening Program: Height and Weight Measurement Training Manual ( Arkansas Center for Health Improvement , 2025 ) . The BMI panel was joined with administrative data on WIC certification to verify WIC participation up to the age of 61 months using a de-identified personal identifier for each kindergartner. Additionally, we linked census tract level socioeconomic variables from the Agency for Healthcare and Research Social Determinants of Health Database for the year 2019. We included Arkansas kindergarteners who were continuously enrolled in WIC from birth through 30 days prior to turning five-years old and with non-missing information on study variables ( Fig. 1 ). We decided to include kindergartners in the sample if their WIC certification was terminated fewer than 30 days before they turned 61 months old ( n = 1616) because WIC certification is typically renewed annually ( U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2025 ). Thus, some parents may not see it as worthwhile to recertify a child for only one additional month of benefits. 2.2. Outcome measure The outcome was a continuous measure of a kindergartner's BMI expressed as a ratio of the 95th percentile, determined by the kindergartner's sex and age according to the CDC growth charts (BMI/BMI 95 ) ( Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024 ). The choice of BMI/BMI 95 as the outcome arises from recent findings that measures relative to the 95th percentile on the sex-specific BMI for age reference charts are preferable to BMI z-scores when the sample includes a sizable fraction of observation with severe obesity ( Freedman et al., 2021 ; Hales et al., 2022 ), as was evidenced from the Arkansas BMI panel. Freedman et al. ( Freedman et al., 2017 ) show that the BMI/BMI 95 measure is more strongly correlated with other measures of adiposity than BMI z-scores and are correlated comparably to BMI z-scores across the full distribution of children. 2.3. Measuring the WIC gap The main independent variable of interest was the number of days between aging out of WIC and starting kindergarten. As noted above, children become ineligible for WIC assistance once they turn 61 months old ( U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024c ). Because children are born at different times throughout the year, the duration between aging out of WIC and starting school will vary for each child, depending on their birthday, resulting in significant variation in the length of time a child may go without WIC assistance before accessing school-based nutrition programs. To calculate the WIC gap, we first determined the number of days between the termination of WIC certification and the child's fifth birthday, which could be up to 30 days. We then added this to the number of days between the child's fifth birthday and the start of kindergarten. ( Fig. 1 ). To simplify the interpretation of the coefficients, the number of days was divided by 365, converting the measure from days to years. 2.4. Covariates The analysis incorporated sociodemographic variables, race/ethnicity (classified as White, Black, Hispanic, or other), meal status (paid, free, or reduced), sex (Male or Female), and language spoken at home (English, Spanish, or other). A year fixed effect was also included to account for increases in BMI during the COVID-19 pandemic ( Jenssen et al., 2021 ). All of these measures were assessed from the BMI panel. Additionally, census tract-level data from the Agency for Healthcare and Research Social Determinants of Health Database was linked to census tract by the child's residence to control for the percentage of Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) participants in the census tract because other food assistance programs can influence a child's weight status ( Barroso et al., 2016 ). The median housing cost was also included as a proxy for socioeconomic status ( Oakes, M., 2010 ). 2.5. Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were computed to compare children enrolled in WIC for a full five years to those who participated in WIC for a briefer period. Chi square tests were used to compare differences in categorical variables and t -tests were used to compare continuous measures. Two primary empirical approaches were used to assess the impact of the WIC gap. First, following earlier research, we used a regression discontinuity design ( Cattaneo and Titiunik, 2022 ), which involves a regression model of the form: (1) BMI / BMI 95 = α 0 + α 1 T i + α 2 S i − c + α 3 T i × S i − c + θ X i + ϵ i where i indicates the child, T i is an indicator taking the value of 1 if the child exceeds 60 months of age, S i is the day number for child's date of birth (1 to 365 or 1 to 366 in leap years), c is the cutoff (July 31 = 212 or 213 in leap years), X i is a vector of the study covariates, and ϵ i is a random error term. In eq. (1) , the α 1 coefficient represents the difference in adiposity as measured by BMI / BMI 95 between kindergartners born before the cutoff for kindergarten enrollment (July 31st) compared to children born after the cutoff. Specifically, α 1 represents the numeric impact of a longer WIC gap, as those who were born before the cutoff would have a shorter period of time without some form of food assistance. The α 2 coefficient represents the slope of the outcome before the cutoff or the impact that each additional day before cutoff has on a kindergartner's BMI / BMI 95 . Finally, the α 3 coefficient represents the difference between the slope after the cutoff compared to the slope before the cutoff. The identification assumption for a valid regression discontinuity design is that households have imprecise control over the date of birth relative to the kindergarten eligibility cutoff date. If this could be strategically manipulated, children with birthdays just before or just after the cutoff may have characteristics that differ from those who do not, and this would result in biased estimate of α 1 . To assess the validity of this assumption, we use the test proposed by McCrary ( McCrary, 2008 ) to assess discontinuity in the density of the running variable, S i , around the kindergarten cutoff date. The McCrary test is a diagnostic tool used in regression discontinuity designs to check for potential manipulation of the running variable around the cutoff point. It evaluates whether there is a discontinuity in the density of the running variable at the cutoff, which could indicate that individuals or entities influenced their assignment to treatment or control groups ( McCrary, 2008 ). In addition, the bandwidth or days before and after the cutoff can influence model results ( Cattaneo and Titiunik, 2022 ). In this context, a shorter bandwidth should be less likely to be biased or more comparable to a randomized experiment; however, the shorter the bandwidth the less statistical power and the greater probability of committing a type II error. Thus, we tested a regression discontinuity approach at three separate bandwidths: 30, 60, and 90 days before or after July 31st, which corresponds to the eligibility cutoff date for a kindergartner's fifth birthday. In addition to the regression discontinuity approach, we also used a linear regression model to assess the overall length of the WIC gap (2) BMI / BMI 95 = β 0 + β 1 WIC _ GAP i + γ X i + u i where WIC_GAP i is the length of the WIC gap in years, u i is a random error term, and X i is a matrix of study covariates. In estimating eqs. (1) , (2) , standard errors were estimated with robust standard errors clustered by census tract of residence. 2.6. Sensitivity analyses The statistical analysis included two pre-planned sensitivity analyses. The first involved stratification by household income. Students must be below 185 % of the federal poverty level to qualify for the reduced-price school meals, and below 130 % of the federal poverty level to qualify for free meals ( Feeding America, 2024 ). To qualify for SNAP benefits, families must also have incomes below 130 % of the federal poverty level ( U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024c ). As a result, some students may continue to receive food assistance through SNAP during the WIC gap, while those above 130 % of the federal poverty level would not receive SNAP benefits before starting kindergarten. To evaluate potential differences in weight status between these two groups, additional models were estimated from samples stratified by those who fell at or below 130 % federal poverty level as indicated by eligibility for free school meals. The second sensitivity test addresses the potential influence of the timing during the school year at which a kindergartner's weight is measured on their weight status. In particular, children assessed well into their kindergarten academic year may show less of an effect from the WIC gap since school nutrition will have been available for a longer period of time. Therefore, two additional models were estimated. One model included a covariate for the month in which the child was measured, while the other model focused exclusively on children measured early in the academic year (i.e., August, September, or October). Data pre-processing was carried out in SAS 9.4, while statistical analysis was performed using R 4.2.2 ( The R Foundation, 2023 ). 3. Results The final sample included 14,045 kindergartners who had a valid BMI measurement ( n = 75,022), participated in WIC continuously for 60 months ( n = 14,331), had no missing data for any covariates (n = 14,265), and had reliable BMIs and deidentified personal identifiers for linkage (n = 14,045) ( Fig. 2 ). Among the study sample who remained in WIC for a full five years (n = 14,045), the average WIC gap was just over six months (mean: 0.56 years; median: 0.57 years; IQR: 0.31–0.82 years) for kindergartners before they started school and the BMI/BMI 95 = 0.915 ( Table 1 ). All variables showed statistically significant differences between those enrolled in WIC for a full five years to those who participated in WIC for a briefer period except for sex and weight expressed as BMI/BMI 95 . Further details regarding the final sample can be found in Table 1 . Graphical representation from the regression discontinuity model in eq. (1) for 30, 60 and 90 days surrounding the July 31st kindergarten eligibility cutoff are presented in Fig. 3 . These estimates indicate no significant effect of aging out of WIC as seen by confidence intervals that include 0 for all α 1 estimates regardless of bandwidth. However, these regression discontinuity estimates may be biased because the McCrary test indicates rejection of the null hypothesis of no discontinuity in the density of the running variable with p &lt; 0.01 regardless of whether a 30, 60 or 90-day bandwidth is used. The linear regression results also found that the WIC gap was not meaningfully associated with BMI/BMI 95 (β = −0.002, 95 %CI: −0.012, 0.008; Table 2 ). Evaluating this estimate of the association at the mean WIC gap of 0.56 years suggests no meaningful association with excess BMI (results not shown). For example, a 67-month-old girl at the 50th percentile height of 1.12 m, this effect would translate into just under −50 g of body weight. Several sensitivity analyses support the primary finding that the WIC Gap does not significantly impact a kindergartner's BMI/BMI 95 . Conclusions about the WIC gap are similar for the model adjusted for month of BMI measurement (Model 2 in Table 3 ) and for the model restricted to kindergartners with BMI measurements early in the academic year (Model 3 in Table 3 ). As shown in Table 3 , models stratified by those below (β = −0.003, 95 %CI: −0.013, 0.007) and above (β = −0.001, 95 %CI: −0.017, 0.015) 130 % of poverty as defined by eligibility for free school meals indicated no significant association between aging out of WIC and BMI/BMI 95 . 3.1. Discussion Previous studies have demonstrated that aging out of WIC can lead to increased food insecurity ( Arteaga et al., 2016 ; Cho, 2022 ) and decreased diet quality ( Smith and Valizadeh, 2023 ). However, no research to date has explored the relationship between aging out of WIC and weight status. This study contributes to the literature by investigating the association between the duration without WIC assistance and weight status in kindergarten among a cohort of WIC participants in Arkansas. Two analytic approaches found no association between aging out of WIC and a kindergartner's weight status defined as their BMI expressed as a ratio of the 95th percentile. The tight confidence interval indicates that this is a precisely estimated null effect and that the study was likely adequately powered to detect fairly small differences in weight that might be associated with the WIC gap if one existed. In addition, the robustness checks which account for the month of measurement for the kindergartner's BMI (Model 2 as a covariate and Model 3 as a subset of measurements in August, September, or October) did not change the null finding that the time spent without WIC assistance has no significant association with a kindergartner's weight status. Furthermore, neither the subset of kindergartners with free meals nor the subset of kindergartners with a paid or reduced meal status indicated a significant association between aging out of WIC and BMI/BMI 95 . These findings indicate that the loss of WIC may not have had a greater influence on kindergarteners' weight status among families who earned above 130 % of the federal poverty level, the income threshold for SNAP. Previous studies, have shown that SNAP participation reduces food insecurity ( Bartfeld et al., 2015 ; Swann, 2017 ) and food insecurity is positively associated with obesity ( Kaur et al., 2015 ; Kral et al., 2017 ; Lee et al., 2018 ). However, there was a significant association between a paid meal status and BMI expressed as a percentage of the 95th percentile for a kindergartner within our original model. Other studies consistently show that higher socioeconomic status is associated with lower weight status among children ( Rogers et al., 2015 ; Williams et al., 2018 ). 3.2. Limitations The population consists of kindergartners who participated in WIC for a full five years and reside in Arkansas ( n = 14,045), meaning that this population may not be generalized to others. Additionally, there is a lack of information on families' participation in other food assistance programs like SNAP, which could impact the kindergartners' access to food ( Poblacion et al., 2017 ). However, adjustments were made for tract-level SNAP participation levels, and school meal status was also considered, indicating that any child with a free meal plan would be eligible for SNAP assistance, as both free meals and SNAP require a family to be below 130 % of the federal poverty level. Finally, although both robustness and sensitivity tests indicate a null result and a series of known confounders were controlled for, it is not possible to fully isolate the effects of the variables of interest from the error term ( Yang et al., 2010 ). 4. Conclusions This study provides no evidence of a significant association between the time spent without WIC assistance (due to aging out of WIC at age five) and a kindergartner's weight status. Aging out of WIC has previously been associated with decreased diet quality ( Smith and Valizadeh, 2023 ) and food insecurity ( Arteaga et al., 2016 ; Cho, 2022 ; Bitler et al., 2023 ). These changes in diet quality and food insecurity in the gap between aging out of WIC at age five and enrolling in kindergarten may be too subtle to influence weight status when children enter kindergarten, but the gap may influence other health factors not examined in this study. Thus, policy makers should consider the pros and cons of extending WIC assistance for a child to six years of age ( WIC Act of, 2023a ; WIC Act of, 2023b ).
