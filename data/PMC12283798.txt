Alpha-thalassemia is a common inherited hemoglobin disorder caused by deletions or point mutations in the HBA1 and/or HBA2 genes, leading to reduced or absent synthesis of alpha-globin chains. Although deletional mutations are predominant, non-deletional mutations can also contribute to the clinical spectrum of the disease, with varying severity. This study aimed to characterize two novel mutations in the HBA1 gene, a 230 bp microdeletion and a missense mutation ( NP_000549.1 :p.Ala29Pro), identified in two unrelated Tunisian families. Additionally, we assessed the clinical, hematological, molecular, and in silico structural impact of these mutations. Peripheral blood samples were collected from affected individuals and family members. Hematological parameters were measured using automated cell counters, and hemoglobin fractions were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Genomic DNA was extracted, and common α-thalassemia deletions were screened via gap-PCR. Negative cases underwent sequencing of HBA1 and HBA2 exons. The novel deletion was confirmed by gap-PCR, gel purification, and sequencing. The pathogenicity of the missense mutation was assessed using in silico tools (PolyPhen-2, SIFT, MutationTaster), and structural modeling was performed using Swiss-PdbViewer and DynaMut to evaluate protein stability and flexibility. In Family A, a 4-year-old proband exhibited mild anemia, microcytosis, hypochromia, and low ferritin levels unresponsive to iron supplementation. A novel heterozygous 230 bp deletion ( NC_000016.10 :g.176695_176925del), named Hemoglobin Ariana, was identified in the HBA1 gene, affecting the 5’UTR, exon 1, and part of intron 1. In Family B, a 4-month-old proband presented with co-inheritance of α-thalassemia and Hemoglobin C. Sequencing revealed a novel missense mutation ( NM_000558.5 :c.85G &gt; C, NP_000549.1 :p.Ala29Pro), designated Hemoglobin Tozeur. In silico analysis predicted the mutation as deleterious, with structural modeling indicating destabilization of the α-globin protein, reduced stability, and altered flexibility near the heme-binding region. This study identifies two novel HBA1 mutations associated with α-thalassemia in Tunisia. The combined clinical, molecular, and structural analyses highlight the importance of comprehensive genetic screening and in silico modeling for accurate diagnosis and improved management of thalassemia cases. Clinically, there are two main types: α-thalassemia and β-thalassemia. Globally, more than 5% of the populations are carriers of α-thalassemia (α-thal) mutations [ 1 ]. Unlike β-thalassemia, which is often caused by point mutations, α-thalassemia is primarily due to deletions and, less frequently, point mutations that reduce (α + ) or completely suppress (α 0 ) the synthesis of α-globin genes located on chromosome 16p13.3 [ 2 , 3 ]. Depending on the number of affected α-genes, four classical clinical phenotypes are recognized: silent carrier (–α/αα or α T α/αα), α-thalassemia trait (–α/–α, α T α/α T α, or––/αα), Hb H disease (–α/–– or α T α/––), and Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis syndrome (––/––). Silent carriers typically exhibit a benign phenotype, while individuals with α-thalassemia trait present mild microcytic, hypochromic anemia. Hb H disease displays variable severity, ranging from asymptomatic to moderate or severe anemia with marked microcytosis and hypochromia. The most severe form, Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis, results from the absence of all α-globin genes and is characterized by severe intrauterine anemia and hypoxia, incompatible with life [ 2 – 4 ]. Alpha-thalassemia exhibits significant genetic heterogeneity, with mutations varying widely across different populations. In Mediterranean countries like Tunisia, both deletional and non-deletional mutations contribute to the disease burden. The–α 3.7 (rightward) deletion is the most prevalent α-globin gene defect In Tunisia, accounting for approximately 4.5% of α-thal alleles, followed by polyadenylation (poly A) signal mutations (1.8%) [ 5 – 7 ]. The clinical presentation can be influenced not only by the type of mutation but also by other genetic and environmental factors, leading to considerable phenotypic variability even within the same family. Continuous characterization of novel α-thal mutations and their potential effects is crucial for understanding phenotypic variability and the molecular basis of α-globin gene defects, as well as to shed light on the origin of the molecular defect in the α-globin gene cluster. The aim of this study was to characterize two novel mutations in the HBA1 gene α −230 bp microdeletion and a missense mutation identified in two Tunisian families with α-thalassemia. Furthermore, we aimed to evaluate the clinical, hematological, and molecular profiles of affected individuals, perform in silico structural analyses to predict the functional impact of these mutations. Additionally, this study highlight that silent α-thalassemia may remain undetected and that its coexistence with conditions such as iron deficiency or other environmental factors can help unmask the disease, facilitating earlier diagnosis and management. Materials and methods Study design and ethical considerations This study was conducted to investigate the clinical, molecular, and structural characteristics of alpha-thalassemia cases identified in two Tunisian families from Ariana and Tozeur. A comprehensive approach combining clinical evaluation, hematological analysis, molecular characterization, and in silico structural modeling was employed to elucidate the genetic and functional impact of the identified mutations. The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local ethic committees for scientific research of Bechir Hamza Children’s Hospital, Tunis, Tunisia. All subjects included in the study received genetic counseling and provided written informed consent for the use of their biological samples for research purposes. Biochemical and hematological analysis A routine blood analysis was performed using an automated cell counter (Sysmex XS-1000i; Sysmex Co., Ltd., Kobe, Japan). Patients with hematological data of microcytosis (MCV &lt; 80 fL) and/or hypochromia (MCH &lt; 27pg) were considered as suspected thalassemia carriers. Hemoglobin separation by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was carried out using the β-Thal Short Program on the variant hemoglobin testing system VARIANT II™ (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) as described previously [ 8 , 9 ]. The subjects’ levels of HbA2 &lt; 2.6% or HbA2 &gt; 3.5% or HbF &gt; 2.0%, or with abnormal hemoglobin retention time were considered as abnormal. Serum ferritin was assayed on the Roche Modular E170 (Hoffman-La Roche Ltd., Dee Why, NSW, Australia) using electrochemiluminescence technology. The examination of hemoglobin stability was performed using isopropanol precipitation. The hemolysate was incubated with 40% isopropanol in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 60 min. Unstable hemoglobins precipitate, forming visible turbidity, whereas normal hemoglobins remain in solution. Elevated HbF levels and methemoglobinemia can affect the accuracy of this test, particularly in newborns, where results may be unreliable. Genomic DNA analysis Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral leukocytes using the PureLink ® Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Mutation analysis of the common a -thal deletion, such as the rightward (-α 3.7 ), leftward (-α 4.2 ), -- MedI , and − α 20.5 was performed using multiplex polymerase chain reactions (gap-PCR) according to the previously described methodology [ 10 ]. A molecular weight marker, TIANGEN 500 bp DNA Ladder (Tiangen Biotech Co. Ltd., Beijing, China), was used for agarose gel electrophoresis. This ladder contains multiple bands, with a prominent 2000 bp band for easy orientation. Negative samples for gap-PCR were screened for non- deletional mutation. All exons including exon-intron boundaries of the HBA1 (Gene ID: 3039) and HBA2 (Gene ID: 3040) genes were amplified by PCR. PCR products were then sequenced bidirectionally by SeqStudio™ Genetic Analyzer System (Applied Biosystems) using BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit. The novel deletion was confirmed by gap-PCR using two primers (Forword primer HBA1 F; GCTCCGCGCCAGCCAAT and reverse primer HBA1 R; CTGCTGTCCACGCCCAT). After PCR amplification, the novel bands corresponding to the deleted region were extracted from the agarose gel using the Wizard ® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System and subsequently sequenced to confirm the presence of the mutation. In Silico analysis The prediction of the pathogenicity of the novel missense mutations and its effect on hemoglobin functional and stability was determined by different online prediction programs based upon evolutionary conservation information, amino acid properties, localization of mutated residues in functional sites. In this study, we used PolyPhen-2 [ 11 ], MutationTaster [ 12 ], and SIFT [ 13 ] to assess the pathogenicity of the reported missense mutation. Furthermore, the three-dimensional structure of the Human Hemoglobin alpha chain deposited in the RCSB Protein Data Bank ( https://www.rcsb.org/structure/1A3N ); 1A3N [ 14 ] was used for further Crystallographic analysis to determine the mutation’s location within the protein structure and its potential effects on protein stability and iron binding. The novel mutation was also evaluated using the DynaMut tool server, which provides insights into changes in protein stability and flexibility [ 15 ]. Results Clinical presentation The two families, who both originated from Tunisia, were referred to our laboratory for thalassemia genetic diagnosis with moderate hematological phenotypes. The first case was a 4-years old girl born out of non-consanguineous marriage in northern Tunisia (Ariana) (Fig. 1 ). Her history included only neonatal jaundice. She was healthy and had no history of anemia, jaundice or transfusions until the age of 4 years at which time he was hospitalized for mild anemia with hemoglobin (Hb) level of 11 g/dL, microcytosis (MCV: 64 fL), hypochromia (MCH: 20.4 pg), and an elevated red blood cell (RBC) count (6.27 × 10⁶/µL), consistent with thalassemia traits. Notably, the proband showed reduced ferritin levels (15 µg/L), and iron supplementation failed to correct this deficiency within the expected timeframe, suggesting an underlying hematological disorder rather than simple iron deficiency anemia. The mother (A.II 4 , 28 years old) displayed a normal hematological profile with no significant abnormalities. In contrast, the father (A.II 3 , 33 years old) presented with slightly reduced Hb A2 (2.5%), borderline low MCH (24 pg), elevated RBC count (6.15 × 10⁶/µL), and normal Hb levels (13.5 g/dL), consistent with alpha thalassemia carrier status (Table 1 ). The second case was a 4-month old baby, second child of non-consanguineous parents from Southern Tunisia (Tozeur), born at 9 months gestation (Fig. 1 ). Her neonatal course was marked by mild anemia, neonatal jaundice, which required phototherapy. She was initially diagnosed as heterozygous for hemoglobin C disease. Upon admission in our service, Hematological analyses revealed a mild microcytic hypochromic anemia with the following parameters: Hb 10.0 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume (MCV) 74 fL, mean corpuscular Hb (MCH) 23 pg, Hb A 61.2.5%, low Hb A2 1.4%, HbC 27%, and Hb F 10.4%. The patient exhibited mild iron deficiency, indicated by a ferritin level of 23 µg/L, likely due to insufficient iron intake from breast milk. The proband’s father (B.II 4 ) Hb A2 level was 2.4%, being slightly lower than the normal content, while both her levels of Hb, MCV and MCH were normal with HbC level of 29%. Both of her mother (B.II 3 ) and her brother (B.III 2 ) show normal hematological parameters (Table 1 ). The HPLC profiles of the first family did not reveal any abnormal peaks indicative of hemoglobin variants or abnormal hemoglobin fractions. The chromatograms showed normal hemoglobin components, including HbA, HbA2, and HbF, within expected retention times. In contrast, the HPLC profile of the second family demonstrated the presence of hemoglobin C in proband and father, characterized by a distinct peak at its expected retention time. The isopropanol stability test revealed no evidence of unstable hemoglobin in the first and the second family. All tested samples remained clear after incubation, and spectrophotometric measurements showed no significant decrease in absorbance, confirming the absence of hemoglobin instability. The common pathogenic deletional mutations were screened using GAP-PCR, but no one was found in the analyzed families, leading us to further investigate the causes underlying the proband’s phenotype. In the first α-thalassemia case (Family A), agarose gel electrophoresis of the PCR products from the HBA1 gene revealed two bands differing in size by approximately 230 bp compared to the control, which showed a single expected band of 880 bp. We hypothesize that the slower migrating band corresponds to the wild-type HBA1 sequence (880 bp), while the faster migrating band results from a 230 bp microdeletion. In contrast, PCR amplification of the HBA2 gene in the proband produced a single band of 880 bp, indicating the absence of deletions in HBA2 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1)). To determine the exact location of the breakpoints, the HBA1 PCR product was sequenced using the forward and the reverse primer. The results demonstrated that this was a novel 230 bp heterozygous deletion, ranging from g.176,695 to g.176,925 ( NC_000016.10 :g.176695_176925del using the GenBank numbering), and covering a portion of 5’UTR, all of exon 1 and a portion of IVS I (Fig. 3 ). Therefore, we have named this new α-thalassaemia deletion as Hemoglobin Ariana (α −230pb ). The reported mutation was initially confirmed by reverse complement sequencing, followed by the purification of the band corresponding to the deleted region and its sequencing, which revealed the mutation in the homozygous state. This mutation was detected only in the father in a heterozygous state, whereas the mother was normal. Nucleotide sequencing of the HBA1 and HBA2 gene of the second case (Family B) revealed the presence of a novel heterozygous G to C Transversion at nucleotide position c.85 in exon 1 of HBA1 Gene ( NC_000016.10 :g.176801G &gt; C / NM_000558.5 :c.85G &gt; C). At the protein level, the (c.85G &gt; C) substitution turns the codon for amino acid p.Ala29 (GCC) into Pro29 (CCC) ( NP_000549.1 :p.Ala29Pro). The proband’s father carried the presence of the same missense mutation as in the proband, while the mother and brother did not carry this nucleotide variation (Fig. 3 ). This novel mutation is designated as “Hemoglobin Tozeur’ according to the typical convention of naming hemoglobin sequence variations. The pathogenicity of the NP_000509.1 :p.Ala29Pro mutation was assessed using PolyPhen-2 and SIFT. PolyPhen-2 predicted the variant as “probably damaging” with a high confidence score, indicating a significant impact on protein function. SIFT classified the mutation as “deleterious,” suggesting potential impairment in protein stability and functionality. Both tools support the pathogenic nature of the mutation. Further in silico predictions were conducted using MutationTaster, which identified the variant as “disease-causing” based on its evolutionary conservation and potential to disrupt normal protein function. The analysis indicated that replacing alanine with proline introduces structural rigidity due to proline’s cyclic structure, potentially interfering with local secondary structure elements. For structural analysis, the wild-type protein structure was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), and the p.Ala29Pro mutation was modeled using Swiss-PdbViewer. Structural visualization using revealed that the Ala29 is located in the middle of the second helix and buried in the core of the protein. The introduction of proline, a residue known for its helix-breaking propensity, disrupted the local α-helical structure, causing a kink that could destabilize the protein’s overall folding. Furthermore, the reported mutation is located near the HEME group and it substitution could affect the oxygen affinity (Fig. 4 a). Protein stability analysis was performed with DynaMut, which predicted a decrease in structural stability upon mutation. The ΔΔG value was calculated as -0.328 kcal/mol, indicating destabilization. The flexibility analysis showed decreased vibrational entropy around the mutation site (ΔΔS Vib ENCoM: -0.321 kcal.mol -1 .K -1 ), reflecting reduced stability and altered dynamic behavior. Notably, the mutation led to the loss of key hydrogen bonds that were crucial for maintaining the structural conformation in the wild-type protein (Fig. 4 b). On the other hand, the replacement of alanine, a helix-stabilizing residue, with proline, commonly found in loop regions due to its high flexibility, causes structural alterations and introduces a bend in the helix (Fig. 4 c). These findings collectively suggest that the NP_000509.1 :p.Ala29Pro mutation has a deleterious effect on the protein’s structure and function, contributing to its pathogenic potential. The structural disruptions observed are consistent with the results of the pathogenicity prediction tools, underscoring the mutation’s role in the observed clinical phenotype. Discussion Alpha-thalassemia is a prevalent hemoglobinopathy characterized by reduced or absent synthesis of alpha-globin chains, leading to a spectrum of clinical manifestations. The silent carrier state, resulting from a single alpha-globin gene deletion (-α/αα), often remains undetected due to minimal hematological abnormalities [ 2 , 3 ]. However, its coexistence with other conditions, such as iron deficiency anemia (IDA) like described in this study can complicate diagnosis and influence clinical outcomes [ 16 ]. Indeed, silent alpha-thalassemia carriers typically exhibit normal red blood cell indices, making routine detection challenging [ 4 ]. Standard hematological tests may not reveal significant anomalies, leading to underdiagnosis as shown in both fathers of the analyzed families A and B. Accurate diagnosis of alpha-thalassemia is crucial for appropriate management. Carriers of alpha-thalassemia, both α 0 or α + generally do not need treatment [ 17 ]. However, in our case, treatments such as iron supplementation should be administered only after excluding alpha-thalassemia, as unwarranted iron therapy can lead to iron overload in these patients [ 18 ]. Comprehensive diagnostic approaches, including molecular testing, are essential to distinguish between IDA and thalassemia traits, ensuring that patients receive appropriate and effective treatment. The 230 bp microdeletion ( NC_000016.10 :g.176695_176925del) detected in Family A spans from g.176695 to g.176925, affecting the 5’ untranslated region (5’UTR), exon 1, and part of intron 1. Examination of the HBA1 sequence revealed the presence of an identical five nucleotide repeat (5’-TCCCC-3’) flanking the breakpoint, suggesting that the microdeletion may have arisen as a result of an unequal crossover and reciprocal recombination in the HBA1 gene. Deletions of this size are rare and may disrupt gene expression by removing critical regulatory sequences necessary for transcription initiation and mRNA stability. Similar deletional mutations in HBA1 have been associated with diverse phenotypic outcomes depending on the extent of the deletion and the affected regulatory elements [ 3 , 19 ]. In this case, the deletion was detected in the heterozygous state in the proband and father. The proband exhibited mild anemia with microcytosis and hypochromia, while the father showed borderline hematological abnormalities. Interestingly, iron supplementation failed to correct the proband’s anemia, emphasizing the importance of differentiating α-thal from iron deficiency anemia through molecular diagnostics. In Family B, the proband presented with co-inheritance of α-thalassemia and Hemoglobin C. Hb C is characterized by the substitution of lysine for glutamic acid at position 6 of the beta-globin chain, leading to hemoglobin precipitation and mild hemolytic anemia. The presence of alpha-thalassemia can reduce the overall globin chain imbalance, thereby decreasing hemoglobin precipitation and ameliorating clinical symptoms [ 20 ]. For example, in a family study, both the patient and the father exhibited Hb C levels of 27% and 29%, respectively, suggesting that the co-inheritance of alpha-thalassemia may have contributed to these relatively moderate Hb C concentrations. The structural analysis highlighted the mutation’s proximity to the heme group, suggesting a potential impact on oxygen affinity. Previous studies have shown that mutations near the heme pocket can significantly alter hemoglobin’s functional properties, leading to conditions such as Hb H disease and other unstable hemoglobinopathies [ 21 ]. On the other hand, the wild type residue in the mutation NP_000509.1 :p.Ala29Pro is a small, helix-stabilizing amino acid, while the mutant (Pro29) introduces structural rigidity, often disrupting α-helices. This substitution likely affects protein folding and stability, as demonstrated by in silico predictions indicating decreased protein stability and altered flexibility. At the same position where Hemoglobin Tozeur (p.Ala29Pro) was identified, a previously described variant in Pakistani patient (Hb Bramall Lane: p.Ala29Thr), detected during routine antenatal screening, was associated with mild erythrocytosis [ 22 ]. In contrast, Hemoglobin Nedlands (p.Ala29Val) was reported as a silent variant without any impact on hematological parameters [ 23 ]. We hypothesize that Valine and Alanine share similar physicochemical properties, explaining the lack of clinical effect in Hb Nedlands. However, Proline in our case, and Threonine in Hb Bramall Lane, differ significantly from Alanine in terms of structure and flexibility, potentially affecting protein conformation and stability. Therefore, conducting in silico analyses based on structural modeling is crucial for understanding the pathogenic potential of such mutations and supporting accurate diagnosis. The correlation between genotype and phenotype in α-thalassemia is influenced by the type of mutation, zygosity, and the presence of co-inherited hemoglobin variants. Deletional mutations typically result in more severe phenotypes when present in homozygous or compound heterozygous states. In contrast, non-deletional mutations, such as missense variants, can have variable effects depending on their impact on protein structure and function [ 24 ]. In general, non-deletional defects involving the HBA1 gene are less severe because this gene encodes two to three times less α-globin compared to the HBA2 gene [ 25 ]. As a result, genetic variants in HBA1 are often associated with silent α-thalassemia and may go undetected unless discovered incidentally or in coexistence with other diseases, as observed in the families analyzed in this study. The phenotypic variability observed in this study underscores the need for comprehensive genetic analysis, including sequencing and in silico modeling, to accurately predict disease severity. This approach can guide clinical management, particularly in regions with high prevalence of hemoglobinopathies. This study primarily relied on in silico analysis to assess the structural and functional impact of the identified HBA1 mutations. While computational modeling provides valuable insights into protein stability and pathogenicity, the absence of in vitro functional assays remains a limitation. Experimental validation through protein expression studies and biophysical characterization would further confirm the effects of these mutations on hemoglobin function. Given the high prevalence of thalassemia in Tunisia and other Mediterranean countries, identifying novel mutations contributes to national screening programs and genetic counseling efforts. Understanding the molecular basis of α-thalassemia enables targeted carrier detection, prenatal diagnosis, and informed reproductive choices. Genetic counseling is particularly important for families with identified mutations, as it provides information on inheritance patterns, recurrence risks, and potential clinical outcomes. In regions with high rates of consanguinity, such as North Africa, these efforts are crucial for reducing the burden of severe hemoglobinopathies. Conclusion This study reports two novel mutations in the HBA1 gene associated with α-thalassemia in Tunisian families: a 230 bp microdeletion (Hemoglobin Ariana) and a missense mutation (p.Ala29Pro, Hemoglobin Tozeur). The combined clinical, molecular, and structural analyses highlight the genetic diversity and complex genotype-phenotype relationships in α-thalassemia. Our findings underscore the importance of comprehensive genetic testing for accurate diagnosis, management, and genetic counseling in affected populations.
