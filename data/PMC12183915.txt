Abstract Background The causal associations between behavioral factors (BFs) and the risk of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and whether liver function mediates these associations, remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to assess these associations. Methods We performed two-sample Mendelian randomization (2SMR) and multivariable MR (MVMR) analysis using summary-level data to assess the associations between BFs and NAFLD. The linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) was used for genetic correlation analysis. Additionally, we utilized NHANES database to assess dose–response relationships. Furthermore, we applied two-sample MVMR approach based on Bayesian model averaging (MR-BMA) to identify the most influential liver function index as a mediating factor, and performed mediation analysis. Results 2SMR results showed that leisure screen time (ST, β = 0.414, P = 5.91e-6) and smoking initiation (SI, β = 0.164, P = 0.012) were associated with NAFLD risk with no reverse causality. LDSC supported these associations (SI: rg = 0.291, P = 1.04e-8; ST: 0.518, P = 5.41e-21). However, MVMR showed that only ST was independently associated with NAFLD (β = 0.334, P = 4.6e-5). There was a linear relationship between ST and NAFLD, and NAFLD risk increased significantly after 5 h of ST. Alanine transaminase level was the most influential index (ALT, MIP = 0.452) and mediated 54% of the ST-NAFLD association. Conclusion ST is independently associated with an increased risk of NAFLD. It is recommended to avoid more than 5 h of ST per day. ALT is the most influential liver function index associated with NAFLD and mediates the ST-NAFLD association. Introduction Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common chronic liver disease worldwide. The global prevalence is estimated at 32.4% and more than half of the adult population is expected to have NAFLD by 2040 [ 19 , 28 ]. Due to its insidious onset, about 20–30% of NAFLD patients develop MA steatohepatitis, which leads to complications such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, posing a substantial burden on public health [ 4 , 36 ]. Behavioral factors (BFs) are one of the major causative factors for NAFLD [ 11 ]. It is indisputable that avoiding risk BFs is beneficial in reducing the risk of NAFLD [ 39 ]. While previous studies have investigated the associations between various BFs and NAFLD, the relationships between specific BFs and NAFLD remain controversial [ 29 ]. These conflicting results are partly attributed to the synergistic interaction between BFs, which is hard to avoid in observational studies. In addition, the dose–response relationships between BFs and NAFLD remain controversial. Liver function indexes are often used to predict NAFLD in clinical practice. In general, abnormally elevated levels of liver enzymes and secretions in routine blood examination suggest that the patient needs further magnetic resonance imaging. However, it remains unknown which liver function index is most strongly associated with NAFLD risk. Additionally, changes in liver function have been observed in people with different BFs, but whether liver function mediates the association of BFs with NAFLD remains unknown [ 1 ]. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a natural randomized controlled trial method using genetic variation as instrumental variables. Since genetic variation is randomly assigned at conception, MR can minimize confounding factors and reverse causation. In this study, we assessed the causal associations between BFs and NAFLD using 2SMR. A series of sensitivity analyses and LDSC were performed to further support the results. MVMR then identified BFs independently associated with NAFLD. Subsequently, we assessed the dose–response relationships between the identified BFs and NAFLD using samples from the NHANES database. Furthermore, we used MR-BMA to identify the most influential liver function index as the mediating factor, and conducted mediation analysis to reveal the potential mechanism underlying the BF-NAFLD association. Methods Data source Our research flowchart is shown in Fig. 1 . Earlier studies have reported in detail the three core assumptions of MR analysis (Independence assumption, Relevance assumption, and Exclusion restriction assumption). Our study was conducted according to the STROBE-MR guideline [ 32 ] (Supplementary Table S1). Details of the data sources are in Supplementary Table S2. All the population is of European descent. 2SMR and sensitivity analysis We assessed the causal associations between BFs and NAFLD using 2SMR. To obtain eligible IVs for the exposure, we selected SNPs with genome-wide significant association ( P &lt; 5e − 8) and removed linkage disequilibrium (LD, clumped at r 2 &lt; 0.001, 10000 kb using European samples from the 1000 Genomes Project). Weak IVs (F &lt; 10) were removed [ 27 ]. In addition, when the number of IVs obtained was insufficient, the association threshold was relaxed to 5e-6 to obtain more IVs associated with the exposure. In the preliminary discovery phase, we used inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method to obtain causal estimates. We further employed Cochran’s Q statistic, MR- pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO), and MR-Egger intercept tests to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity. When an outlier was detected, we removed the outlier and re-performed MR analysis. Reverse 2SMR analysis and LD Score regression (LDSC) were used to further assess the directionality and genetic correlation of the associations identified by 2SMR. In reverse 2SMR, NAFLD was used as the exposure and BFs as outcomes, using the same analytical methods as in 2SMR. MVMR We conducted MVMR to estimate the independent effect of each exposure on NAFLD. The IVs used in MVMR must be associated with at least one of the exposures. Therefore, we first used 5e-8 to obtain SNPs strongly associated with each exposure, and then took the intersection and removed LD [ 31 ]. Dose–response relationships analysis We used a weighted logistic regression model combined with a restricted cubic spline (RCS) to assess the dose–response relationships between BFs and NAFLD. First, we weighted the samples using the weights provided in the demographic data, and standardized the weights to reduce potential bias. We used logistic regression to fit the 3 models with consideration of covariates (Supplementary Table S9), and calculated the OR values to assess the association of BFs with NAFLD. Three nodes were set according to the distribution of exposure data, and the significance of linear and nonlinear associations was evaluated using Wald ratio based on logistic regression. Finally, the RCS curve was plotted to visualize the dose–response relationships. MR-BMA Given the variety of liver function indexes, the use of MVMR may cause collinearity problems. However, MR-BMA can detect true causal factors even when many candidate factors are highly correlated [ 44 ]. Therefore, we used MR-BMA to identify the most influential liver function index as the mediator. In MR-MBA, prior probability was set to p = 0.1, and variance was set to 0.25. The acquisition of IVs was consistent with MVMR. In addition, the maximum capacity of the model was determined by the number of candidates, with 1000 iterations in the shotgun stochastic search. We calculated the posterior probability (PP), marginal inclusion probability (MIP), and model-averaged causal effects (MACE) of each exposure. Subsequently, 1000 permutations were performed to calculate the empirical P -value. Mediation analysis We conducted mediation analysis to explore whether the most influential liver function index identified by MR-BMA mediated the association between BFs and NAFLD. The mediating effect was calculated by (β1*β2)/β0; the direct effect was calculated by β0-(β1*β2) [ 3 ]. Statistical analysis All analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.3.2). P &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results SI and ST were associated with NAFLD among 10 BFs We used 5e-8 to obtain IVs for 9 traits, and 5e-6 was used for the other 1 trait (Same-sex sexual behaviour) due to insufficient IVs. The F statistics of SNPs were all greater than 10 (Supplementary Table S3). IVW results showed that SI (β = 0.164, OR = 1.178, 95%CI:1.037–1.338, P = 0.012, FDRq = 5.91e-5) and ST (β = 0.414, OR = 1.512, 95%CI:1.264–1.808. P = 5.91e-6, FDRq = 0.06) were associated with an increased risk of NAFLD, while other BFs had no significant association (Supplementary Table S4; Fig. 2 ). After FDR correction, only the association between ST and NAFLD remained significant. Nevertheless, as SI showed a significant association before correction and suggested a potential link, it was retained for further analyses. It is worth noting that in the literature providing the data source, ST is considered a proxy for sedentary behavior. Therefore, it is also regarded as sedentary behavior in this context. Sensitivity analysis and LDSC further supported 2SMR results Using NAFLD as the exposure, reverse MR analysis found no significant reverse causal association (Supplementary Table S5). MR-PRESSO global test found outliers for ST, tea intake, alcoholic drinks per week, and coffee intake. Therefore, we re-performed MR analysis after removing the outliers, and found that the results remained (Supplementary Table S5). LDSC results showed that SI (rg = 0.291, se = 0.051, P = 1.04e-8) and ST (rg = 0.518, se = 0.055, P = 5.41e-21) were positively correlated with NAFLD (Supplementary Table S6). MVMR supported independent associations between ST and NAFLD Given the complex correlations between BFs as reported in previous studies, we performed MVMR to identify independent causal associations. MVMR results showed that after adjusting for SI, ST was still associated with NAFLD (β = 0.334, OR = 1.396, 95%CI:1.189–1.64, P = 4.6e-5) (Supplementary Table S7; Supplementary Table S8). However, SI was no longer significantly associated with NAFLD after adjusting for ST, suggesting that this association observed in 2SMR may be influenced by ST. For this reason, SI were excluded from subsequent analyses. There was a linear relationship between ST and NAFLD After a series of screenings, we obtained 7787 samples from the NHANES database (2017–2020; https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/ ) to assess dose–response relationships between ST and NAFLD (Supplementary Table S9; Supplementary Table S10). Baseline table is shown in Supplementary Table S11. Of the 7787 participants, 2952 NAFLD patients were identified based on CAP, with significant differences in ST and covariates. After adjusting for covariates, we found significantly increased risk for 5–10 h (OR = 1.20, 95%CI: 1.037–1.409) and 10–15 h (OR = 1.695, 95%CI: 1.345–2.135) when using 0–5 h as the reference (Supplementary Table S12). Furthermore, we found that there was no nonlinear relationship between ST and NAFLD ( P = 0.986), but rather a linear relationship ( P &lt; 0.001; Fig. 3 ) (Supplementary Table S13). MR-BMA identified ALT as the most influential mediator To further reveal the potential mechanisms underlying the association between ST and NAFLD, MR-BMA was applied to identify the most influential liver function index. According to MIP, ALT was the most influential index (PP = 0.326, MIP = 0.452), and was positively associated with NAFLD risk (causal estimate = 0.421, MACE = 0.239, empirical P = 0.019). However, none of the other indexes had significant association (Supplementary Table S14; Supplementary Table S15). ALT mediated the association between ST and NAFLD To assess whether liver function mediated the ST-NAFLD association, we performed a two-step mediation analysis. It was found that ST was significantly associated with an increased level of ALT (β1 = 0.126), while ALT was significantly associated with an increased risk of NAFLD (β2 = 1.774). We further calculated the proportion mediated and found that ALT mediated 54% of the ST-NAFLD association (Supplementary Table S16; Fig. 4 ). Notably, the ALT measurements (Field 30,620) were derived from baseline serum samples. The MASLD diagnoses were extracted from Hospital Episode Statistics (HES-APC) ICD-10 codes (Fields 41,270), requiring hospitalization events, thereby minimizing potential sample overlap. Discussion In this study, the preliminary 2SMR analysis identified the causal associations of ST and SI with NAFLD. Subsequently, genetic correlation analysis and sensitivity analysis enhanced the robustness of the results. Further MVMR analysis found that ST was associated with NAFLD independent of SI. MR-BMA and mediation analysis identified ALT as an important causative factor for NAFLD and mediated the association between ST and NAFLD. There was no independent causal association between SI and NAFLD. In contrast to our findings, some studies have reported an association between SI and the risk of NAFLD, even in the case of passive smoking [ 17 , 35 ]. Interestingly, a previous study reported that the prevalence of NAFLD in former smokers was not lower than that in current smokers and was partially associated with an increase in BMI after quitting, suggesting that quitting smoking does not protect against NAFLD and that confounding factors may play a role [ 40 ]. Our study found no significant association between SI and NAFLD after adjusting for ST, indicating that previous observational studies may have been influenced by confounding factors. We ventured to speculate that it may be the interaction between SI and ST-induced metabolic changes that led to the non-significant association after adjustment, such as synergistic effect between BMI and active smoking [ 21 ]. Consistent with our findings, a cross-sectional study of healthy Mexico population used ultrasonography to examine 368 current smokers and 565 people who had never smoked, and found no difference in the prevalence of NAFLD between non-smokers and current smokers [ 6 ]. Notably, there were no statistically significant differences in obesity, fasting blood glucose levels, or prevalence of metabolic syndrome among the study population. A MR study by Yuan et al. found that SI was independently associated with NAFLD after adjusting for alcohol consumption. This was not surprising since it adjusted for uncorrelated variable, alcohol consumption, which was found to have no significant association with NAFLD in both our and their MR analyses [ 37 ]. ST was independently associated with NAFLD risk, and there was a linear relationship. Consistent with our findings, prior observational studies found that ST was associated with an increased risk of MASLD [ 13 , 15 ]. Some other 2SMR analyses have also found association between ST and an increased risk of NAFLD [ 41 ]. In addition, several studies have reported that sedentary behavior is an independent risk factor for NAFLD [ 7 , 18 ]. Our further analysis found a significant increase in NAFLD risk after 5 h of ST. Ryu et al. reported that &gt; 5 h/day sitting time significantly increased the risk of NAFLD compared to &lt; 5 h/day [ 30 ]. Tsunoda et al. found that the association between ST and NAFLD risk was not significant after adjusting for BMI. However, the basic physiological indexes of their subjects were low, including BMI, AST, and ALT, limiting its generalizability to a broad population [ 33 ]. Our study found that ALT was the most influential causal factor for NAFLD among 8 liver function indexes. The relationship between ALT and NAFLD is incontrovertible [ 5 ]. ALT is considered a potential biomarker of NAFLD, and is often used for routine blood examination prior to ultrasonography in clinical diagnosis. Previous studies reported that ALT was second only to ultrasound in diagnosing NAFLD in children or adults in accuracy, and was superior to other markers such as AST [ 9 , 25 ]. One important reason for this is that ALT is predominantly distributed in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes and has the highest activity in liver compared with in other tissues [ 2 ]. ST may affect NAFLD risk through ALT. In line with our findings, a prospective study of 10,385 Hispanics/Latinos in the United States found a significant independent association between ST and elevated ALT levels [ 20 ]. Another study adjusted for confounding factors and found that ST was independently associated with ALT [ 16 ]. However, the specific mechanism by which ST increased ALT is largely unknown, but it is generally accepted that it may involve weight, insulin resistance, visceral fat, and dyslipidemia [ 10 ]. The elevation of ALT levels may stimulate lipogenesis, which is activated by impaired cellular redox potential and/or hyperinsulinemia, thus promoting the onset and development of NAFLD [ 24 ]. Meanwhile, ST may also increase ALT levels by promoting fat accumulation, thus creating a vicious cycle and exacerbating the development of NAFLD. We found no significant association between the other 8 BFs and NAFLD. Among them, one of the most controversial is the association between MVPA and NAFLD. Consistent with our findings, some studies found no significant association between MVPA and NAFLD, but VPA was associated with a reduced risk of NAFLD [ 7 , 41 ]. As noted in previous studies, exercise intensity appears to be an important factor in reducing NAFLD risk, and VPA may be superior to MVPA [ 23 ]. Additionally, studies have reported that napping, homosexual sex, tea, alcohol, coffee intake, and fruit intake are associated with NAFLD [ 8 , 12 , 26 , 34 , 38 ]. However, we, like some other studies, found no significant association [ 14 , 22 , 37 , 42 , 43 ]. Such inconsistent observations may be partly explained by confounding factors, measuring method, subjects, and assessment of steatosis. Therefore, larger cohorts or GWAS datasets may be warranted in the future to further explore these associations. Our research has some advantages. First, this is the first study to use MR-BMA to analyze and rank liver function indexes. Second, the MR design minimized reverse causality and confounding factors such as environment. Third, our study largely excluded bias from pleiotropy. Finally, LDSC and sensitivity analysis further enhanced the robustness of our results. Several limitations need to be noted in our study. First, like in any MR study, horizontal pleiotropy was inevitable in our study. Second, although numerous studies have supported the association between ST and NAFLD risk, the sedentary type, duration, and frequency remain unknown and merit further investigation. Third, based on summary-level data, it was difficult to further investigate whether the observed associations differed by age or gender. Fourth, the population of the dataset is predominantly of European origin, limiting its generalizability to other populations. Although our MR analysis strictly adhered to established guidelines and revealed no significant evidence of horizontal pleiotropy through rigorous sensitivity analyses, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of residual undetected pleiotropy. Such pleiotropic effects may introduce hidden confounding, potentially biasing causal estimates. Furthermore, while we minimized sample overlap by utilizing distinct data sources for exposure and outcome, complete elimination of overlap was impracticable. This partial overlap may increase type I error rates and undermine the precision of causal inferences. These limitations reflect inherent challenges in large-scale MR studies but do not invalidate our primary findings, which were robust across multiple pleiotropy-robust methods and sensitivity analyses. Finally, we individually assessed BFs associated with NAFLD. It is suggested that an integrated evaluation of BFs be carried out in future studies, such as multi-behavioral risk prediction models that can combine multiple BFs and calculate weighted averages to be assigned to each BF, thus developing personalized models. Conclusion ST is independently causally associated with NAFLD among 10 behavioral factors. It is recommended to avoid more than 5 h of ST per day. ALT is the most influential liver function index of NAFLD and mediates the association between ST and NAFLD. Supplementary Information Supplementary Material 1.
