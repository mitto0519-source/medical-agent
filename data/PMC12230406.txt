Abstract Objective To evaluate current administrative coding practices for opioid misuse (OM) within the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD) framework and develop standardized documentation recommendations. Design Systematic scoping review following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Setting Analysis of studies using administrative databases, including electronic health records (EHRs), insurance claims, and national healthcare utilization databases. Subjects Studies published in peer-reviewed journals examining administrative codes for OM, excluding those focused solely on illicit drugs, opioid use disorder (OUD), or using only natural language processing/qualitative methods. Methods Comprehensive search of Embase, Medline, Google Scholar, and PubMed databases following PRISMA-S extension guidelines. Three independent reviewers screened articles and extracted data. Study quality was assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Results Of 9561 initial records, 19 studies met inclusion criteria. The use of ICD-10 code F11.9* (Opioid use) emerged as the most referenced method for documenting OM, distinguishing it from OUD methods (F11.1, opioid abuse; F11.2, opioid dependence). Studies demonstrated significant heterogeneity in coding practices, resulting in code-based definitions identifying only approximately 50% of cases compared to more comprehensive clinical assessment approaches. Conclusions While ICD-10 code F11.9* can effectively document OM as distinct from OUD, successful implementation requires consensus on the clinical definition of OM and documentation in the form of clear clinical guidelines and operationalized through enhanced EHR integration. Opioid misuse (OM) represents a complex clinical phenomenon that is related to, but distinct from, opioid use disorder (OUD) and other opioid-related harms. 1 While frequently encountered in clinical practice, OM presents unique challenges for research and surveillance due to its lack of standardized definition and diagnostic criteria. 2 While OM is notably absent from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, 5th edition (DSM-5), 3 which focuses specifically on psychiatric disorders, it also lacks precise clinical criteria in the World Health Organization’s (WHO) International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10), 4 which covers a broader range of clinical scenarios including disordered use. Current definitions encompass multiple dimensions, including the medication’s prescription status, 5–7 intentionality of misuse, 8 , 9 pursuit of euphoric effects, 6 , 10 , 11 and presence of hazardous use patterns. 8 The concept of “loss of control” traditionally associated with substance use disorders presents another definitional challenge, as it may manifest differently in OM compared to OUD. While loss of control over use is a hallmark of OUD, in OM it may present as isolated incidents of taking more than prescribed without meeting full OUD criteria. This conceptual ambiguity is reflected in the literature’s inconsistent terminology, where OM is used interchangeably with terms such as non-medical use, 6 , 7 , 10 , 12–14 problematic use, 15–17 and prescription drug abuse. 18 , 19 The National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) has attempted to provide clarity by defining OM as “taking a medication in a manner or dose other than prescribed; taking someone else’s prescription, even if for a legitimate medical complaint such as pain; or taking a medication to feel euphoria”. 10 However, this definition, while helpful, does not align precisely with available diagnostic frameworks. The clinical identification of OM typically relies on direct patient care approaches, including structured screening tools, prescription fill pattern analysis, urine drug screening, and clinical observation. 5–11 , 20–23 While these methods are valuable for individual patient care they have limitations. 24–26 Population-level research and surveillance necessarily depend on large-scale administrative datasets, including electronic health records (EHRs) and medical claims data. 27–29 This creates a fundamental challenge: how to translate the complex clinical concept of OM into standardized diagnostic codes. 29 This challenge is particularly evident within the ICD-10 framework. 4 , 30 While the WHO provides specific codes for opioid abuse (F11.1) and dependence (F11.2) that correspond to DSM-5 diagnostic categories of mild and moderate/severe OUD, respectively, 3 , 31 there is no similar crosswalk for the concept of OM. The system includes codes for “opioid use, unspecified” (F11.9), but its application in clinical practice and administrative datasets remains inconsistent. 32 This coding limitation forces researchers to rely on potentially proxy measures that may not accurately capture the intended population. 2 The disconnect between conceptual definitions and coding frameworks has led to substantial heterogeneity in research approaches. While qualitative research and natural language processing (NLP) of clinical notes offer valuable insights into the nature and context of OM, 24–26 healthcare quality metrics, research protocols, and population health surveillance primarily rely on structured diagnostic codes. 27–29 In this systematic scoping review, 33 we map the landscape of how clinicians and researchers operationalize OM within observational data, particularly focusing on documentation practices within the constraints of administrative codes. We examine current approaches to defining and coding OM, evaluate existing methods, and analyze implementation challenges. Through this systematic examination of the existing literature, we aim to characterize how this well-recognized clinical concept is captured in administrative data, identify gaps in current coding practices, and provide recommendations to improve both documentation and future research. Methods Search strategy Our methodological approach followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses—Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines and PRISMA Statement for Reporting Literature Searches in Systematic Reviews (PRISMA-S) ( Figure 1 , Supplementary Material S1 and S2 ). 34 , 35 We registered our protocol with OSF (osf.io/ru4j3) and managed all review procedures using Covidence systematic review software to ensure systematic documentation and reproducibility. 36 The search strategy encompassed a comprehensive review of four major databases: Embase, Medline (via OVID), Google Scholar, and PubMed. We structured our search terms around three primary categories: OM and related concepts (including “non-medical use of prescription medications”), medical coding systems, and administrative datasets. Each category included relevant synonyms and MeSH terms, with database-specific terms incorporating common administrative data sources such as “Marketscan,” “Optum,” and “Medicare.” To maintain consistency, we applied identical search strategies across all databases. We supplemented these database searches with grey literature (sources beyond traditional academic databases, such as government reports, conference proceedings, and unpublished research) through Google and MedRxiv. Reference lists of all included articles were manually screened to identify additional studies. Before implementation, a medical research librarian reviewed and verified our search strategy to ensure comprehensiveness and accuracy. (See Supplementary Material S3 .) Studies met our inclusion criteria if they represented original work published in peer-reviewed journals, appeared in English or had available translation before January 2024, and contained algorithms or logic using administrative codes to identify OM. We also required studies to either discuss prescription opioids specifically or not explicitly exclude them. Additionally, studies needed to address at least one of three key areas: OM or inappropriate use of one’s own prescription, non-medical use of others’ prescriptions, or opioid-related encounters suggesting misuse. We excluded studies that focused solely on illicit drugs or explicitly excluded prescription opioids, those that did not discuss OM or related concepts, and studies examining only OUD without addressing misuse. We also excluded conference proceedings, abstract-only presentations, letters to the editor, commentaries, and editorials to maintain focus on peer-reviewed research findings. Studies using NLP or qualitative analysis without administrative codes were excluded as our review focused specifically on structured diagnostic coding practices in observational healthcare data. Screening Our screening process proceeded in three stages. Initially, de-duplication of the search result literature was performed in Covidence. 36 Then three independent reviewers (RWH, MCBA, KTB) screened titles and abstracts using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria within Covidence. 36 Articles passing this initial screen underwent full-text review by an expanded team (RWH, MCBA, KTB, MDC), with each publication independently evaluated by at least two reviewers at both stages. We established a resolution team (RWH, KTB, MDC) to address uncertainties through group discussion and consensus among the entire team (RWH, KTB, MDC, EH, MCBA), ensuring consistent application of our inclusion criteria. Extraction For data extraction, we developed a comprehensive tool within Covidence following established Cochrane review guidelines (see Supplementary Material S3 ). Independent reviewers extracted data using this tool ( Supplementary Material S3 and Table 1 ), with all entries subsequently verified by a second reviewer to ensure accuracy. Our dedicated resolution team addressed any conflicts in reporting through consensus discussions. The extracted data encompassed study characteristics, population characteristics, coding characteristics, and outcome characteristics, with particular attention to OM definitions and the administrative codes used. Quality assessment To evaluate the quality of the included studies, we used a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) 37 specifically adapted for OM research. 38 This scale assessed three key domains: Selection (0-4 points), Comparability (0-2 points), and Outcome (0-3 points). The Selection domain evaluated cohort representativeness and misuse ascertainment, while Comparability assessed control for demographic and relevant factors. The Outcome domain examined assessment methods and follow-up adequacy. Studies received scores out of nine possible points, with higher scores indicating greater methodological rigor. We conducted all quantitative analyses using STATA software (Version 17, StataCorp LLC). Results Search results and study selection Our systematic search yielded a substantial initial dataset of 9561 records across our target databases: Embase (via OVID) ( n = 1900), MEDLINE ( n = 965), PubMed ( n = 896), and Google Scholar ( n = 1415). Grey literature searching contributed an additional 4385 records to our review pool. After a thorough deduplication process that removed 7047 duplicate entries (5808 identified manually and 1239 through Covidence), we retained 2514 studies for initial screening. Following our comprehensive review process, we identified 334 articles for full-text assessment. Of these, we excluded 315 studies: 37 lacked necessary classification codes, 89 were available only as abstracts, and 189 did not contain OM concepts meeting inclusion criteria. This rigorous selection process yielded a final analysis set of 19 studies 39–57 ( Figure 1 ). Scoping review results Analysis of coding practices revealed a hierarchical structure in the WHO ICD-10 framework, with F11 serving as the parent code for opioid-related disorders. Within this framework, F11.1x codes indicate opioid abuse (corresponding to mild OUD in DSM-5), F11.2x indicates opioid dependence (moderate to severe OUD), and F11.9x emerged as the primary code for documenting OM that does not meet criteria for abuse or dependence. Studies demonstrated that the combination of F11.9x with Z79.891 (Long-term use of opiate analgesic) increased identification of potential opioid-misuse diagnoses by 20% compared with using OUD codes alone. Several studies noted important distinctions in code usage as specified by WHO guidance. Notably, F11.9x should not be coded simultaneously with OUD diagnosis codes (F11.1x and F11.2x), while Z79.891 should indicate therapeutic rather than disordered use. This creates a framework for distinguishing appropriate long-term opioid therapy from potential misuse patterns. Quality assessment Our methodological quality review of the 19 included studies 39–57 ( Table 2 ) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale revealed generally strong research practices with some notable areas for improvement ( Table 2 , see Supplementary Material S3 for detailed scoring criteria and results). In the Selection domain, studies demonstrated robust cohort selection practices and clear documentation of OM identification criteria. The Comparability domain showed more variation, with fewer studies achieving maximum scores by accounting for important confounding variables such as socioeconomic status and specific subpopulations. The Outcome domain showed remarkable consistency across studies, reflecting high standards in outcome measurement and follow-up duration. Overall quality scores ranged from 7 to 9, with a median of 8, indicating generally strong methodological rigor across the dataset. A substantial majority (79%) achieved a total score of 8, with only two studies 55 , 56 scoring 7 (10.5%) and two 41 , 57 (10.5%) achieving the maximum score of 9 ( Table 3 ). This distribution suggests broad adherence to quality standards while highlighting opportunities for improvement in handling complex confounding factors. Coding practices and definitions Our analysis of current qualitative definitions revealed that professional medical organizations, scientific societies, and governmental authorities lack consensus in the definition of the concept of OM 5–14 , 18 , 22 , 58 ( Table 4 ). Our analysis of the included studies revealed that all studies were observational, 17 of the 19 were data from the United States, the majority (5) used all-payer claims and were published since 2020 ( Table 5 ). There was significant heterogeneity in the populations of patients studied, prescription versus illicit opioid use, and types of drugs examined ( Table 6 ). Researchers’ primary aim of their study was most commonly author qualitatively defined “opioid misuse” plus OUD (abuse or dependence) ( Table 7 ). Several notable approaches emerged from the literature. Brat et al 44 defined misuse through ICD codes for dependence, abuse, or overdose in selected populations such as in previously opioid-naïve patients. 44 Häuser et al 15 and Follman et al 47 applied a different and very broad approach, examining “problematic use” through F11.* codes for opioid use, abuse, and dependence. 15 A pattern emerged in the operational definitions of OM across studies. All but two of the studies. 45 , 53 used the ICD (ICD-9 or ICD-10 or a combination) coding system to identify OM in the administrative database ( Table 8 ). Two researcher teams, including Lobo et al 53 and Cochran et al, 56 adopted the Sullivan et al 59 semi-quantitative operational definition, which considers multiple factors: the number of unique opioid prescribers, number of unique pharmacies, and days’ supply of both short-acting and long-acting opioids. Within this framework, a total score of 3 or higher across the four domains identifies potential OM, with Lobo et al 53 and others finding that higher thresholds may improve specificity at the cost of reduced sensitivity. 53 Some investigators, such as Campbell et al, 45 implemented a hybrid approach that incorporated both prescription opioid use without a prescription using the Sullivan approach 59 and ICD codes for use (misuse), abuse, and dependence ( Figure 2 ). While others such as Janjua et al 50 developed an algorithm to identify what they termed drug misuse using both ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, which achieved 90% sensitivity and 73% specificity for identifying injection (intravenous) drug use. 50 The transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10 emerged as a pivotal factor in coding practices. Heslin et al 49 found that the introduction of “opioid use, unspecified” codes in ICD-10 accounted for approximately half of the increase in opioid-related codes following the transition. 49 Ko et al 51 observed increased utilization of unspecified codes and examined the relationship between long-term opioid therapy codes and OUD across ICD implementations. 51 This transition period highlighted the evolving nature of OM documentation in administrative data. Ko et al, 51 Hirari et al, 57 and Arifkhanova et al 42 examined specific combinations of ICD-10 codes such as the combination of F11.9x (Opioid Use, Unspecified) and Z79.891 (Long term [current] use of opiate analgesic) to identify patients with OM. The inclusion of codes F11.9x (Opioid Use, Unspecified) and Z79.891 (Long-term use of opiate analgesic) increased identification of potential opioid-misuse diagnoses by 20% compared with using established OUD diagnostic codes (F11.1x and F11.2x) alone. 51 , 57 Analysis of 18.8 million birth and delivery hospitalizations from 2010 to 2017 demonstrated the expanded maternal opioid-related diagnoses codes in ICD-10-CM increased potential OM diagnoses, but did not affect neonatal abstinence syndrome rates. 51 Arifkhanova et al 42 noted in their analysis that WHO coding guidance indicates F11.9x should not be coded simultaneously with OUD diagnosis codes (F11.1x and F11.2x), and Z79.891 should indicate therapeutic rather than disordered use. While Z79.891 indicates therapeutic long-term opioid use and F11.9x indicates disordered use, these codes are not mutually exclusive as patients may exhibit both appropriate therapeutic use and occasional misuse behaviors. This coding complexity reflects the clinical reality where patients may generally use opioids appropriately while having episodes of misuse, highlighting the need for careful documentation that captures this nuanced clinical picture. Validation approaches Methodologically, studies utilized diverse administrative databases, ranging from EHRs and insurance claims data to state-level databases and national healthcare utilization databases. Validation approaches varied considerably, with some studies incorporating chart review validation, comparison with prescription fill data, cross-referencing multiple data sources, or expert consensus review. 40 , 45 , 52 , 53 Most studies used lookback periods ranging from 6 months to 2 years, with several noting the importance of excluding certain encounter types, such as laboratory encounters, to avoid misclassification. 42 , 52 These findings highlight both the current state of OM documentation and the challenges that could be addressed to improve clinical care and research in this domain. Discussion This systematic scoping review reveals fundamental challenges in the conceptualization, diagnosis, and documentation of OM. Our findings highlight a critical gap: administrative coding practices for OM that do not follow the ICD framework likely resulting from the lack of a standardized clinical definition and absence of formal diagnostic criteria. This systematic examination revealed several key themes in how OM is currently documented and areas where improvements could enhance clinical care and research. The first major challenge lies in the ambiguous clinical conceptualization of OM. While organizations like the National Institutes of Health, 10 Centers for Disease Control, 11 and Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 5 provide behavioral definitions of misuse, these descriptions are inconsistent and fail to map precisely to established diagnostic frameworks. 21 This definitional ambiguity is particularly problematic because OM is notably absent from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, 5th edition (DSM-5), 3 creating uncertainty about its status as a distinct clinical entity versus a behavioral pattern that may precede OUD. Our analysis identified another critical issue: The disconnect between clinical observations of OM and available diagnostic codes. Within the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) framework, 4 , 30 OM emerges as a diagnosis of exclusion, encompassing behaviors that deviate from medical norms 5–14 , 22 , 58 but do not meet criteria for opioid abuse (F11.1) or dependence (F11.2). This creates a fundamental challenge for clinicians who must document concerning opioid-related behaviors that fall short of formal OUD criteria. 32 The most significant finding from our systematic review was the identification of the F11.9* code (opioid use, unspecified) as the predominant method for documenting OM. 42 , 51 , 57 This code’s appropriateness stems from the hierarchical structure of the ICD-10 framework, which dictates that patients meeting criteria for abuse (F11.1) or dependence (F11.2) cannot be classified under F11.9. This hierarchical coding structure creates a natural framework for distinguishing OM from both appropriate medical use (documented with Z79.891) and OUD (documented with F11.1 and F11.2). 4 However, the terminology “opioid use” in F11.9 may contribute to ongoing confusion, 49 , 51 despite WHO guidance clearly indicating this code is intended for “disordered use” rather than appropriate medical use ( Figure 3 ). 4 The current state of OM documentation presents several challenges for future research and clinical practice. First, the lack of consensus on clinical definitions hampers validation efforts, with studies showing that diagnosis-based definitions identified only 10%-50% of cases compared to extended definitions, and validation studies often failed to exclude inappropriate diagnostic sites of service. 2 , 52 , 60–63 Second, the disconnect between clinical observation and coding frameworks may lead to underutilization in practice and inconsistent adoption of new technologies such as NLP. 64 Chart validation efforts were particularly hampered by inconsistent documentation patterns, while the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10 introduced new codes without clear validation of their use in practice. 52 , 60 The WHO’s current terminology using “abuse” and “dependence” reflects historically rooted concepts that may not align with contemporary understanding of substance use disorders. Future ICD revisions may benefit from updating this terminology to better align with current clinical conceptualization of OUD severity continuum, moving away from potentially stigmatizing terms like “abuse.” Alternative approaches to identifying OM have emerged to address these coding limitations. The Sullivan et al 59 operational clinical definition, adopted in several studies, 45 , 53 , 56 evaluates multiple factors including the number of unique opioid prescribers, pharmacies, and days’ supply of both short-acting and long-acting opioids. While this approach offers a potentially more comprehensive assessment, it presents its own challenges in implementation and the low quality of evidence for the chosen outcome factors limits epidemiological estimates and clinical utility. 65–67 Limitations Our systematic scoping review has several important limitations that contextualize our findings. First, our search strategy was confined to English-language publications or translations and may have missed relevant studies in other languages. Second, despite comprehensive database searching, the heterogeneity in terminology used to describe OM may have resulted in missing some relevant studies. Third, our focus on administrative coding practices meant excluding studies that used only NLP or qualitative methods, potentially omitting valuable insights into clinical documentation patterns. Fourth, the majority of included studies (17 of 19) were from the United States healthcare system, limiting generalizability to other healthcare systems with different coding practices. Fifth, while this review focused specifically on coding practices, future research should examine how these practices interact with healthcare disparities and access to care, as these factors may influence both the application and interpretation of diagnostic codes. Finally, the rapid evolution of coding practices during the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10, and now toward ICD-11, means some of our findings may need to be reevaluated as coding systems continue to evolve. Recommendations Despite these limitations, our findings suggest several concrete steps that could improve the documentation and study of OM: Accurate Implementation of Current Coding Systems: Healthcare systems could develop clear guidelines for using the F11.9* code specifically for prescription OM following the WHO framework. This includes eliminating miscoding practices and ensuring proper differentiation from both appropriate medical use (Z79.891) and the OUD diagnoses (F11.1, F11.2). Provider training should emphasize the hierarchical relationship between these codes, following WHO’s intended progression of diagnostic severity. 4 Standardization of Clinical Definition: Professional societies and regulatory agencies with subject matter experts could develop consensus on the clinical definition of OM, including clear criteria for distinguishing it from appropriate use and OUD. This standardization is essential for consistent documentation and research. Integration with Diagnostic Frameworks: The American Psychiatric Association could evaluate whether OM warrants inclusion in future DSM editions, potentially as a distinct disorder with specific diagnostic criteria. This evaluation should consider whether OM represents meeting a single criterion of current DSM-5 OUD criteria 3 , 31 (therefore below the minimum criteria for mild OUD) and, if so, which ones are most relevant. Enhanced Documentation Systems: Healthcare systems could improve EHR coding integration that automatically maps clinical documentation of OM to appropriate diagnostic codes (eg, F11.9 in ICD-10 or QE11.0 in ICD-11), reducing the burden on clinicians to navigate complex coding hierarchies. 4 , 30 , 68 Alignment of Classification Systems: Future revisions of both DSM and ICD frameworks could strive for greater alignment in terminology and taxonomy, potentially requiring more frequent updates to maintain consistency with evolving clinical understanding. Conclusion Implementation of these recommendations would address the fundamental challenges identified in our review while building a more robust foundation for both clinical care and research initiatives in this critical public health domain. While identifying appropriate codes represents a crucial first step, the implementation of these coding practices requires careful consideration of validation methods and practical challenges. Therefore, future research should focus on validating these recommendations across diverse healthcare settings and evaluating their impact on clinical outcomes and quality measures. Particularly important is the development of validation protocols that account for the complex nature of OM documentation and implementation guidelines tailored to specific healthcare settings while maintaining consistency in core coding principles.
