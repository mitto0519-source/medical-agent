Abstract The Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behavior Interview (SITBI) has been widely used in Western countries, but its use in Chinese culture is limited. Based on the scarcity of standardized Chinese interviews to assess self-injurious thoughts and behaviors, this study aimed to validate the SITBI Chinese version (SITBI-C). The psychometric properties of the SITBI-C were tested in 195 outpatient adolescents with various levels of suicidality. The test-retest, interrater and concurrent validity were investigated in this study. The analysis of the SITBI-C showed perfect test-retest reliability within one week. The interrater agreement was also excellent, ranging across SITB-C items. The validity indices were good when comparing the responses to the SITBI-C and other interview-based and self-reported measures, suggesting strong convergent validity. Overall, the results of this research suggested that the SITBI-C demonstrated good psychometric properties. The SITBI-C is appropriate for assessing suicidality in the Chinese adolescent population and can be used to assess adolescent suicidal ideation to determine the risk for suicidal behavior. Introduction Suicide is a critical public health issue, particularly among young people. According to the World Health Organization, it is the third leading cause of death among individuals aged 15–29 1 , while in Taiwan, it emerges as the second leading cause of death for those aged 15–24 2 . The national surveillance system has documented a concerning trend, with adolescent suicide rates increasing over the past decade. Notably, the crude suicide death rate for individuals aged 15–24 rose significantly by 11.5% in 2022 compared to the previous year 3 . Furthermore, there has been a substantial increase in person-event records for younger individuals, with a 61.9% rise in those under 14 years old and a 25.8% increase among those aged 15–24 within just one year. This alarming context highlights the importance of understanding self-injurious thoughts and behaviors as significant risk factors for adolescent suicide, as revealed by a recent meta-analysis by Castellví et al. 4 . Self-injurious thoughts and behaviors encompass a range of outcomes, including suicidal ideation and attempts, as well as self-injurious behaviors engaged in without intent to die (e.g., nonsuicidal self-injury). In the United States, a national survey found that 17.6% of high school students reported engaging in nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) 5 , while 17.7% seriously considered suicide and 8.6% attempted suicide within the past year 6 . Similarly, a survey among Taiwanese adolescents indicated that within the past year, 10.1% had engaged in self-harm, and 19% had experienced suicidal ideation 7 . Additionally, the incidence rates for self-harm and suicidal behavior were 4.04% and 1.53%, respectively 8 . This data underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions to address these critical issues among youth. The increasing rates of suicide and self-harm among teenagers highlight the urgent need for a comprehensive understanding of self-injurious thoughts and behaviors. This understanding must address the various phenomena associated with self-injury and the inconsistent use of terminology, definitions, and classifications that have evolved 9 , 10 . Inconsistent terminology significantly impacts the measurement of self-injurious thoughts and behaviors, as the lack of agreement complicates the comparison and interpretation of studies from different parts of the world, where varying terms and definitions may be used for similar behaviors. This inconsistency limits the generalization of research outcomes and can lead to confusion and misinterpretation of data, ultimately affecting the reliability of research findings. Additionally, it results in discrepancies in how behaviors are reported and understood, impacting the measurement and analysis of self-injurious thoughts and behaviors. Furthermore, these issues affect the comparability of research across different cultural and economic contexts and complicate clinical practice by hindering the accurate diagnosis and treatment of self-injurious behaviors 11 . The Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview (SITBI) addresses issues of inconsistent terminology usage in measuring self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITB) 12 . The SITBI provides a structured and uniform approach to assess various types of SITB, including suicidal ideation, suicide plans, suicide gestures, suicide attempts, and nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI). One of the significant limitations in existing measures is the lack of clear and specific definitions for SITB, which leads to difficulties in comparing results across studies and can result in erroneous conclusions. The SITBI addresses this by using consistent methods and items to assess the presence, frequency, and characteristics of different SITB, allowing for valid comparisons within and across studies. The SITBI’s comprehensive and consistent assessment of SITB helps overcome the broad and inconsistent definitions that have plagued previous measures. By differentiating among various SITB constructs, the SITBI provides a more accurate and reliable tool for research and clinical settings. Overall, the SITBI represents a significant advancement in the measurement of SITB by providing a clear, consistent, and comprehensive tool that addresses many of the issues related to inconsistent terminology usage in previous measures. Nock initially proposed a classification system for self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs) that includes two categories that are distinguished by whether they are performed with intent to die: nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) or bodily self-harm performed without intent die, and suicidal outcomes, for which an individual has at least some intent to die 10 . Suicidal outcomes can be categorized into three levels: suicidal ideation (SI), suicide plan (SP), and suicide attempt (SA). NSSI can be classified into suicide gesture (SG), thoughts of NSSI, and NSSI. However, later research indicated that SG should not be classified as NSSI because they involve intentional deception; individuals engage in these behaviors to make others believe they have suicidal intent despite lacking any real desire to end their lives 13 . SG are actions that give the appearance of suicidal intent but are carried out without the actual intent to die. They are often used to communicate pain, seek help, or achieve other goals, and involve a deceptive element to make others believe there is a risk of suicide. This distinction is crucial because SG can occur without direct self-injury, further separating them from NSSI. Therefore, although there may be some overlap between these behaviors, the clear difference in intent and the potential absence of self-injury means that SG cannot be categorized as NSSI or suicidal outcomes. Individuals may engage in SITBs for various reasons, including a nonzero intent to die along with interpersonal functions or a combination of both interpersonal and intrapersonal motivations. Assessment tools should evaluate the multiple functions associated with each reported behavior to enhance classification accuracy and identify the primary function. For example, the SITBI examines various functions related to SA. Researchers and clinicians can utilize this tool to improve classification accuracy by aligning behaviors with the primary functions reported by individuals. The SITBI is a semi-structured interview designed to assess various SITBs and their characteristics in a standardized manner. The SITBI has been translated into multiple languages 14 – 17 and has demonstrated robust psychometric properties. However, there is a paucity of evidence regarding the efficacy of the SITBI within non-Western cultural contexts. Currently, no all-encompassing scale exists in the Chinese language for evaluating SITBs among adolescents. Previous research frequently employs individual scales that concentrate solely on suicidal outcomes or NSSI, whereas others assess specific items from scales that encompass related material. In Taiwan, several validated scales are used to assess adolescent suicide and self-injury, including the Beck Depression Inventory and Beck Suicide Scale 18 , which focus on depression and suicidal ideation. The PHQ-9 assesses depressive symptoms 19 , while the SAD Person characteristics and PIERCE risk assessment cover specific risk factors 20 . However, these scales lack comprehensive assessment of SITBs, unlike the SITBI, which covers both suicidal and non-suicidal self-injury. These scales have been used in research to evaluate adolescents’ mental health, exploring factors like family and school influences, but comprehensive studies on self-injurious behaviors remain limited. The restricted availability of the SITBI in multiple languages may result in the neglect of unique experiences within diverse populations, including culturally specific expressions of suicidal thoughts and behaviors. This study aims to validate the Chinese version of the SITBI (SITBI-C) to increase its usability within Chinese communities due to the lack of standardized Chinese interviews for evaluation. Materials and methods Participants The participants, aged 12–18 years, were required to have a history of self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs) as part of the inclusion criteria. The study participants were recruited from psychiatric outpatient clinics affiliated with a single medical center in Taiwan. Adolescents were excluded from the study only if they were unable to complete the protocol due to psychosis or cognitive deficits. Study procedure The Institutional Review Board of MacKay Memorial Hospital approved the study, which was conducted following relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians. This study framework adheres to the methodology established by Nock 12 , with some differences in the details, including reduced test-retest intervals and additional assessments of concurrent validity, and the use of audio recordings instead of video recordings for inter-rater reliability. The core structure of their approach was maintained while adapting certain aspects to suit our specific research context. This alignment with Nock et al.’s methodology ensures that our findings contribute to the ongoing discourse in this area. Eligible participants were referred for SITBI-C interviews by psychiatrists for suicidality assessment. A subset of participants ( n = 30) underwent reliability assessments to evaluate the SITBI-C. Test-retest reliability was assessed by asking participants whether they had ever experienced SITBs in their lifetime, using the SITBI-C at baseline and again one week later to evaluate consistency in their responses. The one-week interval was carefully chosen to control potential memory effects that could artificially inflate retest scores, balance immediate recall bias, and prevent participants from simply remembering previous specific questions while avoiding potential cognitive state changes that might occur with longer intervals 21 . In addition, interrater reliability was assessed across 10 audio-recorded interviews conducted by three independent raters. Measures The Chinese Version of the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview (SITBI-C) The original version of the SITBI is a structured clinical interview that quantifies the presence, frequency, severity, functions, triggers, and characteristics of a wide range of SITBs 10 . The frequency of SITBs was presented at four-time points: lifetime, past year, past month, and past week. We also analyzed the interrater reliability by correlating the presence of SITBs among raters at each separate time point. The SITBI-C is an adapted version of the original scale, consisting of 169 items across six modules: SI, SP, SA, NSSI, Thoughts of NSSI, and SG. The translation procedure was conducted by a bilingual research team and a bilingual psychiatrist and included both forward and back translation to ensure linguistic accuracy. The translated version was reviewed by five child psychiatrists and a senior researcher with a PhD, who assessed its conceptual validity. Additionally, a senior child psychiatrist reviewed the draft for face validity. The research team ensured that the terminology and conceptual meanings were consistent and equivalent in Chinese and English, with no significant cultural adaptations made. Patient health questionnaire (PHQ-9) The PHQ-9 assesses nine criteria for major depressive episodes over the past 2 weeks 22 . Its Chinese version, validated in our previous school study, demonstrated good internal consistency and test–retest reliability among Taiwanese adolescents 19 . One item (item 9) from the PHQ-9, which measures the self-reported intensity of suicidal ideation, was utilized to evaluate the concurrent validity of the SITBI-C. Childhood interview for borderline personality disorder (CI-BPD) The CI-BPD, an adolescent adaptation of the Diagnostic Interview for DSM-IV Personality Disorders, assesses DSM-IV personality disorders 23 . The CI-BPD was translated by a research team and a bilingual psychiatrist and modified until the back-translated version matched the original English version. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.82. Item 6 evaluated self-mutilation and suicidal efforts to assess the concurrent validity of the SITBI-C. Rosenberg self-esteem scale (RSES) The RSES is a questionnaire consisting of 10 items that assess self-respect 24 . The reliability and validity of the Chinese version of this survey instrument have been demonstrated in Taiwanese children and adolescents 7 , 25 . Beck hopelessness scale (BHS) The BHS is a 20-item questionnaire assessing negative future attitudes that uses a true/false format 26 . Scores on the BHS have reliably been shown to correlate with suicidal outcomes in adult psychiatric patients and have strong psychometric properties in adolescent studies 27 . The Chinese version has been validated, showing strong internal consistency and satisfactory test-retest reliability among Taiwanese adolescents 28 . Difficulties in emotion regulation scale (DERS) Emotional dysregulation was measured using the DERS 29 . Higher scores indicate greater emotional dysregulation. The favorable psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the DERS have been demonstrated among adults 30 and female adolescents 31 . Kiddie-schedule for affective disorders and schizophrenia (K-SADS-E) The K-SADS-E, a semi-structured interview assessing both lifetime and current episodes of psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents 32 , 33 , includes a suicide-related module with a Chinese version adapted to Taiwanese culture 34 . The suicide-related module was applied to evaluate the construct validity of the SITBI-C in the past year. Statistical analyses Descriptive analyses were conducted to calculate means, standard deviations (SDs), and frequencies for the SITBI-C items, participant characteristics, and SITB prevalence rates. The median (interquartile range [IQR]) was reported for variables with a skewed distribution. The test-retest reliability of SITBI-C is classified as a categorical variable, necessitating the use of Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistic. Interrater reliability was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa statistic to gauge agreement among the SITBI raters. Interrater reliability was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa statistic to gauge agreement among the SITBI raters. Both Cohen’s and Fleiss’ Kappa measure agreement levels, categorized by Landis and Koch as follows: almost perfect (&gt; 0.8), substantial (&gt; 0.6), moderate (&gt; 0.4), fair (&gt; 0.2), and slight (&gt; 0) 35 . Statistical significance was set at p &lt; 0.05. The concurrent validity of the SITBI-C was evaluated by correlating the SITBI variables with the PHQ-9 item 9 (SI), CI-BPD item 6 (NSSI or SA), BHS, RSES, and DERS scores using Spearman rank correlations 36 . To adjust for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied (0.008 = 0.05/6). Furthermore, the consistency between the data from the SITBI-C and the K-SADS-E suicide modules was examined using Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistics. Results Participant characteristics The study involved 195 adolescents aged 12 to 18 (mean age = 15.7 years, SD = 1.82), 66.7% of whom were female. Most participants had a high school education (52.3%), followed by middle school (33.8%), college (6.7%), junior college (6.2%), and primary school (1%). Various psychiatric comorbidities were present, including sleep-wake disorders (67.2%), depressive disorders (65.6%), anxiety disorders (60.5%), and neurodevelopmental disorders (46.7%) (Table 1 ). Descriptive results Tables 2 and 3 outline the mean scores and standard deviations for various aspects of SITBs among adolescents. SI is prevalent, reported by 95.4%. Over three-quarters (77.9%) reported lifetime thoughts of NSSI, and a similar proportion (73.8%) reported engaging in NSSI. Most participants (80%) expressed SP, and more than one-third had made at least one SA. The onset of SITBs in our current sample typically occurs between 12 and 14 years of age, with SI being the initial symptom, followed by thoughts of NSSI, NSSI, SP, SA, and SG. The severity of SITBs was rated as moderate to high, and the most common intention for SITBs was ‘getting rid of bad feelings,’ excluding SG. Mental state and school/work difficulties were associated with SITBs, and SI and SA often occurred alongside substance use. Adolescents reported having fewer friends engaging in SITBs before they started, but the presence of SITB-engaging friends did not influence their own decision to start or continue such behaviors. The risk of future SITBs was highest for SI and NSSI thoughts. Test–retest reliability In total, 30 participants from the initial interview were selected for the one-week retest interviews, which specifically focused on the occurrence of lifetime SITBs. The kappa scores for the lifetime presence of each SITB, reported at two-time points, showed perfect agreement (κ = 1) for all six types of SITBs. Interrater reliability Three researchers independently assessed the interrater reliability by reviewing the same 10 participant audiotapes. The original interviewer data and the raters’ data demonstrated complete agreement on the presence of SITBs at four different time points (lifetime, past year, past month, and past week; all κ = 1). The interrater agreement for the frequencies of SITBs at the four time points was excellent (κ = 0.87-1). The examination of intensities at the worst point and average intensities for SI, SP, and thoughts of NSSI demonstrated an excellent ICC value of 0.93-1. The kappa indices for interrater agreement on the intention for various types of SITBs were excellent, ranging from κ = 0.95-1. The raters who scored the triggers for SITBs demonstrated excellent agreement, with an ICC of 0.97-1. The kappa scores for the SITIBI features showed excellent agreement among the three raters, ranging from κ = 0.86 to 1. The interrater agreement for the likelihood of future SITBs occurring was perfect, with all the ICCs equal to 1. Concurrent validity We examined the concurrent validity of the SITBI-C by testing the correspondence of responses to SITBI-C items to assess the presence of SITBs to evaluate the consistency with similar items from the K-SADS-E, PHQ-9 item 9, and CIBPD item 7. We found perfect agreement for lifetime SI, SP, and SA between SITBI-C and K-SADS-E (κ = 1.0). PHQ-9 item 9 assesses SI and thoughts about NSSI during the past two weeks, whereas the closest time frames for the SITBI-C are the past month and the past week. Kappa scores indicated that PHQ-9 item 9 (not at all vs. several days/nearly every day) showed substantial agreement with the SITBI-C regarding the presence of SI during the past month (κ = 0.697) and the past week (κ = 0.787). PHQ-9 item 9 also presented moderate agreement with thoughts about NSSI in the past month (κ = 0.52) and the past week (κ = 0.52). We found a slightly lower agreement for CIBPD item 7 regarding the lifetime presence of suicide attempts (κ = 0.325) and NSSI (κ = 0.461). Table 4 presents the correlations between the frequencies of the SITBI-C variables and the five concurrent validity measures. The results showed statistically significant correlations between the five measures and the frequencies of SITIBI-C modalities in different time frames, except for the SG. While the correlation coefficients between frequencies for SI and PHQ-9 item 9 indicated strong correlations ( r = 0.579–0.687, P &lt; 0.001), correlations with SP, SA, thoughts of NSSI, and NSSI showed moderate correlations. Similar results were found for patient-reported scores on the BHS; the correlation coefficient for the SI surpassed that for the other modalities ( r = 0.353–0.469, P &lt; 0.001). Total scores on the RSES showed inverse correlations with the SITIB-C scores ( r = -0.218-0.505, all P &lt; 0.001). The frequencies of the SITIB-C modalities also showed moderate to high correlations with the total scores for the DERS and item 7 of the CI-BPD ( r = 0.331–0.598, P &lt; 0.001), indicating convergence. On the other hand, correlations between the frequencies of suicidal gestures at all time frames and all five concurrent validity measures were not statistically significant and were thus discriminated ( r = 0.110 − 0.097, P &gt; 0.08). Discussion This study is the first to validate the Chinese version of the SITBI in psychiatric outpatient adolescents in Taiwan. The current study revealed high interrater and test-retest reliability. The validity indices were strong when comparing the responses to the SITBI-C with those of other interview-based and self-reported measures, indicating a high level of concurrent validity. We verified the reliability and validity of the SITBI-C. The current study found similar patterns for SITB intentions observed in all suicide outcomes, with the exception of suicide gestures 14 , 37 , 38 . The most commonly reported reason for SITBs among adolescents was “to get rid of bad feelings”, followed by “get out of doing something”, then “feel something”, and finally “get attention”. However, conversely, “getting attention” is the most common intention for suicide gesture. Our results are consistent with previous findings that attention seeking is the most reason for suicide gesture, while automatic reinforcement is the most common cause of NSSI among adolescents 39 , 40 . Not only for NSSI, we found that other forms of SITBs were often used by adolescents to cope with and regulate their negative and distressing emotions. When adolescents develop SITBs to relieve negative emotional states, the automatic reinforcement of SITB occurs, immediately relieving distress and reinforcing thoughts or behaviors. Conversely, some adolescents use SITB to induce a desired physiological state, such as feeling something when emotionally numb, in which case SITB produces a feeling that is better than feeling nothing at all. These automatic functions—negative reinforcement intended to reduce unwanted emotions, and positive reinforcement intended to create desired feelings—highlight the complex motivations behind SITB, making it more likely to recur whenever similar emotional experiences arise 37 . Automatic reinforcement has been implicated in emotion regulation in SITB, influencing suicidal ideation by creating a cognitive environment in which thoughts of self-harm and death are more likely to occur 37 . When suicide ideation arises, automatic reinforcement may reinforce these thoughts through intense emotional reactions and cognitive distortions. This dynamic can escalate into more serious outcomes, such as making a suicide plan or attempting suicide, especially when combined with stressors or emotional distress. Research has consistently shown that adolescents engage in SITBs as a way to cope with and regulate emotions, particularly when experiencing negative and painful emotions 38 , 39 . Notably, the current study results are different in Western populations. Firstly, we found that school or work issues were significant triggers, ranking second only to mental health, which is different from other Western studies. The current study identified mental state, school/work, and family as the top three triggers. Conversely, the original development study of the SITBI identified mental state, family, and friends/relationships as the leading triggers 12 . Participants reported struggling with academic stress, which is exceptionally high in Eastern cultures. Eastern cultures, such as those in Asia, significantly emphasize academic achievement. This cultural focus can lead to higher levels of academic stress among Eastern adolescents compared to their Western peers. In Confucianism, education is profoundly rooted in tradition and is a crucial pathway to success. Academic expectations are influenced by a significant cultural emphasis that underscores students’ attainment of high educational standards, a concept closely linked to reputation and social standing 41 . In contrast, Western culture views education as important but does not place the same level of emphasis on it as the sole path to success, recognizing instead more diverse pathways to achievement. Although academic stress is a significant global issue, its intensity and impact can vary, with a more pronounced effect in Eastern cultures due to cultural expectations and the substantial time dedicated to academics. This is supported by findings that Asian students face higher expectations, spend significantly more time on schoolwork, and have lower parental satisfaction concerning academic achievements compared to their Western peers 42 , 43 . The intense academic pressure can lead to mental health issues, including stress, anxiety, and, in severe cases. Adolescents who experience high levels of mental distress may struggle to cope with academic pressure. This stress can become overwhelming and lead to SITBs. Several studies have identified a correlation between academic stress and suicide rates in non-Western countries 44 – 46 . Secondly, the current study revealed less adolescent substance use during SITB episodes, in contrast to previous research. The reported percentages of Western participants engaging in SITBs while using alcohol or drugs ranged from 8 to 48% 12 , 14 , 15 . The prevalence of concurrent alcohol and drug use among participants in our study ranged from 2.4 to 4.9%. Alcohol consumption varies significantly among countries due to cultural drinking norms and varying levels of social acceptance. Although alcohol and illicit substance use are less prevalent in Taiwan than in Western countries, there is still a need for prevention programs that specifically target al.cohol and illicit substance use, especially among adolescents with SITBs. The study demonstrated that the SITBI-C has excellent test-retest reliability for all SITBs. The test-retest reliability of the SITBI-C is greater than that of the original version 12 and other versions 12 , 14 , 15 . These strong test-retest values may be attributed to shorter time intervals and clinical stability that typically occurs within a lifetime. Our test-retest reliability interval was one week, which is shorter than the intervals used in previous studies 12 , 14 , 15 , 47 . Correlations between SITBI-C and concurrent validity measures reveal significant insights into its construct validity. The SITBI-C shows strong correlations with item 9 of the PHQ-9, indicating that it effectively reflects the incidence of SI and aligns with established metrics. Moderate correlations with SP, SA, and NSSI demonstrate these constructs are distinct from SI, showcasing SITBI-C’s ability to differentiate between related concepts. The correlation with the BHS suggests that hopelessness is relevant yet distinct from SI, reinforcing SITBI-C’s validity. Inverse correlations with the RSES support the notion that higher suicide risk is linked to lower self-esteem. SITBI-C’s correlations with the DERS and item 7 of the CI-BPD indicate its effectiveness in capturing emotional dysregulation and aspects related to borderline personality disorder. The lack of significant correlations with SG indicates a weak relationship with the assessed constructs, indicating a need for methodological refinement. An insufficient response to SG items was common in SITB studies, possibly due to low support for this item 48 . SG was reported less frequently in studies, highlighting measurement challenges. Researchers note SG’s subjective nature and ambiguous definitions 49 , arguing that distinguishing SG from SA should prioritize suicidal intent over the act’s difficulty. Clarification is needed on interviewee responses to SG questions, emphasizing the importance of a comprehensive evaluation approach that includes qualitative data. Future research should refine SG conceptualization and measurement strategies to better capture its psychological complexity. Overall, SITBI-C illustrates strong construct validity through correlations with both convergent and related but distinct constructs. The SITBI-C demonstrated good psychometric qualities, consistent with the original English version. These results, although they need confirmation, can be considered reliable due to the larger sample size of this study compared to previous studies on the psychometric properties of the SITBI 12 , 14 , 15 , 47 . Additionally, this study demonstrated the feasibility and applicability of assessing omnibus SITBs in general hospitals. Healthcare professionals can play a pivotal role in suicide prevention by applying comprehensive SITB assessments to communicate with suicidal patients. However, it is important to recognize the limitations of our study despite its notable strengths. The current study recruited participants from only one hospital center, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Future research should include community samples to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Classical interrater reliability studies involve participants being interviewed by two independent interviewers. This approach minimizes the influence of the interviewer’s comments and questions on the coders’ separate responses. Conducting duplicate interviews for adolescents significantly increases the time and emotional demands of the study and reduces their motivation to participate. The study focused on the agreement among researchers rather than the agreement between parents and adolescents or between clinicians and adolescents. Gathering information from multiple sources, such as adolescents, parents, and teachers, is typically necessary to assess adolescent psychopathology. This approach improves the validity of assessments and enhances the evaluation of clinical outcomes. Future research could explore integrating multiple informant approaches in different cultural contexts. Conclusions The current investigation revealed that the SITBI-C has strong psychometric properties that are consistent with those of the original English version. This instrument is suitable for evaluating various types of SITBs. The SITBI-C can be utilized in research and clinical settings to diagnose and assess the severity of SITBs using specific definitions. Conducting comprehensive interviews that address triggers, intentions, and participant characteristics is essential for developing effective intervention strategies.
