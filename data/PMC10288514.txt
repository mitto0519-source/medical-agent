Noncaloric sweeteners have enabled a reduction of carbohydrate sweeteners in the diet for many years, thus combating obesity, diabetes, and other health disorders. However, many consumers reject noncaloric sweeteners, as they exhibit delays in sweetness onset, objectionable lingering sweet aftertaste, and the absence of sugar-like mouthfeel. We propose that the temporal taste differences between carbohydrate and noncaloric sweeteners result from slowed diffusion of the latter to and from sweetener receptors through the amphipathic mucous hydrogel covering the tongue. Also, we demonstrate that formulation of noncaloric sweeteners with K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ mineral salt blends markedly attenuates lingering sweetness, an effect believed to be due to a composite of osmotic and chelate-mediated compaction of the mucous hydrogel covering the tongue. For example, sweetness linger values (intensity units in % sucrose eq) for rebaudioside A and aspartame are reduced from 5.0 (0.5 SD) to 1.6 (0.4 SD) and from 4.0 (0.7 SD) to 1.2 (0.4 SD), respectively, by formulation with 10 mM KCl/3 mM MgCl 2 /3 mM CaCl 2 . Finally, we propose that sugar-like mouthfeel is a consequence of K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ activation of the calcium-sensing receptor present in a subset of taste bud cells. Suffice to say, excessive consumption of sugar and other carbohydrate sweeteners (CHOS) is a major contributor to obesity and associated disorders. As a consequence of the aforementioned health issues of excessive consumption of CHOS, there has been greater than a century-long interest in noncaloric sweeteners (NCS), beginning in 1905 with the commercial development of saccharin by Monsanto. 2 Since that time, many additional NCS have been commercialized including the synthetic NCS cyclamate, aspartame, acesulfame-K, sucralose, neotame and advantame as well as natural NCS including stevia sweeteners [i.e., rebaudioside A (REBA) and rebaudioside M (REBM)] and monk fruit sweeteners (i.e., mogroside V and other mogrol glycosides). 3 It is generally accepted that substitution of these NCS for CHOS sweeteners should facilitate weight loss and improve health. However, as reviewed in a recent comprehensive WHO study by Rios-Leyvraz and Montez, “there is no clear consensus on whether non-sugar sweeteners are effective for long-term weight loss or maintenance, or if they are linked to other long-term health effects”. 4 Overall, however, reduction in consumption of CHOS is associated with good health and moderate consumption of NCS is not likely to be a health problem. Many synthetic and natural NCS exhibit clean sweetness, but all have a slow-to-develop sweetness, which then lingers, often quite objectionably. It has been observed that sweeteners with slow sweetness onsets [appearance time (AT)] always exhibit lingering sweet aftertastes [sweetness linger (SL)]. In early work focused on this temporal profile problem, chemists prepared structural variants of NCS in the hope of finding analogues with more sugar-like temporal profiles. Exemplary is a 1981 study of analogues of neohesperidin dihydrochalcone, a sweetener known to have delayed AT and prolonged SL. 5 In this work, none of the 45 analogues prepared exhibited a sugar-like temporal profile and, in fact, one compound exhibited a sweetness that took minutes to be noticed and which then lingered for nearly an hour. 6 In an effort to quantify temporal profile differences between CHOS and NCS, many sensory methods have been developed. 7 − 13 In addition to sensory studies, Hu and co-workers quantified temporal differences between sucrose and aspartame in a human fMRI study. 14 All of these methods either require highly sophisticated equipment (e.g., fMRI 14 method) or provide information on SL over time (e.g., TCATA 13 and other methods), rather than providing a single metric for SL. Also, in our experience, these methods generally show quite variable inter-panelist data. In the work we describe here, we use a descriptive analysis methodology where we are able to quantify a single SL metric and six other sensory attributes in a single test, thereby enabling a simple comparison of SL with and without a taste modulator composition (TMC). We now know, as a consequence of work by Li and co-workers, that human sweet taste follows from activation of a single heterodimeric G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) known as TAS1R2/TAS1R3. 15 Work in many laboratories has led to a detailed understanding of the transduction of sweet taste as has been reviewed. 16 Yet, in spite of the enhanced understanding of the biochemistry of sweet taste, scientists continue to be puzzled by temporal profile differences. Some believe that more potent sweeteners, because of higher receptor binding affinity, would exhibit a prolonged sweetness. However, this rationale is unlikely as demonstrated by an analysis of REBA activity. The apparent equilibrium constant for REBA/receptor binding ( K d ) has been estimated from concentration/response function experiments to be 210 μM. 17 Given that (1) K d is the quotient of the rate constants for REBA/receptor dissociation ( k d ) and association ( k a ) and (2) rate constants for diffusion of small molecules in water reflect diffusion-controlled kinetics (i.e., k a ∼ 1.5 × 10 9 M –1 s –1 ), 18 k d for REBA/receptor dissociation is estimated to be 3.2 × 10 5 s –1 . Then, assuming first-order kinetics for REBA/receptor dissociation, t 1/2 for REBA/receptor dissociation is calculated to be 2.2 × 10 –6 s, and therefore, the amount of REBA still bound to the receptor 60 s after the initial binding is negligible. Thus, strong binding of REBA to the sweetener receptor cannot explain REBA SL and, of course, the same is true for all known NCS. Other rationales for the atypical temporal profiles of NCS have been investigated. Karl and co-workers, in a study on 35 sweeteners to determine if structural determinants of sweeteners may correlate with temporal profiles, concluded that the rationale for sweetener temporal profile differences is complex with a pivotal role due to a combination of structural factors including “number of ketones, aromatic rings, double bonds, and the MlogP”. 19 Deck and co-workers, in a study to determine if the locus of sweetener/receptor binding may be a factor in sweetener temporal profiles, concluded that the binding locus does not have a major effect. 20 Finally, Bülbül and co-workers, in a study of CHOS and NCS, explored the hypothesis that NCS may bind nonspecifically to salivary proteins, thereby delaying initial receptor binding as well as enabling iterative receptor binding perceived as SL. 21 Surprisingly, however, the sweetener found to be most adsorbed in this study was HFCS-55, even though it does not show delayed AT or SL. In our efforts to understand the bio-rationale for delayed AT and SL, we considered many empirical observations including (1) NaCl increases the potencies of stevia sweeteners by ∼3-fold, 22 (2) erythritol, NaCl, KCl, and many osmolytes accelerate sweetness AT and reduce or eliminate REBA SL, 3 , 23 , 24 (3) astringent pre-rinses (e.g., alum) increase perceived sweetness intensity, accelerate sweetness AT, and attenuate the SL of REBA, 25 and (4) a REBA solution, tasted first thing in the morning, without any oral rinse, has low sweetness intensity, markedly delayed AT, and very pronounced SL. 26 As a result of efforts to rationalize these observations, we hypothesized that nonspecific binding of amphipathic NCS to hydrophobic sites in the oral cavity must be responsible for both delayed ATs and prolonged SLs. 27 Continuing in our efforts to understand delayed AT and SL, we noted the significant effects of saliva on taste. 28 In the absence of food, resting saliva is secreted primarily by the submandibular gland and contains a glycoprotein known as mucin, which is responsible for formation of a ca. 25 μm thick hydrogel on the surface of the tongue. In fact, the epithelia of the entire alimentary tract are covered with mucous hydrogels based on one or more of 21 known mucin glycoproteins. The specific mucin responsible for the hydrogel on the tongue is MUC5B. 29 Mucins provide a barrier to pathogens reaching cell surfaces but also affect small molecule diffusion rates. Slowed diffusion of therapeutic drugs through intestinal mucous hydrogels has been a challenge in achieving rapid bioavailability for therapeutic drugs 30 − 33 and has been intensively studied by the Ribbeck laboratory. 34 − 38 In salivary gland cells, the MUC5B glycoprotein is maintained in a compact structure as a Ca 2+ chelate. However, on secretion, the mucin glycoprotein is released from Ca 2+ binding and expands into a hydrogel coating on the tongue and oral epithelium. Given the foregoing information on the MUC5B mucin glycoprotein hydrogel acting as a barrier to sweeteners’ diffusion to their receptors on lingual taste bud cells, a hypothesis emerges as to the probable bio-rationale for their differing temporal profiles. Clearly, sweeteners must traverse the 25 μm MUC5B hydrogel coating the lingual epithelium in order to reach taste bud cells, and it is apparent that small polar molecules (e.g., sucrose, glucose, fructose, etc.) are able to make this journey rapidly. However, by analogy with the slowed diffusion of amphipathic therapeutic drugs through the mucous hydrogel in the small intestine, it is obvious that amphipathic sweetener molecules (e.g., aspartame, sucralose, neotame, REBA, etc.) engage in nonspecific binding to hydrophobic sites in the MUC5B hydrogel on the tongue and be delayed in reaching their receptors, thereby explaining longer ATs. Then, after receptor dissociation, they again bind in the MUC5B hydrogel, delaying diffusion away from the receptor and enabling iterative receptor activation experienced as SL. This hypothesis is consistent with all of the aforementioned empirical observations on NCS as well as delays in absorption of amphipathic therapeutic drugs. Given the aforementioned hypothesis as to the bio-rationale for the atypical temporal profiles of NCS, in this report, we develop formulation technologies for NCS aimed at accelerating their diffusion to and from sweetener receptors. Materials and Methods Chemicals used in this study were purchased from commercial suppliers or obtained from manufacturers. A trained descriptive analysis (DA) sensory panel was used in this study to assess the effects of formulation changes on sweetener system tastes, and standard statistical methods were employed to determine significant differences. Sensory Analysis of Sweetener Formulations Descriptive analysis (DA) methodology was used in this study as described by Carr and co-workers, 39 but as elaborated by Sensory Research, Ltd. (Cork, Ireland) in order to capture sweetness linger and mouthfeel information in addition to sweetness, sourness, saltiness, bitterness, and astringency attribute intensities in a single sensory experiment. 40 In screening experiments, DA analysis with blind tasting of samples was carried out by a single trained subject with three replications. Confirmation experiments were carried out with a trained panel of 15 subjects. Inclusion in a 15-person panel required that panelists were able to correctly rank a series of 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10% sucrose solutions in order of increasing sweetness intensity. Qualified subjects were then trained to gain familiarity with the taste attributes (1) sweetness (sucrose), (2) sourness (citric acid), (3) saltiness (sodium chloride), (4) bitterness (caffeine), (5) mouthfeel (glucose) and (6) astringency (alum) based on the tastes of aqueous solutions. Following familiarization with these six taste attributes, panelists were trained in intensity scaling with a range of sucrose solutions (2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15% sucrose). In the training program, panelists were provided with these sucrose standard solutions for the purpose of memorization of their perceived intensities on a 0–15 scale. Panelists were then provided sucrose solutions as unknowns and required to correctly rate sweetness intensities on a 0–15 scale. In the next stage of training, panelists were instructed to rate the intensities of sour, salty, bitter, mouthfeel, and astringent samples based on the learned 0–15 sucrose intensity scale. In addition to rating the intensities of the aforementioned six attributes, panelists were trained in the rating of sweetness linger (SL). On tasting the sample and quantifying intensities of sensory attributes 1–6, the sample is expectorated @ 15 s, the mouth gently rinsed with water (1 × 15 mL), and the water expectorated @ 30 s. The tongue and mouth are then held motionless and attention is paid to sweetness buildup over the next 2.0 min. The perceived sweetness intensity @ 2.0 min is defined as SL. Ten percent sucrose and 500 mg/L REBA, in water, are references and are defined as having SLs of 0 and 5, respectively, under these conditions. Experimental formulations were prepared and subjected to DA panel testing by the methods described above, where formulations are rated on 0–15 scales for the following taste attributes: sweetness intensity (SwI), sourness intensity (SoI), saltiness intensity (SaI), bitterness intensity (BI), mouthfeel intensity (MI), and astringency intensity (AI). In addition, formulations were rated for the temporal taste attribute SL. In sensory test sessions, the number of samples evaluated was limited to 4. Following screening experiments, testing on promising formulations was carried out with a trained panel as described above. Briefly, the sensory test protocol during formulation screening as well as in DA panel testing was as follows: (1) Randomize the samples. (2) Rinse the mouth with 15 mL of distilled water with gentle agitation followed by expectoration. (3) Take 15 mL of the sample into the mouth with gentle agitation for 15 s, rate SwI, SoI, SaI, BI, MI, and AI on 0–15 scales, and record the ratings. (4) Expectorate the sample, gently rinse the mouth with 15 mL of water, and expectorate at 15 s. (5) Note any change in perceived SwI over the next 2.0 min and record perceived SwI at 2.0 min (SL). (6) Rinse the mouth with 15 mL of distilled water with gentle agitation for 15 s and expectorate. (7) Wait for a minimum of 15 min and repeat with the next sample. Statistical Analysis of DA Panel Data For each experiment, sensory attribute ratings for DA panel data were averaged to obtain means and standard deviations (SDs) determined in Excel. Standard errors (SEs) for each sensory attribute rating were then calculated with the formula SE = SD/N 1/2 , where N is 15, the number of panelists participating. The mean sensory attribute ratings for each DA panel experiment were plotted in bar charts, and error bars of 1 SE were illustrated. The DA panel MI and SL data were analyzed using a two-factor ANOVA model using StatPlus:mac software. The factors were assessor and sample. The significance of the sample effect was assessed based on the P-value associated with the F-ratio for the sample effect. If the sample effect P -value &lt;0.05, it was concluded that there were significant differences among the average attribute intensities of the samples. For each attribute, if the sample effect was significant at the 95% confidence level ( P -value &lt;0.05), the average intensity ratings of the samples were compared using Tukey’s HSD post hoc test to determine which samples were significantly different from each other. Interest in this study was focused on the two sensory attributes MI and SL. So, annotated means tables, specifically for these two sensory attributes, were prepared to show statistically significant differences between sweeteners with and without taste modulators. All DA panel data and statistical analyses are provided in the Supporting Information . Results and Discussion As already commented, it is known that mucin glycoproteins are maintained in the compact form before salivary gland secretion by chelation to Ca 2+ ions. So, it was obvious to formulate NCS with a calcium salt in the hope that chelation would cause mucin shrinkage, or the creation of pores, so as to enable NCS to diffuse to and from the receptor more rapidly. The likelihood that shrinkage of the lingual mucin hydrogel may occur in the presence of Ca 2+ ions is supported by a porcine submaxillary mucin study by Jentoft and co-workers where dynamic light scattering showed rapid contraction on addition of CaCl 2 in the range of 33–500 mM. 41 Similarly, Bansil and Turner reported that mucins in the presence of Ca 2+ , at a concentration comparable to that in secretory cells, can undergo a reversible 100–200-fold shrinkage. 42 Further, Su and co-workers observed significant compaction of human and bovine mucins on addition of CaCl 2 , even at 5 mM. 43 In order to test this possibility, 500 mg/L REBA was formulated with CaCl 2 at 10, 30, and 100 mM in (1) water, (2) pH 3.2 citric acid (CA)/trisodium citrate (TSC) buffer, and (3) pH 2.4 phosphoric acid. These solutions were then evaluated by the DA panel testing protocol with results given in Table 1 . The CA/TSC pH 3.2 buffer and the pH 2.4 phosphoric acid systems were used in this study, as these are common in commercial lemon-lime- and cola-flavored beverages. We were encouraged to find that in all three systems, the SL of REBA decreased significantly in the presence of CaCl 2 . However, with the exception of the CA/TSC buffer system, it was necessary to go to a 30 mM CaCl 2 concentration in order to realize a ∼25% reduction in REBA SL. Very noticeable off-tastes, particularly saltiness, bitterness, and astringency, were prominent @ 30 mM and higher concentrations of CaCl 2 . Specifically, the reductions in REBA SL observed for 10, 30, and 100 mM CaCl 2 in water were 1, 26, and 46%, in CA/TSC buffer, 18, 49, and 62%, and in the phosphoric acid system, 4, 27, and 59%, respectively. We then considered that Mg 2+ ions might be more effective than Ca 2+ ions in chelating mucin because of their higher association constant for citrate ions, presumably because of the higher charge to ionic radius. 44 So, the sensory experiment was repeated using MgCl 2 , with results given in Table 2 . As in the CaCl 2 experiments, we were encouraged to find that in all three systems, REBA SL decreased significantly in the presence of MgCl 2 . However, as was the case with CaCl 2 , in every system, it was necessary to go to high MgCl 2 concentrations in order to approach a 25% SL reduction. Very noticeable off-tastes were prominent at these higher concentrations of MgCl 2 . Specifically, the reductions in REBA SL observed for 10, 30, and 100 mM MgCl 2 in water were 0, 20, and 39%, in CA/TSC buffer, 8, 44, and 59%, and in the phosphoric acid system, 5, 16, and 30%, respectively. As noted above in the discussion of empirical observations on the SL of NCS, both NaCl and KCl were observed to be effective in attenuation of aspartame and REBA SL, albeit only at high concentrations that have a significant salty off-taste. Even though CaCl 2 was found more effective than NaCl in compaction of porcine submaxillary mucin, 42 we evaluated the effect of KCl on the SL of REBA in a direct comparison to the effects of MgCl 2 and CaCl 2 in a CA/TSC buffer system. The results of this study are given in Table 3 . We were surprised to find in this study that KCl was comparable in effectiveness to both MgCl 2 and CaCl 2 in the reduction of REBA SL. Specifically, REBA SL reductions promoted by KCl at 5.4, 10.7, 20, and 30 mM were observed to be 4, 9, 19, and 30%, respectively, while SL reductions for 5.9, 11.8, 20, and 30 mM MgCl 2 were observed to be 4, 10, 21, and 32%, respectively, and SL reductions for CaCl 2 at 5.9, 11.8, 20, and 30 mM were observed to be 6, 12, 27, and 41%, respectively. It was our expectation that only multivalent ions like Ca 2+ and Mg 2+ would have this effect on SL, as they would be more effective in creating cross-links between carboxyl groups on mucin sialic acid moieties as proposed by Chernick and co-workers. 45 However, our results, at least at low pH, show that KCl is comparable in SL reduction to both CaCl 2 and MgCl 2 . Again, however, a concentration of 20 mM KCl, MgCl 2 , or CaCl 2 was required to realize significant SL reductions. Quite possibly, it could be that the effect observed on the SL of REBA with all of KCl, MgCl 2 , and CaCl 2 is, in significant part, an osmotic effect, causing mucin hydrogel shrinkage and enabling accelerated diffusion of REBA to and from the sweetener receptor. Resting saliva is predominantly a submandibular salivary gland secretion and is reported to be quite hypotonic with an osmolarity of ca. 30 mOsM. 28 However, the osmolarity of the aqueous pores within the mucin hydrogel is unknown but may be substantially lower. If this is the case, addition of a solution of 40–60 mOsM KCl, MgCl 2 , or CaCl 2 above the mucin hydrogel covering the tongue may cause significant mucin hydrogel shrinkage, thereby enabling accelerated diffusion of sweeteners to and from the sweetener receptors. For MgCl 2 and CaCl 2 , the effect could be both an osmotic effect and an effect on mucin cross-linking, both effects leading to mucin compaction, but where their total effects are similar to the osmotic effect of KCl. The osmotic mechanism is consistent with the observation noted above that erythritol and many nonionic osmolytes also attenuate REBA SL. It is to be noted, however, based on the above data, that none of KCl, MgCl 2 , or CaCl 2 , used individually, solves the problem of REBA SL, since all of them require a concentration of at least ∼20 mM to enable a significant reduction in SL. At such high concentrations, all exhibit significant off-tastes. In pharmacology, mixtures of drugs with the same activity, but acting by different mechanisms, are often observed to be supra-additive or synergistic. For this reasoning, we did an experiment in pH 3.1 CA/tripotassium citrate (TPC) buffer comparing the activities in REBA SL reduction of low concentrations of KCl, MgCl 2 , and CaCl 2 with the SL reduction of a KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 blend taste modulator composition (TMC) to explore the potential of synergy in the hope of identifying a mixture showing significant reduction in SL but without salty or bitter off-tastes. The results of this experiment are illustrated in Figure 1 . The data in Figure 1 show that the sucrose formulation and the REBA/KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 formulation are significantly lower in SL and higher in mouthfeel intensity (MI) than all of the other samples. Specifically, the REBA/KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 formulation is 69% lower in SL than the REBA control sample, albeit higher than the sucrose sample showing no SL. Also, while the REBA/KCl, REBA/MgCl 2 , and REBA/CaCl 2 samples do show a significant increase in MI over the REBA control, none of them show MI close to that of the sucrose control. Only the REBA/KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 formulation shows MI comparable to the sucrose control; in actuality, its MI is 26% greater than that of the sucrose control. Finally, none of the REBA samples with KCl, MgCl 2 , or CaCl 2 show any increase in SwI over the REBA control, while, interestingly, the REBA/KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 formulation is higher in SwI than the REBA control. This study demonstrates that the REBA formulation with KCl, MgCl 2 , and CaCl 2 blend TMC shows significant supra-additivity of SwI and MI with a supra-suppression in SL, relative to the effects anticipated based on additivity. Thus, surprisingly, the data support the conclusion that the effects of KCl, MgCl 2 , and CaCl 2 in a ternary blend composition are synergistic. It is noteworthy that through the use of a blend of KCl, MgCl 2 , and CaCl 2 , we were able to achieve a marked improvement in the taste of a REBA formulation in CA/TPC buffer. Specifically, the REBA formulation with the KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 TMC showed significantly increased MI and reduced SL. It is rational to question whether these taste improvements observed for REBA are unique to REBA formulations or if this effect may be general to other NCS. Thus, we evaluated the effect of a 10 mM KCl/3 mM MgCl 2 /3 mM CaCl 2 TMC on the tastes of 11 sweeteners, specifically sucrose, sodium saccharin (SAC-Na), sodium cyclamate (CYC-Na), aspartame (APM), acesulfame-K (ACE-K), sucralose (SUL), neotame (NTM), advantame (ADV), REBA, REBM, and monk fruit sweetener (MFS). For this experiment, we used isosweet concentrations of each sweetener calculated based on known sweetener C/R functions. 3 Since the SwI of 500 ppm REBA is calculated to be equivalent to 7.1% sucrose, the C/R functions of the other sweeteners were used to determine concentrations predicted to be isosweet with 7.1% sucrose. The results of this experiment are given in Table 4 . The data from this experiment are consistent with the earlier results on REBA alone where the KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 TMC reduced SL. Clearly, the SL reduction effect is general for NCS with the 10 mM KCl/3 mM MgCl 2 /3 mM CaCl 2 TMC reducing SL for SAC-Na (53%), CYC-Na (72%), APM (71%), ACE-K (78%), SUL (74%), NTM (74%), ADV (65%), REBA (76%), REBM (76%), and MFS (79%). It is of note for this experiment that all 11 formulations containing the TMC exhibited a faint salty taste. However, the perception of a faint salty off-taste was not uniform among panelists. For example, for CYC-Na and NTM, ca. 20% of panelists reported a weak salty off-taste. Most noteworthy in this experiment is that the TMC dramatically increased the perception of sugar-like MI for all sweeteners, even including sucrose. In every case, the sugar-like MIs reported for the TMC-fortified samples were greater than the MI of 10% sucrose. This serendipitous finding of strong sugar-like mouthfeel is fortuitous, since low MI is a shortcoming of all NCS. The question is as to the bio-rationale of this pleasant sensory effect. In 2012, Maruyama and co-workers reported that the calcium-sensing receptor (CSR) is expressed in a subset of taste bud cells and that these cells, when activated, exhibit a taste that they referred to as kokumi taste and translated as “mouthfulness”. 47 Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the strong pleasant sugar-like MI observed when sweeteners are formulated with the TMC is a consequence of activation of the CSR by Ca 2+ ions. It is also known that the Mg 2+ ion is an agonist at the CSR 48 , 49 and that the Na + ion and presumably the K + ion are allosteric modulators. 50 Thus, it seems probable that the pleasant sugar-like mouthfeel observed on addition of the KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 TMC to sweetener solutions is a consequence of activity at the CSR by K + , Mg 2+ , and Ca 2+ ions. Interestingly, it has been reported that glucose at 25 mM (4.5%) is an allosteric modulator of the CSR, and thus, it could well be that the CSR is the entity responsible for the pleasing sugar-like mouthfeel perception of CHOS solutions like sucrose and high-fructose starch syrups. 51 While activation of the CSR by K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ is an attractive rationale for the pleasant sugar-like mouthfeel we observe in our formulations, some findings are inconsistent with this pathway. For example, it is known that the amino acids l -phenylalanine ( l -Phe) and l -tryptophan ( l -Trp), as well as the peptide glutathione (GSH), are positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) of the CSR, so it would be predicted that these compounds would potentiate the MI of a KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 solution. 52 However, they do not. The bitter taste thresholds for l -Phe and l -Trp were both estimated to be ca. 3 mM by tasting serial dilutions in CA/TPC buffer. A solution of GSH in CA/TPC buffer showed no bitter or other off-taste at 0.1 mM. Of these three CSR PAMs, GSH was reported to have &gt;100-fold higher affinity for the CSR than either l -Phe or l -Trp and an EC 50 of 0.08 μM. 53 However, sensory experiments with 500 mg/L REBA/10 mM KCl/3 mM MgCl 2 /3 mM CaCl 2 in CA/TPC buffer with 3 mM l -Phe, 3 mM Trp, or 0.1 mM GSH all showed no increase in MI over the control sample. As the EC 50 for Ca 2+ at the CSR has been reported to be 4.2 mM, it was considered that PAM effects for l -Phe, l -Trp, and GSH may be more easily observed at lower Mg 2+ and Ca 2+ concentrations. However, on repeating the experiment with 500 mg/L REBA/10 mM KCl/1 mM MgCl 2 /1 mM CaCl 2 , still no enhancement of MI was observed for any of l -Phe, l -Trp, or GSH. So, at this time, the bio-rationale for the very pleasant sugar-like mouthfeel of formulations containing the K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ TMCs can only be tentatively assigned as CSR-mediated. Recently, Karl and co-workers hypothesized that the sensory diversity for the sweeteners sucrose, sucralose, REBM, and neohesperidin dihydrochalcone might differentially change saliva flows and saliva viscoelastic properties following tasting these different sweeteners. 54 Further, they hypothesized that such saliva effects might be the bio-rationale for the perceived mouthfeels of different sweeteners. However, while differences in saliva flow rates between panelists were noted, no significant differences in the viscosities of saliva were observed for any of these sweeteners. Therefore, saliva viscosity does not appear to be an explanation for the perceived mouthfeel of sweetener formulations. In summary, while signaling initiated by the CSR remains the simplest bio-rationale for the sugar-like mouthfeel of K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ mineral salt TMCs, the mechanism for this pleasing sensory effect cannot be stated with certainty. Given the pronounced effects of the K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ mineral salt compositions on the MIs of CHOS and NCS formulations, we also investigated the effects of a 10 mM KCl/5 mM MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O/5 mM Ca(Lactate) 2 ·5H 2 O TMC on umami taste, specifically on the taste of MSG. We observed a 148% increase in the MI of a 2245 mg/L MSG solution. Quite clearly, the results of this study on the effects of the KCl/MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O/Ca(Lactate) 2 ·5H 2 O TMC on the taste of MSG show that the MI effects of K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ mineral salt compositions are general, not just an effect to be observed with sweetener solutions and are independent of anion identity. In experiments discussed above, we have found that the KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 TMC is effective in the reduction of the SL of all NCS and in the addition of sugar-like MI to formulations of them as well. Also observed is that this KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 TMC even increases the sugar-like mouthfeel of sucrose. Experiments were then carried out to explore the potential for the improvement of the tastes of a range of commercial beverages by spiking K + /Mg 2+ /Ca 2+ TMCs into them. Examples include the following. Zero-Calorie Cola Beverages In our early formulation studies with the KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 TMCs, faint salty off-tastes were sometimes observed, an effect presumed to be due to high concentrations of chloride ions, the latter being a known driver of salty taste perception. 55 − 58 Therefore, in this study of spiking a TMC mineral salt composition into cola products, the low chloride KCl/MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O/Ca(Lactate) 2 ·5H 2 O TMC was employed. Two commercial zero-calorie cola beverages, one sweetened with SUL/ACE-K and the second with APM, were evaluated with and without the TMC. The results of these studies are given in Figures 2 and 3 . It is noteworthy that in these two experiments with reduced chloride concentration, perceived saltiness intensities for both the SUL/ACE-K and APM colas were no greater than the commercial products used as controls. However, the cola beverage pHs increased significantly, which affected beverage sourness. For this reason, the pHs of these samples were adjusted downward by addition of H 3 PO 4 . In every case, however, the TMCs significantly reduced SL for both SUL/ACE-K and APM zero-calorie colas and very dramatically increased sugar-like MI perception. SL reductions for the SUL/ACE-K cola were 43 and 59%, respectively, for the two concentrations of the TMC. Similarly, SL reductions for the APM zero-calorie cola were 36 and 66%, respectively. This experiment further demonstrates that the taste improvement effects are independent of anion identities and are due to the K + , Mg 2+ , and Ca 2+ cations. REBA-Sweetened 50% Calorie-Reduced Orange Juice Beverage In view of the dramatic effects on the sugar-like MIs of noncaloric sweetener as well as sucrose formulations, we evaluated the effect of two concentrations of the KCl/MgCl 2 /CaCl 2 TMC on the taste of a 50% calorie-reduced orange juice product. The results of this study are illustrated in Figure 4 . The calorie-reduced REBA-sweetened orange juice beverage showed modest SL, which was significantly reduced by the TMC. Specifically, 39 and 62% SL reductions were observed for the two concentrations of TMC. In addition, the control beverage deriving 50% of its sweetness from REBA did show significant sugar-like MI, which was markedly enhanced by the TMC. Specifically, 88 and 111% increases in MI were observed for the two concentrations of TMC. Full-Calorie Citrus-Flavored Carbonated Beverage In view of the dramatic effects on the sugar-like MIs of NCS, we evaluated the effect of two concentrations of the KCl/MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O/Ca(Lactate) 2 ·5H 2 O TMC on the taste of a commercial citrus-flavored carbonated soft drink sweetened with HFCS-55. The results of this study are illustrated in Figure 5 . The HFCS-55-sweetened carbonated beverage did not show any perceptible SL but did exhibit significant sugar-like MI. Interestingly, on addition of the KCl/MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O/Ca(Lactate) 2 ·5H 2 O TMCs, the beverage exhibited sharply increased sugar-like MI. Specifically, MI increases of 32% (39% for pH-adjusted beverage) and 66% (74% for pH-adjusted beverage) were observed for the HFSS-55-sweetened full-calorie beverage. It is noteworthy that this TMC caused a marked increase in pH due to its buffering activity, an effect that was countered by addition of citric acid to adjust pH to that of the commercial product used as a control. Similar results were observed on spiking the KCl/MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O/Ca(Lactate) 2 ·5H 2 O TMC into a commercial sucrose-sweetened citrus-flavored carbonated beverage, and results are provided in the Supporting Information . In summary, we have identified a mechanism-of-action-based formulation technology, employing mixtures of K + , Mg 2+ , and Ca 2+ mineral salts, which significantly reduce the sweetness linger of all NCS and introduce high levels of sugar-like mouthfeel for all sweeteners, both CHOS and NCS, as well as for umami formulations. Based on the fact that the lingual epithelium is covered by a 25 μm thick layer of mucin hydrogel, which impedes the diffusion of amphipathic molecules such as NCS to the sweetener receptor, and the reports that the Ca 2+ ion promotes the compaction of mucin hydrogels, it is apparent as to the mechanism whereby our K + , Mg 2+ , and Ca 2+ TMCs enable accelerated diffusion to and from taste bud receptors. These accelerated diffusions explain the general reduction in SLs for NCS. We propose that both Ca 2+ and Mg 2+ ions bind to the mucin hydrogel so as to cross-link mucin glycoprotein carboxylate, and possibly sulfate moieties, thereby causing shrinkage and compaction of the mucin hydrogel covering the lingual epithelium. The mechanism for NCS SL reduction promoted by KCl seems unlikely to be the same as that of the Mg 2+ and Ca 2+ mineral salts. We propose that the effect of KCl is in combination with the Mg 2+ and Ca 2+ mineral salts, to create a hyperosmotic solution above the hydrogel, thereby contributing to hydrogel shrinkage and facilitating accelerated NCS diffusion to and from taste bud receptors. We suggest that the observed synergy in K + , Mg 2+ , and Ca 2+ mineral salt taste modulator compositions is a consequence of the simultaneous action of two pathways for mucin compaction, Mg 2+ and Ca 2+ ion cross-linking of mucin and an osmotic effect of the mineral salts. Finally, there is a dramatic increase in sugar-like mouthfeel observed in formulations with the K + , Mg 2+ , and Ca 2+ mineral salt compositions. We speculate that this marked improvement in sugar-like mouthfeel may be driven by the activation of taste bud cells expressing the calcium-sensing receptor by both Ca 2+ and Mg 2+ ions, with possible allosteric modulation by K + ions.
