Background In the recent past an incremental incidence of scurvy has been reported in the pediatric population, especially in children with neuropsychiatric disorders and a selective diet. Musculoskeletal manifestations, such as limp and refusal to walk, usually represent the main causes of access in the emergency room. Nowadays, new patterns of manifestations have been demonstrated, such as hematological and cardiological changes including pulmonary hypertension, rarely resulting in potential lethal complications. Case presentation : We describe five pediatric patients affected by developmental delay or autism, with a restrictive diet, diagnosed with scurvy during the hospitalization: 3 pre-school aged boys presenting with typical clinical presentation, due to severe malnutrition; a 6-year-old girl with refusal to walk and a very high titre of inflammatory markers, without typical signs of scurvy; a 4-year-old boy with severe pulmonary hypertension, rarely related to vitamin C deficiency. In all the cases, a prompt resolution of the symptoms after the administration of vitamin C has been observed, confirming the diagnosis. Conclusion At present, scurvy is a more contemporary condition than previously thought. It is fundamental to recognize both typical and atypical manifestations to avoid delay of diagnosis or rarely life-threatening complications. Malabsorption and hematological disorders (transfusion-related iron overload, bone marrow transplant recipients and chronic graft versus host disease) seems to be other possibly associated comorbidity and recently some cases of scurvy have also been registered in otherwise healthy children, with a prevalence of males regarding gender distribution; however, to date its actual worldwide prevalence is difficult to estimate [ 4 , 5 ]. Evidence of undetectable or low plasma vitamin C levels (&lt; 4 mg/dL) is still considered the diagnostic gold standard, but if clinical presentation is strongly suggestive, it is also possible to start an ex juvantibus treatment, confirming the diagnosis in the case of prompt resolution of the symptoms (Fig. 1 ) [ 4 ]. Early clinical manifestations are characterized by general symptoms (irritability, inappetence, mild fever) and dermatological changes (petechiae, ecchymosis, corkscrew hair); whereas gingival (swelling and bleeding gums, rarely loosening of teeth) and musculoskeletal signs (arthralgia, myalgia, limp, and joint swellings) define a condition of overt scurvy [ 3 ]. Hematological changes, such as anemia and elevated inflammatory markers, have been newly mentioned as possible consequences of vitamin C deficiency. Few data have been recently reported regarding cardiological changes. It seems that vitamin C deficiency may directly cause a form of reversible pulmonary hypertension that, if unrecognized, may lead to cardiac arrest, permanent sequelae, and death [ 5 ]. The treatment consists of a substitutive therapy with ascorbic acid: no specific regimen of treatment is defined in pediatric age; oral administration is adequate even in the most severe cases and intravenous treatment should be reserved in case of suspected or confirmed malabsorption [ 5 , 6 ]. Evidence suggests that a dose between 100 and 300 mg per day leads to complete resolution of early and typical symptoms within 1 month [ 7 ], then it is possible to have a vitamin C supplementation at a lower dose (100–150 mg per day) for another 2–3 months or until definitive resolution of symptoms [ 8 ]. The resolution of bone changes may take several weeks. No sufficient data are nowadays reported concerning atypical manifestation [ 5 ]. Then, because most children develop scurvy as a result of severe food selectivity, long-term nutritional supplementation, nutritionist consultation, and dietary changes should be required to prevent recurrence [ 7 ]. Case presentation Case 1 F., a 2-year-old boy affected by a developmental delay (poor language, no masticatory function, selective diet), came to our attention for refusal to walk, diffuse musculoskeletal pain and loss of weight. In familial anamnesis poor socio-economic conditions (great-grandmother as caregiver) stands out. The auxological evaluation highlighted a condition of severe malnutrition: Weight &lt; 3 °P, Height 5 °P, W/H 70%. He presented bad general conditions, with hypotonic and flexed lower limbs, swollen and bleeding gums and diffuse petechiae. Laboratory and instrumental data excluded all the possible causes of organic malnutrition (Fig. 2 ). In the suspicion of scurvy, we started a substitutive intravenous therapy with ascorbic acid 300 mg/day: in 2 days, he was able to maintain bare weight; after 7 days, a complete remission of the clinical manifestation was observed, and he was able to walk with assistance. Plasma vitamin C level (not available during recovery) confirmed the diagnosis of scurvy. An oral supportive therapy (200 mg/d) was maintained. After 2 months, he was able to walk independently and thanks to the oral nutritional supplements and a better family and dietary education, he gained 2 kg. After 10 months, no signs of scurvy were found, however, despite a better varied diet, an oral supplement of 100 mg/d of vitamin C was maintained. Case 2 N., a 6-year-old girl affected by a developmental delay, with a selective diet and a history of recurrent fever, was admitted to our unit for refusal to walk, abdominal pain and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 18 xN, ESR 120 mm/h). Auxological parameters were normal: Weight 50 °P, Height 75 °P, BMI 25 °P. At the physical examination she presented discreet general condition, irritability, muscular pain and petechial manifestation in lower limbs. Laboratory and instrumental data allowed to exclude all the possible organic condition explaining the association between deambulatory disfunction and elevated inflammatory markers, as infectious (osteomyelitis, septic arthritis), autoimmune (juvenile idiopathic arthritis AIG, chronic nonbacterial osteomyelitis CNO), neoplastic (leukemia, Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma) or neurological diseases (Fig. 2 ). In addition, a mild pulmonary hypertension (PH) was found (sPAP 25–30 mmHg). During the etiological work up, due to the nutritional habits and the clinical presentation, the hypothesis of scurvy was considered, so she started supportive oral therapy with ascorbic acid 300 mg/day. After 2 days she was able to walk with uncertain deambulation; after one week of therapy, she was able to walk normally, with evidence of normal levels of CRP, decreasing trend for ESR (100 mm/h) and normalization of pulmonary pressure. Plasma vitamin C levels (not available during recovery) confirmed the diagnosis of scurvy. After a 2 months therapy, she presented normal levels of inflammatory markers. An oral supplementation of ascorbic acid (100 mg/d) was maintained. No evidence of elevated inflammatory index were found in further evaluations (Table 1 ). Case 3 R., a 4-year-old boy affected by autism, with a selective diet, came to our attention for refusal to walk. Auxological parameters were normal: Weight 15°P, Height 25°P, BMI 15°P. He presented very bad general conditions, very pale skin, petechiae and oedema in lower limbs, antalgic position, swollen and bleeding gingiva. The laboratory exams showed a severe microcytic anemia (Hb 6,3 g/dL, MCV 68 fL), hence he needed a blood transfusion. Further, he presented a 3/6 mesocardiac holosystolic murmur. During the cardiological workup (thorax X-ray, electrocardiography and echocardiography), a severe pulmonary hypertension (PH) was found: systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) 75–80 mmHg, inferior vena cava (IVC) normally collapsed, with right ventricular (RV) dilatation and global ventricular dysmotility, with preserved pump function (EF 55–60%). No signs of left hearth disease or valvular hearth disease were identified. Laboratory exams excluded all the possible systemic (renal, metabolic, hematological, autoimmune diseases) or infective conditions. Further, chest imaging did not find any pulmonary cause (Fig. 2 ). Once excluded all the possible main causes of pediatric PH, a substitutive intravenous therapy with ascorbic acid 300 mg/day was started because of the clinical presentation evocative for scurvy. After 3 days of therapy a considerable improvement was noticed (sPAP 40–45 mmHg, moderate PH) and after 5 days a complete resolution of the cardiac condition was obtained (pre-discharge echocardiography: normal heart in anatomy and function, sPAP &lt; 25 mmHg), confirming the diagnosis of scurvy related PH (Fig. 3 ). An oral supportive therapy (200 mg/daily) was maintained. No evidence of heart sequelae or signs of scurvy 2 months later or in the further evaluations during the following 10 months. However, due to the persistent selective diet, an oral supportive vitamin C integration of 100 mg/d was maintained. Case 4 B., a 4-year-old boy with a highly selective diet (complete refusal to fruits and vegetables), came to our attention for limp and diffuse musculoskeletal pain. The auxological described a condition of severe malnutrition: Weight &lt; 3 °P, Height 25 °P, BMI &lt; 3 °P. He presented bad general conditions, with irritability, swollen gums and pilaris hyperkeratosis, hypotonic lower limbs with diffuse musculoskeletal pain during the walk (especially in the right lower limb), with impossibility of making more than 4 steps. No trauma in his recent history; the absence of fever within normal laboratory findings (CRP negative) excluded the possibility of a septic arthritis or an osteomyelitis; radiological exams (lower limb radiography, hip ultrasound) did not find any osteo-articular causes, as fractures, spondylodiscitis, osteochondritis, hip synovitis or bone tumors. In the suspicion of scurvy, we started substitutive intravenous therapy with ascorbic acid 300 mg/day: after 1 day, he was able to maintain bare weight and walk with assistance without any painful symptom; after 4 days, a complete remission of the clinical manifestation was observed, and he was able to walk without assistance. Plasma vitamin C level (not available during recovery) was normal, however, the clinical findings associated with the prompt resolution of the symptoms after the treatment with vitamin C confirmed the diagnosis. Further, an accurate revision of the radiological exam conducted by a pediatric orthopedist found the presence of typical abnormality of scurvy as the Frankel line (Table 2 ). An oral supportive therapy (300 mg/d) was maintained for 1 month. After 3 months, with a 100 mg oral therapy with vitamin C, no signs of scurvy were noticed in the follow up. After 12 months, many different foods were introduced in his diet thanks to a better familiar compliance, so that oral vitamin C treatment was unnecessary, and no other relapse of scurvy were noticed. Case 5 C., a 5-year-old boy affected by a developmental delay (poor language, selective diet), came to our attention for refusal to walk. The auxological described a condition of severe malnutrition: Weight &lt; 3 °P, Height 10 °P, BMI &lt; 3 °P. He presented bad general conditions, with swollen and bleeding gums, hypotonic and flexed lower limbs with diffuse musculoskeletal pain and perifollicular hyperkeratotic papules. Laboratory and instrumental data excluded all the possible causes of refusal to walk, as musculoskeletal, orthopedic, neurological or oncological conditions. In the suspicion of scurvy, we started substitutive oral therapy with ascorbic acid 300 mg/day: in 5 days, he was able to maintain bare weight; after 9 days, a complete remission of the clinical manifestation was observed, and he was able to walk with assistance. Plasma vitamin C level (not available during recovery) confirmed the diagnosis of scurvy. An oral supportive therapy (300 mg/d) was maintained. After 2 months, he was able to walk independently, no signs of scurvy were noticed. After 6 months, thanks to a family and dietary education, he was able to eat many different foods and he gained + 3,5 kg, thus vitamin C integration was no more necessary. Discussion and conclusion Despite the knowledge of the disease and of the main clinical features, to date several cases and series have documented a notably delay on the diagnosis due to the huge amount of clinical conditions that scurvy can mimic [ 3 ]. Customarily patients present with limp, inability to maintain bare weight or refusal to walk, as proved by our three patients described (Table 3 ) [ 5 ]. In the first and in the last case described, the association between severe malnutrition and specific clinical signs of vitamin C deficiency suddenly reinforced the suspicion of scurvy, definitely confirmed by the evidence of low levels of plasma vitamin C. Moreover, the fourth case shows how scurvy is more a clinical than a biochemical diagnosis and still despite normal values of plasma vitamin C, the presence of typical signs and their prompt resolution after the treatment with vitamin C allows to confirm the diagnosis. However, the presence of isolated symptoms may be confusing and atypical signs recently described can rarely complicate the clinical presentation [ 3 ]. For example, the presence of hematochezia associated with severe anemia led us to think about an inflammatory bowel disease as first possibility in a patient successively diagnosed with scurvy (case 6, Table 2 ) [ 9 ]. If we analyze the second case described, only a careful history collection and an accurate differential diagnosis gave rise to the hypothesis of scurvy in the 6-year-old female child with refusal to walk and high titers of inflammatory markers. In current scientific literature, data collected in the last decades show 8 studies, limited to case series or reports [ 10 – 17 ], evaluating the relationship between vitamin C deficiency and inflammatory markers. A recent narrative review collected the most of them, demonstrating that more than a half (59%) of the patients assessed presented elevated values of ESR and/or PCR [ 5 ]. Thus, a direct relationship between Vitamin C deficiency and the increasing of inflammatory markers has been hypothesized, possibly due to a loss of the antioxidant effect provided by vitamin C or as a consequence of a pro-inflammatory effect mediated by the subperiosteal or muscular hemorrhage [ 16 ]. CRP levels tend to normalize rapidly, meanwhile ESR levels require at least 1–2 months [ 5 ]. According to the third patient of our series, only few pediatric cases of PH have been reported in literature. First case dates back to 2013 [ 18 ] and to date only 6 pediatric cases are described [ 19 – 23 ]. Two possible mechanisms may explain a direct relationship between vitamin C deficiency and PH: an altered nitric oxide (NO) production, due to the function of vitamin C of promoting the degradation of NO synthetase inhibitor, increasing the availability of NO; alternatively, a possible hypoxia inducible factor (HIF) dysregulation, since vitamin C represents a cofactor for the family of prolyl hydroxylases that serve as cellular oxygen sensors and regulate the activity of HIF [ 21 ]. Despite uncommon, if unrecognized and untreated, PH may run to severe complications up to life-threatening conditions, as shown in the latest cases in literature [ 19 – 21 ]: patients with undiagnosed scurvy who experienced a cardiac arrest during general anesthesia for invasive diagnostic procedures, in one case needing ECMO and resulting in thalamic and cerebral hemorrhagic and ischemic strokes [ 20 ]. Further, many diagnostic and therapeutic approaches are proposed, delaying the appropriate treatment and the time of recovery, as described by Satawiriya et al. [ 22 ] in their case series of 2 children diagnosed with scurvy related PH: a 2-year-old boy presenting with a cardiac arrest, needing cardiopulmonary resuscitation and a 6-year-old boy presenting with cyanosis and dyspnea, both receiving invasive diagnostic exams (as right cardiac catheterization) and hemodynamic supportive treatment (as milrinone and sildenafil) before starting the treatment with vitamin C, with a complete resolution of the symptoms within 2 weeks − 1 month respectively. Anyway, no cardiac or further sequelae were identified in the 1 year follow-up, confirming PH associated with scurvy is completely reversible with appropriate investigation, diagnosis, and treatment [ 22 ]. Whereas, in our experience, the well-timed recognition of scurvy and the suspicion of a PH vitamin C deficiency related allowed us to treat it earlier and as a result a complete resolution of the severe clinical presentation was obtained in only 5 days. Further, it is important to highlight that also the 6 y.o. girl of our series presented with an asymptomatic, mild PH, promptly remitted in a few days of ascorbic acid therapy. As regards to anemia, it is well known that it can occur because of the contemporary loss of other vitamins such as B12 and folates (macrocytic anemia) or secondary to bleeding (normocytic anemia) [ 5 ]; while a sideropenic microcytic anemia can be directly explained as a direct consequence of vitamin C deficiency, due to the involvement of ascorbic acid in the iron absorption in the bowel [ 8 ]. Finally, it appears clear that an interdisciplinary teams, including nutritionists, psychologies and social workers when necessary, is essential in managing the follow up of these patients, and in preventing the deficiency of the vitamin C and the possible consequences of scurvy in many population at risk, particularly in patients with neurodevelopmental disorders; psychological and nutritionist support should be mandatory in order to understand the reason of child’s feeding behavior, to highlighting the relationship between the caregiver and to facilitate child’s acceptance of oral food intake in case of restrictive diet disorders [ 6 ]. To conclude, we emphasize the concept that unexplained inflammatory signs, anemia or pulmonary hypertension must not discourage the idea of scurvy if strongly suggested by clinical presentation or history collection. Eventually, PH is a rare and potentially life-threatening complication but reversible as all the clinical features of scurvy if promptly recognized and treated.
