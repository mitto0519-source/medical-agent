Background Incidental triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) abnormal findings could be detected on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), whether these incidental findings were causes of symptoms can confuse clinicians. The purpose of this study was to demonstrate the MRI features of triangular fibrocartilage (TFC) disc in asymptomatic adults’ wrists and explore the association with age, gender and ulnar variance. Methods Healthy adult volunteers who reported no ulnar-sided wrist pain were recruited and underwent MRI examinations. The thickness and angle of TFC and ulnar variance were measured on the mid-coronal slice, and coronal morphology and signal characteristics of the TFC disc were assessed. Regression analyses were performed to evaluate the relationships between the MRI features of TFC and age, gender and ulnar variance. Results Eighty-four asymptomatic adult volunteers (42 men, 42 women; mean age of men and women, 37 years and 46 years, respectively) were included, with 23 participants having a TFC defect. Ulnar variance was the influencing factor significantly associated with thickness, angle and morphology of TFC (all p &lt; 0.001). The angles and prevalence of a more distally stretched disc were higher in women compared to men. Age was not a statistically significant factor of TFC thickness and angle, but age had a mediating effect [12.43% ( p = 0.016)] on the association between ulnar variance and TFC morphology, while the direct effect of ulnar variance accounted for 87.57% ( p &lt; 0.001). Additionally, multivariate logistic regression results showed that an increase in age was significantly associated with an increased prevalence of abnormal TFC signal and discontinuity. Conclusions Ulnar variance is the most significant factor in determining TFC thickness, angle, and morphology. Age did not affect thickness and angle directly but may influence the relationship between ulnar variance and TFC morphology, and age is associated with an increased prevalence of abnormal signal and discontinuity in the TFC. This study offers a reference for clinicians and a comprehensive consideration and analysis, including age, ulnar variance, clinical history and physical examination should be made when interpreting MRI findings. TFCC is an important structure to maintain the stability of the ulnocarpal joint and distal radioulnar joint and allow the stable motion of the wrist and rotation of the forearm [ 2 ], and TFCC injury is considered as a common cause of the ulnar-sided wrist pain [ 3 ]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), especially high-resolution MRI has been widely used for evaluation of TFCC pathology. However, several studies have proved that incidental TFCC abnormal findings, like changes in morphology or signal intensity, are observed in asymptomatic participants, including healthy adolescents and asymptomatic adults [ 4 – 6 ], which makes it a challenge to interpret MRI findings correctly. In addition, there are several studies that have found TFCC signal abnormalities on MRI are more common with increasing age [ 5 , 7 , 8 ]. Sugimoto et al. [ 6 ] found TFC disc defects could be detected in teens and the prevalence of articular disc defects in participants in their twenties and thirties were as frequent as older than 40 years, and the authors believed that aging was not a prime cause for TFC abnormalities. Moreover, several studies have studied the relationship between TFC thickness and ulnar variance, [ 5 , 9 , 10 ] finding that ulnar variance and TFC thickness were negatively correlated, namely, an increase in ulnar variance leads to a decrease in TFC thickness. Although many studies have investigated the individual relationship between age, ulnar variance and TFC, few have integrated the three variables along with gender to analyze the relationships. Since TFC is located in the space between the distal ulna and ulnar carpus, whether the changes in TFC thickness, angle, signal and morphology are related to aging, gender, or ulnar variance leads to the reduced space, or the three, remains questionable. Additionally, in the clinical setting, it is common to find a thinner TFC in younger patients undergoing MRI for wrist pain, and whether this is the cause of the symptom or an incidental “abnormal” finding often make clinicians confused. The purpose of this study was to investigate TFC findings in asymptomatic adults using high-resolution 3T MRI, and explore the relationships among age, gender and ulnar variance. Methods This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics review board and informed consent was obtained from all study participants. Participants The study was a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data. We recruited healthy adult volunteers who reported no ulnar-sided wrist pain. Any participant with wrist pain, history of wrist trauma, prior wrist surgery, tumor, arthritis, congenital anomalies and contraindication for MRI, such as claustrophobia and metal implants, were excluded from participation. Magnetic resonance imaging examinations MRI examinations was performed with a 3T MRI unit (Philips Achieva, Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) with a dedicated eight-channel wrist coil. All participants were placed in prone position with the examined arms raised up and above the head (superman’s position), and the wrist in the magnetic isocenter pinpointed by the laser mark. MRI sequences included proton density imaging with fat-suppression (PD FS) in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes (TR/TE 2500-2609ms/35ms), and T1-weighted fast spin-echo in coronal plane (TR/TE 326ms/15ms); slice thickness was 2 mm; interslice space was 0.2 mm; NEX was 2–4; field of view was 80–90 × 80–90 mm; and voxel was (0.25–0.31) mm × (0.35–0.45) mm × (2.0-2.8) mm. Image analysis All images were interpreted independently by two musculoskeletal radiologists who had 9 years and 13 years of experience, respectively. Any discrepancy was resolved through discussion with a senior radiologist with more than 15 years of experience in musculoskeletal imaging. The average values of the two reviewers for quantitative data and consensus for qualitative data were used as the final analysis. Qualitative analysis included the morphology of TFC on coronal PD FS images and the signal intensity and continuity of TFC. According to Van der Post’s research in healthy adolescents [ 4 ], the coronal morphology of TFC was classified as: I, slightly radially tilted asymmetrical bowtie; II, shorter and more horizontal; and III, more distally stretched (Fig. 1 ). Additionally, we also defined the signal intensity and continuity of TFC as three types: I, continuous and homogeneous hypo-intensity; II, slightly increased signal without extension to the surface and TFC without discontinuity; and III, higher signal intensity as fluid signal and with perforation, discontinuity or tear. The mid-coronal slice, determined by three orthogonal planes and on which TFC appeared as the thinnest, was selected for the quantitative analysis, including the measurement of TFC thickness, TFC angle and ulnar variance. The TFC thickness was measured at the thinnest part (Fig. 2 ), and in the cases of TFC perforation or full-thickness tear, the measurement of TFC thickness was abandoned and statistically analyzed with multiple imputation. The TFC angle and ulnar variance were measured in all participants and referred to the methods described by Yoshioka et al. [ 9 ], but unlike their methods, ulnar variance was measured between the cortical surface rather than cartilage surface (Fig. 2 ). Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.2.0, and statistical significance was defined as p &lt; 0.05. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Cohen Kappa coefficients were used to evaluate the consistency of continuous data and qualitative variables data between the reviewers, respectively. All correlation coefficients were interpreted as poor (≤ 0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), or excellent (0.81-1.00) agreement [ 4 ]. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess whether continuous variables followed a normal distribution. The normally distributed data were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), whereas the non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (M) and interquartile ranges (Q1, Q3). Qualitative variables were expressed as frequency counts. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to assess the independent influencing factors for TFC thickness and angle. Logistic regression analyses were carried out to identify independent influencing factors for TFC morphology, signal, and continuity. The mediation analysis was performed by using the “mediation” package in R 4.2.0. Results Participants characteristics We prospectively recruited 87 asymptomatic volunteers to undergo MRI examinations. Three were excluded from this study because of poor image quality. Eighty-four participants [men, 42; women, 42; mean age of men and women, 37 years (ranging from 22 to 72 years) and 46 years (ranging from 21 to 79 years), respectively] were included in the final analysis. There were 23 participants (27.38%, 10 men, 13 women, mean age 53 years, ranging from 21 to 79 years) had TFC defect, including 3 cases younger than 30 years old, 14 cases older than 50 years old. TFC thickness and angle The ICC values between the two reviewers for TFC thickness, TFC angle, and ulnar variance ranged from 0.90 to 0.99, indicating excellent agreement. The median TFC thickness of all participants was 0.97 mm, and the mean TFC angle and mean ulnar variance of all participants were 28.34° and 0.60 mm, respectively (Table 1 ). Participants were divided into 3 groups based on age and the corresponding measurement in different age groups and gender were shown in Table 1 . The multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated that ulnar variance was the only influencing factor significantly associated with TFC thickness ( p &lt; 0.001) and showed a negative correlation. For every millimeter of increase in ulnar variance, a decrease of 0.162 mm in TFC thickness was found. According to the multiple linear regression analysis, both gender and ulnar variance were statistically significant influencing factors of TFC angle ( p &lt; 0.05), and ulnar variance and TFC angle showed a positive correlation. TFC angle in women were higher than men (B = 3.238). For every millimeter of increase in ulnar variance, an increase of 5.122° in TFC angle was found (Table 2 ). TFC morphology and signal intensity The interobserver agreement for defining the coronal morphology and the signal intensity and continuity of TFC were excellent (k = 0.91, 0.99). The number of TFC coronal morphology and TFC signal and continuity distributed by age groups and gender were shown in Table 3 . We re-classified TFC coronal morphology as two groups for logistic regression analysis: categorized type I and II as slightly tilted or horizontal TFC, and type III as more distally stretched TFC. Multivariate logistic regression results demonstrated that age, gender, and ulnar variance were independent factors influencing TFC morphology in coronal plane (all p &lt; 0.05, Table 4 ). Compared to men, the prevalence of a more distally stretched TFC increased by 4.50 times (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.19–17.10; Table 4 ) for women. An increase in age was significantly associated with an increased prevalence of a more distally stretched TFC (odds ratio [OR], 1.07; 95%CI, 1.02–1.12; Table 4 ). For every millimeter of increase in ulnar variance, the prevalence of a more distally stretched TFC increased by 4.31 times (95%CI, 2.21–8.41; Table 4 ). Although ulnar variance was the influencing factor of TFC coronal morphology ( p &lt; 0.001), there was a mediating effect of age on the association between ulnar variance and TFC morphology. The mediation analysis revealed that the mediating effect of age accounted for 12.43% of the total effect, indicating that the indirect effect accounted for 12.43% on the association between ulnar variance and TFC morphology (ulnar variance → age → TFC morphology, p = 0.016). The direct effect of ulnar variance accounted for 87.57% ( p &lt; 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression results demonstrated that age was the independent factor influencing TFC signal and integrity. An increase in age was significantly associated with an increased prevalence of abnormal TFC signal and discontinuity (OR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.06–1.19 for type II; OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.07–1.20 for type III; Table 4 ). Discussion TFCC abnormal findings are present on MRI in both asymptomatic and symptomatic individuals [ 8 , 11 ]. Just like this study, there were 23 participants (27.38%) that had TFC perforation, defect or tear, which was similar to Iordache et al. [ 5 ] but slightly different, who reported a TFCC tear (including articular disc and attachment) in 25% (26 of 103) of asymptomatic wrists. Since TFC abnormalities are found in asymptomatic wrists, it is imperative to understand the appearance and morphology of the TFC in asymptomatic wrists to avoid misdiagnosis or misattribution. The median TFC thickness of all participants in this study was 0.97 mm, which was consistent with previously reported data in volunteers and cadaveric wrists [ 9 , 12 ]. Previous studies have primarily focused on the relationship between ulnar variance and TFC thickness [ 5 , 9 , 10 ], age and ulnar variance [ 9 , 13 – 15 ] with few studies focusing on TFC thickness and age in adults. In this study, we integrated all of these variables to explore the relationships and we measured ulnar variance between the cortical surface instead of cartilage surface because the MRI bone measurements were the closest to the true value [ 16 ]. We found that ulnar variance was the only influencing factor significantly associated with TFC thickness, while age was not. Age did not influence TFC thickness, which was consistent with a study on elderly cadaveric wrists [ 17 ]. Thus, older adults could have a thick TFC, and a thin TFC could be seen in younger adults, which may not be the true cause of symptom in symptomatic wrists. However, the sample size of participants was limited, and it is necessary for future studies to increase the sample size to determine the normal range of TFC thickness in the population. There was a negative correlation between ulnar variance and TFC thickness, and a positive correlation between ulnar variance and TFC angle, which could be attributed to an increased ulnar variance causing a reduced available space between ulnar head and ulnar carpus [ 18 ]. Additionally, TFC angle in women was significantly higher compared to men in our study. We speculate that this phenomenon may be related to hormones and different activity levels. However, the authors could find no research to study the relationship between gender and TFC angle. TFC coronal morphology could be influenced by gender and age. Compared to men, the prevalence of a more distally stretched TFC increased in women, which was corresponding to the results of TFC angle. In this study, a more distally stretched TFC could be seen in 4 participants younger than 30 years old, and increased to 19 older than 50 years old (Table 3 ). Therefore, older participants were more likely to have a more distally stretched TFC than younger participants. Additionally, this study found ulnar variance was an influencing factor of TFC morphology, which is consistent with prior investigations [ 18 ]. However, this study also found age partially mediated the relationship between ulnar variance and TFC coronal morphology, indicating ulnar variance has a direct and indirect effect on TFC but the direct effect predominated. Thus, TFC morphology was affected by age and ulnar variance, but it appeared to be mainly related to the available space between ulnar head and ulnar carpus that changed with ulnar variance. In this study, 4 participants had TFC signal and continuity changes in younger than 30 years old and increased to 26 in participants older than 50 years old (Table 3 ), confirming that age was significantly associated with the prevalence of abnormal TFC signal and discontinuity, findings consistent with prior investigations [ 5 , 7 , 8 ]. However, different from Iordache et al. [ 5 ], whose study could not demonstrate an association between age and TFC full-thickness tears, our study showed per year increase in age, the prevalence of TFC defect, perforation, discontinuity or tears increased by 1.13 times. Additionally, there was 1 participant younger than 30 years old who had slightly increased signal without surface extension and increased to 12 in participants older than 50 years old, which was more likely to be explained as mucoid degeneration or degenerative changes of TFC. This study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample size was small, a larger sample size would be needed to confirm our findings and determine the normal range of TFC thickness in the population. Secondly, the position in which patients were scanned (superman’s position) is slightly pronated [ 19 ], and pronation may lead to an increase in ulnar variance [ 20 ]. However, participants in this study were all scanned in the superman’s position, which would attenuate the influence of position. Furthermore, the TFC proper and the radial portions of the distal radioulnar ligaments form a rigid complex with the radius and their shape does not change during pronation and supination [ 20 ]. Lastly, we only focused on the articular disc, namely the TFC, and participants who reported no ulnar-sided wrist pain were included and did not undergo a detailed physical examination. Conclusions In asymptomatic adults’ wrist, age was not an independent influencing factor of TFC thickness, and younger people may have a thin TFC. Gender may affect TFC angle and morphology. An increase in ulnar variance was significantly correlated with a decrease of TFC thickness, an increase of TFC angle, and a more distally stretched TFC would be expected. TFC signal changes and degeneration, perforation, discontinuity or tear could be present in asymptomatic wrist, which would increase with age. Therefore, the “abnormalities” of TFC could be demonstrated on MRI, which were not necessarily the true cause of pain for symptomatic people, and in the clinical setting, we should combine imaging findings with the patients’ age, history, clinical performance and physical examination closely to make a comprehensive diagnosis.
