A debate continues regarding the chronic health impacts of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Central to this debate is how to interpret randomized trials showing lack of cardiovascular benefit from continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). Several narratives have emerged to comprehend these results, which might be grouped into three schools of thought. First is the possibility that CPAP benefits only certain patients who might be identified by symptoms or polysomnographic features other than the apnea–hypopnea index ( 1 ). Second is the possibility that OSA is an innocent bystander to harms of underlying visceral obesity ( 2 ), which is not improved by CPAP. Third is the possibility that years of living with OSA causes irreversible harm, such that CPAP is too little, too late. The translational study by Badran and colleagues (pp. 1001–1012 ) in this issue of the Journal appears to be designed with the third viewpoint in mind ( 3 ). Authors used an established animal model of OSA, exposing mice to chronic intermittent hypoxia (IH) during the light phase (when mice tend to sleep) to simulate the O 2 profile of patients with severe OSA ( 4 ). The IH exposure was performed for 16 weeks (21% O 2 alternating with 6.5% O 2 , 12 h/d). Mice were then exposed to “partial normoxic conditions,” 4 hours of IH per day for 6 weeks, intended to simulate residual untreated OSA if a patient were using CPAP for 4 hours a night. Some “recovering” mice were injected thrice weekly with navitoclax (NAV), a senolytic agent (more on this term later). This experimental group was called IH4-NAV. Other recovering mice were injected with inactive vehicle, a group called IH4-VEH. Authors studied a broad panel of outcomes, including cognitive function (as measured by a novel object recognition test), coronary artery function, insulin tolerance, lipid profile, intestinal permeability, blood lymphocyte methylation (to gauge epigenetic “age acceleration”), adipose and liver macrophage infiltration, IL-6 and p16 concentrations in adipocytes (surrogate markers of inflammation and senescence, respectively), as well as other biomarkers associated with cellular senescence. After 16 weeks of IH exposure, virtually every outcome assessed was abnormal. Mice exhibited impaired object recognition, elevated blood pressure, impaired coronary artery endothelium-dependent relaxation, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, intestinal permeability, and higher epigenetic age. In tissues examined, senescence markers were also increased. Most outcomes remained abnormal after IH4-VEH recovery (other than blood pressure, which decreased) but improved or normalized after NAV injections. This study is striking for several reasons. First is the extent of systemic dysfunction induced by 16 weeks of IH, which led to neurologic, vascular, metabolic, and epigenetic effects that were not reversed by partial recovery. Second is the extent of harm that persisted despite partial recovery for 6 weeks. This finding indicates that even shorter periods of IH exposure can induce injury and calls into question the view that 4 h/night of CPAP use is sufficient. Third is the impressive effect of NAV on reversing nearly all of these outcomes. If effects of IH are viewed from the lens of accelerated aging, then NAV seems to be the proverbial Fountain of Youth. On the other hand, there are several caveats limiting the clinical relevance and mechanistic insights of this study. After 16 weeks of IH (which has limitations as a model of OSA [ 4 , 5 ]), reducing the duration of IH exposure to 4 h/d was meant to simulate partial treatment. This is an interesting translational model, but it sacrifices insights into understanding the effects of IH, effects of NAV, and their interaction, deserving of a classical 2 × 2 experimental design. One is left to wonder about the effects of NAV per se on normoxic mice (i.e., is there a floor effect in which NAV has no impact?). Conversely, what are the effects of NAV during ongoing IH exposure 12 h/d (i.e., is there a ceiling effect in which NAV would have been ineffective?). As it stands, the experiment only allows us to conclude that NAV works in the “goldilocks” state of partial recovery from IH. Second, it is not likely that a single common pathway (senescence) is responsible for all aspects of IH. Studies in our laboratory found that the mechanism of IH-induced hyperglycemia originates from carotid body–stimulated adrenocortical activation ( 6 , 7 ), whereas IH-induced hyperlipidemia results from reduced lipid clearance and hypoxic suppression of thermogenesis ( 8 , 9 ). Navitoclax is senolytic because it targets senescent (aged or damaged) cells that may contribute to age-related diseases ( 10 ). NAV was discovered from the Abbott Oncology Bcl-2/Bcl-xL inhibitors program, which sought to activate the proapoptotic arm of the Bcl-2 pathway to target cancer cells ( 11 ). Whether this proapoptotic effect is what mediated benefits in this study is not yet clear. To be sure, authors found changes in several biomarkers suggesting senescence including increased β-galactosidase staining in liver, increased expression of p16 (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A, which controls the cell cycle) in aorta and fat tissue, and lower lamin B1 (a nuclear structural protein that decreases with age) in liver. However, these markers each have their limitations and do not clearly connect mechanistically to the phenotypes (cognitive, vascular, hemodynamic, metabolic, epigenetic) measured. The field of senotherapy seeks to target and eliminate senescent cells or their downstream effects to improve human health. Authors of this study have taken an early step in showing conceptual validity toward this approach in a mouse model of OSA, but more evidence is needed to show mechanisms, clinical utility, and safety.
