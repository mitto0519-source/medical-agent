ABSTRACT Background To determine if the COVID‐19 pandemic influenced students' cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), this study examined the prevalence of meeting a health‐related standard for CRF in public school students before, during and after the pandemic. Methods A repeated cross‐sectional study design was used, and multilevel logistic regression was employed to compare the prevalence of students meeting the CRF standard during the four school years preceding the pandemic (2016–2017 to 2019–2020) with the 2 years during the pandemic (2020–2021 to 2021–2022) and with 2 years after the pandemic (2022–2023 to 2023–2024). Students' CRF data were provided by 21 school districts that, between 2016–2017 and 2023–2024, consistently participated in a statewide survey of health‐related fitness. Results Before the pandemic 52.4% of students met the standard for CRF. This prevalence was reduced to 44.3% during the pandemic (AOR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.67, 0.70) and remained reduced at 47.1% after the pandemic (AOR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.76, 0.79). Implications for School Health During periods of societal disruption, such as a pandemic, school personnel should adopt practices aimed at supporting students' CRF. Conclusions The COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with reduced CRF in public school students, and this trend persisted for at least two school years following the pandemic. Introduction Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is one of the strongest indicators of health, as it is strongly associated with risk of multiple chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, Type 2 diabetes, and several cancers [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ]. In children and adolescents, better CRF has been associated with more favorable cardiometabolic risk profiles, reduced risk of overweight, better bone health, and higher levels of academic achievement [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ]. Recent studies have also documented that CRF is associated with mental health [ 9 ], findings which further document the importance of youth fitness. Despite these documented health benefits and longstanding efforts to promote fitness in youth through school and community‐based programs [ 10 ], surveys show that significant percentages of school‐aged children and adolescents fail to meet accepted standards for CRF [ 11 ]. The COVID‐19 pandemic produced a major disruption in the lives of children and youth, with major disruptions evident in school education practices and community programming. Numerous studies have examined the impacts of the COVID‐19 pandemic on health and well‐being of youth, but studies of fitness are quite limited. Several studies in Europe concluded that the COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with reduced CRF in youth [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ], but larger scale studies in US samples are needed to examine the impact in American students. Studies to date also have not examined change in health‐related fitness after schools and communities resumed normal activities. Evaluations of longitudinal patterns across the full time course (i.e., before, during, and after school closures and restrictions) are needed to fully elucidate the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on the health and well‐being of youth. The predominant fitness test battery used in US schools is FitnessGram, which provides a multicomponent assessment of health‐related physical fitness [ 16 ]. A strength of the FitnessGram battery is that it provides criterion‐referenced standards, the Healthy Fitness Zone (HFZ), based on research which documents associations between fitness level and health parameters in youth [ 17 ]. The HFZ for CRF was established based on the documented association between CRF and risk of metabolic syndrome in youth [ 18 ]. Thus, when examined at the population level, the percentage of youth achieving the HFZ has important implications for public health. While FitnessGram is used nationally, South Carolina is one of the few states that uses it annually to assess health‐related physical fitness in students on a statewide basis [ 19 ]. Schools across South Carolina have conducted this assessment since 2016, documenting students' CRF levels across eight consecutive school years that can be characterized into three distinct periods: (1) school years preceding the pandemic (2016–2020), (2) school years affected by the pandemic (2020–2022), and (3) school years characterized by the return to typical practices (2022–2024). Accordingly, these annual surveys provide an opportunity to determine if students' CRF changed in association with pandemic‐related disruptions in school and community physical activity programs for youth and how they have recovered follow resuming activities as usual. Hence, the purpose of this study was to compare CRF levels during three distinct time periods of the COVID‐19 pandemic (operationalized as pre‐pandemic, pandemic, and post‐pandemic) among South Carolina students (fifth grade, eight grade and high school), overall and across sociodemographic characteristics, including sex, grade level, poverty status, and race and ethnicity. 2. Methods 2.1. Study Design This study employed a repeated cross‐sectional design in making comparisons for the prevalence of students meeting a health‐related standard for CRF across school years preceding the COVID‐19 pandemic (2016–2017 to 2019–2020), during the pandemic (2020–2021 to 2021–2022), and following the pandemic (2022–2023 to 2023–2024). CRF was assessed as the percentage of students meeting the HFZ standard for CRF as measured in the FitnessGram test protocol. A repeated cross‐sectional design was used because such a design is common in public health research in which comparisons are made across cycles of a surveillance system. In South Carolina, the FitnessGram test has been administered on a stateside basis annually since the 2016–2017 school year, and accordingly, it constitutes a surveillance system for health‐related fitness in the state's public school students. Test administration and data management procedures have been standardized through a collaboration between the SC Department of Education and the SC Department of Public Health. For the 2019–2020 school year, only data collected before March 11, 2020, were included, as SC school closures due to the COVID‐19 pandemic began on that date. Consistent with classifications used in CDC‐related research on COVID‐19 [ 20 ], we operationalized the 2020–2021 and 2021–2022 school years as the years affected by the pandemic. While the pandemic began in February–March 2020, the influence of school and community policies was more formalized during the two subsequent school years. Comparisons were made for the total samples available in each school year. Furthermore, the influences of sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status were examined in the total samples and separately for boys and girls. 2.2. Participants The FitnessGram was administered by physical education teachers during physical education classes in schools in participating school districts. Test results were entered into a data management system that routed data to the SC Department of Education, which added student demographic information and created a statewide FitnessGram database for each school year. In most school years, FitnessGram data were available for students from 54 of 79 South Carolina school districts, but participation was reduced due to the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020–2021 and 2021–2022. Districts were included in the present study if they consistently collected and submitted FitnessGram data for each of the eight school years. The final sample included students in 21 districts from across the state, with representation from 15 of 46 counties. Yearly district‐level sample sizes varied widely, proportionate to district enrollment. Across the 8 years, mean district‐level sample size was 1540.6 ± 1791.3. Data were included for students in fifth grade, fifth grade and high school, the grades included in the annual FitnessGram testing in South Carolina. This study was reviewed by the University of South Carolina's Institutional Review Board and determined to be nonhuman subjects research. 2.3. Instrumentation and Procedure 2.3.1. Student Characteristics Student demographic information on sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status was provided by the SC Department of Education. Sex was expressed as male or female. Grade levels included fifth grade, eighth grade, and high school (9th–12th grades). Race and ethnicity groups included non‐Hispanic white, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic or Latino, and other racial groups (including two or more races). Student poverty status (yes/no) was based on student enrollment in Medicaid, Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) or Foster Care Services, and/or student homelessness/migrant status. 2.3.2. CRF Physical education teachers administered tests of CRF to students during physical education classes. Teachers selected one of the three available test items for CRF—the Progressive Aerobic Cardiovascular Endurance Run (PACER) test, a 1‐mile run test, or a 1‐mile walk test [ 16 ]. The PACER test, which most teachers (88%) selected, is a multistage, progressive test that involves running across a 15‐ or 20‐m space at an increasing pace and is measured as the number of laps completed before the student falls behind the prescribed pace [ 16 ]. While the PACER test is the preferred test, schools can use any of the three tests to obtain estimates of CRF for use in school and district applications. Previous research has supported the agreement of estimates from these indicators for school‐based fitness evaluations [ 21 ]. For the present analysis, the age‐ and sex‐specific criterion‐referenced standards from FitnessGram were used to identify students who achieved the HFZ for CRF. The percentage of youth achieving the CRF HFZ served as the primary outcome measure. 2.4. Data Analysis Student characteristics were calculated as percent of the sample by sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status for each year of the study and for each of three groups of school years. Also, the percent of students in the CRF HFZ was calculated for each study year overall and for the three groups of school years by sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status. School years were grouped into three periods: (1) pre‐pandemic : school years preceding the pandemic, including 2016–2017, 2017–2018, 2018–2019, and 2019–2020 (before March 11, 2020); (2) pandemic : school years characterized by widespread disruption of school schedules and practices due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, including 2020–2021 and 2021–2022; and (3) post‐pandemic : school years characterized by returns to more typical education practices, including 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 [ 20 ]. Multilevel logistic regression models (Proc Glimmix, SAS Version 9.4) were used to compare CRF HFZ between students who provided data during the pre‐pandemic period with those measured during the pandemic and post‐pandemic periods. The initial analysis adjusted for sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status for the total group. Individual models were then calculated for groups formed on the basis of sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status, adjusting for these student characteristics as appropriate. In all models, children were nested within schools and schools were nested within districts, and the pre‐pandemic period served as the reference group. Odds ratios and 95% confidence limits were reported. 3. Results Descriptive statistics for students included in the analytic dataset are summarized in Table 1 . Summaries are provided for each school year sample and for each of the three groups of school years. The sample sizes varied across the school years due to differences in the number of participating students in the 21 school districts that were included in the analysis. The number of students in the analytic sample was relatively consistent across the four pre‐pandemic years and the last 3 years observed in this study. However, the sample was substantially decreased during 2020–2021 due to the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic. The demographic characteristics of the samples were consistent across the school years for sex, grade level, and poverty status (Table 2 ). During the 2020–2021 school year the percentage of white students was greater, and the percentage of Black students was lower. The study compared CRF HFZ levels across three distinct time periods: pre‐pandemic, pandemic, and post‐pandemic. Table 3 presents the percentages of students meeting the CRF HFZ from 2016–2017 to 2023–2024 overall and by sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status. Percentages of students meeting the CRF HFZ during the pandemic (44.3%) were lower compared to pre‐pandemic (52.4%, p &lt; 0.001), and some rebound during the post‐pandemic school years (47.1%) compared to during pandemic ( p &lt; 0.001). In general, this pattern was consistent when examined in subgroups based on sex and race/ethnicity (see Figure 1 ). However, for poverty status, higher socioeconomic students (poverty = no) appear to be the only subgroup to nearly recover to pre‐pandemic CRF levels. Table 4 provides a summary of logistic regression analyses that were applied to compare the odds of students meeting the CRF HFZ across three distinct time periods (pre‐pandemic, pandemic, post‐pandemic). For the total samples and for each of the demographic subgroups the odds of attaining the CRF HFZ were significantly reduced ( p &lt; 0.05) during the pandemic and post‐pandemic periods (compared to pre‐pandemic). Overall, students were less likely to achieve the CRF HFZ during school years affected by the pandemic compared to pre‐pandemic school years, after adjusting for sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status (AOR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.67, 0.70). During the post‐pandemic school years, some recovery of CRF was observed but students remained less likely to achieve the CRF HFZ. Overall, students were less likely to achieve the CRF HFZ compared to the pre‐pandemic period, after adjusting for student characteristics (AOR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.76, 0.79). Similar patterns were observed across demographic subgroups including sex, grade level, race and ethnicity, and poverty status. Compared to their respective counterparts during the pre‐pandemic period, students in the following subgroups were less likely to achieve the CRF HFZ during the school years affected by the pandemic: Hispanic (45% less likely), middle school (40%), high school (35%), non‐Hispanic Black (37%), other racial groups (38%), and low socioeconomic students (poverty = yes; 35%). During the post‐pandemic timeframe similar patterns were observed, with Hispanic (38%), middle school (34%), high school (30%), non‐Hispanic Black (28%), other racial groups (28%), and low socioeconomic students (poverty = yes; 25%) being less likely to achieve the CRF HRZ compared to their counterparts the during pre‐pandemic period. 4. Discussion This study was designed to evaluate longitudinal changes in CRF across 8 years to examine the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on youth. The timespan captured three distinct time periods, which enabled evaluation of changes due to COVID‐19 pandemic closures as well as the recovery from these disruptions. The results revealed significant declines in youth CRF. Compared to pre‐pandemic, students were 28% and 18% less likely to achieve a health‐related standard for CRF during the pandemic and post‐pandemic school years, respectively. This was observed in large and diverse samples of students drawn from 21 school districts. The declines during the pandemic were substantial and affected all sociodemographic groups, though the study found some notable differences across demographic groups. Declines were generally larger in adolescents (eighth grade and high school) than in children (fifth grade). Larger declines in fitness were observed among Hispanic and non‐Hispanic Black students, and students from lower socioeconomic households. These findings are consistent with emerging literature documenting the differential and detrimental impact of the pandemic on lower income families across numerous health‐related outcomes [ 22 , 23 ]. The novel aspect of the study is that the longitudinal design enabled evaluation of the changes in fitness following resumption of school and community programming during the post‐pandemic school years. When examining the proportion of students achieving the standard for CRF HFZ from the pandemic to post‐pandemic periods, we observed greater rebounds in CRF among higher socioeconomic students, non‐Hispanic white students, and elementary school students. Among these groups, the largest gains in CRF were observed among students with higher socioeconomic standing, who experienced a near full rebound in CRF during the post‐pandemic period. Slower improvements in CRF levels were observed from the pandemic to post‐pandemic period among the remaining sociodemographic groups. Of note, Hispanic students experienced the largest declines in fitness during the pandemic school years as well as the smallest gains in CRF from the pandemic to post‐pandemic period. During the post‐pandemic period, Hispanic students and older students were the least likely to achieve the health‐related standards for CRF—suggesting these groups were most impacted by the COVID‐19 pandemic. The declines in CRF reported in this study are congruent with findings from an examination of National Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) data, which revealed significant declines in the prevalence of youth attending physical education classes daily, playing on sports teams, and performing strength exercises during the pandemic [ 24 ]. Studies in other countries have also reported similar findings using different samples and assessments. For example, a study of French adolescents observed declines of 10%–16% in a composite fitness indicator [ 13 ]. Significant declines in raw PACER test performance also occurred between 2019 and 2021 in large samples of Korean [ 25 ], Chinese [ 26 ], and Japanese [ 15 ] (declines observed in 20‐m shuttle run [standardized (Cohen's) effect size (ES) = 0.109 per annum]) students and in smaller samples of youth from England (mean total shuttle runs from 23.4 to 20.6) [ 27 ] and Spain (prevalence of HFZ before and after COVID‐19 confinement was, respectively, 79.8% and 76.4%) [ 12 ]. The authors are not aware of any studies that have examined recovery or rebounds in CRF level of students following significant declines observed during the pandemic. Hence, our findings provide crucial insights into the potential long‐term impacts on health following the COVID‐19 pandemic. 4.1. Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and Equity The results of the present study have important public health implications, because the FitnessGram criterion‐referenced standards used in the analyses are linked to established health‐related outcomes [ 16 ]. The standards have been widely used in other surveillance applications [ 28 , 29 , 30 ] and have been well supported in other reviews of youth fitness standards [ 31 , 32 ]. It is noteworthy that the CRF HFZ standards were specifically established to identify age‐ and sex‐specific values that reflect risks for metabolic syndrome [ 17 ]. Thus, the observed population shifts in youth CRF levels can be viewed as indicating population shifts in cardiometabolic health in youth. The population shifts in the percentage of youth achieving the CRF standards (i.e., decreases during the pandemic and general increases in the subsequent years) demonstrate the sensitivity of these indicators to change. Physical activity has been shown to be linked to self‐reported health outcomes in youth [ 33 ], and the present study suggests that presumed shifts in physical activity patterns (attributable to COVID‐19 restrictions) may also manifest in changes in CRF over time. The results of the study also underscore the value and importance of this type of surveillance initiative for monitoring health status in student populations. The need for enhanced physical activity surveillance methods has been documented recently in a prominent report by the National Academies of Science Engineering and Medicine [ 34 ]. The report focused on youth and specifically emphasized the value of using FitnessGram data to monitor these types of changes [ 35 ]. The present study provides a clear example and a compelling rationale to support this type of broad, state and national monitoring of physical fitness in youth. The results have independent value for school and public health leaders but have added value when linked with other systems. For example, the integration of data with other state and national policy datasets such as the C.L.A.S.S. (Classification of Laws Associated with School Students) database developed by the National Cancer Institute would enable systematic evaluations of links with school‐based policies that may influence youth. Previous work has hinted at the policy implications associated with the availability of physical education [ 36 ] and recess [ 37 ], but the systematic collection of outcome data on physical activity and physical fitness are needed to establish a more robust surveillance system. Thus, continued tracking of FitnessGram data and links with C.L.A.S.S. would enable more effective evaluations of the factors that influence physical activity and health in youth. The effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic are still being actively investigated, and the present study provides novel insights about the impact of the pandemic on youth. Untangling the contributions from school‐, community‐, and family‐related activity on the observed changes in CRF is not possible. However, the results do suggest the importance of providing opportunities for youth to be physically active. Health researchers and practitioners have emphasized the public health importance of school physical activity programming for decades [ 38 ], and policy‐related work has subsequently demonstrated the impacts of policies on youth behaviors and outcomes [ 39 , 40 ]. To date, few studies have observed associations between population‐level declines in CRF when school and community programming is disrupted. Of greater concern is the differential rates of rebound after the return of school and community programming. It is well‐documented that physical activity behavior is influenced by a large and diverse set of factors operating at levels ranging from personal to societal. While school‐based factors are particularly salient in youth, it is recognized that the COVID‐19 pandemic may have reduced physical activity through multiple environmental and social channels. Additional research is needed to understand the factors that influence these observations. 4.2. Strengths and Limitations A strength of the analyses conducted in this study is that they included data only from school districts that consistently provided data across all 8 years, including the periods before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic. In addition, this is one of the first US studies to examine population‐level shifts in CRF among a large, diverse cohort of students. Some limitations should be noted. First, the design does not allow us to infer the causal relationship between CRF and school disruptions due to the COVID‐19 pandemic. Second, while teachers received training in how to administer the FitnessGram protocol, it is likely that variability occurred across teachers in the manner in which these assessments were administered and scored. In particular, it is a strength that data were available for four school years before the onset of the pandemic. These data provide a reliable baseline against which the pandemic and post‐pandemic periods were compared. The COVID‐19 pandemic may have further altered how fitness assessments were conducted due to public health practices and protocols. Finally, our sample included only schools that consistently conducted in‐school fitness assessments across the observation period. It is possible that changes in fitness were different among schools that maintained remote learning for an extended period of time during the pandemic. Given the extensive research documenting the importance of schools in promoting physical activity, it is plausible that our study underestimated the declines in fitness, as we did not assess students who were learning remotely during the pandemic. 5. Conclusions Students were less likely to achieve a health‐related standard for CRF during the four school years following the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, compared to the years before the pandemic. This is almost certainly due to reductions in students' levels of physical activity, which were likely negatively influenced by reduced access to school‐based opportunities for physical activity. In addition, reduced access to community‐based physical activity programs, and parent‐prescribed limitations on physical activity in the home and neighborhood, may have exacerbated the situation. Despite resuming typical school and community practices related to physical activity, CRF levels remained significantly lower following the onset of the pandemic. Because CRF is a key component of health during childhood and adolescence, the findings of this study suggest that the health status of US school‐aged youth was profoundly and negatively affected by reduced physical activity during the COVID‐19 pandemic. This observation indicates that preparations for future large‐scale societal disruptions should include actions aimed at maintaining the physical activity levels of children and youth.
