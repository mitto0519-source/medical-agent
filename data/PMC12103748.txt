Abstract Background Excessive sedentary behavior is highly prevalent in developed nations and is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) morbidity and mortality. As such, health agencies have provided general recommendations to “sit less, move more” by interspersing brief periods of activity. However, a lack of empirical evidence describing how often (e.g. every 30 min, every 60 min) and for how long (e.g., 1 min activity bouts, 5 min activity bouts) sedentary time should be interrupted (a “sedentary break”) to yield health benefit has precluded more quantitative, actionable guidelines. To date, rigorous and methodical dose escalation experiments have not been conducted to elucidate effective and tolerated sedentary break doses. The objective of the proposed study is to determine the minimally effective dose (e.g., the lowest dose) for two elements of a sedentary break (frequency and duration) that yields improvements in established CVD risk factors. The maximally tolerated dose (e.g. the highest dose that does not cause undue physical/psychological distress) for both frequency and duration of sedentary breaks will also be determined. Methods This study is a randomized crossover trial conducted under laboratory conditions among 324 adults without chronic medical conditions. Participants complete two trial conditions (8 h each), a sedentary break (intervention) condition and an uninterrupted sitting (control) condition, in a randomized order. The sedentary break condition consists of 1 of 25 possible frequency/duration combinations (e.g. walk every 30 min for 5 min), selected according to a Bayesian adaptive randomization method. Primary outcomes used to inform the adaptive randomization are glucose and blood pressure serially assessed over each trial condition. Constructs of dose toxicity (tolerability, safety, physical exhaustion/fatigue, and affect) are also serially assessed. Discussion This study will elucidate the minimally effective and maximally tolerated frequency and duration of a sedentary break that yields improvements in established CVD risk factors, information important for the development of quantitative sedentary behavior guidelines. The registration ID is NCT05353322 . Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-22250-0. Introduction Technological advancements in the past 50 years have led to an increasingly sedentary lifestyle in developed nations [ 1 – 4 ]. Changes in transportation, communication, the workplace, and domestic entertainment have fostered environments in occupational, home, and social settings that now demand or encourage prolonged sedentary behavior [ 4 ]. US adults now spend 11–12 h/day sedentary (~ 77% of their waking day) and accrue ~ 48% of their sedentary time in prolonged sedentary bouts &gt; 30 min [ 5 ]. Evidence has accumulated to indicate that sedentary behavior is strongly associated with incidence of CVD risk factors, incidence of CVD, and CVD and all-cause mortality [ 6 – 9 ]. Importantly, the deleterious effects of sedentary behavior appear to be independent of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) [ 6 ]. Thus, sedentary behavior is believed to represent a unique aspect of the physical activity profile that carries clinical relevance outside of one’s exercise habits [ 10 ]. On the strength of the evidence that sedentary behavior is an important determinant of CVD and longevity, most health agencies have expanded their physical activity recommendations beyond promoting MVPA and now also advocate for reductions in sedentary time [ 11 – 19 ] Recommendations to “sit less, move more” are indicated for all age groups [ 7 ]. However, these guidelines stop short of making specific recommendations about how to reduce sedentary time with respect to dose. The lack of specific recommendations is attributed to a dearth of empiric data to inform more quantitative guidelines [ 7 ]. Accordingly, there is a critical need for studies that compare different doses of reduced sedentary time on health outcomes to inform further development of evidence-based guidelines [ 7 , 20 ]. Previous studies have shown that sedentary time is associated with health risks particularly when accumulated in prolonged, uninterrupted bouts (e.g. sitting for hours at a time) [ 21 , 22 ]; suggestive that regularly breaking up sedentary time with short bouts of activity may be essential to mitigating the harms of sedentary behavior. Mechanistic evidence suggests that breaking up sedentary behavior with intermittent activity benefits multiple systems relevant to cardiometabolic health including glucose regulation, lipid metabolism, BP, and vascular function [ 23 , 24 ]. However, several important questions remain including (1) How frequent should periods of prolonged sedentary time be interrupted? (2) What is the appropriate duration of these breaks in sedentary time? Answering these research questions has important clinical and health policy implications. For example, a future guideline might recommend: “Individuals who habitually sit for &gt; N hours should move every N minutes for N minutes to offset the CVD risk incurred from prolonged sedentary time.” However, exact recommendations cannot be made based on the existing evidence. Phase I and II trials are the foundation of a successful intervention development process wherein the goal is to establish the “right” dose (or dose combination for multiple agents/elements) for further clinical evaluation of efficacy and eventually effectiveness. Too high a dose can result in poor adherence, displeasure, or injury; too low a dose decreases efficacy. Thus, a phase I-II trial design entails dose escalation (and de-escalation) to identify minimally effective and maximally tolerated doses, with the fate of an agent in subsequent phases (III and IV) largely influenced by a careful trial design to identify these doses. Although a systematic framework for early phase development of behavioral interventions exists [ 25 , 26 ], rigorous and methodical dose escalation experiments have largely not been conducted to elucidate efficacious and tolerated sedentary break doses. Without well-informed data on dosing, full-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) targeting sedentary behavior may be fruitless because the tested doses have potential of being inefficacious or not well tolerated. Well-controlled laboratory studies constitute an ideal setting to elucidate optimal sedentary break doses as they ensure strict adherence to dosing protocols and allow for precise control of extraneous influencing factors. Many experimental studies have been conducted to test various elements of a sedentary break. In a systematic review on the effects of sedentary breaks on cardiometabolic health indicators under laboratory conditions, 44 studies were identified [ 23 ]. Limitations of these prior studies are summarized in Table 1 . Small sample sizes, poor generalizability, methodological issues, and limited dosing information (58% of studies tested a single dose; none tested &gt; 3 doses) preclude determination of efficacious sedentary break doses. Notably, only 9 frequency/duration combinations have been tested across all identified studies; 6 of which utilized a frequency of breaks ≤ 30 min (a likely intolerable dose). As such, it was concluded that research is needed to identify the timing and duration of activity breaks that are likely to impart the most benefit. To date, few studies have applied a dose finding methodology. Without use of dose finding methodology, researchers in previous studies largely relied on a best-guess approach to select doses rather than using empirically derived evidence. It is unclear whether higher or lower doses than those selected would yield similar, better, or worse effects. Thus, the minimally effective and maximally tolerated doses of a sedentary break have yet to be established. Furthermore, existing studies have focused on a single element (e.g. manipulated frequency alone); while fixing the remaining elements (e.g. duration, type, intensity) [ 23 ]. Such an approach is flawed as there may be more than one possible dose pair (frequency-duration) that is efficacious when considering combinations of sedentary break elements. For example, a study testing frequency at 2 doses (30 min and 90 min) while fixing the duration (e.g., 3 min) could find that a 30 min frequency, but not a 90 min frequency, yields cardiometabolic benefit and thus conclude that a 90 min frequency is not efficacious. However, had the duration of activity element also been varied; it’s possible that a 90 min frequency is efficacious at certain durations (e.g. 7–10 min). No prior study has taken the multiple element combination issue into account. Thus, while prior studies have been helpful to establish that sedentary breaks confer cardiometabolic benefit, they have limited value for informing optimal dosing for full-scale RCTs. Studies that test multiple doses and take into account the multiple elements of a sedentary break (i.e., frequency and duration) are needed to inform optimal sedentary break dosing. Furthermore, establishing a range of effective doses will be important for developing viable public health guidance that can accommodate most individuals and be readily adopted. We address these quantitative dosing questions in the proposed study, BREAK2. We utilize a novel Bayesian adaptive randomization method that takes into account the complexities of dual sedentary break elements to systemically derive empirical frequency/duration combinations for testing in subsequent trials. The overall objective of BREAK2 is to determine the minimally effective dose (i.e. the lowest dose) that yields cardiometabolic benefit for two sedentary break elements (frequency and duration). We will also determine the maximally tolerated dose (i.e. the highest dose that does not cause undue physical/psychological distress) for both frequency and duration of sedentary breaks. Elucidating the minimally effective and maximally tolerated frequency and duration of a sedentary break that yields improvements in established CVD risk factors will represent an important step in developing quantitative sedentary behavior guidelines. Study design Study overview BREAK2 is an adaptive dose-finding study designed to determine the minimally effective dose of sedentary breaks that yields cardiometabolic benefit in adult participants in a laboratory-controlled environment. The study entails a randomized cross-over design wherein the condition (intervention or control condition), the order of the conditions, and the intervention dose (one of 25 possible doses selected randomly based on an adaptive protocol) are all randomized. The study is conducted at Columbia University Irving Medical Center, a large quaternary academic medical center serving a dense urban area in Northern Manhattan (New York City, New York, USA). The study has followed the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Intervention Trials (SPIRIT) [ 27 ] and the full checklist can be found in the Supplemental Materials. Eligible participants complete two 8-hour visits, separated by a washout period (Fig. 1 ). On the sedentary break (“intervention”) visit, a participant is prescribed via adaptive randomization to take sedentary breaks at a pre-specified frequency and duration; on the control visit, the participant sits uninterrupted. The order of the intervention and control visits is randomized for each participant. The study tests 25 combinations of frequency (5 doses: every 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min) and duration (5 doses: 1, 3, 5, 7, 10 min). The statistical design utilizes a Bayesian dose-finding method, called adaptive PIPE, developed specifically for this study [ 28 , 29 ]. Briefly, the first participant is assigned to the highest sedentary break combination (i.e., every 30 min for 10 min). The dose assignments of subsequent participants are determined adaptively in real-time based on the most recent estimate of the minimally effective dose(s) using all available primary outcome data accrued at the time of randomization. The primary outcome of BREAK2 is a beneficial cardiometabolic response to sedentary breaks defined as a clinically significant reduction in blood pressure (BP) or glucose relative to the uninterrupted sitting control condition (see Dose Determination below). A dose is considered effective if the cardiometabolic response rate is &gt; 60%. We plan to randomize a total of 324 participants and expect that 275 participants will have complete data to evaluate. Toxicity constructs (safety, tolerability, safety, and physical/psychological discomfort) are also measured for the sedentary break doses and are used to determine the maximally tolerated dose as the secondary aim (see Secondary Outcomes below). The trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov on April 29, 2022 ( NCT05353322 ). The study is approved by Columbia University Medical Center’s institutional review board. All participants provide informed consent with consent obtained by research staff. The study consent form is provided in the Supplemental Materials. Pilot study In a study (BREAK1) conducted in 2018, we tested the feasibility of the study protocol and evaluated preliminary effectiveness of 4 frequency/duration dose combinations (every 30 min for 1 min, every 30 min for 5 min, every 60 min for 1 min, and every 60 min for 5 min) on BP and glucose [ 30 ]. The current study utilizes a similar protocol, modified to account for the collection of additional measures and serial measurements that did not conflict with timing of the additional sedentary break doses tested. Participants Participants are eligible to participate in the study if they meet the following criteria: ≥18 years of age and English or Spanish speaking. Participants are excluded if they have any of the following: diagnosed chronic medical condition including overt CVD, active smoking, pregnancy, use of medications or over-the-counter drugs that influence glucose metabolism, or a pre-existing musculoskeletal condition that would preclude periodic walking. Because the study entails consumption of a controlled diet, participants are also excluded if they have a food allergy to wheat, eggs, milk or other dairy, gluten, fructose, or nuts, or have a dietary restriction (vegan, kosher, or halal). Recruitment and enrollment Participants are recruited through flyers, campus emails, newsletters, marketing/table events, and health fairs/community events at Columbia University Medical Center (a large medical center in an urban setting), Columbia University, and 4 other universities in New York City with whom we have established memoranda of understanding. Additionally, we recruit online (Craigslist, Meetup, CUMC sponsored RecruitMe, Facebook) and via local newspaper/radio ads to target surrounding neighborhoods. Recruitment materials direct interested participants to a secure web page (URL) where they can read the full study description. After providing consent, participants are asked to complete an online screening process and a demographics questionnaire. Those who are deemed eligible are contacted to schedule an enrollment/familiarization visit. At this visit, participants are acclimated to the treadmill and those comfortable and capable of walking at the required treadmill speed are asked to complete study consent, medication pill bottle review (participants are instructed to bring in any medication they currently take), arm circumference measurement, and anthropometric measures. Participants are also familiarized with all study devices (e.g., glucose and BP monitors) and procedures at this visit. They are then instrumented with two accelerometer devices and instructed to complete a 7-day accelerometer protocol run-in period. To promote study retention, participants are compensated $20 for completing the 7-day accelerometer protocol and $125 for completing each laboratory visit ($270 total). Accelerometer protocol run-in period Habitual sedentary behavior, physical activity, and sleep are assessed for 7 days using two devices: activPAL (V.4, Glasgow, UK) and GENEActiv (Activinsights, Cambridgeshire, UK). The 7-day accelerometer protocol is further used as a run-in period to screen out participants unlikely to be adherent to study procedures [ 31 ]. The activPAL is a thigh-worn triaxial accelerometer and inclinometer that has been validated for determining step counts, posture (sitting/lying, standing or stepping), sedentary time, and sit-to-stand and stand-to-sit transitions [ 32 – 35 ]. The activPAL is covered with a nitrile sleeve and is worn by participants on the midline of their dominant thigh, one-third of the way between the hip and knee via a breathable adhesive dressing (Hypafix or Tegaderm) in accordance with manufacturer specifications. Participants are fitted with the activPAL device during the enrollment visit and are provided instructions regarding the correct orientation in which to wear the activPAL monitor. Participants are instructed to wear the device continuously for 7 days and to not remove the monitor unless it is to be fully submerged in water (e.g., swimming, bath). Nitrile sleeves and dressings to reattach the monitor are provided. The activPAL is initialized by the activPAL software (V8.11.7.95) using the manufacturer default settings including a sampling frequency of 20 Hz. On completion of the 7-day accelerometer protocol, the time stamped 1 s ‘epoch’ data file and the ‘event’ data file from the activPAL software are exported as.csv files for processing and analysis in R. The GENEActiv is a wrist-worn triaxial accelerometer that has been validated for measuring sleep, sedentary behavior, and physical activity [ 36 – 38 ]. Participants wear the device on their non-dominant wrist and are instructed to wear the device continuously, 24 h a day, for the duration of the 7-day protocol. The GENEActiv is initialized by the Activinsights software, with a sampling rate of 30 Hz used. On completion of the 7-day accelerometer protocol, the raw accelerometer data are extracted and processed using the R-package GGIR [ 39 ]. Along with the two accelerometer devices, participants are provided with a three-page, paper-based diary to record daily sleep (i.e., time into bed, time lights off) and wake (i.e., wakeup time, out of bed time) times, times when the activPAL device is removed (if any), and work times (if any) and location (remote or in-person). At the end of the 7-day period, participants return the devices and diary in-person or via mail using a pre-paid envelope. Only participants who wear both devices for ≥ 20 h on 7 days are then scheduled for the laboratory study visits. Randomization and masking Eligible participants complete trials of two conditions (sedentary break condition or control condition) in a randomized order. The sedentary break dose consists of 1 of 25 possible frequency/duration combinations (e.g. every 30 min for 10 min); selected according to the adaptive randomization protocol. A member of the data management team not otherwise involved in this study assigns participants to their order of trial conditions and their sedentary break dose via use of a web application (Shiny app) to generate randomization code on the morning of the first visit. This member is also responsible for determining response outcomes of each participant upon completion of laboratory visits and inputting response into the web application to inform adaptive randomization. Study personnel conducting laboratory visits and participants are blinded to trial condition and sedentary break dose up until commencement of the trial visit. Furthermore, study personnel conducting laboratory visits are blinded to response outcome status of the participants throughout the course of the study. Pre-trial protocol Participants abstain from caffeine, alcohol, and exercise (e.g. no physical activity beyond activities of daily living) for 48 h prior to laboratory visits and maintain any medication regimen. To reduce diet-induced variations in the study outcomes, diet intake is standardized prior to each laboratory visit. Participants are provided with 2 days of food (3 meals and one snack per day) packed in a cooler, provided to participants at a food pick-up visit 3 days prior to laboratory visits. Each meal is individualized to meet 30% of daily estimated energy requirements [ 40 ]. The snack is individualized to meet 10% of daily estimated energy requirements The target macronutrient profile is 12–15% energy from protein, 55–58% from carbohydrate and 29–31% from fat, with daily intakes of 3600 mg of sodium and 2800 mg of potassium. Menus are verified for accuracy by an independent analytical food testing laboratory (Eurofins Food Testing, Madison, Wisconsin). Isocaloric and micronutrient-controlled meals are designed and prepared by the Bionutrition Research Core at the Irving Institute for Clinical and Translational Research at CUIMC. Participants are instructed to consume only the study food provided, to finish each meal completely, and to return all containers unwashed including any uneaten food items. Adherence is monitored by weighing the unwashed food containers and any food returned by the participant. Additionally, participants maintain a food log to record mealtimes and any deviations from the meal protocol. Trial laboratory visit protocol On trial condition days, participants arrive at the laboratory in the morning after a 12 h fast. After voiding, body weight is measured. An IV catheter is then inserted into the brachial vein, a BP cuff is placed on the non-dominant arm, and a heart rate monitor is fitted across the chest. After instrumentation, the participant rests for 30 min (e.g. the ‘steady-state’ period [-0.5 h to 0 h]) in an upright chair. After this steady-state period, BP is measured and a blood sample is collected through the IV. Each 8-hour trial thereafter commences upon starting consumption of a standardized breakfast (0 h), with the time taken to consume (&lt; 20 min/meal) replicated in subsequent conditions. At 4.0 h, participants consume lunch. Meals are standardized between trials using the protocol described above (see ‘Pre-Trial Protocol’ section). If a participant does not consume 100% of their laboratory meals during their first study visit, their meals for the second visit are redesigned to match their intake from the first visit exactly. BP is measured, blood samples collected, and questionnaires/tests administered at serial intervals throughout all trials. BP measures and blood samples are collected immediately before activity breaks (should they overlap). An example of data collection time points for sedentary break frequency/duration dose of every 60 min for 1 min is shown in Fig. 2 . Participants complete trials under the supervision of the research staff. Participants sit upright in an ergonomic chair throughout all trials and are instructed to minimize excessive movement, only rising from the chair to void. Lavatory visits are standardized to minimize unscheduled activity. Participants consume water ad libitum during the first trial and are instructed to replicate the volume consumed in their subsequent trial visit. Participants are instructed to read or work quietly on a laptop and to avoid activities that may influence arousal levels (e.g. watching TV, phone calls, music). Activity breaks entail light walking on a treadmill at 2.0 mph (0% grade) in accordance with our pilot study which demonstrated effects of activity breaks on the primary outcomes of the current study (glucose and BP) [ 30 ]. Walking was selected as the activity modality because, compared with other aerobic activities, it is a popular, familiar, convenient, and free form of activity that can be incorporated into almost every life setting [ 41 ]. Light intensity walking at 2.0 mph was selected to improve study generalizability. Activity intensity during walking breaks is monitored using heart rate (Polar H10) and rating of perceived exertion (RPE, Modified Borg 0–10 scale) [ 42 ]. Washout period A 4- to 14-day washout period between trial visits is used to eliminate potential carryover effects. Participants are instructed to resume their habitual physical activity during this washout period. Clear instructions about expected behaviors to maintain (e.g. exercise, sedentary time) are provided after each visit. Participants wear a GENEActiv accelerometer as described above (see ‘Accelerometer Protocol Run-In Period’ section above) to monitor for any potential changes in activity during the washout period. Primary outcomes Multiple CVD risk markers were selected to inform the minimally effective contour (MEC) to account for the possibility that improvements after sedentary behavior modification could manifest in different pathways to varying degrees. BP and glucose were selected as primary outcome measures, informed by strong theoretical models that have implicated hypertension and diabetes as intermediary pathways to CVD, evidence that acute reductions in BP and glucose elicited by bouts of physical activity largely account for and are predictive of the BP and glucose reductions that result from long-term training, and recognition by clinical guidelines that acute BP and glucose lowering via medication or physical activity are clinically meaningful [ 16 , 43 – 48 ]. Blood pressure BP is measured using a SpaceLabs OnTrak 90,277 oscillometric BP monitor (Spacelabs Healthcare, Snoqualmie, WA) and a standardized protocol. The device has been previously validated and is recommended for clinical use [ 49 ]. Measures are obtained using an appropriate sized cuff, on the nondominant arm, while participants are seated with back supported and feet flat on the floor. Participants rest their arm on a desk/chair arm so that the cuff is at heart level and are instructed to hold their arm still during measurements. Participants and research staff conducting trials are blinded to BP measures, with the device set to hide readings. BP measurements are collected at 0:00 (twice), 0:30, 1:20, 1:50, 2:50, 3:20, 4:20, 4:50, 5:50, 6:20, and 7:20. These timepoints were selected because they represented the timepoints across all 25 sedentary break doses that (1) did not overlap with a scheduled sedentary break and (2) where participants were seated for at least 5 min prior to measurement. BP measures over each 8-h study visit are summarized by subtracting the measurement at the beginning of the visit (the average of 2 measurements taken at 0:00 h) from the average of measurements during the 8-h period (i.e. change from baseline). Glucose Glucose is measured using the Freestyle Libre Pro (Abbott, Alameda, CA), an interstitial continuous glucose monitor (CGM) that is FDA-approved and validated for the estimation of blood glucose levels [ 50 , 51 ]. Glucose levels are recorded by the device at 15-min intervals. The CGM is fixed over the participants’ deltoid area on the dominant arm by research staff 3 days before trial visits (at the food pick-up visit) to account for acclimation of the CGM to the participant’s body. Participants and research staff conducting trials are blinded to glucose readings as the CGM readout can only be viewed via a dedicated reader made unavailable to participants/staff during the study visit. Glucose measures over each 8-h study visit are summarized using incremental area under the curve (iAUC). Secondary outcomes Adopting a pharmacotherapy approach wherein the maximally tolerated dose is the highest dose that does not cause unacceptable side effects (e.g. toxicity); toxicity in behavioral interventions is defined as undue physical or psychological distress or excessive burden that yields low compliance or high withdrawal [ 52 , 53 ]. Four constructs will be assessed to evaluate sedentary break “toxicity” and inform maximally tolerated dose: Tolerability Tolerability will be defined as full completion of the sedentary break protocol. That is, if the sedentary break dose is every 60 min for 5 min (a total of 40 min of activity over the 8 h visit); the participant is able walk for a full 5 min every 60 min (thus walking 40 out of a possible 40 min). Participants are encouraged to fully complete each sedentary break, but are instructed to discontinue any sedentary break if needed. Research staff document the completion of each break. Criteria for defining dose-limiting toxicity: failure to complete 100% of the target activity time. Safety Adverse physiological and musculoskeletal complications will be monitored including hypoglycemia (determined by CGM glucose levels &lt; 54 mg/dL [ 54 ]), exaggerated BP response (determined by systolic BP ≥ 210 mmHg for men and ≥ 190 mmHg for women [ 55 ]), or musculoskeletal injury (at the end of the intervention condition participants are asked to report any such occurrences that are not directly observed by research staff at study visits). Criteria for defining dose-limiting toxicity: glucose levels &lt; 54 mg/dL for &gt; 15 min , systolic BP ≥ 210 mmHg for men and ≥ 190 mmHg for women 51 or observed/reported musculoskeletal injury. Physical exhaustion/fatigue The physical exhaustion subscale of the Exercise-Induced Feeling Inventory (EFI) is used to assess physical exhaustion and fatigue. This subscale consists of 3 exhaustion/fatigued-related items (“Fatigued”, “Tired”, “Worn-out”) and response options on a 5-point scale from 0 (do not feel) to 4 (feel very strongly) [ 56 ]. Responses are summed with higher scores indicating greater perceived physical exhaustion. The EFI was specifically developed to measure the transient properties of acute physical activity and thus was selected to evaluate physical exhaustion/fatigue rather than other general fatigue measures. The physical exhaustion subscale of the EFI has high internal consistency, factorial validity, and concurrent validity and is sensitive to change with physical activity manipulations and predicts future exercise participation [ 56 – 59 ]. The EFI physical exhaustion subscale is administered at 0:00, 4:00, and 8:00 h for each intervention condition. Criteria for defining dose-limiting toxicity: Strong feelings of exhaustion/fatigue (EFI subscale score ≥ 9). Affective valence (i.e. pleasure/displeasure) Consistent with the principle of psychological hedonism, people tend to repeat behaviors that feel good and avoid behaviors that feel bad [ 60 ]. When applied to physical activity, those who experience a positive affective response to physical activity are more likely to repeat it in the future [ 61 ]. As such, core affective valence (that is, good or bad feelings; pleasure or displeasure) in response to physical activity is posited as an important determinant of future physical activity [ 62 , 63 ]. Rather than focus on discreet emotions (e.g. anxiety, depression), affective valence is measured because it (1) is pervasive in human experience, both within and outside the context of moods/emotions (one can have displeasure and not be depressed/anxious); (2) encompasses the entire affective domain, and (3) has a strong theoretical basis regarding its relationship to behavior (e.g. psychological hedonism). To measure affective valence, the Feeling Scale (FS) is used, a validated single-item measure of pleasure-displeasure recommended for assessment of physical activity-specific affect [ 64 , 65 ]. Participants are asked to rate their present feelings on an 11-point good/bad bipolar scale from − 5 (very bad) to + 5 (very good). The FS is administered at 0:00, 1:00, 2:00, 3:00, 4:00, 5:00, 6:00, 7:00, and 8:00 h for each intervention condition. Criteria for defining dose-limiting toxicity: Negative affective valence (FS &lt; 0) reported &gt; 1 time during trial. Exploratory outcomes Mood The Profile of Mood States (POMS) short-form questionnaire is administered at three timepoints (0:00, 4:00, and 8:00 h) and is used to assess mood. The POMS has six dimensions of mood state: depression-dejection, anger-hostility, tension-anxiety, confusion-bewilderment, fatigue-inertia, and vigor-activity. Subscales are summed (with exception of the vigor-activity score which is subtracted) to create an overall mood score, with higher scores indicating worse mood. The POMS has been reported to have adequate levels of internal consistency, as well as good criterion and construct validity [ 66 , 67 ]. Cognition A cognitive test battery (Cogstate) is administered at two time points (0:00 and 8:00 h) using a commercially-available web-based platform (Cogstate, New Haven, CT). Three cognitive domains are tested: working memory and attention (One-Back Test), psychomotor function (Detection Test), and executive function (Groton Maze Learning Test). The One-Back Test (~ 3 min test duration) is a card-based test wherein a playing card in the center of the screen turns over every 2 s and participants must answer “yes” or “no” regarding whether or not the new card is the same as the previously presented card. Speed and accuracy of performance on this task is measured, with lower reaction times and higher accuracy scores indicating better performance. The Detection Test (2 min test duration) is a card-based test wherein participants are asked to respond as quickly as possible when a card shown face down in the center of the screen turns over by clicking a computer mouse until either 25 correct responses are obtained, or the maximum time limit (2 min) is reached (whichever occurs first). Performance speed is measured with lower scores indicating better performance. The Groton Maze Learning Test (~ 5 min test duration) uses a maze learning paradigm, where the response time and total number of errors made when attempting to learn the same hidden pathway across five trials presented consecutively is calculated. Lower reaction time and error scores indicate better performance. These tests have minimal practice effects after initial familiarization, do not have ceiling or floor effects, have good test-retest reliability, and have acceptable criterion and construct validity [ 68 – 72 ]. To minimize practice effects the Cogstate battery provides a large number of equivalent alternative forms by having a large stimulus set from which exemplars are randomly chosen at run time, resulting in a different set of exemplars that are used each time an individual takes a given test. The cognitive battery is administered by study staff on a laptop computer. An introductory ‘practice’ test is presented to participants before each task. Test order remains the same throughout visits and the same laptop computer and response method is used to implement every battery for all participants. Acceptability Acceptability of the sedentary break frequency/duration dose combinations was evaluated using a five-item questionnaire (“I found the movement breaks program easy to follow”, “I found the movement breaks program enjoyable”, I found the length of today’s movement breaks to bee to long”, “I found the frequency of today’s movement breaks to be too often”, “I would be willing to follow today’s dose for a longer period of time [such as 3 months] in my everyday life”) with responses on a 5-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The acceptability questionnaire is administered at 8:00 h for the sedentary break condition. Blood glucose and insulin To assay for blood glucose and insulin, blood samples are collected via IV catheter into grey-top plasma tubes with sodium fluoride/potassium oxalate as stabilizers and serum tubes at serial timepoints for each trial, centrifuged, and stored at -80 °C for batch analysis. Glucose is assayed using a hexokinase method on a Roche Integra 400 plus (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN). Insulin is assayed using a chemiluminescent immunoassay on the Immulite 1000 autoanalyzer (Siemens, Los Angeles, CA). Inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation for both assays are &lt; 3%. Participants are permitted to opt out of IV insertion, as such blood samples are only available on a subset of study participants. Work engagement and performance The influence of various sedentary breaks doses on work engagement and performance is also of critical importance to evaluate dose tolerability for employed adults and will be essential for informing employer/workplace uptake. Employment and remote work capabilities are not inclusion criteria for the study and participants are given discretion to work or not work during study visits. For participants who choose to work during study visits, work engagement and performance are assessed. Work engagement is assessed using a Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9), adapted for momentary administration [ 73 , 74 ]. The 9-item questionnaire assesses three dimensions of work engagement: vigor, dedication, and absorption, using a 7-point Likert scale to report frequency of experiences (e.g. “I am immersed in my work”). The scale has good internal reliability and test-retest reliability [ 73 , 75 ]. Subjective work performance is assessed using select items from the World Health Organization Health and Work Performance Questionnaire (HPQ), adapted for momentary administration [ 76 ]. The HPQ includes two items that measure performance quantity (e.g. ““How often did you not work at times when you were supposed to be working”) and three items that measure performance quality on a 5-point Likert scale; along with an overall rating of work performance on a 0 to 10 scale. The HPQ has been validated against employer records and was designed to be equally relevant across a range of occupations [ 76 ]. The UWES-9 and HPQ are administered at 8:00 h for each condition. Additional measures Demographic information (age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income, partner status, employment status, occupation) and medical history are collected via online questionnaire. Body weight is measured at each study visit and height is measured at the study enrollment/familiarization visit using a digital scale and stadiometer (Seca 769, Seca GMBH, Hamburg). Waist circumference is also measured at the study enrollment/familiarization visit using a stretch-resistant tape measure. Data management BP and CGM data are downloaded using the manufacturer software and are screened for implausible values. Other study data are collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based application designed to support data capture for research studies [ 77 ]. REDCap includes built-in features for ensuring high data quality including an intuitive interface for data entry, real-time data validation and integrity checks, and audit trails for documenting all data entry/management activities. Collected data are entered into REDCap by a trained research team member. To ensure data quality, range and aberrant data value checks will be conducted to evaluate possible errors. Dose determination In this dose-finding study, we will explore a dose-response surface of 25 combinations of frequency (5 doses: every 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min) and duration (5 doses: 1, 3, 5, 7 and 10 min). The first participant will take sedentary breaks every 30 min for 10 min (the highest dose). The sedentary break dose (e.g. frequency and duration) of subsequent participants will be determined using an adaptive PIPE algorithm (Fig. 3 ), briefly described below. Technical details of the PIPE algorithm are provided in the Supplemental Materials. Outcome evaluation After each participant completes the two trial conditions, cardiometabolic response of the participant (defined by BP and glucose iAUC) will be determined and entered immediately to a PIPE web application. A positive response is defined as meeting any of the following criteria, compared to control: (1) mean reduction in systolic BP ≥ 4 mmHg (2), mean reduction in diastolic BP ≥ 2.5 mmHg, or (3) glucose iAUC reduction ≥ 15%. The commensurate reductions in systolic (≥ 4 mmHg) and diastolic (≥ 2.5 mmHg) BP were selected because it is comparable to the acute and chronic BP lowering effects of aerobic exercise; a recommended first-level therapy for hypertension treatment, is clinically meaningful as it would yield a ~ 13–15% reduction in risk of CVD and ~ 17–22% reduction in risk of stroke if sustained, and is commensurate with the reductions in BP observed in our pilot study [ 30 , 78 – 81 ]. A 15% reduction in glucose iAUC was selected because it is comparable to the reductions in glucose iAUC observed with acute and chronic aerobic exercise, is clinically meaningful as it would yield a ~ 15% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes if sustained, and is commensurate with glucose iAUC reductions observed in previous experimental studies [ 82 – 87 ]. Posterior updates using PIPE Using all available measured outcome data of enrolled participants, we will update the posterior estimates of response rates of all combinations using a Bayesian projection-based estimation method [ 29 ]. A dose is estimated to be effective if its posterior estimate of the response rate exceeds 60%; and an interim minimum effective contour (MEC) will be determined based on these estimates. Adaptive randomization The sedentary break condition for the next participant will be randomly selected from the MEC and its adjacent doses subject to acceptable toxicity profile described in Step 4 below. Dose-limiting toxicity monitoring A sedentary break dose is considered to have an un acceptable “toxicity” profile if &gt; 20% of participants exhibits any four constructs of the toxicity (see Secondary outcomes). Let p denote the probability of toxicity associated with a given dose, with a beta prior with a = 0.2 and b = 0.8. During the study, we will continuously monitor each dose for toxicity based on the following Bayesian criterion: a consideration for closing a dose condition will be invoked if the posterior probability that p exceeds 20% is high, namely, Prob( p &gt; 0.2| Data) ≥ 0.98. Applying this rule to up to 30 participants at a condition will yield an 89% probability of stopping a condition with a 45% toxicity rate with a median stopping time 12 participants, and a 5% erroneous stopping probability to a condition with 18% toxicity rate. Once a dose condition is closed, no future participants will be randomized to that dose per Step 3 above. Analysis for primary outcomes Reductions in BP and glucose iAUC for each dose combination will be summarized with descriptive statistics (i.e., mean and standard deviation). For primary analyses and for the purpose of dose escalation, we will analyze data according to treatment assignments in participants with complete BP and CGM data. “As-treated” analyses will also be conducted as sensitivity analyses. The primary aims of the proposed study are to identify, for each fixed duration, the minimum sedentary frequency that demonstrates a reduction in systolic BP, diastolic BP, or glucose iAUC (Aim 1a) and, for each fixed frequency, the minimum sedentary duration that demonstrates a reduction in systolic BP, diastolic BP, or glucose iAUC (Aim 1b). A feature of the adaptive PIPE is that it pairs interim dose determination with continual estimation of the MEC during the study. At the end of the study, the primary aims will be achieved by estimating the MEC using PIPE based on the final data in all study participants. Example scenarios of the MEC are shown in Fig. 4 . We will perform subgroup analyses and estimate the MEC by gender and age (&lt; 45 vs. ≥ 45 years). If there are sufficient sample size in a subgroup, we will also explore MEC by race (white, black, other), ethnicity (Hispanic vs. non-Hispanic), BMI (normal vs. overweight/obese), and MVPA (&lt; 150 vs. ≥150 min/week) subgroups in exploratory analyses. We will also evaluate the MEC for each measure (BP and glucose iAUC) separately. Analysis for secondary aims Rates of the four dose-limiting toxicity constructs will be summarized by proportions for each combination separately, as well as together as a composite toxicity endpoint. In addition, the maximally tolerated dose combinations will be estimated using PIPE [ 29 ]. These analyses, together with the primary analysis, will inform the best frequency/duration dose to move forward with in long-term trials. For example, if the primary analysis identifies three combinations based on BP/glucose (Fig. 4 , Panel B), the combination with higher positive affective valence, higher tolerability, etc. may be chosen for further testing based on totality of evidence. Exploratory analyses Mood (expressed as change from baseline), cognitive performance (expressed as change from baseline), blood glucose and insulin (expressed as iAUC), and work engagement and performance will be compared between the sedentary break and control conditions, and dose-response will be analyzed using Bayesian linear regression. Sample size Estimation We expect 324 participants will enroll in this study. Allowing for 10% attrition and 5% missing data, we expect that N = 275 participants will have complete data for both trial days. With N = 275, the adaptive PIPE algorithm will have a true positive probability (TPP) of 0.82 and false discovery probability (FDP) of 0.049 averaged across all possible dose-response configurations with a null response rate of 60% and an alternative response rate of 80%. The TPP is the probability of classifying a true effective dose as effective, and it is analogous to statistical power in a single hypothesis test setting (while this trial deals with 25 hypothesis tests). The FDP is the probability of selecting an in effective dose among the estimated minimally effective dose. The calculation of TPP and FDP is described in the Supplemental Materials. In contrast, with the same sample size of N = 275, a non-adaptive balanced randomization design (i.e., 11 participants per combination) using the same estimation procedure at a trial’s end would give a TPP of 0.57 and an FDP of 0.064. This underlines the advantages of using adaptive PIPE in this trial. Discussion Excessive sedentariness is a contributor to CVD risk and is a major public health burden. Several health agencies have provided general recommendations to “sit less, move more” by interspersing brief periods of activity. However, a lack of empirical evidence describing how often and for how long sedentary time should be interrupted to yield health benefit has precluded quantitative guidelines and remains a research priority. Without specific dose targets to recommend to the public (a hallmark of behavior change theories [ 88 ]); public health initiatives targeting sedentary time may have minimal effectiveness. Critically, without rigorously tested dosing information; RCTs targeting sedentary behavior may be null; bearing the risk of testing inefficacious or intolerable doses. Following a systematic framework for development of behavioral interventions, the proposed study constitutes a necessary Phase I-II dose finding study to identify efficacious sedentary break doses under ideal conditions. The objective of the proposed study is to determine the minimally effective dose (smallest dose) that yields cardiometabolic benefit for two sedentary break elements (frequency and duration). We will also determine the maximally tolerated dose (highest dose that does not cause undue physical/psychological distress) for both frequency and duration of sedentary breaks. Elucidating this dose range (minimally effective dose to maximally tolerated dose) will represent an important step in the development of quantitative sedentary behavior guidelines; providing information that will help ensure efficacious and tolerated doses are tested in future trials.
