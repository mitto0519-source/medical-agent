The work cannot be changed in any way or used commercially without permission from the journal. CASE: Jaxon is a healthy 12-year-old boy who is referred to your clinic for medication management. He was diagnosed with ADHD using a validated questionnaire at age 9 years. He is currently prescribed OROS methylphenidate 54 mg capsules once daily in the morning, which he has taken for 3 years. This has generally worked well to improve his attention, focus, task persistence, and task completion. However, his parents now report increasing disruptive behaviors over the past 18 months, both at home and at school. There have been no major stressors over this period nor significant life changes or trauma in Jaxon's history. Jaxon's teachers report that although he remains in his seat, he constantly “squirms” and moves about. He also makes quick, jerky movements with his hands several times each class period. They report no specific trigger or activity for when this movement occurs, but report it being more intense when Jaxon is working hard to concentrate and stay focused. His mother also reports noticing the frequency increase if Jaxon thinks he is in trouble or when he is very excited (e.g., when he is playing video games). His parents share home videos to support these concerns. His teachers report problems with attention, fidgeting, and talking out of turn. They also report that his movements distract and disrupt the class. These reports are most often from his afternoon classes. Academically, he is doing well. He turns in most of his work, but his grades have dropped this semester. His teacher reports that he seems to lack the focus to complete his assignments and he rushes through them to go to the next activity. When you talk to Jaxon, he reports that he likes school, but he does not like how often he gets into trouble. He admits he gets embarrassed during quiet time when his hands randomly “move without me moving them.” When observing Jaxon, you notice that he fidgets in his seat, often moving his hands over anything that is holding his attention (toys, papers). He is also verbally impulsive, interrupting the discussion with his mother multiple times during the visit. Twice, you witness a brisk single jerk of his right hand. It originates at the wrist and rotates away from his body with his hand closed. There was no accompanying vocalization with this movement. His parents are requesting a medication adjustment or change. What would you do? One of the most central is the balance between medication efficacy and side effect mitigation. Based on the history presented, it seems as though this balance was previously weighted positively toward good efficacy with minimal side effects. It now seems, however, that his current medication regimen is not providing as much benefit as it did in the past. This is evidenced by his difficulty paying attention, being more disruptive, and seeming to be in constant motion. In addition, the newly described wrist movements are concerning for a new-onset tic. A high proportion of children with ADHD have a comorbid tic disorder, but not every child treated with stimulants experiences an exacerbation of their tics. 1 Furthermore, not every child's tics are severe or troublesome and thus may not always warrant intervention. 2 In Jaxon's case, he reports feelings of embarrassment related to his hand movements. His psychosocial concerns, combined with less-than-optimal control of his ADHD symptoms, make a change in his pharmacologic treatment regimen a logical next step to consider. One study has shown that fewer children treated with methylphenidate will see worsening of tics than those treated with other stimulant medications, specifically dextroamphetamine. 2 Jaxon is already receiving treatment with methylphenidate, which suggests that switching to a different type of stimulant may not be the answer. Instead, the addition of an alpha-2 agonist such as guanfacine or clonidine may be more effective—not only in helping to suppress the tics but also in improving control of ADHD symptoms. The data to support the use of an alpha-2 agonist to suppress tics is somewhat mixed, but several studies have shown promising results. 3 , 4 Demvihin Ihyembe, MD A key tenet of the diagnostic criteria of ADHD is the presence of constant movement like fidgeting, tapping, squirming, etc. This is typically the most observable and disruptive manifestation of ADHD. 5 In some instances, these movements take on a repetitive pattern, thus expanding the differential to include conditions where repetitive movements are central to the condition and diagnosis, e.g., autism spectrum disorder, tic disorders, and Tourette syndrome. In broad terms, self-stimulatory behaviors, aka “stimming,” are generally unconscious, can be calming/soothing, and provide relief by doing the behavior. 6 By contrast, children who suffer from tics like Jaxon, especially complex tics, are usually aware of, distressed by, and find relief when the tic is over. The current clinical practice guidelines for the assessment and treatment of children and adolescents with complex attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder recommend assessing the level of impairment of each comorbid diagnosis and adjusting management as needed. Chronic tic disorder co-occurring with ADHD is a common and challenging presentation in Developmental-Behavioral Pediatrics with a prevalence of ∼20%. 6 Meta-analysis of controlled trials does not support an association between new-onset or worsening of tics and psychostimulant use. 7 , 8 However, clinical practice suggests reconsidering stimulant medication in children already diagnosed with tics or with a strong family history because of fear of unmasking/inducing tics or worsening that which is already present. 8 First-line management of tics is comprehensive psychoeducation, addressing/managing triggers, and improving coping strategies. 9 Targeted behavioral interventions like Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT) and Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) are both effective evidence-based treatments for chronic tic disorders. 21 Pharmacological treatment should be considered if the tics moderately/severely interfere with daily functioning. If a child's ADHD symptoms are already well-managed on stimulant medications, the recommendation is to continue stimulant medication for several weeks while trialing the first-line pharmacological treatment for tics, alpha-2 agonists. However, if ADHD symptoms are more impairing, the recommendation is to treat with stimulants because these remain the first-line pharmacologic treatment of ADHD if the tics are mild to moderate, have subsided, or have responded well to treatment. Second-line pharmacotherapy of tics is antipsychotics, with atypical antipsychotics preferred because of fewer side effects. 9 Tamar Caceres, MD There are several important considerations for management of this case. These include age, comorbidities, and the educational/emotional impact of Jaxon's symptoms. Characterization of Jaxon's new movements is the critical first step. The differential of the repetitive movements described include tics, stereotypies, and myoclonus. Tics and stereotypies both commonly co-occur in ADHD. 10 Genetic generalized epilepsies, including juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, also have high rates of neurobehavioral symptoms, including ADHD. 11 These movements could likely be differentiated by direct observation and in discussion of the timing of episodes, sensory urge, or associated sensations and suppressibility. Standardized severity measures should be used to quantify and track symptom control. 12 , 13 Although there has been concern in the past that stimulants can cause tics or unmask tic disorders, 14 current evidence does not support this. 14 , 15 When tics appear as an adverse event later in the course of stimulant treatment, as in Jaxon's case, this is even less suggestive of a causal connection. It is important to look for a history of subtle tics before stimulant use. In addition, evaluation for additional comorbidities that could increase tic severity, such as anxiety or depression, 14 or academic or social stressors should be considered. There is mixed evidence to support a difference in onset or worsening of tics in methylphenidate versus amphetamine stimulant derivatives or dose; however, given Jaxon's impairing ADHD symptoms, I would consider a trial of a different stimulant class and increased school support with a 504 plan should be recommended. Jaxon and his family should be educated on tics and their natural course and treatment options that include medication and cognitive-behavioral therapies, such as CBIT. Based on family preference, switching to a nonstimulant ADHD medication that is also beneficial for tics, such as guanfacine or atomoxetine, could be considered. 16 For modification of Jaxon's current medication regimen, it is important to consider if his tics or ADHD are currently having a greater functional impact on his life so that targeted treatment can be prioritized. Lewis-Esquerre, PhD To determine whether the reported repetitive behaviors are symptoms of a tic disorder, administration of the Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS) is recommended. 17 Motor and vocal tic behaviors are assessed separately across 5 domains: number, frequency, intensity, complexity, and interference. Administration of this instrument also facilitates a detailed discussion about repetitive behaviors that, in my experience, has helped patients develop awareness of antecedents of the behavior, details of the behavior itself, and consequences of the behavior in the targeted environment. The data garnered from this discussion then informs the intervention. If these movements are identified as tic behaviors, then CBIT, which includes Habit Reversal Training (HRT), can be used to help lessen tic triggers and help Jaxon learn exercises to use when tics are most interfering. CBIT is not a tool that can be used to eliminate tics because tics are a chronic neurological condition, but rather a strategy to be used in certain environments to help better control tic behaviors. 18 After psychoeducation provided by the clinician, CBIT encompasses 2 phases; the first phase is to identify and change the internal (i.e., emotions, bodily states, thoughts) and external (i.e., environmental stimuli, behavior of others) environments that result in tic exacerbation; whereas the second phase is to identify an effective competing response exercise, which helps the patient learn to tolerate the sensation of a premonitory urge while preventing the tic behavior response (exposure and response prevention or ERP). In my practice working with children with chronic tic disorders, I have found success using CBIT along with ERP to lessen the negative impact of tic behaviors, reduce shame, and improve patient self-advocacy. These therapies have demonstrated significantly reduced impairments related to tics and are very helpful for children with or without comorbidities such as ADHD because they include working with families and in educational settings. 19 , 20 This approach is a particularly good fit for Jaxon's current presentation and has proven to have good feasibility and acceptability. 20 Exploration of the external environment, such as peer or teacher reactions, could yield additional opportunities to modify or eliminate tic triggers. COMMENTARY Jason Fogler, PhD, Elizabeth Diekroger, MD The treatment of ADHD is complex and multifactorial, and this solution is just one of many possible combinations to address Jaxon's difficulties. In the spirit of shared decision-making honoring caregiver's and children's concerns are essential to the rapport building needed for effective management of complex ADHD. His family and treatment team will likely need to progress through various rounds of trial and error before finding an acceptable resolution. To effectively direct the focus of treatment, the SDBP Complex ADHD Guideline 6 recommends continually weighing the impairment stemming from tics against the impairment stemming from ADHD symptoms. Counseling for the patient emphasizing that his symptoms represent a comorbid tic disorder that is likely to wax and wane over time 1 unrelated to his stimulant, and discussion of the natural trajectory of tic disorders is critical because this will likely affect the perception of benefit or adverse events associated with medication trials in the future.
