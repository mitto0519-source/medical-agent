Background Liver diseases are a public health problem in developing regions, and the majority of them are asymptomatic. Evidence on the current burden of liver disease and associated context-specific risk factors is scarce in the study area, where various risk factors for liver disease, including khat chewing, smoking, and aflatoxin exposure, are common. This study was to assess the magnitude, patterns, and factors associated with liver disease among patients visiting governmental hospitals in eastern Ethiopia. Methods A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among randomly selected 388 clinically suspected adults for liver disease visiting a public hospital in Dire Dawa, Ethiopia. Data was collected using a combination of structured questionnaires, physical examinations, and investigations through interviews, medical chart reviews, liver enzymes, and the results of ultrasound examinations. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to determine the factors associated with liver disease. Candidate variables in bivariable analysis were selected based on p -values &lt; 0.25, biological plausibility, and evidence of associations from previous studies. The level of significance was considered at a P -value &lt; 0.05, and the adjusted odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval was reported. Results A total of 388 (92%) adults were included and liver disease was diagnosed among 295 (76%; 71.5–80.2) of the study participants, where chronic liver disease due to non-viral (65.1%) and viral causes (34.9%) were prevalent. The risk of liver disease was higher among females (AOR = 3.43; 1.80–6.55) and under grade twelve (AOR = 3.90; 1.95–7.82) compared to counterparts. Furthermore, alcohol consumption (AOR = 3.14; 1.33–7.32), khat chewing (AOR = 1.69; 0.91–3.15), a history of hospitalization (AOR = 4.20; 2.29–7.11), and a history of intestinal parasite infection (AOR = 1.17; 0.55–2.49) were found to be positively associated with an increased risk of liver disease among adults. Conclusions Liver disease detection rate was found to be high and could be associated with substance use (alcohol and khat), history of hospitalization, and worm infection, which can be incorporated into the prevention strategies of liver diseases. The liver performs various biochemical functions necessary for the removal of waste and normal health [ 3 ]. However, this organ is a victim of many external exposures resulting in liver diseases. The manifestations of liver disease are diverse, ranging from mild to severe metabolic derangements, which are more pronounced in the advanced stages of the disease [ 4 ]. More importantly, up to 50% of individuals with underlying liver disease have no symptoms, causing the illness to progress. The symptomatic patients usually present with a very non-specific sign and symptoms like fatigue or excessive tiredness, lack of drive, abdominal pain, and sometimes itching [ 5 , 6 ]. The liver could be adversely affected by many environmental, dietary, and microbial exposures and could cause liver diseases [ 2 , 7 ]. Liver disease refers to a chronic progressive condition characterized by liver cell dysfunction [ 8 ] and typically presents with enlargement of the liver in acute cases and liver shrinkage in majority of chronic insults, spleen, cutaneous hepatic stigmata, and abnormal liver function test results [ 9 , 10 ]. However, due to the liver's substantial reserve capacity, measurable changes in metabolic function may not be evident until significant damage has occurred [ 5 ]. Metabolic derangement is most evident in patients with advanced liver disease, and the manifestations are similar regardless of the initial etiologic insult [ 11 – 14 ]. Since the many functions of the liver may be affected to varying degrees in individual patients, no single test effectively measures the overall state of liver function [ 7 ]. The pattern and prevalence of liver disease vary geographically and among different ethnic groups due to various practices and risk factors [ 15 ]. Huge socioeconomic health disparities exist in sub-Saharan Africa, where the region harbors 24% of the global disease burden [ 16 ]. Chronic liver disease (CLD) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [ 7 ], encompassing conditions such as chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [ 17 ]. For instance, in Ethiopia, 11.4% of medical admission are due to liver diseases. The state of CLD arises from persistent liver inflammation and hepatocellular necrosis, often caused by viral infections or exposure to toxic substances like alcohol, aflatoxins, and others [ 18 ]. Hepatitis A, B, and C are among the leading causes of viral hepatitis, with hepatitis B and C infections posing a substantial global health challenge [ 5 , 6 ]. Liver diseases pose a significant burden on public health globally, including in Ethiopia [ 2 , 11 , 19 , 20 ]. The recent WHO estimate showed that 354 million people are victims of viral hepatitis infection, mainly hepatitis B and C, contributing to 4.54 million deaths annually [ 21 ]. Moreover, CLD is the most prevalent form of liver disease, contributing to 2.2% of deaths and 1.5% of the total disease burdens, causing an estimated 1.3 million deaths [ 13 , 22 ]. Additionally, Metabolic-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MASLD) characterized by excessive fat accumulation in the liver and primarily associated with obesity, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome [ 23 , 24 ] and alcoholic liver disease related to alcohol abuse are common as well [ 11 , 25 – 27 ]. Despite the significant impact of liver disease on public health with increasing trends [ 12 ], it remains a neglected issue in many developing countries, including Ethiopia [ 27 , 28 ]. Patients with liver diseases often receive limited information and attention, and there is currently a lack of baseline data on the patterns and associated factors of liver disease in specific regions, such as Dire Dawa. Understanding the prevalence and risk factors of liver diseases in Dire Dawa city administration is crucial for developing targeted strategies to mitigate the burden of these conditions in the community [ 2 , 7 , 13 , 20 , 22 , 29 ]. Hence, such evidence would provide valuable insights for regional health authorities, health facilities, and the chronic disease care unit to formulate evidence-based strategies for the prevention and management of liver diseases in the region, ultimately reducing the morbidity and mortality associated with these conditions. Therefore, this study aims to assess the magnitude, patterns, and associated factors of liver diseases among patients visiting health facilities in Dire Dawa city administration. Materials and methods Study area and period This study was conducted in the Dilchora referral hospital located in Dire Dawa, which is located in the eastern part of Ethiopia and lies between 900 27' and 900 49' N latitudes and between 410 38' and 420 19' E longitudes. It has 2 public and 3 private hospitals, 8 urban and 7 rural health centers, 36 clinics (34 private and 2 non-governmental organizations), and 38 health posts. In Ethiopia there are more than 120 million population. This hospital is serving millions of people from Dire Dawa, Harar, east Harari, and still from the Somali region. Dire Dawa town has an estimated population size of 535,000 (270,000 and 265,00) which is within the catchment of the hospital [ 30 ]. Hence, the patient flows are very diverse, with diverse sociocultural and economic lifestyles. In the study area, cigarette smoking, khat chewing, social life, and other relevant risk factors for liver disease are common, which might predispose individuals to a variety of viral, alcoholic, non-alcoholic, and other forms of liver disease [ 31 , 32 ]. More importantly, khat chewing and cigarette smoking are highly prevalent. Further unrestrictive social interaction, along with limited health literacy about the communicable type of liver disease (viral hepatitis), could further increase the transmission, especially among those from rural areas. The city administration is categorized into 9 urban and 38 rural kebeles. The study was conducted in the Dire Dawa city administration from March to June 2022. Study design and population A facility-based cross-sectional study design was employed to examine the magnitude, patterns, and associated factors of liver disease among clinically suspected clients with liver disease who were visiting Dilchora referral hospital in Dire Dawa city administration. All clinically suspected clients for liver disease (presumptive cases or potential cases) aged above 18 years visiting the outpatient department of the hospital were the source population. These cases were screened using the typical signs and symptoms of liver diseases: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, especially right upper quadrat pain, jaundice, lower extremity swelling, abdominal swelling, dark urine, or pale stool. All systematically (randomly) selected presumptive cases with any of the signs and symptoms suggestive of liver diseases (abdominal pain, nausea, diarrhea, dark colored urine, fatigue, vomiting, right upper quadrant pains, yellow-colored eyes, and others) who were visiting the hospital outpatient department during the study period were the actual study population. Eligibility criteria All clinically suspected clients for liver disease (as mentioned under population) who visited the Dilchora referral hospital outpatient department in Dire Dawa city administration during the study period were included. Presumptive clinical indicators of liver diseases such as nausea, vomiting, yellow discoloration of the eyes and skin (jaundice), ascites, edema, and other symptoms were employed for diagnosis. Those outpatient department visitors who were less than 18 years old and/or who were seriously ill and unable to communicate were excluded from the study. In addition, those with established liver disease (based on clinical record and history) and being treated were excluded from the study as this could overestimate the burden of liver disease among clinically suspected clients at the OPD ( n = 16) (Fig. 1 ). Sample size estimation Objective I focus on evaluating the scale and patterns of liver diseases through descriptive analysis. To determine the sample size for this objective, we utilized the suggested prevalence of liver disease (P).The sample size was calculated using a single proportion formula by considering the following assumptions: the prevalence of liver disease (P) of 50% has been taken because no context-specific evidence is available and the occurrence of the problem is usually rare among the general population, where we need a larger sample to capture such rare outcomes; a 95% confidence level and 5% degree of precision; a margin of error (d = 0.05); and Z α/2, the standard normal value at a 95% confidence level (1.96). Hence, the sample required was 422, considering the 10% non-response rate. Objective II, on the other hand, delves into exploring the factors linked to liver disease by employing Adjusted Odds Ratios or regression coefficients in sample size calculations. We have also considered sample size calculation for the second objective to capture an adequate sample for both objectives using EPiInfo. Thus, considering alcohol consumption (AOR = 2.8), herbal medicine (AOR = 14) and tooth extraction (AOR = 2.95) [ 33 ], the required samples were 316, 64, and 264. Finally, comparing the sample size calculated for both objectives, we have considered the higher sample size calculated for the first objective; hence, the final sample size became 422. Sampling procedures Dilchora Hospital was purposely selected for having the highest number of Outpatient Department (OPD) visits and the highest caseload out of the two public hospitals in the city. It is selected as it is a governmental hospital found in the Dire Dawa, serving a wider catchment area in the eastern part of the country. A systematic random sampling technique was employed for all the patients visiting the government hospital. Based on the average OPD visit report for the last three months and the expected proportion of suspected liver disease clients, the sample fraction was calculated (k = 2). After randomly selecting the first respondent, clients from each of the two sample fractions were included in the OPDs that fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Since the number of patients fulfilling the criteria was low (below 50%), we took a longer data collection period so as to ensure systematic sampling and get a more representative population compared to consecutive sampling. For each selected participant, the data was collected using history, physical examination, laboratory, and radiological examination results. Briefly, through rough screening of 4650 OPD visitors over the three years period, a total of 850 suspected liver disease cases were considered in this study and in the meantime, we found 16 established cases of liver disease and these cases were excluded although they sign and symptoms of liver disease as the exact source population of our study are only suspected and unconfirmed cases of liver disease. Among these, a systematic random sample of 422 clients were eligible. Depending on the sequence of identification of suspected cases, random samples were selected every two sample interval until we reached 422. However, a total of 34 cases were not willing to participate in the current study despite they were randomly selected, and hence, the final study subjects were 388 (Fig. 1 ). Data collection tool and procedures A combination of interviewer-assigned interviews, physical examinations, relevant laboratory assessments, and radiological examinations were employed to conduct the current study. The questionnaire was prepared after reviewing previous literature, and the tool was prepared in English and translated to local languages for actual data collection (Supplementary File 1). Data was collected by interviewing patients using a standard data collection format prepared in English, considering the variables. The other data collection was by laboratory test and use of abdominal ultrasound examination. Every selected patient was assessed with the techniques of history-taking and physical examination and sent to the laboratory for testing the liver function test and assessing the patterns, and finally an ultrasound examination was conducted. Any inconsistencies in the data collection procedure and outcome confirmation were handled by the investigators and the physicians accordingly. An abdominal ultrasound was conducted by a radiologist to identify any problems linked to liver disease. In addition, for liver enzymes, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase enzymes were quantified. Hence, a 2 mL venous blood sample was taken by a trained laboratory technician and analyzed using a liver function test analyzer machine. The results were reported in international units per liter. Data quality control To assure data quality, we carried out a careful design of data collection procedures, pretesting, appropriate recruitment procedures, and one-day training on the objective of the study, the selection of study participants, how to maintain confidentiality of information, the contents of the questionnaire, how to fill out the data collection format, data quality management, and follow-up for data collectors and supervisors. Intensive supervision was done by investigators and supervisors during the whole period of data collection. A random sample of registration forms was reviewed by investigators to ensure the reliability and trustworthiness of the data before data collection, and the investigators also made random cross-checks for their completeness, accuracy, and consistency at the end of each day, where corrective measures were undertaken with all the research team members. Remarks were given during the morning on how to eliminate or minimize errors and take corrective actions. The data was checked for completeness and consistency and then coded, entered, and stored on the computer using Epi Info version 3.2.2 software. A pretest was performed by 5% of the population at a non-selected hospital to check the consistency of the data collection tool and the activity of the data collectors. After pretesting the questionnaire, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated by using SPSS window version 23 to test the internal consistency (reliability) of the item (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82) which was good. In addition, content validity will be cross-checked by another internist. Variables in the study and definition The dependent variable of the current study was the presence of liver disease or having a diagnosis of liver disease. Hence, liver disease was diagnosed using history, physical examination (presence of ascites, varices, and splenomegaly), laboratory findings (decreased serum albumin, increased prothrombin time, and altered liver enzymes), and ultrasound findings (portal hypertension, splenomegaly, varices, and ascites). The respective lab tests were conducted in accordance with the standard procedures [ 28 ]. The diagnosis of liver disease was declared using a combination of clinical features suggestive of liver disease, ultrasound findings (presence of an irregular liver surface or liver parenchyma heterogeneity), and elevated liver enzymes consistent with acute or chronic liver diseases [ 34 ]. Clinical and laboratory parameters were obtained at the outpatient department before admission. Analytical liver injury was defined as at least two of the following: aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels ≥ 74 &lt; 300 U/L, AST/alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ratio &gt; 2, and total bilirubin &gt; 1.2 mg/dL. Abdominal ultrasound was used to identify steatosis, hepatomegaly, heterogeneous liver, and portal hypertension [ 6 ]. Portal hypertension is defined as elevated blood pressure within the portal venous system, usually due to liver-related factors, with a hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) surpassing 5 mmHg. This condition leads to complications like collateral vessel formation, splenomegaly, ascites, and varices [ 34 ]. The independent variables in the study include socio-demographic characteristics of patients (age, sex, place of residence, education, income, occupation), type of liver diseases, history of alcohol consumption, history of parasite infection, nutrition, harmful traditional practices (FGM, traditional dental extraction, traditional skin laceration, male home circumcision), use of sharp materials, sexual history, sharing of sharp materials, history of admission to hospital, IV drug abuse, family history of liver diseases (vertical transmission), history of blood transfusion, history of surgery, history of contact with jaundice patient, and cultural related factors (home delivery, uvulectomy, and tattooing). Khat chewing refers to any history khat consumption for the purpose of stimulation or other while a habit of khat chewing is defined as usual or day to day consumption of khat substance as lifestyle. Khat (Catha edulis) is a flowering plant native to East Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. The leaves and stems of khat contain alkaloids, such as cathinone, which have stimulant effects similar to amphetamines. Data processing and analysis Data were cleaned, edited, coded, and entered into Epi-Info version 3.2.2 and exported to SPSS version 23 for Windows. Exploratory data analysis was carried out to check the levels of missing values and the presence of influential outliers. The findings were presented using statistical tables and figures. We have also reported the frequency, percentage, and mean. The normality of a continuous variable was checked using normality plots and tests. We employed both visual normality plots, such as Q-Q plots, and statistical tests of Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to assess the normality of data distributions. The variables follow approximate normal distribution. To assess the degree of association between the independent variables and liver disease, we employed a binary logistic regression after checking the assumptions. Hence, sequential bivariable and multivariable logistic regression were applied. Candidate variables for the multivariable logistic regression model were selected based on the following criteria: those independent variables significantly associated with liver disease in bivariable logistic regression at a p -value below 0.25, biological plausible, and important predictors of liver disease from previous literature [ 35 – 37 ]. The strength of the association was evaluated using a crude and adjusted odds ratio and presented along with the 95% confidence intervals. A P -value ≤ 0.05 was used as a cutoff point to determine statistical significance in multiple logistic regressions for the final model. Hosmer and Lemeshow’s as well as the Omnibus test were used to test the model’s fitness [ 35 ]. Multicollinearity was checked using the inflated standard error (above 2), and variance inflation factor of above 10 [ 38 ], and interaction effects were also evaluated accordingly [ 39 ]. Ethical approval The study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from the Dire Dawa University Ethical Review Board. Additionally, a support letter was obtained from the respective government structures. Ethical clearance was further secured from the Dire Dawa University Research Ethical Review Committee. All the information collected during the study will be used solely for research purposes and handled with strict confidentiality. It will not be disclosed to any individuals other than the investigators. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from each study participant, ensuring their understanding and agreement to participate. Results Baseline characteristics of participants A total of 388 study participants were approached and interviewed in this study out of 422 potential samples, with overall response rate of 92%. The mean age of study participants was 38.3 years (± 13.7 years), with a minimum and maximum age of participants ranging from 18 to 80 years. Regarding the gender of the clients, more than half (60.1%) of the clients were males. A total of 204 (52.6%) were from rural areas. Concerning their educational status, 106 (27.3%) and 96 (24.7%) attended college and were in grades 9–12, respectively, and 64 (16.5%) of them were illiterate. The majority of the study clients, 95 (24.5%), were farmers in occupation, followed by housewives (17.5%), privately employed (8.0%), and government employed (12.1%) (Table 1 ). Magnitude of common risk factors for liver diseases Regarding the substance use patterns, 98 (25.3%) reported consuming alcohol in different amounts. A total of 203 (52.3%) reported chewing khat, while 143 (36.9%) had the habit of chewing kha regularly. Among the study participants, 41 (10.6%) and 58 (14.9%) had tattoos on their bodies and had tooth extractions through traditional methods. A significant percentage of women reported having delivered at home in the past. More importantly, only 4 (1%) reported to have a habit of sharing sharp materials with others (Table 2 ). Magnitude and patterns of liver diseases among clinically suspected patients As indicated in Fig. 2 . Among a total of 388 clinically suspected clients for liver disease, liver disease was diagnosed in 295 (76%; 95% CI: 71.5–80.2) of the study participants. A total of 24% of the study participants were screened negative for liver disease based on liver function and ultrasound examination results. Regarding the patterns of liver diseases, a total of 192 (49.5%) had a non-viral liver disease, and 34.9% had a viral liver disease. Among these, the hepatitis B and C tests were positive, accounting for 71.2% and 20.4%, respectively. The average bilirubin level was 1.47 ± 0.95 mg dl-1. The mean SGOT/AST and SGPT/ALT levels of the study participants were higher than the normal range (0–56 units per liter), which was 73.9 ± 51.7 units per liter for SGPT/ALT and 75.7 ± 43.7 units per liter for SGOT/AST. The majority of the liver disease patients were found to have had chronic liver disease with other comorbidities (160), while only 11 had hepatomegaly, 8 had cirrhosis, and 11 had portal hypertension (Table 3 ). Factors associated with liver disease among clinically suspected liver disease clients We explored the potential risk factors for the development of liver diseases among adults using logistic regression. Hence, we found that sex, educational status, residence, substance use, history of hospitalization, and blood transfusion were found to be major risk factors for the development of liver diseases among adults. Being male (COR = 1.87 (1.13–3.110) and attending higher education (COR = 4.85; 95% CI: 2.67–8.81) increased the risk of developing liver disease by two and four folds, respectively. While alcohol consumption (COR = 3.53; 95% CI: 1.75–7.20) increased the risk by 3.53 times, khat chewing also increased the odds of liver disease by 2.33 (COR = 2.33; 95% CI: 1.44–3.77). Furthermore, a recent history of surgery (COR = 3.09; 95% CI: 1.19–8.03) and hospitalization (COR = 7.19; 95% CI: 4.29–12.0) could significantly increase the risk of developing liver disease. On the contrary, history of contact with jaundiced individuals did not predict the risk of having liver disease (COR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.54), and the majority of the clients with liver disease did not have such exposures which could be related to contracting the disease from asymptomatic carriers or the majority of the patients had non-viral liver diseases (non-communicable) (Table 4 ). We fit an adjusted model using a stepwise regression approach. Thus, those variables with p-values below 0.25 in bivariable logistic regression and/or other biologically plausible variables were considered in the multivariable model, as indicated in Table 5 . Hosmer and Lemeshow’s goodness of fit was 0.151, which indicates a better fit model under the null hypothesis. In addition, we have checked for multicollinearity among the independent variables and the VIF values were below 5 and the standard errors were below 1, which indicate absence of multicollinearity. Those females (AOR = 3.43; 95% CI: 1.80–6.55) who attended until grade twelve (AOR = 3.90; 95% CI: 1.95–7.82) had more than three-fold increased odds of being diagnosed with liver disease compared to males and those illiterates, respectively. While alcohol consumption (AOR = 3.14; 95% CI: 1.33–7.32) increased the risk of liver disease by 3.14 times, khat chewing was also associated with a 69% higher risk of liver disease (AOR = 1.69; 95% CI: 0.91–3.15). Moreover, history of hospitalization (AOR = 4.20; 95% CI: 2.29–7.11) and history of intestinal parasite infection (AOR = 1.17; 95% CI: 0.55–2.49) were positively associated with an increased risk of liver disease among adults (Table 5 ). Discussion Liver diseases are becoming the common public health problems, especially in developing countries, where many risk factors are concentrated due to low socioeconomic status and poor access to tertiary care [ 7 , 20 ]. Hence, we conducted this study to quantify the occurrence of liver diseases among clinically suspected clients for liver diseases. Due to the higher costs of laboratory investigation and the low prevalence among the general population, we limited our target population to the clinically suspected clients as a more efficient, ethical, and feasible strategy. Hence, we found that liver disease was diagnosed in 295 (76%; 95% CI: 71.5–80.2) of the study participants, where the major diagnosis was CLD due to non-viral (65.1%) and viral causes (34.9%) based on the liver function and ultrasound examination results. A study from Nepal showed alcoholic liver disease 50 (38.5%), viral hepatitis 44 (33.8%), and liver abscess 11 (8.5%) were the common liver diseases. Also, chronic diseases were caused by alcohol 45 (60.8%), followed by hepatitis B and C infections (14.8%) [ 40 ]. In comparison to our study, although MASLD and complications from alcohol are the most frequent etiologies of liver disease in developed countries, viral hepatitis B and C are prevalent in Ethiopia, increasing the risk for cirrhosis in addition to chronic exposure to liver toxins [ 41 ]. Regarding the causes of CLD, chronic HBV infection was identified in 55 (36.7%); other etiological agents (8%) were identified. No etiological factors were identified in the remaining 83 (55.3%) patients [ 42 ]. However, it should be noted that the potential role of khat chewing, pesticide exposures, aflatoxin exposures, alcohol consumption, and other unhealthy lifestyle should not be underestimated especially in this particular study settings. The findings from our study suggest a potential association between liver diseases and certain sociodemographic characteristics. Specifically, our results indicate a higher prevalence of liver diseases among females and individuals with lower educational grades. These findings shed light on the complex interplay between sociodemographic factors and the risks of liver diseases, highlighting the need for targeted interventions and public health strategies to address the socioeconomic disparities observed. Previous studies have also highlighted such huge disparities among socioeconomic classes [ 16 , 21 ]. On the contrary, a previous study showed that the risk for viral hepatitis was found to be higher among males (38%) compared to females (31.8%). These could be associated with the concomitant occurrence of previously mentioned risk factors among males and those among low socioeconomic classes. Also, the risk of transmission could be very high among those who are uneducated, of low socioeconomic class, and from rural areas. Hence, further research is warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms driving these associations and to develop effective interventions aimed at reducing the burden of liver diseases, particularly among vulnerable populations. Substance use-related factors such as alcohol consumption, khat chewing, IV drug abuse, and others could predispose to the risk of having liver disease of viral or non-viral causes. In this study, alcohol consumption showed to increase the risk of liver disease by 3.14 times, and khat chewing was also associated with a 69% higher risk of liver disease. Furthermore, a study from Ethiopia indicated that the use of traditional herbal medicine could significantly increase the risk of liver disease by 14-fold. Similarly, another study indicated that alcohol drinking increased the risk of liver disease by 2.8 times (AOR = 2.80; p-value below 0.05). It is not surprising that alcohol is metabolized by the liver, and excess and habitual alcohol consumption result in liver toxicity and cirrhosis. But the current strategies to limit daily alcohol consumption are not sufficient to change people’s attitudes [ 43 ]. Another study from Spain showed that men were at increased risk of alcohol consumption and increased risk of liver disease [ 44 ]. It is also associated with the risk of obesity and alcoholic or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in the long run [ 14 , 24 ]. A trend study in the west showed that the prevalence of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease is increasing at alarming rates [ 45 ]. The other common substance use in the area is khat chewing, which is strongly linked to higher alcohol consumption and/or cigarette smoking, and exposure to toxic pesticides, which ultimately increase the risk of liver diseases [ 46 ]. Furthermore, consumption of alcohol was associated with chronic liver disease (AOR: 8.23; 95% CI: 3.76–12.70) and elevated liver enzymes [ 41 ]. Khat chewing and exposure to different pesticides and chemical could increase the risk of liver diseases. For instance, a study from Harar, Ethiopia, also depicted that khat chewers were 2.64 times more likely to develop liver disease as compared with non-chewers. Specifically, low khat exposed were 3.58 times more likely to have liver disease as compared with the non-chewers, with a dose response relationship between khat chewing and liver diseases. The higher and the more frequent consumption of khat could further aggravate the risk of CLD [ 32 ]. Beyond khat exposure, exposure to cigarettes, and other substances could be associated with a higher risk of CLD [ 47 ]. Moreover, the frequent application of pesticides to khat and other cereals could expose consumers for a long period of time, increasing the risk of CLD [ 46 ]. Moreover, history of hospitalization (AOR = 4.20 and history of intestinal parasite infection (AOR = 1.17) were positively associated with an increased risk of liver disease among adults. Helminths that infect the liver and hepatobiliary system include nematodes (roundworms), cestodes (tapeworms), and trematodes (flatworms or flukes). The majority of morbidity and mortality from these infestations is caused by the host immune response to the larvae or adult worm, resulting in liver diseases [ 48 ]. Schistosoma infection of the hepatobiliary system is a main cause of chronic liver diseases [ 49 ]. Hence, liver parasite infections, mainly protozoa and helminths, are common to cause liver disease. However, due to the asymptomatic nature of such an illness, high clinical suspicions coupled with targeted investigations are very important [ 50 , 51 ]. However, we used self-reported intestinal worm infections where we might not capture the actual occurrences precisely. Previous hospitalization might predispose them to an increased risk of exposure to viral and other causes of hepatitis. Furthermore, such clients might have prolonged exposure to toxic medications and other herbs, predisposing them to liver diseases. Beyond the above-mentioned factors, some traditional procedures conducted without proper infection preventions- tooth extraction, circumcision, uvulectomy, and others-might pose an increased risk for transmissible hepatitis virus infections [ 25 , 52 ]. A study from Ethiopia showed that dental extraction via traditional methods increased the risk of liver diseases by 3-folds (AOR = 2.9; p-value below 0.05) [ 33 ]. Despite not reaching to statistical significance, uvulectomy and dental exactions were associated with a higher odds of liver disease. However, due to the chronic nature of the disease, it may be difficult to trace their exposures precisely. Moreover, interventions targeting such risk factors could prevent future risks of liver diseases. On the contrary, we found that history of contact with jaundiced individuals did not predict the risk of having liver disease (COR = 3.58; 95% CI: 1.84–6.95), whereas the majority of the clients with liver disease did not have such exposures. These could indicate that the source of infection could not necessarily be symptomatic patients; rather, patients could significantly increase the risk during subclinical illness as well [ 20 ]. Understanding the long-term impact of risk factors like obesity, alcohol use, viral hepatitis, and genetics on chronic liver disease progression is crucial. Longitudinal studies can track these factors over time to identify high-risk individuals and evaluate the effectiveness of preventive strategies such as lifestyle changes, vaccinations, and early screenings in mitigating disease progression, leading to improved patient outcomes and reduced burden. Limitations of the study Although this study involved a rigorous laboratory and abdominal ultrasound examination to confirm the diagnosis of liver diseases, the findings of the current study should be interpreted in light of some shortcomings. For instance, this study is not meant to reflect the magnitude of liver disease occurrence among the general population and is still for those outpatient visitors. Thus, the findings of this study were to reflect the incidence of liver disease among clinically suspected liver disease cases. The cross-sectional design nature of the study could restrict the establishment of causal relationships and only allows for the identification of associations. Moreover, the reliance on self-reported substance use and history of intestinal worm infections could have introduced recall bias. The study's limited sample from a single health facility raises concerns about the generalizability of the results to the broader population. It is crucial to address to take this restricted sample which might impact the external validity of the study's conclusions and the extent to which they can be applied to other settings or populations. Some tests requested by reviewers are not available and some important variables like Human Immune Virus infection status were not ccaptured, which could limit the reliability and sensitivity of our diagnosis of liver disease. Except for such shortcomings, the findings of this study showed the occurrence of liver disease and its types using a more practical methodology, less frequently used so far. Conclusion and recommendations The magnitude of liver disease among adults with clinically presumptive signs and symptoms of liver disease was higher and could be more predictive of actual liver diseases. The majority of the clients had raised liver enzymes and prominent ultrasound findings. In general, CLD, viral hepatitis, and liver disease from multiple causes were common in the study area. The odds of developing liver disease could be predicted by sex, substance use (alcohol and khat consumption), history of hospitalization, and blood transfusion, which could be targeted in interventions addressing liver disease prevention and control strategies. Enhanced behavioral change communication through various approaches to minimize the risk factors for liver disease is needed through the health bureau, hospitals, and health extension workers. Thus, context-specific strategies are needed to address the potential knowledge gaps on the major risk factors of liver diseases, with a special emphasis on hepatitis virus infections, khat chewing, alcohol consumption, and others.
