Background Few studies have explored the relationship between the number of children and depression in older adults, especially across multiple countries. Methods In this Multi-National Cross-Sectional Analysis, we included the Health and Retirement Study (HRS, US), the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), and the Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI), encompassing a total of 20 countries. The number of children was based on self-reports from the participants. The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD) and the European Depression Scale (EURO-D) were used to assess depression. Logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between the number of children and depression, adjusting for potential confounders. A random-effects meta-regression model was used to examine differences in the relationship between the number of children and depression between Asian and Western countries. To explore potential non-linear relationships, restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were utilized, and threshold effect analyses were performed to validate. Additionally, subgroup analyses were stratified by age, sex, and marital status. Results A total of 110,015 participants from six datasets were included. The prevalence of depressive symptoms ranged from 10.7% in the UK to 48.8% in Poland. Childlessness was most common in ELSA (15.8%), followed by SHARE (9.6%) and HRS (8.4%), while lower proportions were observed in CLHLS (1.5%), KLoSA (2.3%), and LASI (3.1%). In Western countries, childlessness was not significantly associated with depression (HRS: OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.83–1.23; ELSA: OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.69–1.17; SHARE: OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.97–1.15). However, in Asian countries, it was linked to a higher risk (CLHLS: OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.29–3.12; KLoSA: OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.24–2.55; LASI: OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.19–1.49). A random-effects meta-analysis confirmed this regional difference (β = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.15–0.55; P &lt; 0.001). A non-linear association was observed between the number of children and depression in LASI and SHARE, with risk initially decreasing and then rising beyond a threshold. Subgroup analysis found a stronger association among ≤ 75 years individuals and men in KLoSA. In HRS, childlessness was associated with a higher risk of depression only among those living alone. Conclusions The relationship between the number of children and depression varies across countries. Individuals in Asian countries may be more prone to depression than those in Western nations when they do not have children. Aging is often accompanied by significant emotional changes [ 2 ]. Among older adults, depressive symptoms—such as persistent sadness, sleep disturbances, reduced social engagement, and even suicidal tendencies—are among the most common mental health challenges they face [ 3 ]. Studies estimate that up to one-third of older adults experience depressive symptoms, which tend to be more persistent and severe compared to those observed in younger individuals [ 4 ]. Among the many factors contributing to the development of depression, pregnancy, and parenthood stand out as particularly unique influences on mental health outcomes [ 5 ]. The intensification of population aging has led to a decline in the proportion of individuals of reproductive age, contributing to lower fertility rates and a rising childlessness rate. Consequently, an increasing number of individuals remain childless throughout their lifetime [ 6 , 7 ]. Beyond demographic shifts, parenthood itself presents a complex dynamic in shaping mental health in later life. On one hand, children can serve as a crucial source of emotional support, financial assistance, and caregiving during medical emergencies [ 8 , 9 ]. On the other hand, raising children entails financial burdens, caregiving responsibilities, and potential intergenerational conflicts, which may also contribute to psychological distress [ 10 , 11 ]. There has been extensive research on the relationship between the number of children and depression [ 5 , 12 – 19 ]. Despite the growing body of literature, most existing studies are limited to specific countries or regions, failing to capture the influence of cross-cultural differences in family structures, societal expectations, and support systems. This has given rise to some seemingly contradictory conclusions. For instance, a study on older adults in the UK found that having either one child or three or more children was linked to a higher risk of depression [ 16 ]. Another study on Chinese older adults found that childlessness significantly increases the risk of depression [ 18 ]. Meanwhile, a study focusing on older Korean adults identified a U-shaped relationship between the number of children and depression in later life, suggesting that both childlessness and having many children may contribute to elevated risks of depression [ 20 ]. To address these gaps, this study conducted a multi-national analysis using data from six large cross-sectional studies encompassing 20 countries across Asia, Europe, and North America. By examining the relationship between the number of children and depression in individuals aged 50 years and older, this study aims to provide a comprehensive, cross-cultural perspective on this association. Furthermore, we investigate whether these associations differ between Asian and Western countries, offering insights into how cultural norms, social structures, and family support systems may influence the relationship between parenthood and depression in later life. Given the rising prevalence of childlessness and its potential implications for mental health, a clearer understanding of these patterns could inform policies and interventions aimed at supporting the psychological well-being of aging populations worldwide. Methods Study population We included six cross-sectional studies: Wave 14 of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS, 2018) from the United States; Wave 8 of the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA, 2016–2017) from the United Kingdom; Wave 2 of the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE, 2006–2007); Wave 8 of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS, 2017–2019); Wave 7 of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA, 2018); and Wave 1 of the Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI, 2017–2021). Participants younger than 50 years were excluded, along with those missing data on depression status, number of children, or key covariates. After excluding these participants, a total of 12,926 participants from HRS, 5,027 from ELSA, 33,981 from SHARE, 7,437 from CLHLS, 5,931 from KLoSA, and 44,533 from LASI were included in the analysis. We utilized publicly available, de-identified data from the datasets. Since ethical approval was obtained for the original surveys, additional approval was not required for this study. Exposures The number of living children was determined through self-reports from the participants. According to the experience from other articles, participants were divided into three groups based on the number of children: 0 children, 1–3 children, and more than three children [ 13 ]. Additionally, participants were also categorized into a binary variable based on childlessness. Outcomes Depressive symptoms were assessed using standardized self-reported scales. In the six cross-sectional studies included in this research, the 8-item CES-D (CESD-8) was used in HRS and ELSA. The 10-item CES-D (CESD-10) was administered in CLHLS, LASI, and KLoSA. SHARE utilized 12-item Euro-D. These scales measured depressive symptoms based on self-reported responses to symptom-related questions. The EURO-D scale demonstrates good psychometric properties as an indicator of depressive symptomatology in older adults [ 21 ]. Its comparability with the CESD-8 scale was supported by a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.7603 (95% CI: 0.7522–0.7684) [ 22 ]. Additionally, factor analyses indicate that the shorter CES-D versions (CESD-8 and CESD-10) capture the same underlying dimensions as the original 20-item CES-D and sacrifice little precision, which further supports their comparability [ 23 ]. According to commonly recommended thresholds, a score of 3 or higher on the CESD-8, 10 or higher on the CESD-10, and 4 or higher on the Euro-D is typically used to indicate clinically relevant depressive symptoms [ 24 , 25 ]. Covariates We included the following covariates: age, educational level (less than high school, high school, more than high school), sex (male or female), body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, kg/m²), marital status (living alone/living with a partner), history of diabetes (yes or no, self-reported), history of hypertension (yes or no, self-reported), history of stroke (yes or no, self-reported), history of cancer (yes or no, self-reported), history of heart-related diseases (yes or no, self-reported), and household income(categorized into country-specific tertiles: Q1, Q2, Q3). Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, with continuous variables presented as medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs) and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages).To examine the association between the number of children and depressive symptoms, logistic regression models were employed, adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, education, income, BMI, and history of chronic diseases. A random-effects meta-analysis compared associations between Western (USA, UK, Europe) and Asian (China, Korea, India) populations. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were utilized to explore potential non-linear relationships. Additionally, threshold effect analyses were performed to validate these findings. Subgroup analyses were conducted, stratified by age, sex, marital status, and urban residence (urban vs. non-urban; not available in the ELSA cohort), with interaction terms tested using Wald tests. We conducted a sensitivity analysis by recategorizing the number of children into three groups: 0, 1–2, and more than 2 (&gt; 2). All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3), with statistical significance set at P &lt; 0.05 (two-tailed). Results Baseline characteristics of participants Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of participants across the six studies (HRS, ELSA, SHARE, CLHLS, KLoSA, and LASI). ELSA and SHARE had the lowest average number of children (2.0), while HRS, CLHLS, KLoSA, and LASI reported higher averages (3.0). Similarly, the mean age of participants varied, with CLHLS having the oldest cohort (82.0 years) and LASI the youngest (62.0 years). The prevalence of depressive symptoms ranged from 10.7% in the UK to 48.8% in Poland. Childlessness was most common in ELSA (15.8%), followed by SHARE (9.6%) and HRS (8.4%), while lower proportions were observed in Asian countries, including CLHLS (1.5%), KLoSA (2.3%), and LASI (3.1%). Marital status also varied, with more individuals living alone in CLHLS (47.9%) than in other studies. The mean number of children among individuals with and without depressive symptoms was 2.34 and 2.25 in SHARE; 3.17 and 3.07 in HRS; 2.13 and 2.08 in ELSA; 3.88 and 3.78 in LASI; 3.53 and 3.49 in CLHLS; and 2.98 and 2.75 in KLoSA, respectively. Association between the number of children and depression Table 2 presents the relationship between regional differences in the association between the number of children, childlessness, and depressive symptoms. In Western countries (USA, UK, and Europe), childlessness was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms, with odds ratios (ORs) of: HRS: 1.08 (95% CI: 0.90–1.29), ELSA: 0.88 (95% CI: 0.68–1.15), and SHARE: 0.94 (95% CI: 0.97–1.15). In contrast, in Asian countries (China, Korea, and India), childlessness was associated with a significantly higher risk of depressive symptoms: CLHLS: 1.98 (95% CI: 1.27–3.08), KLoSA: 1.77 (95% CI: 1.23–2.54), and LASI: 1.27 (95% CI: 1.14–1.42), all statistically significant. A random-effects meta-analysis further confirmed that childlessness in Asian countries was associated with a significantly higher risk of depressive symptoms compared to Western countries (β = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.15–0.69; p = 0.002). Restricted cubic spline and threshold effect analyses between the number of children and depression A non-linear association between the number of children and depression was observed across the SHARE, CLHLS, KLoSA, and LASI. The RCS curves showed an initial decrease in depression risk with an increasing number of children, followed by a subsequent increase (Supplemental Figs. 1–4). The threshold effect analyses consistently confirmed significant associations on both sides of the inflection point in the LASI and SHARE (Table 3 ). However, in the CLHLS and KLoSA, significant associations were observed below the inflection point, while no significant associations were found above it (Table 3 ). Subgroup analysis of childlessness and depression Subgroup analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between childlessness and depressive symptoms across age, sex, and marital status (Table 4 ). Age-stratified analysis revealed a stronger association among younger participants (≤ 75 years) in KLoSA (OR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.38–3.11, P for interaction = 0.025). Sex-stratified analysis indicated that the association was more pronounced among men in KLoSA (OR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.46–4.23, P for interaction = 0.030). Marital status-stratified analysis showed that in HRS, childlessness was associated with a higher risk of depression only among those living alone (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.08–2.06, P for interaction = 0.039). Sensitivity analysis The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Table 5 . A comparison with the primary analysis (Table 2 ) reveals that redefining the child number categories did not substantially alter the outcomes. For the SHARE cohort, we observed a slight strengthening of the association for the 1–2 children group ( p = 0.045) compared to the 1–3 children group in the primary analysis ( p = 0.055). This shift is consistent with the U-shaped relationship identified by our RCS analysis and threshold Effect Analysis. Discussion We found a regional difference in the relationship between the number of children, childlessness, and depression. In Western countries (USA, UK, Europe), childlessness was not significantly associated with depression, whereas in Asian countries (China, Korea, India), it was linked to a higher risk of depression. A random-effects meta-analysis further confirmed this regional disparity. Additionally, in India and Europe, the relationship between the number of children and depression followed a U-shaped pattern. In contrast, in the UK and the USA, the number of children was not significantly associated with depression. Compared to previous studies, our study covers multiple countries across different cultures, providing a more comprehensive perspective. The conflicting conclusions in previous studies may be due to the lack of consideration of cultural and economic differences across regions. The stronger association between childlessness and depression in Asian countries may be attributed to cultural and socioeconomic factors. Culturally, family structures are traditionally intergenerational, and having children is often viewed as a fundamental life expectation. The absence of children can result in increased social stigma, reduced social support, and a heightened sense of isolation [ 26 , 27 ]. In contrast, Western societies emphasize personal autonomy and voluntary childlessness is more socially accepted [ 28 ]. As shown in Table 1 , childlessness was most common in ELSA at 15.8%, followed by SHARE at 9.6% and HRS at 8.4%. In comparison, lower proportions were observed in Asian countries, including CLHLS at 1.5%, KLoSA at 2.3%, and LASI at 3.1%. Economic factors also play a crucial role. In these countries, social security systems are less developed than those in Western nations, making older adults more dependent on their children for daily living and healthcare support [ 29 – 31 ]. In this context, the absence of children can contribute to economic insecurity and increase the risk of depression. Of course, we cannot overlook that this association is primarily observed among the current generation of older adults. In recent years, younger generations’ perspectives may have shifted, especially in East Asia, where attitudes toward childbearing and intergenerational support have gradually evolved. The U-shaped relationship between the number of children and depression observed in India and Europe suggests that while having no or few children may increase the risk of depression due to social and financial insecurity, having a larger number of children may also elevate the risk. Older adults with fewer children may lack emotional and practical support, leading to loneliness and vulnerability. In contrast, a larger number of children can introduce financial and caregiving pressures despite offering potential support. Previous studies have highlighted that excessive caregiving responsibilities can negatively impact mental health, particularly among older adults who may already be experiencing physical decline [ 32 ]. Additionally, having multiple children can impose further burdens on families. Evidence provided by Angrist suggests that numerous children have a significant negative impact on marital stability, increase the risk of poverty, and make mothers more susceptible to hypertension and obesity [ 33 ]. Subgroup analyses in Korea revealed that the association between childlessness and depression was more pronounced among men and individuals under the age of 75. This may be attributed to traditional gender roles, which place a strong emphasis on men’s responsibility to continue the family lineage and provide for future generations [ 34 , 35 ], potentially leading to greater psychological distress when they remain childless. Additionally, older individuals may have gradually adapted to societal expectations regarding parenthood over time, mitigating the psychological impact of childlessness. A study has also indicated that childless older adults generally have fewer sources of support, but as they reach more advanced ages, this disadvantage tends to diminish [ 36 ]. These findings highlight the influence of gender and generational shifts in societal norms on mental health, particularly in cultures where family lineage continues to hold significant importance. Although childlessness was not significantly associated with depression in the overall HRS dataset, further analysis revealed that among individuals living alone, childlessness was linked to a higher risk of depressive symptoms. Research has consistently shown that loneliness is strongly associated with a higher risk of depression, in particular in older adults [ 37 , 38 ]. The absence of a spouse and children may exacerbate feelings of loneliness, as these close familial relationships often serve as primary sources of emotional and social support [ 39 ]. Without these connections, individuals may have fewer opportunities for meaningful interactions, leading to increased social isolation [ 40 ]. Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the proportion of childless individuals in China and Korea was relatively low, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect more nuanced associations. Future studies with larger sample sizes in these populations are needed to confirm our findings. Second, while we analyzed SHARE as a single dataset, we did not account for potential differences between Asian and Western European countries. Social norms, government support systems, and economic conditions vary significantly across Europe, which could influence the relationship between childlessness, the number of children, and depression [ 5 ]. More granular analyses are needed to explore these regional disparities within Europe. Third, different harmonized depression scales were used across countries (CESD-8, CESD-10, and Euro-D), which—despite being validated and comparable—may still differ in their sensitivity and specificity. This could affect the comparability of depression prevalence and associations across countries. Finally, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, we did not account for the possibility of reverse causality—individuals with depression may be more likely to have fewer children or remain childless [ 14 , 19 ]. This potential bidirectional relationship warrants further investigation. Longitudinal studies incorporating more detailed psychosocial data will be essential for understanding childlessness’s long-term mental health effects in different cultural contexts. Conclusion This study highlights significant regional differences in the relationship between childlessness and depression in older adults, with childless elderly individuals in Asian countries facing a higher risk of depression. This may be due to the reliance on children for elderly care in traditional Asian cultures, societal expectations of family continuity, and relatively weaker social security systems. As declining birth rates and population aging accelerate, the weakening of family support structures may further isolate childless older adults, increasing their vulnerability to mental health issues. Strengthening social support systems, encouraging elderly participation in social activities, and improving access to mental health services are essential in mitigating these risks. Future research should adopt a multicultural perspective to explore the long-term impact of changing family structures and assess effective support measures to enhance elderly well-being.
