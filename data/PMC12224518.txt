Background Although ordering food online has become a leading lifestyle factor among the Chinese urban population, its health impact is understudied. This study aims to examine this aspect, focusing on online takeaway food impacts related to foodborne illness, nutrition, and suboptimal health (i.e., an intermediate status between being healthy and diagnosed with a disease) on consumers. Methods A snowball sampling method was adopted, targeting urban consumers aged 18–59 across mainland China by posing an e-questionnaire survey. Information collected includes demographic data, foodborne illness occurrence, nutrition and suboptimal health impacts when ordering online takeaway foods. The questionnaire was adapted from the Diet Quality Questionnaire and Sub-Health Measurement Scale version 1.0. Descriptive statistical methods, Mann‒Whitney test, and Spearman’s correlation test were employed to analyse the data. Results A total of 610 questionnaires were included. Consumers ordering online takeaway foods at least once per week (hereon termed as ‘frequent consumers’) often experienced fake reviews (50.71%), hygiene issues (54.03%) and foodborne illness (58.29% reporting lower gastrointestinal symptoms). Frequent consumers had lower diet quality, with higher Global Dietary Recommendation (GDR)-Limit scores (Mann-Whitney, p = 0.0011), lower GDR-Healthy ( p = 0.0033) and overall GDR scores ( p &lt; 0.0001) than infrequent consumers. Additionally, the frequency of online takeaway food consumption was negatively associated with overall GDR scores ( p = 0.0392). After adjusting for demographic characteristics, physical subhealth scores were lower among frequent consumers ( p = 0.0157), with differences in GDR-Limit ( p = 0.0087), GDR-Healthy ( p = 0.0221) and overall GDR scores ( p &lt; 0.0001) remaining; after further adjusting for lifestyle factors, the differences in GDR-Healthy and overall GDR scores persisted. While the differences in the aforementioned scores were not significant within the medical subgroup after adjustment. Conclusions This study suggests that online takeaway food consumption in urban China is a significant lifestyle, posing a risk of foodborne illness and associating with poorer diet quality and physical health status to a certain extent. Monitoring of food safety and fake review in food delivery should be strengthened, along with public education on nutrition. Further clinical research should evaluate the association between online takeaway food consumption and health risks. Meituan Waimai and Ele.me are the two most popular food delivery platforms in China, accounting for over 90% of the market [ 1 ]. Boosted by the three-year COVID-19 pandemic [ 2 , 3 ], China is expected to lead global online food delivery revenue by 2023, according to Statista [ 4 , 5 ]. By the end of 2023, 49.9% of Chinese internet users had used online food delivery services, totaling over 500 million users [ 6 ]. Consequently, online takeaway food consumption has become a crucial lifestyle factor for medical researchers in China. The online takeaway food model differs from offline takeaway or dine-in-options because consumers have less access to information about the food preparation environment, delivery process, and the raw ingredients. As the preparation environment and raw ingredients are invisible to online consumers, the risk of hygiene deficiencies and related foodborne illnesses could be high. Long delivery time and improper storage can also contribute to these risks [ 7 , 8 ]. Additionally, the prevalence of fake reviews hinders consumers from identifying substandard food options [ 9 ]. Studies conducted in Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou reported multiple outbreaks of clustered infectious gastroenteritis caused by Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, and norovirus, linked to online food delivery services [ 10 – 12 ]. These outbreaks highlight an emerging channel of foodborne illness. However, to our knowledge, no studies have investigated the prevalence of fake reviews, hygiene issues and foodborne illnesses associated with online takeaway food based on consumer reports. This gap is significant, given that “nausea” and “vomiting” are frequently cited as negative experiences on social media [ 13 ]. Beyond acute health risks, chronic health risks should also be a concern with online takeaway food consumption. Numerous studies from western countries have shown that takeaway food consumption is associated with chronic health impacts, including a higher Body Mass Index (BMI) and other risk factors for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, such as elevated blood lipids and insulin resistance. This is because takeaway food is often deficient in vitamins and minerals and high in salt, fat, and calories [ 14 , 15 ]. These studies mainly focus on fast food, which is mostly consumed as takeaway food in Western countries. However, the variety of takeaway food offered by the food delivery industry in China is much broader. Therefore, it is intriguing to investigate whether the association of online takeaway food consumption with nutritional health differs across the Chinese population. Nutritional health is defined by the World Health Organization as “a diversified, balanced and healthy diet to prevent malnutrition all its forms as well as a range of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) and conditions” [ 16 ]. Unfortunately, studies on this topic are scarce and deserves more attention. Additionally, although a few studies have analysed the nutritional composition of popular Chinese online takeaway foods [ 17 , 18 ], none have evaluated the overall nutritional impact on consumers by analyzing their nutritional scores. The use of disposable tableware is another factor contributing to chronic health risks that associated with online takeaway food consumption. A study analyzing 146 takeaway food samples packaged with plastic tableware in China found that individuals who order takeaway food 1–2 times per week may intake approximately 170–638 microplastic particles [ 19 ]. Preclinical studies have shown that accumulated microplastics in the human body induce oxidative stress and inflammation, which are associated with neurodegenerative diseases, autoimmune disease, and neoplasia [ 20 ]. Furthermore, studies from China have found that carcinogenic plasticizers like phthalates and their byproduct chloropropanol, as well as heavy metals (cadmium, lead, antimony, nickel, and cobalt), can migrate from plastic and plastic-coated paper food packaging into the human body through online takeaway food consumption, thereby increasing cancer risk with repeated exposure [ 21 – 23 ]. Despite the numerous chronic health risks associated with online takeaway food consumption, there is still very limited research evaluating the suboptimal health level of consumers. Suboptimal health is defined as a physical state between health and disease and is an important risk marker for chronic diseases [ 24 ]. Considering the above, this study, for the first time, elucidates the association between online takeaway food consumption and overall health in China from three aspects: foodborne illness, nutritional health, and suboptimal health level based on consumer reports. This study fills the current research gaps and provides both consumers and researchers with a more comprehensive understanding of the health impacts associated with online takeaway food consumption. Methods Study design, sampling, ethics statement This study was a cross-sectional survey investigating health impacts associated with online takeaway food consumption among young and middle-aged adults (18–59 years old) in cities across mainland China. The questionnaire was administered electronically via Wenjuanxing, a widely recognized and secure platform ( https://www.wjx.cn/app/survey.aspx ) and distributed through WeChat mobile application groups using a snowball sampling approach [ 25 ]. The e-questionnaire was delivered between March and April 2023. Participation was open to all. The consent form was displayed on the first page of the e-questionnaire and only consented respondents could access to the formal questionnaire. Respondents received no monetary or other compensation from research team. A total of 699 questionnaires were collected. Completed questionnaires were deemed invalid and excluded from data interpretation if they had response times less than 300 s, mismatched IP addresses and reported residence, respondents from rural areas or foreign countries, respondents outside the age range of 18–59 years, or if they contained severe logical inconsistencies or repeated submissions. The valid response rate was 87.26%. Questionnaire development and data interpretation The questionnaire (details are provided in the additional information ) comprises four parts. The first part gathered demographic information and lifestyle factors, including gender, age, height, weight, education level, professional field, disposable income range, city size, and current habits such as smoking, alcohol drinking, late bedtime, and exercise. Respondents provided their height and weight in fill-in-the-blank questions. The graded answering options for disposable income were based on the latest information provided by the National Bureau of Statistics of China [ 26 ]. The first-tier cities and new first-tier cities in the city size were listed according to the latest information given in the report of the Urban Institute [ 27 ]. “Late bedtime” referred to going to bed after midnight at least once a week in the data interpretation, while “exercise” meant at least 30 min of sport per week regularly. BMI (Body Mass Index, kg/m 2 ) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. The second part of the questionnaire focused on the online takeaway food consumption habits (meals only, excluding desserts and milk tea, the same applies for subsequent sections), experiences with fake reviews (takeaway food suppliers offering small monetary incentives as refunds for five-stars reviews on food delivery platforms), hygiene issues identified by consumers, and the occurrence of upper (i.e., nausea, vomiting, and upper abdominal pain) and lower gastrointestinal (GI) (i.e., diarrhea) symptoms by consuming online takeaway food. “Frequent takeaway food consumption” was defined as ordering takeaway food at least once per week on average (“Frequent consumers”), referring to respondents who selected option C or D for Question 22 and simultaneously selected option B, C, D, or E for Question 23 (see the attached questionnaire for details). In contrast, “Infrequent takeaway food consumption” was defined as ordering less frequently or rarely (“Infrequent consumers”), encompassing all remaining respondents. Information on the consumption of milk tea or desserts (including both online and offline) was also collected in this section, with all subsequent descriptions referring to consumption of at least once per week. The third part was the Chinese version of the Diet Quality Questionnaire (DQQ), a standardized global dietary tool developed by Herforth et al. of the Global Diet Quality Project [ 28 , 29 ]. The DQQ data were assessed using the Global Dietary Recommendations (GDR) scores: GDR-Limit (0–9), GDR-Healthy (0–9), and the overall GDR score (calculated using the formula: GDR-Healthy - GDR-Limit + 9, ranging from 0 to 18), as per the DQQ Indicator Guide 2023 [ 30 ]. In large US and Brazilian populations (ages 15+), the higher GDR-Limit scores predict unhealthy food (e.g., foods high in sugar, fat, and salt, processed meats, red meat, etc.), correlating with the Healthy Diet Indicator 2020 (HDI-2020) and its components, aligned with WHO guidelines. The GDR-Healthy and overall GDR scores are positively associated with the intake of health-protective foods (e.g., fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, whole grains, dietary sources of fiber) and outperform the Food Group Diversity Score (FGDS) [ 31 ]. The scores’ validity has also been confirmed in a Vietnamese female population (aged 15+) through statistical comparison with the FGDS [ 32 ]. The fourth part utilized the Sub-Health Measurement Scale version 1.0 (SHMS v1.0), comprising 39 questions, to assess respondents’ general health status over the past four weeks across three dimensions: physical, mental, and social health. For example, physical dimension questions included: “Do you have difficulty climbing 3–5 flights of stairs?” and “Do you have difficulty walking 1500 meters?”. The SHMS v1.0 employs a 5-point scale with both positive and negative items. Raw scores are converted to a 100-point scale for statistical analysis using the formula: (raw score - theoretical minimum score) / (theoretical maximum score - theoretical minimum score) * 100. Higher scores indicate better health in each dimension [ 33 , 34 ]. The first and second parts are self-developed and refined through pilot testing ( n = 20). In the final version, these two sections showing an average split-half reliability of 0.93 and a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value of 0.94 based on the main survey data. The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were 0.64 for questions related to online takeaway food consumption behavior in the second part, and 0.75 for lifestyle factors in the first part, indicating satisfactory convergent validity. The third and fourth parts used validated scales originally in Chinese. For third part, this questionnaire has been validated in China using large-scale data [ 35 ]. Whereas, fourth part was designed by Chinese researchers, the SHMS v1.0 has demonstrated strong validity and reliability in the Chinese population, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients exceeding 0.9 for internal consistency, test-retest reliability close to 0.7, and correlation coefficients near 0.8 with the well-recognized 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) [ 33 , 34 , 36 , 37 ]. Statistics GraphPad Prism, version 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc. San Diego, CA, USA), Microsoft Excel, version 16 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and R language, version 4.4.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) were used for statistical calculation and plotting. The split-half reliability was calculated using the ‘splitHalf’ function from {psych} package, the KMO value was determined using the ‘KMO’ function from {psych} package, and AVE value was obtained using {semTools} package in R. The graded answers in the first and second parts were sequentially numbered (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, etc.) for the statistical analysis, and binary variables were coded as 0 or 1. Counting data was described by the number of cases (percentage). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and the Mann‒Whitney Test was used for comparison between groups. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used to calculate the correlations. P values were calculated as two-tailed, and those below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To minimize the impact of potential confounding factors, we matched age, gender, education level, city size, disposable income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, bedtime habits and exercise habits between frequent consumers and infrequent online takeaway food consumers for comparison. Matching was performed using the ‘MatchBalance’ function from the {Matching} package in R. Results Characteristics of study participants A total of 610 valid questionnaires were included in the data analysis. Participants were from 32 provinces and municipalities in mainland China (excluding Qinghai), aged 18–59 years old, with BMI ranging from 15 to 50 kg/m2. The detailed population characteristics are shown in Table 1 . General characteristics of online takeaway food consumption A total of 34.59% ( n = 211) of participants ( n = 610) ordered online takeaway food at least once per week currently and were classified as frequent consumers. The remaining 65.41% were classified as infrequent consumers (Fig. 1 A). The most popular food choices were Asian cuisine and Western fast food, selected by 79.62% and 63.98% of frequent consumers ( n = 211), respectively (Fig. 1 B). Among frequent consumers ( n = 211), 48.34% ordered takeaway food at least 3 times per week (Fig. 1 C) and 60.66% ordered takeaway food for longer than one year (Fig. 1 D). Frequent consumers were younger than infrequent consumers (Spearman’s correlation test, p &lt; 0.0001, r = -0.3041), and more likely to live in larger city ( p = 0.0001, r = -0.1546). There were no significant differences in gender ( p = 0.0866), education level ( p = 0.3164), or disposable income ( p = 0.0783). Frequent consumers were also more likely to smoke (Spearman’s correlation test, p = 0.0039, r = 0.1166), drink alcohol ( p = 0.0012, r = 0.1306), consume milk tea or desserts ( p &lt; 0.0001, r = 0.3698) and stay up past midnight ( p &lt; 0.0001, r = 0.1951) at least once per week. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups regarding exercise habits ( p = 0.4392). Fake review, hygiene situation of online takeaway food and associated gastrointestinal symptoms Among the frequent consumers ( n = 211), 50.71% had experienced fake reviews (Fig. 2 A), and 54.03% encountered hygiene problems with online takeaway food (Fig. 2 C). Compared to the group of infrequent consumers, frequent consumers reported an increased propensity to encounter fake reviews (Mann‒Whitney , p &lt; 0.0001; Fig. 2 A) and hygiene issues ( p &lt; 0.0001; Fig. 2 C) from online takeaway food consumption; and higher occurrences of lower gastrointestinal symptoms ( p &lt; 0.0001; Fig. 2 D) or upper ( p = 0.0091). The hygiene problems reported by all respondents who encountered such issues ( n = 246, 40.33% of 610) included spoiled takeaway food (33.98%), utensil cleaning supplies structure mixed in (32.32%), contamination of insects such as flies and cockroaches (12.71%), sand (11.05%) and hair (3.87%), listed in descending order of frequency (Fig. 2 B). A sub-analysis on frequent consumers revealed that 36.49% ( n = 77) of them experienced upper GI symptoms from online takeaway food consumption, and 58.29% ( n = 123) experienced lower GI symptoms (Fig. 2 D). Of those who experienced lower GI symptoms ( n = 123), 8.94% ( n = 11) sought medical help at healthcare institutions. Within the group of frequent consumers ( n = 211), the frequency of experiencing lower (Spearman’s correlation test, p = 0.0001, r = 0.2593) and upper ( p = 0.0171, r = 0.1640) gastrointestinal symptoms from online takeaway food consumption was positively associated with the frequency of online takeaway food consumption. Higher consumption frequency within this group also increased the likelihood of encountering fake reviews (Spearman’s correlation test, p = 0.0013, r = 0.2195) and hygiene issues ( p = 0.0025, r = 0.2074). Dietary health, BMI and subhealth level The GDR-Limit scores were significantly higher for frequent consumers compared to infrequent consumers (Mann‒Whitney, p = 0.0011), while the GDR-Healthy scores and overall GDR scores were significantly lower among frequent consumers ( p = 0.0033 and p &lt; 0.0001). Infrequent consumers had higher BMI (kg/m 2 ) (Mann‒Whitney, p = 0.0118). There were no significant differences between the two groups in the physical subhealth (Mann‒Whitney, p = 0.2183), mental subhealth ( p = 0.5467), social subhealth ( p = 0.8982), or total subhealth scores ( p = 0.4796). Moreover, the frequency of online takeaway food consumption was negatively associated with the overall GDR score (Spearman’s correlation test, p = 0.0392, r = -0.1421). After matching for age, gender, city size, education level, disposable income ( n = 392), frequent consumers ( n = 196) still exhibited significantly higher GDR-Limit scores; as well as significantly lower GDR-Healthy and overall GDR scores, and physical subhealth scores compared to infrequent consumers ( n = 196)(Mann‒Whitney). No significant differences were found in BMI (kg/m 2 ) or in the other subhealth scores (Model 1, Table 2 ). After further adjustments for smoking status, alcohol consumption, bedtime habits and exercise habits (Model 2, Table 2 ), the differences in GDR-Limit scores and physical subhealth scores were not observed, whereas differences in GDR-Healthy scores and overall GDR scores remained significant (Mann‒Whitney). Subgroup analysis: participants with a medical background Of the overall valid sample ( n = 610), 257 participants (42.13%) had a medical background, representing the largest subgroup (for a detailed disciplinary breakdown of the overall valid sample and demographic characteristics of medical subgroup, see additional information ). Among them, 27.63% ( n = 71) were classified as frequent consumers; of these, 46.48% ( n = 33) reported ordering takeaway food more than three times per week, and 67.60% ( n = 48) ordered takeaway food for over one year. Asian cuisine (80.28%) and Western fast food (49.30%) are respectively the most popular takeaway food choices. Frequent consumers were younger than infrequent consumers (Spearman’s correlation test, p &lt; 0.0001, r = -0.3742), had a higher education level ( p = 0.0465, r = 0.1243), and were more likely to live in larger cities ( p = 0.0002, r = -0.2313). They were also more prone to consume milk tea or desserts ( p &lt; 0.0001, r = 0.2505) or staying up past midnight at least once per week ( p = 0.0010, r = 0.2045). However, there were no significant differences between the groups in terms of alcohol consumption ( p = 0.8526), smoking status ( p = 0.3637) or exercise habits ( p = 0.0673). Infrequent consumers exhibited significantly higher GDR-Healthy (Mann‒Whitney, p = 0.0009) and overall GDR ( p &lt; 0.0001) scores compared to frequent consumers, while the GDR-Limit scores ( p = 0.2445) and BMI (kg/m 2 ) ( p = 0.0861) did not differ between the groups. After adjusting for demographic characteristics ( n = 122), none of these four measures differed significantly (Mann‒Whitney, in the order mentioned above: p = 0.4431, p = 0.2229, p = 0.7850 and p = 0.5377). However, the difference in social subhealth scores became significant after matching (Mann‒Whitney, p = 0.0317), whereas no significant differences were found in physical (Mann‒Whitney, p = 0.4012), mental ( p = 0.1162), social ( p = 0.0526), or total subhealth scores ( p = 0.0936) before matching. Discussion This study explored the association of online takeaway food consumption with health among young and middle-aged urban populations in mainland China, focusing on three aspects: foodborne illness as an acute health risk, nutritional health as a metabolic chronic health risk, and suboptimal health as an indicator of overall chronic health risk. Our results indicated that fake reviews, hygiene issues and foodborne illness associated with online takeaway food were common. Additionally, frequent online takeaway food consumers were more likely to exhibit lower diet quality and physical subhealth levels, while the subgroup analysis of consumers with a medical background showed no evidence of these impacts after adjustment for demographic characteristics. Our study found that more than half of frequent consumers experienced GI disorders from online takeaway food: 36.49% reported upper GI symptoms and 58.29% reported lower GI symptoms, both positively associated with consumption frequency. Additionally, the group of frequent consumers also reported higher occurrences of lower and higher gastrointestinal symptoms from online takeaway food compared to the group of infrequent consumers. These findings suggest that foodborne illness linked to online takeaway food is relatively common in China. A separate study of young Chinese individuals aged 18–30 found that nearly 50% reported GI symptoms after consuming online takeaway food, supporting our results [ 18 ]. Although numerous consumers mentioned above have encountered foodborne illnesses associated with takeaway food, only 8.94% of frequent consumers who experienced lower GI symptoms in this study sought medical help at healthcare institutions, suggesting that the true incidence may be underestimated by both the public health system and food hygiene and safety supervision authorities. This may be due to the mild and self-limiting nature of most foodborne gastroenteritis cases, with people typically seeking medical care only for severe symptoms like bloody diarrhea lasting longer than three days [ 38 ]. Additionally, previous studies on foodborne illness in China have not differentiated between online and offline takeaway food, nor have they distinguished illness occurring at home from illness caused by homemade food [ 39 , 40 ]. Future research should address these gaps, as emerging studies indicate that food delivery is a growing source of foodborne illness, capable of spreading across wide geographic areas in a short time [ 10 – 12 ]. Regulations have been issued by the Chinese government requiring food to be properly packaged and sealed during delivery to help consumers identify potential contamination and emphasizing the regulation implementation of food preparation processes to minimize foodborne illness associated with online takeaway food [ 8 ]. However, due to low entry requirements and supervision challenges, many “black workshops” without physical stores still operate on Chinese delivery food platforms, often with poor hygiene standards [ 9 , 41 ]. This study showed that a higher proportion of frequent consumers reported experiencing hygiene problems from online takeaway food. Over half of frequent consumers have encountered hygiene problems, including spoiled food and physical contamination like flies and cockroaches, revealing persistent poor hygiene situations in the food delivery industry. Online reviews are the primary reference for consumers choosing delivery food, but the prevalence of fake reviews (over 50% in this study among frequent consumers) hinders the identification of substandard vendors, increasing the risk of foodborne illness [ 9 ]. Therefore, China should further strengthen the regulation of business practices and hygiene safety in the food delivery industry. In addition to acute health impacts, frequent online takeaway food consumers showed higher GDR-Limit scores and lower GDR-Healthy and overall GDR scores than infrequent consumers. Moreover, the frequency of online takeaway food consumption was found to be negatively associated with overall GDR scores. After matching for demographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, disposable income, city size), the differences remained consistent. These results indicate that online takeaway food consumption is independently associated with poor diet quality. This study also showed that Asian cuisine and Western fast food were the two most popular choices, while healthier options like fruits and salad were less ordered. A study conducted in Chengdu, a new first-tier city in China, revealed that Asian takeaway food, in pursuit of better taste and higher sales, often exceeds recommended levels of energy, salt, fat, and carbohydrates while being deficient in vegetables, fruits, and whole grains [ 17 ]. A large-scale study among young Chinese individuals found that more than half of the takeaway foods ordered by them did not meet the nutritional quality recommendations [ 18 ]. The study by Dai et al. [ 18 ] also showed that nutritional health is often considered less important than taste, price, and delivery speed when ordering takeaway food. Collectively, these results from previous research aligned with our findings, demonstrating the concerning dietary trends associated with online takeaway food consumption. After further adjusting for lifestyle factors, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, bedtime habits, and exercise habits—noting that smoking, alcohol consumption, and late bedtime are associated with online takeaway food consumption in this study—the difference in GDR-Limit scores was no longer significant between frequent and infrequent consumers. Notably, previous studies have shown that smoking and alcohol consumption correlate with preferences for high-sugar/high-fat foods, all of which activate the central reward system [ 42 – 44 ]. Within the subgroup of respondents with a medical background, the difference in GDR-Limit scores was not significant even before adjusting for demographic characteristics. This indicates that dietary choices among online takeaway food consumers may be considerably shaped by intrinsic factors; in this case, their medical education may predispose them to avoid high-sugar/high-fat foods promoted by delivery food platforms—in our study, the proportion of the medical subgroup ordering Western fast food as takeaway food (49.30%) was lower than that of the overall sample (63.98%). A similar phenomenon was observed by Jiang et al. [ 45 ] among Chinese medical students. This underscores the critical need for enhanced public education on nutrition and health. Moreover, frequent consumers from the medical subgroup tended not smoke or consume alcohol more, which also aligns with the insignificant difference in GDR-Limit scores. Interestingly, though frequent consumers in the overall sample had higher GDR-Limit scores than infrequent consumers, their BMI were not higher, even after matching for demographic characteristics. The study of Jiang et al., a cross-sectional study among Chinese college students found that takeaway food consumption was significantly associated with a preference for high- sugar/high-fat food and increased BMI [ 45 ]. However, their study [ 45 ] did not exclude dessert and beverage, which could account for differences in our results. Consistent with our findings, two cross-sectional studies among adults in rural Australia and Iranian university students found no association between takeaway or fast food consumption and BMI, nor was the consumption frequency associated with BMI, as the poor dietary habits can spill over into food choices for cooking at home. However a significant association between takeaway food consumption and abdominal obesity has been demonstrated [ 46 , 47 ]. A recent review highlighted that BMI is limited in indicating obesity and is insufficient for assessing and managing health risks associated with obesity [ 48 ]. Therefore, further research is needed to determine whether abdominal obesity is associated with online takeaway food consumption among Chinese adults. After exploring the foodborne illness and nutritional health impact, we now shift our focus to the overall health status of online takeaway food consumers. Following the adjustment for demographic characteristics, the previously non-significant physical subhealth score emerged as significantly lower among frequent consumers compared to infrequent consumers within the overall sample, suggesting that frequent consumers demonstrate a deteriorated physical health status relative to their infrequent counterparts. A study among adults from UK also demonstrated a significant association between takeaway food consumption and poorer health status [ 49 ]. However, after further matching for lifestyle factors, this difference dissipated, potentially indicating that the association of smoking and alcohol consumption has relatively greater impacts on health status from a cross-sectional perspective. In line with this, within the medical subgroup, frequent consumers did not smoke or drink alcohol more, and there was also no significant difference in physical subhealth scores after adjusting for demographic characteristics. Nonetheless, given that delivery food platform facilitates the accessibility of high-sugar/high-fat foods while failing to promote dietary diversity—as evidenced by the fact that, after adjustment, GDR-Healthy scores and overall GDR scores among frequent consumers were not higher in the medical subgroup and were even lower in the overall sample compared to infrequent consumers—the impact of online takeaway food consumption on health continues to require prospective attention. Furthermore, previous retrospective studies have linked the use of plastic food containers, common in takeaway food packaging, with polycystic ovarian syndrome in Chinese women as well as congenital heart disease [ 50 , 51 ]. Therefore, further clinical studies, particularly prospective studies, are crucial for a better understanding of the association between online takeaway food consumption and chronic diseases or suboptimal health status in the future. Ultimately, it is important to note several limitations of this study. As a cross-sectional study with a small sample size, and the use of the snowball sampling method, which may introduce population bias—especially since over one-third of the respondents have a medical background. One needs to be cautious when interpreting and generalising the findings. Additionally, the small sample size and even smaller subgroup samples hindered a more precise adjustment for lifestyle factors based on their frequency and duration, potentially affecting the results. Furthermore, unconsidered confounding factors may have influenced the findings, particularly in relation to BMI and Subhealth scores. The reliance on self-reported questionnaires could also introduce biases due to recall inaccuracies. Finally, since the main subject we wanted to study is takeaway meals— a choice many urban residents are forced to make due to the pressures and fast pace of life, we separated milk tea and dessert consumption in the study design. As observed in this study, frequent consumers tended to have higher milk tea or dessert intake, both in the overall sample and in the medical subgroup. Milk tea consumption is very popular and represents a growing source of sugar intake in China; however, it is not included as an item in the GDR-Limit score. This omission may warrant consideration in future revisions. Conclusion This study found that online takeaway food consumption is prevalent in China, especially among young people and in larger cities, with Asian cuisine and western fast food being the most popular choice. Fake reviews, hygiene issues, and foodborne illness are common in online takeaway food consumption. Additionally, frequent consumers demonstrated poorer diet quality and physical health status to some extent, but this association may be influenced by coexisting lifestyle factors and vary among different populations, as no persistent differences were observed in the subgroup with a medical background. These findings indicate a need for enhanced supervision of commercial fraud and food safety in the delivery food industry, improved public education on food nutrition, and further studies. This is the first study to examine online takeaway food consumption in China from multiple key perspectives based on consumer reports, providing a deeper understanding for policymakers to guide improvements in population nutrition and health in the future.
