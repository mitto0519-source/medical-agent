ABSTRACT Aim To explore Taiwanese adolescents' experiences with asthma. Design Exploratory qualitative study using semi‐structured face‐to‐face interviews, framed within Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory. Methods Data were collected until saturation was reached. The interviews focused on adolescents who experienced asthma during their developmental stages. Data were managed using NVivo V12 program. Data Sources This study was conducted at an outpatient paediatric clinic located within a tertiary teaching hospital (anonymised for review) in Taiwan. Results Seventeen Taiwanese adolescents with asthma were interviewed, resulting in the emergence of eight main themes: ‘asthma exacerbation and its impacts’, ‘maintaining physical comfort’, ‘increased compliance with medication or treatment regimes’, ‘peer interactions’, ‘available resources at school’, ‘uncertainty’, ‘changes in social participation to manage symptoms’, and ‘conflicts in asthma care across different generations of family caregivers’. Conclusion Taiwanese adolescents with asthma experience regular disruptions to their social participation and school attendance. As a result, maintaining peer relationships may be challenging. Implications for the Profession and/or Patient Care Understanding the experiences of adolescents with asthma provides insights in optimising asthma care at home and school settings. Our findings highlight the importance of enabling teachers to respond appropriately to the needs of adolescents with asthma. A lack of understanding among teachers can aggravate adolescents' fear of embarrassment when using an inhaler in the presence of their peers. This may lead to reduced social interactions and feelings of isolation. To create a supportive environment, it is crucial to equip teachers and peers to recognise and respond to asthma symptoms. Impact This study addressed the lack of understanding of the developmental needs of Taiwanese adolescents with asthma. Adolescents' home and school environments impact asthma management. Families, teachers and healthcare professionals must consider the developmental experiences of adolescents living with asthma in order to provide appropriate care. Reporting Method COREQ was used. Patient or Public Contribution The data consists of answers given that comprise responses provided by patients during interviews. Background Globally, asthma is one of the most common noncommunicable diseases across all age groups (Asher et al. 2021 ; Dharmage et al. 2019 ). Although the exact cause of asthma remains unknown, several risk factors contribute to its development, including environmental allergens, climate change, family history, ethnicity and socio‐economic factors (Sio and Chew 2021 ; Takkinsatian et al. 2022 ). In adolescents with asthma, perceived stress and anxiety at personal, family and community levels have been associated with an exacerbation of asthma symptoms (Landeo‐Gutierrez and Celedón 2020 ). Disagreements between adolescents with asthma and their parents or family caregivers, along with conflict or turmoil within the family or caregiving environment, contribute to adolescents' emotional distress and anxiety, ultimately impacting their well‐being of adolescents (Landeo‐Gutierrez and Celedón 2020 ). Exacerbations of asthma may lead to frequent school absences and difficulties participating in social activities or interacting with their peers (Chang et al. 2024 ; Landeo‐Gutierrez and Celedón 2020 ; Robinson et al. 2022 ). Adolescents may perceive that they are being treated unfairly (Kaplan and Price 2020 ). Asthma symptom management has improved over recent decades (Kosse et al. 2019 ). However, emergency department admissions have increased for adolescents diagnosed with asthma and living in Taiwan (Liang et al. 2019 ). Asthma management includes managing triggers, preventing exposure to allergens, complying with medication, monitoring asthma symptoms and maintaining a healthy lifestyle (Born et al. 2024 ; Tseng et al. 2020 ). Mental health assessments should be considered when exploring psychological triggers for asthma symptoms. A healthy lifestyle can improve the quality of life for adolescents with asthma (Kosse et al. 2020 ). Physical activity has been shown to increase symptom‐free days and enhance quality of life (Panagiotou et al. 2020 ). A meta‐analysis found that the physical activity levels of adolescents with asthma do not differ from their healthy peers (Mackintosh et al. 2021 ). However, a fear of asthma attacks has emerged as the main barrier to exercise in this population. Many adolescents with asthma withdraw from exercise as a coping strategy (Winn et al. 2018 ). Medication adherence plays a significant role in asthma symptom management. Adolescents with asthma report are wanting to avoid the embarrassment of taking medication in front of their peers (Kaplan and Price 2020 ; Mackintosh et al. 2021 ). These studies were primarily conducted in Western countries with contexts that do not reflect Taiwanese adolescents' experiences of managing asthma at home, school or in their communities. Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory ( 1974 ) explains how adolescent development and interactions are influenced by multiple levels of surrounding environmental systems. First, the microsystem encompasses an adolescent's direct relationships and immediate environment, such as interactions with parents at home, teachers and classmates at school and healthcare providers like nurses and doctors. Second, the mesosystem refers to the interactions between individuals within the microsystem. For example, it may encompass collaborations between teachers and parents for asthma management, family attitudes towards asthma shaping peer perceptions of the condition or coordinated efforts by doctors and school nurses to manage an adolescent's asthma at school. Third, the exosystem includes indirect environmental influences, such as larger social systems that impact the adolescent while not directly involving them. For instance, adolescents with asthma are affected by school asthma care policies or established asthma routines. Finally, the macrosystem encompasses societal and cultural contexts. As the outer layer of the ecological system, it affects the individual by influencing all other systems. For example, cultural beliefs about illness influence disease perception and care. This theory has been used in previous studies focusing on the developmental contexts that are amenable to changes influencing children's and adolescents' health and learning (Lee et al. 2023 ; Li et al. 2021 ). Bronfenbrenner theorises that change or modification to any level of the development environments can impede or enhance developmental outcomes for young people (Burakgazi 2025 ). Utilising Bronfenbrenner's theoretical lens, this research hypothesises that enhancing asthma management skills for teachers, parents and the young people within the microsystem environment will optimise asthma and developmental outcomes for the young people. 2. Aims This study aimed to explore Taiwanese adolescents' experiences of living with asthma. 3. Methods 3.1. Research Design An exploratory qualitative study was conducted using semi‐structured interviews. This approach allowed participants to elaborate on their thoughts (DeJonckheere and Vaughn 2019 ). 3.2. Setting and Sample After obtaining ethical approval, a purposive sampling strategy (Shorten and Moorley 2014 ) was used to identify potential participants at a paediatric outpatient clinic located in southern Taiwan. The final sample size was determined once data saturation was evident. Saturation is reached when no new information emerges (Hennink and Kaiser 2022 ; Moorley and Cathala 2019 ). Adolescents aged between 10 and 19 years, diagnosed with asthma and receiving treatment with asthma medications were invited to participate in this study. Asthma severity refers to how regularly asthma symptoms occur and their degree of impact on daily activities. In mild persistent asthma, symptoms occur more than twice per week but not daily, with minor limitations in activities. In moderate persistent asthma, symptoms occur daily, sometimes affecting sleep and physical activity. Severe persistent asthma is characterised by symptoms multiple times per day, significantly limiting daily activities and quality of life (Venkatesan 2023 ). 3.3. Data Collection 3.3.1. Recruitment The principal investigator (PI) contacted the nurse in charge daily for identifying potential participants. PI then checked for their eligibility prior to explaining the purpose of the study, participants' rights during the research process and the requirements for participation. Written consents were obtained from the adolescents and their guardians. 3.3.2. Interviews The interview questions were based on a review of relevant literature (Vazquez‐Ortiz et al. 2020 ). Initial interview questions were pretested, and the results suggested that the interview schedules required flexibility. The study therefore conducted semi‐structured interviews in a private room at times and locations that were convenient for participants. Open‐ended interview questions and additional prompting questions allowed the interviews to explore the participants' experiences in managing asthma. The questions aimed to explore any difficulties that the participants faced in managing their condition at home or school. They were encouraged to elaborate on their thoughts, the personal impact of living with asthma and their experiences with current asthma treatment. Each interview lasted 30–40 min. During and after each interview, handwritten field notes were taken to record important observations, including nonverbal behaviours, ways of interacting with other participants and the researcher's impressions of the participants. Interviews were conducted until data saturation was achieved and no new themes emerged (Nasrabadi et al. 2021 ). 3.4. Data Analysis The interview data were transcribed into text files and uploaded to NVivo V12 (QSR 2020 ) software. Data analysis was conducted through the theoretical lens. The data were retained in the original Taiwanese language. Data analysis and interpretation were conducted by two bilingual researchers, independent research team (T‐JT and C‐JW), who actively discussed their codes to reach agreement. Participant statements (interview quotes) were translated into English. This method minimises the loss of word and phrase meaning (Smith et al. 2008 ). Credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability are addressed through data collection, analysis, interpretation and verification (Nasrabadi et al. 2021 ). The interviews were conducted by qualified principal researchers. The research team was trained in qualitative research. All interviews were audiotaped and transcribed verbatim to ensure accuracy. The findings were verified with the participants to confirm whether they appropriately reflected their meanings. The direct quotes used to support our findings enhance the validity and originality of the study. 3.5. Ethical Considerations The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the participating hospital (Reference No.: 201701466B0C502). The research aims and procedures were explained to the adolescents and their parents or guardians. Participation was voluntary, and participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without impacting their care or caregiver relationships. The data are nonidentifiable, stored on a secure server with password protection, and access is restricted to the research team. 4. Findings 4.1. Participants Seventeen adolescents were interviewed. Participants were aged between 10 and 17 years. More than half (58.82%) had mild persistent asthma, four had moderate asthma, two had severe asthma, and one had mild intermittent asthma (Table 1 ). 4.2. Themes Themes were revealed through application of Ecological Systems Theory. Figure 1 illustrates the microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem and macrosystem for this study. The microsystem refers to direct interactions with the adolescents, including their family, peers, teachers and school. The mesosystem encompasses interactions between different microsystems in the adolescents' lives. In this context, these interactions included parent–teacher communications about asthma care. The exosystem incorporates both formal and informal social structures influencing adolescents' experiences, such as school policy. The macrosystem refers to cultural elements that affect adolescent development, including the cultural ideologies, attitudes and social conditions in which adolescents are immersed. 4.2.1. Asthma Exacerbation and Its Impacts Participants stated that their asthma symptoms worsened according to their personal history and weather. For example: …As my father has a history of allergies and when the weather is bad (shrugs) sometimes, it will happen; after all, it is not contagious, and it is not my innate willingness to be like this… (P12) …After I moved here [seaside city], I felt that when the climate changed, I would breathe heavily, scream, cough, and cough. … If you don't have cold symptoms, you won't have a sudden attack. Oh, when an attack occurs at night. It's more common at night… At first, allergies only occur at night, before going to bed. Well, most of that kind of thing happens more often when the seasons change. When the climate changes, your breathing will become stuffy, and you will keep coughing and coughing… (P13) …When you get up, it's the same as when you start coughing and feel uncomfortable. It is also possible that when the temperature is too cold, you start to itch… It's just that you may be hot and cold, or the temperature difference between morning and evening is too big… (P6) 4.2.2. Maintaining Physical Comfort Participants discussed their efforts to maintain physical comfort when experiencing symptoms at home and school. For example: …I will continue exercising at school. If I feel out of breath, I walk slowly; otherwise, I rest. When I feel uncomfortable, I do not like letting my classmates know. They ask questions as if I am uncomfortable. Unlike others, I find places to rest. (P9) Because I can't breathe, it feels even more breathless when I lie down, so I put another pillow on top of the pillow, wake up a little, sit up, and then close my eyes to watch. Can I fall asleep? (P3) 4.2.3. Increased Compliance With Medication or Treatment Regimes Participants discussed their efforts to increase compliance with medication or treatment regimes. For example: Sometimes, I forget to take my medicine, so I just put it in the dental cup. You have to brush your teeth every night, and you know you need to take medication when you see them. (P9) Then, sometimes I eat irregularly, and my mother would ask me to eat, and I would eat. (P17) Because my sister has asthma, she needs to take medicine at night, so she will remind me when she takes medicine. My sister never forgets; she is very punctual. She takes half an hour to go to bed… (P7) In fact, sometimes I forget. Later, I set up an alarm clock on my mobile phone. This is noisy but effective. (P12) 4.2.4. Peer Interactions The participants also expressed their experiences of interacting with others. In particular, they felt that they were disengaged from others and ‘alienated’. For example: Because the COVID‐19 pandemic was ongoing at that time, my classmates were very afraid of being infected. They (classmates) thought that I (with asthma) would be contagious, so they started to marginalise me. They did not like talking to me… don't touch me… My classmates will push my hand away… (P11) After taking the medicine, I feel dizziness, my body shakes, and my classmates think I am strange…I feel afraid… I do not want to talk to my classmates…I might be regarded as, um…someone who is not easy to get close to…so usually, if I wait until the side effects are over after taking the medicine, then I dare to communicate with others so that my classmates will not think that I am sick and pass it on to her. (P11) Will my skin be itchy and then turn red? My classmates did not know this at that time. Because I used to wear coats very often to cover it up, they did not see the red part. (P15) 4.2.5. Available Resources at School The participants articulated formal and informal asthma treatment guidelines that influenced their experiences. For example: If asthma occurs at school, I will go directly to the tutor! The tutor will deal with it; the tutor will call the school nurses and then ask the school nurses what to do… the classmates call the tutor who was diagnosed with asthma and had experience in dealing with asthma. (P12) I can take the stairs, but I gasp all the time, and my breathing becomes shallow, and the quality is bad for a long time. At that time, I felt tight in my chest after running, so I was taken to the health room, and the school nurse said that I had to learn how to breathe. (P15) 4.2.6. Uncertainty The participants expressed uncertainty about the long‐term efficacy of asthma medications and treatments. For example: I have been taking medicine for a long time… and have been visiting my doctor regularly for a long time. I am not sure if this had anything to do with steroids. Now, I am worried because I have been taking this drug for years. Will it still work? I read in the news that steroids lose their effectiveness if you take too much. Also do they ruin your ability to grow taller? (P16) I know have to come back often for follow‐up… It seems that my asthma satiation is under control… I hope there might be a new medicine to treat my disease. My inflammation levels have not decreased. But I don't know if I will get better when I am getting get older (P6) 4.2.7. Changes in Social Participation to Manage Symptoms The participants described several ways in which asthma symptom management changed their levels of social participation at school. For example: Tug‐of‐war, running, and jogging, are relatively rare. My father told the teacher that I was angry with my breathing, so the teacher reduced my work and some of my strenuous exercise. If I do not run like them, it will be OK. (P13) In physical education class, I would sit and rest or do homework rather than take activities …or the teacher would come up with something that allowed me to choose alternatives… (P3) Just tell the teacher that if you provide proof to the Academic Affairs Office, you don't have to take physical education classes and can just watch your classmates. (P11) 4.2.8. Conflicts in Asthma Care Across Different Generations of Family Caregivers Cultural conflicts between different generations of family caregivers were also identified. For example: My grandmother and I were asleep in the same room, and the bed was covered with straw mats. I could not sleep well at night; I would be out of breath and get up frequently. Mother told grandmother that I could not sleep on the straw mat, but grandmother said it would be cooler in the summer…Grandma and mother sometimes quarrel. Grandma said not to take any medication. She said the more you take it, the worse it will be, and it will harm your body, but my mother wants me to take the medicine regularly. (P6) …My mother always tells me that I must clean my room myself and can't eat cold food, like ice cream or cold beverage, however, my grandmother did say that it is ok to eat or drink whatever I want…so, they had a fight about “ice food” and others' asthma management….my asthma… (P16) These themes were derived by exploring multiple levels of the Bronfenbrenner's developmental environment, such as family, school, friends, teacher‐parent, school policy and cultural belief. All these themes have direct or indirect impacts on Taiwanese adolescents with asthma. 5. Discussion This study aimed to explore the experiences of Taiwanese adolescents with asthma in managing their condition in everyday life. Eight main themes were identified from the interview data: ‘asthma exacerbation and its impacts’, ‘maintaining physical comfort’, ‘increased compliance with medication or treatment regimes’, ‘peer interactions’, ‘available resources at school’, ‘uncertainty’, ‘changes in social participation to manage symptoms’ and ‘conflicts in asthma care across different generations of family caregivers’. Most participants had mild, persistent asthma. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies (Meltzer et al. 2020 ; Wallace‐Farquharson et al. 2023 ) in observing that patients with self‐ or parent‐perceived mild asthma do not consider their symptoms to be serious; therefore, they do not take action to manage their condition. However, while slow symptom onset may not be life‐threatening, rapid onset may require immediate medication and hospitalisation. From our interviews, it was evident that the participants were unwilling to engage in physical activity because of the fear that it would exacerbate symptoms such as breathlessness. However, moderate exercise can help individuals develop interpersonal skills, alleviate feelings of loneliness and reduce airway inflammation (Mackintosh et al. 2021 ). Participants also reported decreased motivation to engage in various forms of social participation. These findings are similar to previous studies that identified teachers' lack of asthma knowledge, embarrassment over asthma symptoms, inability to keep up with peers and fear of having an asthma attack as barriers to physical exercise (Brynjulfsen et al. 2021 ; Winn et al. 2018 ). Participants noted that teachers did not allow students with asthma to engage in physical activities, because it could aggravate symptoms to such an extent that the affected students could require emergency care. Teachers often reduced the amount of exercise that adolescents with asthma were required to perform, even asking them to perform static activities instead. This response is commonly observed in teachers managing children and adolescents with chronic illnesses (Getch et al. 2019 ). Our results revealed that teachers had insufficient knowledge of asthma management, which is a common phenomenon worldwide (Brynjulfsen et al. 2021 ). Teachers should receive specific and up‐to‐date education concerning asthma management, promoting the benefits of exercise for all students' physical, mental and social development. Social development is a crucial component of adolescence. According to Erikson's theory (Ragelienė 2016 ), adolescents view interactions and relationships as a central part of their lives. This is a period when young people begin to build their sense of self‐identity and are increasingly influenced by their peers (Ragelienė 2016 ). If adolescents are not accepted by their peers, they may feel insecure, have low self‐esteem and experience peer pressure. These factors affect disease management (Chalmers et al. 2022 ; Ragelienė 2016 ). As highlighted by the adolescents interviewed in this study, asthma symptoms affected their peer interactions. When experiencing symptoms, medication must be taken to prevent those symptoms from worsening. Participants reported that they felt as if they were treated differently for needing to take medication to manage their symptoms. Some adolescents chose to ignore or tolerate their symptoms; others temporarily distanced themselves from crowds to self‐manage their condition. Our findings revealed that adolescents with asthma experience peer discrimination, which profoundly impacts their health and well‐being (Cheng et al. 2022 ). For example, during peer interactions, participants were asked whether asthma was contagious and could be spread through physical contact. This made them feel embarrassed or rejected. Adolescents who experienced peer discrimination did not seek help from their teachers; rather, they believed their peers would no longer consider them to be sick or contagious if they could conceal or control their symptoms. For this reason, adolescents with asthma isolated themselves from their classmates when feeling unwell. Participants reported that they often had to seek help from teachers and justify to their peers why they were not fit to participate in social activities at school. This echoes the findings of a previous study that investigated how adolescents adapt to peer‐induced stress‐related activities (Vazquez‐Ortiz et al. 2020 ). For example, adolescents explained how to seek help from their peers in different situations, including instances when they delegated support‐related activities to health professionals and teachers. A previous study (Ragelienė 2016 ) found no correlation between asthma and peer interaction. However, the more severe the asthma symptoms, the higher the number of days spent in the hospital, the less time patients spent socialising, and the more isolated and lonely they felt (Al‐Motlaq and Al‐Sheyab 2018 ). Another study revealed that adolescents with asthma deal with their emotions by suppressing their feelings, which places them at risk of peer victimisation (Özyurt et al. 2021 ). Furthermore, some adolescents expressed that asthma control is a lifelong responsibility and wished for ongoing support. Kosse et al. ( 2020 ) found that providing appropriate assessments and support to adolescents can be challenging for health professionals, suggesting that improving adolescents' motivation can help them overcome these concerns. Previous studies also demonstrated that adolescents' quality of life is associated with the effectiveness of their asthma management strategies (Kosse et al. 2020 ; Winn et al. 2018 ). This study found that participants were able to identify their own asthma management strategies, such as setting up reminders to take medications, thereby reducing asthma attacks. From the perspective of Ecological Systems Theory, culture and beliefs directly influence an individual's values and beliefs about health (Jackman et al. 2022 ). Over time, people of different age groups are influenced by the cultural and social values of their respective eras, resulting in different interpretations of physical health. In addition, Western medications are believed to damage health by causing the body to catch cold. Therefore, the traditional cultural view on asthma in Taiwan is to avoid contact with cold things, including Western asthma medication. In Taiwan, it is traditional for three family generations to live together, especially in the rural areas. Family care is regarded as a woman's responsibility (Ng and Indran 2021 ). However, caregivers may have different opinions on asthma care, which may lead to conflict and indirectly affect the condition. Our findings showed that grandmothers were extremely involved in caring for adolescents and often interfered with adolescents' autonomy in treatment adherence. Thus, these findings demonstrate the merits of utilising the concept of ecological systems to understand environmental influences on asthma symptom perception and treatment. 6. Strengths and Limitations of the Work Although this study provides insights into the experiences of Taiwanese adolescents with asthma, the findings cannot be generalised to a broader population. This is because of the nature of the qualitative method and the contextualised understanding of participants' experiences. This study was based in Taiwan, which has its own cultural and healthcare systems, thereby limiting the applicability of our findings to other countries. Limitations of recruiting participants from a single setting and focusing on patients' experiences in managing asthma by the condition's severity are also acknowledged. 7. Recommendations for Further Research Future research into asthma management in adolescent patients should address their perspectives from a multi‐systems approach. Future investigations in other geographic contexts are recommended, as well as the cultivation and evaluation of current asthma self‐management programmes integrating adolescents' parents, peers, teachers and school nurses. 8. Conclusion This study provides a deeper understanding of Taiwanese adolescents' daily experiences of living with asthma. The theoretical lens of Ecological Systems Theory was used to understand the environmental features and complex dynamics involving children, their families, intergenerational beliefs and community resources. Our findings highlight the challenges that adolescents face, particularly with peer interactions, engaging in ‘normal’ activities and conflicts among family caregivers. Our study contributes to the literature on asthma management from the perspective of Taiwanese adolescents', highlighting developmental needs, as well as home and school environment impacts on managing and living with asthma. It is critical that families, teachers and healthcare professionals are aware of these needs and work together to provide a supportive environment to empower adolescents towards effective self‐management.
