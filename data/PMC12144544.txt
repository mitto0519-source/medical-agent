This review synthesizes current evidence on the timing of antihypertensive medication, with a focus on circadian BP rhythms and patients with specific BP patterns or comorbidities. Bedtime dosing may benefit individuals with nocturnal hypertension and nondipper BP patterns, but large trials, such as the TIME study, have shown no significant cardiovascular outcome differences between morning and bedtime dosing. However, the optimal timing of antihypertensive medication for patients with distinct BP rhythms or comorbidities remains uncertain. Future research should investigate the potential benefits of personalized medication timing tailored to BP patterns and clinical conditions. Additionally, treatment strategies should consider BP rhythms, comorbidities, and adherence to optimize outcomes, paving the way for more effective management of hypertensive patients with complex clinical profiles. Increasing evidence indicates that nondipper blood pressure (BP) patterns and morning hypertension are strongly linked to adverse outcomes in hypertensive patients [ 1 , 2 ]. Consequently, the objective of BP management has evolved from achieving office BP control to ensuring comprehensive 24-h BP regulation. Currently, the challenge lies in utilizing the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of antihypertensive drugs to determine the optimal administration time, thereby minimizing BP variability and restoring the dipper BP pattern. However, there is no consensus on whether administering antihypertensive drugs in the morning or at bedtime is more advantageous [ 3 ] (Tables 1 and 2 ). This article reviews prior research on the timing of antihypertensive medication administration, aiming to provide recommendations for hypertensive patients with varying BP rhythms. Evidence was synthesized from studies identified through targeted searches of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library databases. Keywords such as ‘hypertension’, ‘antihypertensive medications’, ‘circadian blood pressure pattern’, ‘bedtime dosing’, and ‘morning dosing’ were used. Studies published in English between 1995 and 2024 were considered. The review focused on randomized controlled trials (RCTs), meta-analyses, and observational studies analyzing the timing of antihypertensive medication administration in relation to BP rhythms and comorbidities, such as obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), glaucoma, and silent cerebral infarction (SCI). We defined ‘no relevant studies’ as those that did not provide direct or indirect insights into the timing of antihypertensive medication in relation to circadian BP rhythms, target organ damage, or comorbidities. Therefore, we included studies that examined BP rhythm characteristics, investigated the relationship between BP rhythms and target organ damage, or evaluated the efficacy of antihypertensive drugs in the general hypertensive population. To maintain focus on our study objectives while ensuring scientific rigor, we excluded studies that did not analyze BP rhythm patterns, target organ damage, or antihypertensive medication timing in any capacity. Non-English studies, studies published before 1995, as well as case reports, letters, editorials, and studies with insufficient methodological details were not considered. A total of 63 studies met our inclusion criteria, comprising 14 RCTs, 43 observational studies, and 6 meta-analyses or systematic reviews. These studies were conducted across multiple regions, including North America, Europe, and Asia, providing a diverse geographic representation. The sample sizes varied significantly, ranging from small-scale crossover studies with fewer than 50 participants to large-scale cohort studies with tens of thousands of participants. The heterogeneity in study design and population characteristics underscores the complexity of determining the optimal timing of antihypertensive medication in different patient subgroups. Details of these studies and their findings are discussed in subsequent sections. Bedtime antihypertensive dosage: potential advantages Intrinsic factors within the body, such as genetic and neuroendocrine factors, not only influence 24-h fluctuations in BP but also impact the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination of medications [ 4 ]. The pharmacokinetics and efficacy of antihypertensive drugs could theoretically differ depending on the time of day they are administered [ 5 ]. A 2022 consensus on managing morning hypertension [ 6 ] highlighted its strong association with cardiovascular risk, with better predictive power for stroke compared to nocturnal BP. This consensus recommends considering bedtime dosing based on individual circumstances. Furthermore, several studies have shown that nocturnal hypertension is linked to asymptomatic organ damage in hypertensive patients [ 7 , 8 ] and independently predicts cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in the hypertensive population [ 9 ]. Consequently, administering antihypertensive medication before bedtime to control nighttime hypertension, restore the circadian BP pattern, and prevent morning spikes appears to offer a theoretical survival advantage. The MAPEC (Monitorización Ambulatoria para Predicción de Eventos Cardiovasculares) and the Hygia Chronotherapy study, both conducted by the same Spanish research team, provide the most compelling evidence supporting the benefits of bedtime medication. The first prospective, randomized, open-label, blinded endpoint trial, known as the MAPEC study, published in 2010 [ 10 ] demonstrated for the first time after a median follow-up of 5.6 years that taking at least one antihypertensive medication at bedtime instead of upon waking effectively improved BP control and significantly reduced the incidence and mortality of CVD in 2156 patients. In 2020, the larger scale Hygia Chronotherapy study [ 11 ] confirmed these findings, with a median follow-up of 5.6 years among 19 084 eligible hypertensive patients. The study showed that the bedtime medication group experienced greater reductions in both SBP and DBP related to sleep compared to the morning medication group, leading to a 45% reduction in composite cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death). Additionally, during the follow-up, when the proportion of patients taking statins was similar in both groups, the bedtime medication group had lower creatinine and LDL cholesterol levels, higher HDL cholesterol, and eGFR levels compared to the morning medication group at the study's end. Furthermore, the Syst-Eur trial [ 12 ], which included 4695 elderly participants with systolic hypertension, found that treatment with nifedipine at bedtime reduced the primary endpoint of stroke risk by 42% compared to the placebo group ( P = 0.003), and overall CVD outcomes were reduced by 31% ( P &lt; 0.001). A similarly designed but potentially biased Syst-China study [ 13 ], involving 2394 older hypertensive patients followed for 3 years, reached similar conclusions. The HOPE study [ 14 ] examining ramipril also yielded comparable results, demonstrating a significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality and myocardial infarction. However, these studies have methodological limitations. Both the MAPEC and the Hygia Chronotherapy trial lacked a uniform medication protocol, with MAPEC having a relatively small sample size issue. Additionally, concerns have been raised about the clarity of randomization in the Hygia Chronotherapy trial [ 15 – 17 ]. Despite being the strongest evidence for bedtime dosing, the extremely low dropout rates in MAPEC (2%) and Hygia (0.4%) led to seemingly implausible benefits, such as a 50% reduction in cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in the Hygia Chronotherapy trial, with only a 1.6/1.2 mmHg difference in systolic/diastolic ABP (ambulatory blood pressure) between baseline and follow-up over 48 h. In MAPEC, a 60% reduction in cardiovascular events and mortality was observed, with an even smaller ABP change of 1.1/0.2 mmHg. This has led some experts to question the validity of their findings [ 18 ]. The Syst-Eur trial, Syst-China, and HOPE studies prescribed antihypertensive drugs at different times, including bedtime, making it difficult to directly compare the benefits of bedtime dosing versus morning dosing. Therefore, their conclusions do not definitively address whether bedtime medication is superior to daytime dosing. Bedtime antihypertensive use: no additional advantage observed The HARMONY trial, a 24-week investigation involving 103 hypertensive participants, discovered that the conventional antihypertensive drug's administration time – whether morning or evening – had no significant impact on daytime, nighttime, or 24-h BP fluctuations, nor did it differentiate between the quality of life of those taking medication at different hours [ 19 ]. Furthermore, the study recommended that patients should receive their antihypertensive medications at the time that suits them best, favorably promoting simpler treatment regimens and longer lasting medications whenever feasible. The TIME study, a substantial 2022 publication [ 20 ], surpassed the Hygia Chronotherapy trial in participant numbers and meticulously instructed randomly assigned participants on the specific timing of all antihypertensive drugs. After an average follow-up period of 5.2 years, the study revealed that the timing of medication intake did not directly influence cardiovascular outcomes. It implies that the optimal dosing schedule can be tailored to each individual Despite these promising findings, both the HARMONY and TIME studies have limitations. The HARMONY trial, with a 24-week duration, may have been underpowered due to its small sample size and lack of robust statistical validation. Similarly, the TIME study, while larger in scope than the Hygia Chronotherapy trial failed to standardize the type of antihypertensive medication administered, which could limit the generalizability of its conclusions. Furthermore, neither study adequately stratified participants based on their distinct BP patterns, such as nondipper and extreme dipper, to determine if the timing of medication administration might differently affect their respective responses. As previously mentioned, given the scarcity of high-quality clinical evidence and the inconsistent results from previous studies, it remains uncertain whether bedtime dosing of antihypertensive medications provides greater benefits compared to taking them during waking hours for hypertensive patients. There is still ongoing investigation into which factor – nocturnal hypertension or disrupted BP rhythm – is more strongly linked to increased damage to target organs and adverse outcomes in these individuals [ 21 ]. Theoretically, all types of circadian BP patterns can be observed in individuals with nocturnal hypertension. This was indeed observed by Kim et al. [ 22 ], where a portion of their study population displayed nocturnal hypertension with varying BP patterns. Interestingly, while nocturnal hypertension is recognized as an independent predictor of target organ damage in hypertension regardless of circadian rhythm [ 21 ], studies by Ohkubo et al. [ 23 ], Hermida et al. [ 24 ], and Borrelli et al. [ 25 ] have shown that individuals with normotensive nondipper patterns have a cardiovascular risk comparable to those with hypertension and dipper patterns. This suggests that the absence of the typical BP dip might be as detrimental as hypertension itself, highlighting the importance of considering not just the average BP but also its variability over time. Therefore, considering the significance of BP circadian rhythms in determining the prognosis and outcomes of hypertensive patients, this article, focusing on BP patterns, synthesizes evidence supporting the timing of medication for individuals with different dipper patterns. Hypertensive patients with dipper pattern In light of the ongoing debate surrounding the bedtime administration of antihypertensive medications and concerns regarding nocturnal BP dips, the World Hypertension League and the European Society of Hypertension suggested in their 2022 consensus statement [ 18 ] that routine bedtime dosing should not be advocated, advocating instead for monotherapy with long-acting agents or morning-dosed combinations. However, the TIME study [ 20 ] later contradicted this by reporting no increased fracture risk in the bedtime dosing group. In fact, participants in the bedtime group displayed a slightly lower fall risk ( P = 0.048) and fewer symptomatic adverse events compared to the morning group. Similar findings emerged from MAPEC [ 10 ] and the Hygia Chronotherapy trial [ 11 ], indicating no heightened side effects from bedtime dosing compared to morning dosing. Liu et al. [ 26 ] and Serinel et al. [ 27 ] further indicated that when employing long-acting antihypertensives, nocturnal BP reductions were comparable between morning and bedtime dosing. Therefore, for hypertensive patients using long-acting medications, there seems to be no discernible difference in either safety or efficacy between morning and bedtime dosing. The decision on the optimal timing should be personalized, considering each patient's preference for the most compliant time of day, as enhancing adherence to treatment appears to be the key to effective therapy. Beyond the complex interplay of intrinsic neuroendocrine systems, research has elucidated that individual behavioral traits, particularly activity level and wakefulness, significantly contribute to BP patterns [ 28 ]. A study demonstrated that restricting physical activity led to reduced daily BP fluctuations [ 29 ]. Surprisingly, shift workers’ BP rhythms can invert when they work nights and rest during the day [ 30 ], linking their increased cardiovascular risks [ 31 – 33 ] to disrupted circadian rhythms from shift work. Animal studies provide preliminary evidence for this mechanism [ 34 – 35 ]. However, research on the optimal medication timing for night-shift hypertensive patients, specifically whether morning or evening dosing is best, is limited due to excluding these workers in some studies. Considering behavioral shifts in BP patterns and antihypertensive drug characteristics, evening dosing might be more advantageous for night-shift workers with dipper BP patterns, especially those using medications with short half-lives and potent effects. Theoretically, this approach aligns better with the potential evening peak in BP for these individuals. Hypertensive patients with extreme dipper pattern Hypertensive patients with combined glaucoma A study examining 85 patients with normal intraocular pressure glaucoma revealed that nocturnal low BP, either spontaneous or induced by medication, heightened the risk of visual field loss [ 36 ]. Pillunat et al. [ 37 ] found that extreme dipper pattern patients with normal daytime BP had a greater risk of visual field defects compared with extreme dipper pattern daytime hypertensive patients. This suggests that circadian BP rhythms play a crucial role in glaucoma progression. Jesús and colleagues found a strong association between extreme nocturnal drops in SBP and DBP and glaucomatous optic neuropathy (GON), with odds ratios of 19.78 and 5.55, respectively [ 38 ]. Importantly, the study also indicated that nondipper and reverse dipper patterns did not increase GON risk. The reasoning behind this may lie in inadequate blood supply to the eye's optic nerve when nocturnal BP plummets [ 39 ]. As glaucoma patients struggle with self-regulating ocular circulation, insufficient blood flow to the optic nerve head can lead to ischemia or GON. Despite its small sample size and focus on the Hispanic population, the study brings insights into time-based therapy for hypertension. For hypertensive patients with both extreme dipper BP pattern and glaucoma, particularly those using short-acting, potent medications, morning dosing seems to offer a safer option than bedtime dosing to minimize the risk of GON development. Further research with larger, diverse populations would help refine these recommendations. Hypertensive patients with combined silent cerebral infarction/cognitive decline Though nondipper and reverse dipper BP patterns are prevalent among hypertensive individuals, the potential harm linked to extreme dipper BP should not be overlooked. SCI, often underappreciated compared to symptomatic cases, has significant implications. Wu J. et al. analyzed data from over 1.4 million Chinese aged 80 and above, revealing a sex-standardized prevalence of 37.33% for SCI [ 40 ]. The American Heart/Stroke Association consensus highlights that patients with SCI face a 1.5–3.3 times higher risk of subsequent symptomatic stroke compared to the general population [ 41 ]. Furthermore, research indicates that SCI doubles the risk of dementia and Alzheimer's disease [ 42 ]. Closely related to SCI is leukoaraiosis, which is associated with both stroke development [ 43 ] and cognitive decline [ 44 , 45 ]. Kario et al. [ 46 ] discovered that BP rhythm plays a role in SCI development, with hypertensive patients exhibiting an extreme dipper BP pattern having the highest incidence. Yang et al. [ 43 ] found that this specific BP pattern acts as an independent risk factor for severe cerebral leukoaraiosis in patients with SCI. Zhao et al. [ 47 ] also observed a correlation between the extreme dipper BP pattern and progressive cerebral infarction. The mechanism by which excessive drop in BP is closely associated with SCI and leukoaraiosis may be [ 48 ], for the elderly hypertensive population, susceptible to SCI and cerebral leukoaraiosis, cerebrovascular self-regulation is impaired due to atherosclerosis and other factors, and the excessive drop in BP at night in patients with the extreme dipper BP pattern decreases the cerebral small-vessel perfusion pressure. Additionally, some studies have found that hypertensive patients with extreme dipper BP pattern are strongly associated with an excessive rise in BP in the morning and with upright hypertension, which may partly explain the occurrence of stroke in the morning in patients with extreme dipper BP pattern [ 48 , 49 ]. These findings emphasize the need for careful management and monitoring in such patients. Consequently, for hypertensive patients, particularly the elderly, who exhibit an extreme dipper pattern of BP in conjunction with SCI and/or cognitive decline, efforts to normalize abnormal BP rhythms and prevent excessive morning BP surge are clearly deserving of careful consideration in the context of antihypertensive management. For these patients, the timing of antihypertensive medication administration can be a critical factor. Taking antihypertensive drugs before bedtime may potentially lead to excessive BP reduction during the night and subsequent excessive morning BP surge, particularly in those using short-acting medications. While respecting hypertensive patients’ preferences regarding medication timing, it appears that, theoretically, administering antihypertensive medications in the morning may be safer than doing so before bedtime for these patients, given the risks associated with extreme dipper patterns. Hypertensive patients with combined asymptomatic myocardial ischemia The impact of the extreme dipper BP pattern on cardiovascular health remains a subject of controversy. Verdecchia et al. 's study in 2022 [ 50 ] did not support a heightened cardiovascular risk linked to this pattern when compared to the dipper one. Similarly, a 2007 meta-analysis [ 9 ] discovered no escalated risk of cardiovascular events associated with the extreme dipper BP pattern. However, Pierdomenico et al. [ 51 ] reported that antihypertensive treatment actually augmented nocturnal myocardial ischemia incidents in hypertensive individuals with both an extreme dipper pattern and coronary artery stenosis. A 2016 meta-analysis [ 52 ] revealed that hypertensive patients with the extreme dipper pattern, whether untreated or under treatment, demonstrated varying levels of risk compared to those with the dipper pattern. Amah et al. [ 53 ] further observed that in a hypertensive population without concurrent coronary artery disease and undergoing treatment, patients with the dipper hypertension pattern showed better subendocardial viability, as indicated by the Buckberg index, aortic stiffness, and central augmentation index (AIx), than those with the extreme dipper pattern. Moreover, a study utilizing coronary computed tomography [ 54 ] found that young adults with the extreme dipper BP pattern were at a heightened risk of developing coronary artery calcification by middle age, despite having normotension, raising additional concerns about long-term cardiovascular implications. Hence, in individuals with underlying asymptomatic myocardial ischemia, particularly hypertensive patients featuring the extreme dipper BP pattern alongside coronary artery stenosis, addressing abnormal circadian BP variations and guarding against excessive nocturnal dips could potentially improve cardiac outcomes. Therefore, administering antihypertensive medications in the morning might hold greater advantages, especially for those relying on short-acting agents. The rationale behind this suggestion lies in the potential to maintain a more stable BP profile during periods of nocturnal ischemia, thereby reducing the risk of ischemic events. Hypertensive patients with nondipper/reverse dipper pattern Hypertensive patients combined with obstructive sleep apnea OSA, a significant contributor to secondary hypertension [ 55 ], is intricately linked to CVD through its association with disrupted nocturnal BP patterns, often characterized by a nondipper pattern in patients with OSA [ 56 – 60 ]. Nocturnal hypoxia-induced micro arousals and heightened sympathetic activity contribute to this abnormal BP patterns [ 48 ]. Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP), the gold standard for treating hypertension in OSA patients, does demonstrate BP-lowering benefits [ 27 ], but its long-term compliance remains low [ 61 ], and its antihypertensive impact is relatively limited [ 62 ]. As a result, the timing of antihypertensive medication in OSA patients has recently garnered research interest. Kasiakogias et al. 's findings [ 63 ] suggest that in hypertensive individuals with newly diagnosed moderate or severe OSA, bedtime dosing outperforms morning dosing in controlling office SBP, nocturnal SBP, and DBP. Additionally, nighttime dosing was more beneficial in restoring normal BP rhythms. This simple adjustment to medication timing could benefit patients who struggle with CPAP adherence. However, Serinel et al. 's study [ 27 ] with higher evidence strength reported no significant nocturnal BP reduction from evening perindopril administration compared to morning dosing in patients with class III hypertension and moderate to severe OSA. Instead, they observed that morning dosing led to a greater daytime BP reduction than nighttime dosing. In light of the lack of high-quality, relevant studies and the preliminary nature of the findings from both studies, temporal therapies for hypertensive patients with OSA remain an area of ongoing investigation. Consequently, hypertensive individuals with nondipper or reverse dipper BP pattern who also have OSA, particularly those who are on perindopril, are currently advised against taking their antihypertensive medications at night. Instead, it is suggested that these medications may exert a more beneficial effect when administered in the morning. Isolated nocturnal hypertension Since the seminal description of simple nocturnal hypertension [isolated nocturnal hypertension (INH)] by Li et al. in 2007 [ 64 ], this condition has often been under-recognized due to its apparent normalization during the day and the practical challenges of using ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) for widespread screening. The nondipper pattern of BP, characterized by a failure of BP to decrease at night, is particularly prevalent in those with INH. Despite its stealthy nature, INH's prevalence is not negligible. Studies have revealed that INH affects a significant portion of the population, with rates as high as 19% among African Americans [ 65 ]; 12.9% in a diverse study population reported by Salazar et al. [ 66 ] and 20.4% among Chinese individuals with concurrent chronic kidney disease [ 67 ]. Furthermore, research has established a strong link between INH and increased risk of target organ damage, as well as heightened morbidity and mortality from CVD [ 21 ]. Consequently, INH warrants the same level of proactive management and antihypertensive treatment as daytime hypertension, to mitigate its potential health risks. Therefore, for INH patients, given the pharmacokinetics and efficacy of antihypertensive medications, administering them before bedtime rather than in the morning appears both logical and advisable, particularly for those using short-acting agents. This approach warrants further investigation to substantiate its potential advantages. Nonetheless, for individuals with INH who are averse to taking long-acting antihypertensive medications in the morning, the option of administering suitable antihypertensive drugs at bedtime could be a viable consideration, pending the results of further studies. Hypertensive patients with combined damage to target organs, such as the heart, brain, and kidneys Increasing evidence suggests that patients with nondipper and particularly reverse dipper patterns of hypertension face a heightened risk of CVD. One of the contributing factors to this increased risk is nocturnal hypertension, which results from an inadequate decrease in BP during the night. Nocturnal BP levels serve as a more potent predictor of CVD than do daytime mean BP levels, and achieving control over nocturnal BP is associated with a substantial reduction in the risk of all-cause mortality and CVD incidents [ 68 ]. The significance of circadian rhythm disturbances in nondipper and reverse dipper abnormal BP patterns on target organ damage is gaining recognition [ 52 , 69 , 70 ]. The proposed mechanism is that the inadequate nocturnal BP reduction keeps the heart and kidneys in a chronic state of elevated workload, leading to cardiac remodeling and renal dysfunction. Moreover, elevated nocturnal BP can induce the degeneration of small cerebral arteries and deep penetrating arteries, heightening the risk of cerebrovascular diseases. Considering the hazards posed by inadequate nocturnal BP decline, it is widely acknowledged that for patients exhibiting such patterns, there is a dual approach that should be taken. On one hand, it is essential to correct the underlying pathophysiological factors that contribute to the inadequate nocturnal BP reduction. This may involve reducing sodium intake for salt-sensitive individuals and enhancing sleep quality for those with nondipper pattern hypertension. On the other hand, chronotherapy of antihypertensive medications has also been explored. Studies such as the MAPEC and the Hygia Chronotherapy trials have demonstrated that bedtime dosing of antihypertensives can lower the risk of CVD compared to morning dosing and is beneficial in restoring the dipper pattern. However, the TIME study did not yield similar results. Consequently, current guidelines from the United States and Europe do not recommend altering therapy based solely on the status of an abnormal descending BP rhythm [ 71 ]. Therefore, for such patients, daily morning doses of long-acting antihypertensive medications are generally preferred. Nevertheless, for those who struggle with poor nocturnal BP control or exhibit high adherence to nighttime medication, incorporating an additional suitable antihypertensive agent during bedtime or utilizing bedtime dosing might offer a more effective treatment strategy. Morning hypertensive patients Excessive morning increases in BP can result in morning hypertension, a condition frequently associated with both persistent nocturnal hypertension and nocturnal hypotension [ 6 ]. A study by Jaewon Oh et al. [ 72 ], involving 1070 patients with cardiovascular risk factors, revealed that 34.9% of morning hypertension cases were linked to nocturnal hypertension following ABPM. Furthermore, this issue disproportionately affects Asian hypertensive populations [ 73 ]. Given the heightened risk of CVD associated with morning hypertension, it has garnered significant attention. The HOPE Asia Network's 2022 consensus emphasizes the importance of managing morning hypertension to a target of below 135/85 mmHg, regardless of office-based readings [ 6 ]. Consequently, for patients experiencing a morning surge in BP due to nocturnal hypotension, it is indeed advisable to take long-acting antihypertensive medications in the morning. This timing can also mitigate the premorning dose increase in BP. In patients with morning hypertension resulting from nocturnal hypertension, theoretically, adding antihypertensive medication at bedtime or administering it then could be a viable option if morning dosing of long-acting antihypertensive drugs is not effective. Moreover, bedtime administration of antihypertensive medication appears to be more suitable for individuals with INH and morning hypertension (Fig. 1 ). Hypertension and frailty in older adults Frailty and hypertension are highly prevalent in the elderly population. Among individuals aged 85 years and older, the prevalence of frailty reaches up to 30% [ 74 ], while hypertension affects over 80% of this age group [ 75 ]. These two conditions not only frequently coexist but also share common pathophysiological mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress [ 76 ]. Frailty exacerbates the adverse effects of hypertension, such as CVD, cognitive decline, and functional impairment. Among frail older adults, abnormal BP rhythms, such as nondipper or reverse-dipper patterns, as well as nocturnal hypertension, are particularly common and are often accompanied by excessive BP variability [ 77 , 78 ]. These abnormal BP rhythms are strongly associated with an increased risk of target organ damage and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [ 79 – 81 ], underscoring the need for clinicians to carefully consider BP rhythm characteristics when formulating antihypertensive treatment strategies. ABPM is a critical tool for assessing BP rhythms and BP variability, and its role is even more pronounced in the frail elderly population. According to a study by Koudelka and Sovová et al. [ 77 ], approximately 75% of frail individuals with well-controlled office BP exhibit masked uncontrolled hypertension (MUH) on ABPM. MUH is strongly associated with the risk of CVD, cognitive decline, and the progression of frailty [ 25 , 74 ]. Additionally, some older adults may experience excessive nocturnal BP reduction (extreme dipper), often related to inappropriate use of antihypertensive medications. This condition may lead to cerebral hypoperfusion, exacerbating cognitive dysfunction and functional decline [ 43 , 46 ]. Conversely, nondipper and reverse-dipper BP patterns are often accompanied by nocturnal hypertension, which increases cardiovascular risk, aggravates target organ damage, and impairs blood flow regulation [ 22 , 25 , 52 ]. Therefore, ABPM provides essential data on diurnal and nocturnal BP patterns, enabling personalized antihypertensive treatment decisions that aim to restore normal BP rhythms while avoiding the risks associated with overtreatment. In managing frailty and hypertension, the timing of antihypertensive medication administration is crucial and should account for both BP rhythms and comorbidities. For extreme dippers, nighttime administration of antihypertensive medications may exacerbate excessive nocturnal BP reduction, increasing the risk of cerebral hypoperfusion and cognitive decline. In such cases, morning dosing is recommended. On the other hand, for patients with nondipper or reverse-dipper BP patterns and concomitant nocturnal hypertension, bedtime dosing may help restore a dipper BP pattern and reduce cardiovascular risk. In addition to pharmacological therapy, integrating non-pharmacological interventions, such as physical activity and nutritional support, is essential for improving overall health in frail patients. For example, the SPRINTT trial demonstrated that multicomponent interventions, including resistance training and protein supplementation, significantly improved mobility and reduced frailty in older adults, indirectly supporting better BP control and cardiovascular health [ 82 , 83 ]. In conclusion, the management of hypertension in frail older adults requires an individualized and multidimensional approach. ABPM plays a pivotal role in providing the critical data needed to guide personalized treatment decisions. Combining l -arginine supplementation and other adjunctive therapies with lifestyle interventions may further enhance cognitive and physical health outcomes [ 76 ], ultimately improving the quality of life for this vulnerable population. DISCUSSION Recent findings from the BedMed and BedMed-Frail studies [ 84 ] indicate that for hypertensive patients without a history of glaucoma, there is no significant difference in the overall efficacy between morning and evening administration of antihypertensive medications. These results suggest that the timing of medication should prioritize patient adherence, as adherence plays a central role in achieving effective BP control. However, these findings do not diminish the importance of personalized treatment, particularly for hypertensive patients with specific BP rhythm abnormalities or comorbidities, such as OSA, GON, cognitive decline, and SCI [ 20 ]. These subgroups may experience unique challenges due to their BP patterns, such as nondipper, reverse-dipper, or extreme dipper rhythms, which may require individualized adjustment of medication timing to prevent further worsening of BP rhythm abnormalities and to mitigate comorbidity risks [ 85 ]. The management of comorbidities remains a critical component of optimizing outcomes in hypertensive patients. However, current research lacks a systematic and in-depth analysis of the characteristics of patients with different BP patterns and their associated comorbidities [ 86 ]. This limitation highlights a significant gap: conclusions drawn from studies on the general hypertensive population may not be fully applicable to these specific subgroups. For example, OSA is often associated with nondipper or reverse-dipper BP patterns, while extreme dippers may face risks such as cerebral hypoperfusion and ischemic damage, particularly in conditions like GON or SCI. Addressing these differences requires tailored strategies that are informed by BP rhythm and comorbidity profiles. In addition to pharmacological approaches, integrating nonpharmacological interventions, such as physical exercise, nutritional support, and adherence-enhancing programs, is essential for managing comorbidities and improving overall health. This is particularly important in frail, elderly patients, where combining lifestyle adjustments with pharmacological treatment has been shown to significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy and improve patient adherence [ 84 , 85 ]. Future directions and clinical implications While adherence remains the cornerstone of hypertension management, the timing of antihypertensive medication should be tailored to individual BP patterns and comorbidities to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. Personalized treatment strategies are indispensable for addressing the unique challenges posed by subgroups with specific BP rhythm abnormalities or comorbidities. For example, OSA patients may benefit from a different medication timing strategy compared to those with extreme dipper patterns or SCI. Such tailored approaches can help restore normal BP rhythms, prevent target organ damage, and reduce adverse outcomes. Future research should focus on stratified patient management, investigating the complex interplay between BP rhythms, comorbidities, and medication timing. This will help refine treatment strategies for specific subgroups and ensure that recommendations better address the needs of diverse patient populations. Moreover, integrating pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions into a comprehensive management strategy has the potential to improve cardiovascular outcomes and enhance the overall quality of life for hypertensive patients, particularly those with complex clinical conditions. Finally, this review recognizes its inherent limitations. Unlike systematic reviews or meta-analyses, it does not follow a predefined methodology for literature selection or quality assessment, which may affect the generalizability of its findings. The heterogeneity of the included studies, such as differences in study designs, populations, and outcomes, further limits the ability to make definitive conclusions. Additionally, robust data for specific subgroups, such as patients with abnormal BP rhythms or multiple comorbidities, remain scarce. Despite these limitations, this narrative review highlights the importance of personalized treatment strategies, identifies gaps in current research, and provides a foundation for future high-quality studies.
