Background: The tribal population in India is characterized by low socioeconomic status and facing issues like marginalization, discrimination, cultural disruption, and displacement. These challenges have significant effects on their mental health, but research on psychiatric conditions among them is limited. Aim: We aim to find existing psychiatry literature and to estimate the prevalence of depression and anxiety among them. Methods: We carried out systematic searches across several databases like PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane, EBSCOhost, ProQuest (Health research collections), PsycNet, Ovid, and Google Scholar, without limiting the publication period. Results: Our search yielded 107 relevant articles, which included systematic reviews, prevalence studies, review articles, scale development studies, intervention studies, qualitative and miscellaneous studies. The prevalence of mental health disorders varied across the studies with depression ranging from 3% to 43%, anxiety upto 8%, mania from 0.04% to 0.39%, schizophrenia from 0.07% to 0.52%, intellectual disability from 0.1% to 0.6%, epilepsy from 0.2% to 1.03%, and dementia or cognitive impairment from 0% to 42.92%. Meta-analysis, of studies on depression, 15 studies were included, and the pooled prevalence was found to be 14% (with a confidence interval of 8% to 22%). For anxiety disorders, 8 studies showed a pooled prevalence of 3% (confidence interval 0% to 7%). In conclusion, this systematic review found 107 relevant studies. However, in the remaining northeastern states of Assam, Manipur, Sikkim, and Tripura, tribal people constitute 10–40% of the population.[ 3 ] The Human Development Index of tribal communities is comparatively lower with 40.6% of the tribal population living below the poverty line and about 41% of the tribal population in India being illiterate.[ 2 ] Even though a vast diversity exists among the different tribal communities in India, they share a commonality of having social marginalization, discrimination, and poverty. The tribal population suffers the burden of various health problems, malnutrition, mental health, and addictions complicated by poor health-seeking behavior. The tribal community lags behind the national average on several health indicators, with women and children being the most vulnerable, a greater burden of morbidity and mortality, and limited access to health care services.[ 4 ] The tribal population also faces multiple burdens of diseases like malnutrition; communicable diseases like malaria (46% of total malaria cases, 70% of P. falciparum)[ 4 ] and tuberculosis (703 cases per 100,000 against 256 in the nontribal population) continue to be rampant.[ 5 ] The National Mental Health Survey (2016) estimated that one in every ten Indians is suffering from some form of mental disorder.[ 6 ] However, tribal populations suffer a disproportionate burden of both communicable and noncommunicable diseases, along with significant undernutrition.[ 7 ] Studies have reported a higher mortality of 2.6% among indigenous populations compared to 2.2% among nonindigenous populations.[ 8 ] Furthermore, the tribal population may have greater vulnerability to mental health issues for multiple reasons – the impact of rapid urbanization and social changes altering their lifestyles, beliefs, and community living. Prevalence estimates from past research hint at increased alcohol and substance use, high suicide rates, risk of depression, and intimate partner violence. However, there are gaps in knowledge about the burden and nature of mental disorders among the tribal communities.[ 9 10 ] One of the earlier systematic reviews was limited to community-based studies within four databases from 1990 to 2021.[ 10 ] Yet another study reviewed databases from 1980 to 2018.[ 9 ] However, tribal identity was not certain in a few included studies, and none of these two systematic reviews performed meta-analysis for any psychiatric condition. One bibliometric analysis was done for health-related research on tribal populations. It mostly found research related to traditional medicine, nutrition, or noncommunicable disease, and only seven studies were related to behavioral health out of 395 studies.[ 11 ] It becomes pertinent to gather information and to study the mental health status in the tribal populations of India. Therefore, we planned this review to update and summarize all psychiatric research studies related to the tribal population of India to mark the actual status of tribal psychiatry in India, to understand the deficits, and to help in future scientific research in this area. METHODOLOGY The primary aim of this systematic review was to investigate the existing literature on tribal psychiatry in India. Additionally, we planned a possible meta-analysis of the prevalence of psychiatric disorders among the tribal population of India. Specifically, we sought to answer the following research questions: What psychiatric research has been conducted on mental health issues among Indian tribal communities to date? What is the prevalence of various psychiatric disorders, specifically depression and anxiety, among the tribal population in India? Eligibility criteria: A comprehensive protocol was developed before commencing this systematic review. The protocol was prepared and finalized in consultation and discussion with all authors to ensure adherence to prespecified methodologies. The inclusion criteria were any psychiatry-related studies done. The study population should be defined as tribals either with or without nontribal controls. The studies should have been done among the tribal population of India. The exclusion was for case reports and letters to editors. Information Sources: To identify relevant studies, we conducted systematic searches across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Sciences, Cochrane Database, EBSCO host, Pro Quest (Health research premier collections), PsycNet (PsycINFO), Ovid, and Google Scholar. Search strategy: The search terms included combinations of keywords related to tribal communities, psychiatry, psychiatric diagnosis, mental health, and India. We used exact keywords, and a combination of keywords is provided with a supplementary file . The search was conducted from inception to July 2024 and was limited to English-language publications. Selection process: Two researchers (AKB and AN) did all these searches independently, only according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, cross-references of most of the studies were checked by two researchers, BK and PP. The methodology and search terms were planned and executed for the prevalence of depression and anxiety. In addition, we manually screened the reference lists of included studies, government websites, and websites of NGOs working with tribal populations in India and contacted experts in the field and the chairperson of tribal psychiatry of the Indian Psychiatric Society for additional references. Data collection process: All six authors (AKB, AN, PP, BK, AK, and DS) independently selected literature-extracted data and cross-checked each other’s data extraction. Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all identified records to assess their eligibility for full-text review. Data items: We picked up every article dealing with the tribal population of India, within domains of tribal psychiatry, whether qualitative or quantitative or review papers. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Full-text articles were retrieved for all potentially eligible articles and assessed against the inclusion criteria [PRISMA flow chart: Figure 1 ]. We categorized all selected studies into carefully thought subgroups, like 1. Review papers, 2. Prevalence, 3. Interventional, 4. Qualitative, 5. Interventional 6. Scale development, and 6. Miscellaneous studies. A standardized data extraction form was created to capture relevant information from the included studies within every subgroup of studies. This information included study characteristics, participant demographics, mental health assessment tools, intervention details, and outcomes. Study risk of bias assessment: The methodological quality and risk of bias in each study were assessed using established tools, such as the methodological quality of the included studies, which was evaluated by “The JBI Quality Assessment Tool for Prevalence Studies” by three reviewers (PP, DS, and AK). Any discrepancies with scoring were resolved with discussion among all reviewers. If any required information was missing or unclear, then we tried to contact the author of the original study through emails and telephone for clarification of facts, which was incorporated in our findings. For cross-sectional, observational prevalence subgroup of studies, we recorded the total sample size (denominator) and showed the percentage or number of diagnosed conditions (numerator). Synthesis Methods: Data synthesis involved a narrative summary of the findings from the included studies. We categorized the results according to the research questions and themes identified during the review. A quantitative meta-analysis was conducted to estimate pooled effect sizes for the prevalence of depression and anxiety. Statistical Analysis: We computed the pooled proportion of the presence of diagnosed depression and separately with diagnosed anxiety disorder. The pooling proportion from raw cell counts was done using Freeman–Tukey double Arcsine transformation and the exact confidence interval for the individual studies. The exact method was used for computing a 95% confidence interval. Heterogeneity between the studies was examined by Higgins I 2 statistics in which heterogeneity of more than 50% was considered high. We used the metareg command in STATA software version 13 (Stata Corp. 2013. Stata Statistical Software: Release 13. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP). We also carried out subgroup and meta-regression analyses to examine whether the source of heterogeneity could be found. Meta-regression across older and newer publications and JBI quality of observational study was also done. Ethical Considerations : We registered with PROSPERO [CRD42024526891. Dated 31.03.2024) for the prevalence of depression and anxiety. RESULTS For this review, we searched nine databases and Google and in an attempt not to miss any tribal-related research, we also contacted many resource centers, tribal ministry websites, various libraries, and concerned persons. It all accumulated a total of 11,546 articles ( Figure 1 : Prisma flow chart), with obvious ineligibility and duplication removal of 6688 articles, which were removed and further screened by title of 4858 articles; with abstract screening, it reduced to 373, and finally, full article review extracted 107 articles found to be associated with psychiatric research among tribal populations of India. We categorized the types of studies as follows: A. Systematic reviews (n = 2), B. Prevalence Studies (n = 43), C. Reviews (n = 3), D. Scale Development (n = 2), E. Interventional Studies (n = 5), F. Qualitative Studies (n = 14), and G. Miscellaneous others (n = 38) studies [ Table 1 ]. Systematic reviews: We found two systematic reviews related to mental health among the tribal population of India.[ 9 10 ] In 2020, Devarapalli et al. [ 9 ] conducted a systematic review of Mental health research on scheduled tribes in India. They found 32 relevant studies, and out of these, five studies were about the consumption of alcohol and opioids. Other studies were about common mental disorders and sociocultural aspects. Yet another systematic review, “A systematic review of community-based studies on mental health issues among tribal populations in India”, was conducted by Verma et al .[ 10 ] in the year 2022. This review included studies from 1990 to May 2021, and this review found 11 studies, out of which seven studies were about alcohol use disorder showing a pooled prevalence of 40%. Other studies were about suicidal attempts, anxiety, depression, and other mental health conditions. Prevalence Studies: We found 43 prevalence-related studies [ Table 2 ]. There are studies with the prevalence of multiple diagnostic categories, whereas many are about the prevalence of addictive substance use. There are heterogeneous methodologies and tools used for these studies. We tabulated all studies with important features, findings, and JBI ratings to determine their quality [ Table 2 ]. The maximum number of prevalence studies was for the prevalence of depression (n = 15), and for the prevalence of anxiety, we found eight studies. Therefore, we carried out a meta-analysis of the prevalence of depression and anxiety. Prevalence studies for the other diagnostic categories were very few; hence, they were not considered for meta-analysis. These studies were as follows: Dementia/mild cognitive impairment (n = 5) ranged from 0 to 42.92%, the cumulative total sample size (denominator) was 4070, and the number of diagnosed conditions (numerator) was 132 (3.24%). For schizophrenia, four studies found a range from 0.07% to 0.52%. The cumulative numerator/denominator was 9/5579 = 0.16%. For mania, five studies found a range from 0.04% to 0.39%. The cumulative numerator/denominator was 10/6350 = 0.16%. For mental retardation, we found five studies ranging from 0.1% to 0.6%, and the cumulative numerator/denominator was 28/6350 = 0.44%. For epilepsy, five studies ranged from 0.2% to 1.03%; the cumulative numerator/denominator was 32/6350 = 0.50% [ Table 2 ]. We found 16 studies related to the prevalence of substance use among the Indian tribal population; these studies estimated the prevalence of alcohol in 15 studies, tobacco in 12 studies, Opium in three, and betel quid in two studies [ Table 2 ]. However, most studies assessed only alcohol use rather than abuse or clinically relevant alcohol dependence syndrome, hence not considered for meta-analysis. Reviews: There are review articles addressing tribal populations and belief systems about mental health[ 55 ] and about psychological and mental health challenges among the Dimasa Kacharis tribes.[ 54 ] There are some viewpoints and commentaries that highlight the psychological health burden faced by tribal populations, like culture, gender, inequality,[ 81 ] acculturation,[ 82 ] changing world or displacement,[ 56 ] communicable diseases, noncommunicable diseases, malnutrition, and mental health issues including dissociative states.[ 116 ] The prevalent concern is the deterioration of mental health status among individuals who are marginalized or socially excluded tribal populations. Scale Developments: We found two such studies, the first proposing an emotion scale as an Indian Adivasi subjective wellbeing scale in Hindi and local dialect.[ 57 ] Another study was about developing a cognitive screening instrument, which may be suitable for the tribal (Bharmour) elderly (60 years and above) population of Himachal Pradesh, India.[ 58 ] Interventional Studies: We found five interventional studies. Tripathy et al .[ 59 ] conducted a study to improve maternal birth outcome and depression by participatory group intervention among tribal women who had given birth in three districts of Jharkhand and Orissa, with a sample of 228,186 on 18 clusters by stratified randomization for each intervention strategy to address neonatal mortality rate and maternal depression or control group. NMR was 32% lower in intervention clusters (odds ratio 0·68 at 95%; CI 0·59–0·78) during the 3 years and 45% lower in years 2 and 3 (0·55 at 95%; CI 0·46–0·66). They did not find any significant effect on maternal depression. Mohan and Kumari[ 60 ] carried out a pilot study on the effectiveness of yoga on the positive-negative affect and self-esteem among tribal male adolescents and found significant improvement in positive affect and a significant reduction in negative affect. Koorankot et al .[ 61 ] studied to evaluate solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) and to get scientific knowledge into the applicability of solution-focused practice in Indian tribal community settings with good effect on depression. Sarkar et al .[ 62 ] conducted a study to assess the effect of combined life skills-based health empowerment intervention on the resilience of school-going adolescents in a tribal area. Using a Solomon four-group design, they carried out a quasiexperimental study with 742 teenagers in two Purulia, West Bengal. Three months following the intervention’s conclusion, post-test results on resilience, self-determination, internal health locus of control, and pathological behavior were collected. This study showed that the intervention enhanced resilience, but also internal health locus of control and self-determination were found to be enhanced. Qualitative Studies: We found 14 qualitative studies [ Table 3 ], where most of the studies were focused on psychosocial determinants of alcoholism or betel quid use.[ 64 65 67 68 69 72 ] Other studies were related to subjective wellbeing/QOL,[ 75 78 ] perceived beliefs about the etiology of mental illness,[ 71 73 ] stigma,[ 74 ] and acculturation.[ 66 ] Miscellaneous Studies: In this review, other miscellaneous studies are presented in Table 4 , and the summary table of qualitative studies is detailed in Table 3 . Meta-analysis of Prevalence of Depression. We searched the search engines as described in the methodology section with diagnostic entities independently with all possible psychiatric diagnostic terminology. Finally, in studies reporting the estimation of depression among tribals of India, we found 15 studies with a cumulative population of 26,120, out of which 4022 (15.40%) persons were diagnosed with depression [ Table 2 ]. We carried out a random effect meta-analysis with these 15 studies and found a pooled prevalence of 14% (95% CI = 08% to 22%) with high heterogeneity between studies (I 2 = 99.57%; P = 0.00) [ Figure 2 ]. For subgroup analysis, we categorized one group with studies using the diagnostic schedule (n = 7) and another using rating scales score (n = 8) for diagnosis. With seven studies of diagnostic schedules, the prevalence was 5% (95% CI = 03% to 07%), whereas with rating scales, the prevalence was 25% (95% CI = 20% to 31%) [ Supplementary Figure 1 ]. There were three studies[ 39 41 51 ] that assessed depression among the geriatric population (aged above 60 years), and the pooled prevalence was 13% (95% CI = 03% to 30%); and for the remaining twelve studies, it was 15% (95% CI = 7% to 25%). Meta-analysis of Prevalence of Anxiety We found eight studies reporting the prevalence of anxiety disorders among the tribal population; they ranged from 0.20 to 19.69. The summary of the studies included (n = 8) in the systematic review is shown in Table 2 . Five studies were conducted in West Bengal, and one each in Karnataka, Arunachal Pradesh, and Rajasthan. The sample size of the studies ranged from 218 to 2363. These cross-sectional studies involving a cumulative 7290 participants were included, and the detected cases of anxiety were 178 (2.44%). We carried out a random effect meta-analysis with these eight studies and found a pooled prevalence of anxiety disorders of 03% (95% CI = 00% to 07%) with high heterogeneity between studies (I 2 = 98.52%; P = 0.00) [ Figure 3 ]. DISCUSSION Types of research among tribal populations based on various contexts (global/regional and study designs) : The tribal population is poorly represented in psychiatry research. In the current time of rapid globalization, industrialism, and rapid urban expansion along with political and social marginalization and socioeconomic inequalities, the indigenous population is deprived of health care and healthcare research.[ 8 ] This is reflected by the paucity of studies related to psychological health among the tribal populations; as for the estimated over 100 million Indian indigenous population, we could find only 107 total studies. This is not just an Indian scenario, but it is internationally true;[ 117 ] also, we could not find many studies from many countries across the globe, and there is no information or research or even accurate demographics or health data on the Indigenous population, around the world, specifically Africa, China, South Asia, and the former Soviet Union.[ 118 ] In our review, most of the literature consists of prevalence and observational studies among tribal populations. We could find 43 prevalence studies, 14 qualitative, five interventional studies, and two related to scale development, as well as 36 miscellaneous studies [ Table 1 ]. However, only a few methodologically robust studies were found, and usually, sample sizes were not adequate [ Table 2 ], and this deficit is also concurred by the previous systematic review.[ 9 ] The overall JBI scoring for quality assessment is low (mean 6.34), and for about 27 epidemiological studies, the sample size was less than 1000. Our review found that most studies were cross - sectional and lacked longitudinal prospective design. In India, the government promotes research and tribal population-related projects, but there is a lack of studies on the tribal population; this may be due to a lack of overall interest, limited infrastructure, geographical remoteness, scarcity of healthcare professionals, cultural and language barriers and conceptual framework, appropriately validated tools, and challenges to obtaining coherent, representative samples.[ 119 120 ] Countries like Australia are using a robust population-based birth cohort study for indigenous Australians for the prevalence of psychiatric morbidity.[ 121 ] Prevalence Studies: Prevalence estimates (global/regional etc.): We found that prevalence estimating studies are the most popular, counting 43; however, the results have wide variance. Prevalence rates varied by disorder and ranged from minimum to maximum across studies, which are as follows: depressive disorder (3–43%); anxiety disorders (0–8%); mania (0.04%–0.039%), schizophrenia (0.07–0.52%), mental retardation (0.1–0.6%), epilepsy (0.2–1.03%), and dementia/cognitive impairment (0–42.92%). These prevalence rates (0.1–0.6%) for intellectual disability among the tribal population are strikingly low when compared to a systematic review among the overall Indian population reporting 1% (95% CI 0.5–1.6) ( P &lt;0.001, I 2 =98%).[ 122 ] The prevalence of schizophrenia (0.07–0.52%) also seems lower than that of another Indian study (0.42%).[ 123 ] The tribal population seems resilient to dementia as indicated by one study that found zero tribal participants, but 32 (1.6%) nontribal control found suffering from dementia among 2000 participants.[ 20 ] However, studies are too few to generalize the findings. The difference may also be attributable to older versus newer diagnostic systems and tools/schedule/symptom rating scales. Some of the older studies were done in 1977, 1980, and 1986 and measure the prevalence of common mental disorders, which also included epilepsy, mental retardation and hysteria in addition to depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia-like disorders. For some of the studies, a diagnostic schedule was used, which may be the reason for the low prevalence rate.[ 43 44 45 47 48 ] Many recent studies are fully dedicated to a single diagnostic entity with just one rating scale, which usually leads to a higher estimation of prevalence.[ 39 42 46 52 ] Based on the number of studies, we could proceed with the meta-analysis for the prevalence of depression and anxiety. However, we skipped alcohol “use” and other substance use as most of the studies did not define substance abuse or dependence syndrome. Many studies are conducted with claims like “an epidemiolocal study of tribal area/tribal district/tribal dominated area”, with their mixed tribal nontribal population, and exact distribution of the tribal population not reported.[ 124 ] We even contacted a few authors regarding clarification of such a tribal population, and it was found that data were collected during health camps, and although almost most of the people were tribal, it was not recorded due to some reason; hence, it was not reported.[ 125 ] These studies do not truly represent the tribal population, and we did not include such studies in this review. Prevalence of Depression: Meta-analysis : We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of depression and anxiety. There is very high heterogenicity (I 2 = 99%) across studies which may be attributable to many factors like population difference, methodological issues, and use of different rating scales or diagnostic tools or diagnostic systems. Various tribal groups are not aware of modern concepts of psychological problems. Indigenous peoples live with beliefs and practices among themselves in a unique way, which needs to be understood and assessed for diagnosis and management. For depression, we found 15 studies, out of which two studies included tribal populations, representing only a few states from the whole of India; thus, there is a poor representation of the tribal population of 28 states and eight union territories of India. These studies estimated the prevalence of depression with all methodological quality variation and diagnostic difference/assessment tools being used. The first one was by Nandi et al. in 1977, which reported a prevalence of 6% by using a “Case Detection Schedule”,[ 43 ] whereas newer studies are reporting depression up to 27% among Tribal Migrant Rural Women of Ranchi District in Jharkhand.[ 42 ] The sample size of these studies ranged from 100 to 12,215. These 15 studies screened a cumulative of 26,120 participants, and the detected cases were 4022 (15.40%). Out of these 15, eight studies used rating scales for diagnosis of depression. In contrast, seven studies used interviews or diagnostic schedules. On subgroup analysis, we found a much higher prevalence with rating scales 25% (95% CI = 20% to 31%) in comparison to the prevalence of depression measured by interviews or diagnostic schedules, which was 5% (95% CI = 03% to 07%). This is found to be in concordance with the finding of another systematic review and meta-analysis that estimates derived from symptom scales were significantly higher than estimates from diagnostic instruments.[ 126 ] This highlights the need for appropriately validated diagnostic tools in such a special population. We carried out a meta-regression for the year of publication, and we found a significant increase in the prevalence of depression from older time to recent studies. This perhaps reflects modernization’s effects on the population and/or changing diagnostic systems for depression. The prevalence is found low in comparison to a recent analysis of the elderly (60 years and above) population in India which found a prevalence of depression at 34.4% (95% CI: 29.3–39.7)[ 127 ] and a pooled prevalence of depression among rural adolescents in India, it was 27% (95% CI: 17–38%, I 2 = 99.08%)[ 128 ] but higher than the pooled prevalence of 12.4% (95% CI 9.4–15.9) by Dhiman et al. 2023.[ 129 ] This difference may be attributable to the population itself as our population consisted of a few exclusive geriatric populations, a few child and adolescent populations and mixed-age populations. Also, heterogeneous tools to diagnose and rating scales for depression were used across the studies. However, a systematic review and meta-analysis from America[ 130 ] reported a prevalence of depression or dysthymia among the Indigenous population with control to find the odds ratio of 1.30 [95% CI = 0.76–2.23]. However, heterogenicity (I 2 = 96%) was very high similar to ours meta-analysis and their prevalence rate of depression ranged from 4% to 38%. An Australian systematic review similarly found 4.3–51% of major depressive disorder.[ 131 ] Prevalence of Anxiety: With regard to anxiety disorders, we found only nine studies and with random effect meta-analysis, the prevalence of anxiety was 3% (95% CI = 01% to 09%) among the tribal population of India; however, the I 2 % was 98.48, suggesting high heterogenicity among studies. The prevalence of anxiety disorders seems lower than expected, which could be due to a small number of total studies, older studies, older diagnostic classificatory systems, and the use of diagnostic schedules. This prevalence is lower than that of the previously reported individual study of 8.2% among elderly tribes in India.[ 39 ] We did metaregression for the year of publication and found a significant increase in the prevalence of anxiety disorders from older years to recent years. The estimation of anxiety disorder is found to be low; this may be attributable to the fact that most of the nine studies used interview schedules for diagnosis, in contrast to depression studies where rating scales were used in the majority of the studies. Our study indicates low anxiety (3%) among tribals compared to 11.6% (95% CI 8.1–15.7) reported recently by a meta-analysis of Indian studies.[ 129 ] Our finding is also very low in comparison to studies among the Australian Indigenous population reporting a prevalence of 17.2–58.6% for anxiety disorder.[ 131 ] Addiction-Related Studies: Four qualitative studies with mixed-method design, in-depth interviews, focused group discussions, and qualitative interviews were identified. A study conducted in South India[ 67 ] finds that initiation of alcohol use at a younger age is attributable to parental influence, home environment, and peer pressure. Another qualitative analysis[ 33 ] on school children attributed the easy availability of tobacco, as a means of cleaning teeth, and the local practice of collecting mahua tree flowers for local alcohol. Alcohol use was shown to be troublesome in a study conducted on the Paniya tribe in Kerala,[ 72 ] particularly in the younger population. The problem is further compounded by the easy access to both licit and illicit liquor. Research on the prevalence of alcohol usage differed across different studies and different states ranging from 16% to 67%. Similarly, tobacco usage as chewer/smokeless tobacco use ranged from 22.8% to 41.8%, for smokers, 2.9% to 18.8%, and the overall tobacco use ranged from 24% to 84% across various studies. The study on opium use was limited to Arunachal Pradesh, where the prevalence was 4.8%, and on betel quid use, which was done in Kerala, where the prevalence was 64% [ Table 2 ]. Strengths and limitations: The strength of our review lies in its comprehensive inclusion of studies on mental health conducted among tribal populations in India, searching nine databases. We included studies on every mental health condition we could find, covering cross-sectional observational, qualitative, and interventional studies presented in tables. We were also able to carry out a meta-analysis of the prevalence of depression and anxiety. However, we found very high heterogeneity I 2 and tried subgroup analysis across various diagnostic schedules and rating scales and found large differences. Due to the limited research available on tribal populations, we felt it was necessary to include as many studies as possible; still, we could have missed a few studies due to certain search terms or some other reasons. Unfortunately, there were a few articles on the prevalence of certain psychiatric conditions, resulting in limited implications. There might have been more elaborative discussion, but for the sake of word limits and relevance, we skipped some discussion. Barriers/Limitations and Future Guidelines in psychiatric research among tribes in India: Research in psychiatric health among tribal populations faces several unique barriers that can make it challenging to conduct effective and culturally sensitive studies. A linguistic barrier is there if researchers do not speak the local language or understand the dialects, ensuring that participants fully understand the research and consent to participate is challenging, especially when literacy levels are low among them or when there is a lack of trust in outsiders. Many tribal communities live in remote areas with limited access to healthcare facilities, making it difficult for researchers to reach participants and for participants to access follow-up care.[ 36 ] In addition to language, standard diagnostic tools and questionnaires need to be culturally appropriate or relevant to the experiences of tribal populations. It is suggested that training and employing persons from that particular tribal community and translation and validation studies of psychometric tools may resolve these issues. High levels of poverty in tribal communities create economic pressures attributing to paucity of participation/turning up for follow-up in research. There is often a lack of baseline data on mental health in tribal populations, making it difficult to design studies and interventions that are well targeted. Often, research is conducted without adequate input from the community, leading to studies that do not reflect the community’s needs or priorities.[ 11 ] This results in low participation rates and research findings, which is not actionable or relevant to the community. Some psychiatric conditions may be expressed differently across cultures. The concept of a symptom may vary from culture to culture, making diagnosis and assessment challenging. It is suggested that governments, organizations, and researchers should promote cross-cultural adaptation of population-based screening standard tools.[ 132 ] Mental health in tribal communities is often intertwined with issues such as substance abuse, domestic violence, and historical trauma. Addressing these intersections can be complex and requires a holistic approach that goes beyond traditional psychiatric research methods. Incorporation of culturally safe care to regain dignity and improve health outcomes is needed for indigenous populations.[ 132 ] Furthermore, we need improvement in the quantity and quality of mental health research. There is a need to emphasize a need of research guidelines specifically for tribal populations in the Indian context; for example, even the framing of the title and abstract should include words like TRIBE and INDIA. We may be missing out on many studies, which were titled similar to “BHILS of RAJASTHAN”. We finally accommodated names of all Indian states in our search terms, but there are over 700 tribes in India, and each tribe’s name cannot be accommodated in search terms. Another important consideration should be the definition of population as we found a good number of studies quoting samples that were collected/titled as “tribal-dominated areas/tribal districts”[ 124 ] and tribal villages. Still, nowadays, almost every village/area or district is a demographically mixed population with varying ratios. Hence, it is critical to provide the exact demographic identity of the study participants. It is suggested to develop Psychiatry-related Research Guidelines for the tribal population in India, tool translation, and validation. Another important step may be to maintain a tribal research repository on psychiatric and related issues by the Indian Psychiatric Society or in collaboration with other government and semigovernment agencies for instantly updated information for researchers and policymakers. CONCLUSION This systematic review found 107 tribal psychiatry-related research in India, including two systematic reviews, 43 prevalence studies, three reviews, two scale development, fived interventional studies, 14 qualitative studies, and 38 miscellaneous studies. The ranges of prevalence rates among these studies were: depressive disorder (3–43%), anxiety disorders (0–8%), mania (0.04%– 0.039%), schizophrenia (0.07–0.52%), mental retardation (0.1–0.6%), epilepsy (0.2–1.03%), and dementia/cognitive impairment (0–42.92%). On a random effect meta-analysis, the prevalence of depression and anxiety was found to be 14% (95% CI = 08% to 22%) and 03% (95% CI = 00% to 07%), respectively. We felt the need to improve tribal psychiatry-related research qualitatively and quantitively, for which specific research guidelines, tool translation/validation, and a tribal research repository are needed.
