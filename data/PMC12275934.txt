Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition with a complex genetic basis. Large-scale whole-exome sequencing and whole-genome sequencing studies, with increasing sample sizes and improved ancestral diversity, have significantly advanced the discovery of ASD-associated genes. In addition to identifying coding variants, whole-genome sequencing has facilitated the detection of risk noncoding variants in regulatory elements such as enhancers, promoters, and untranslated regions, prompting experimental validation of their functional impact on neurodevelopment. A deeper understanding of ASD genetic liability has revealed the interplay between rare and common variants. Moreover, genetic liability varies by sex and phenotype profile, underscoring the complexity of ASD’s genetic architecture. While the clinical application of these genomic insights remains in early stages, progress has been made in gene-based therapeutic development, the interpretation of noncoding risk variants, and the use of polygenic score for risk stratification. In this review, we summarize key findings from large-scale genomic studies, explore the role of coding and noncoding variants in ASD, and discuss emerging opportunities for translating these discoveries into clinical practice. Early microarray technologies enabled copy number variant (CNV) analysis, identifying recurrent de novo CNVs, which underscored the significant contribution of de novo variants (DNVs) to ASD risk. In parallel, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) revealed the role of common inherited single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in a polygenic contribution to ASD liability. The advent of next-generation sequencing enabled the identification of rare and low-frequency genetic variants, including single-nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) studies revealed an enrichment of de novo single nucleotide variants (SNVs) in ASD cases, particularly protein-truncating variants (PTVs) in constrained genes that are highly intolerant to loss-of-function mutations ( Satterstrom et al., 2020 ). In addition, rare inherited variants were also found to contribute to ASD risk, though their individual effects are more subtle. By leveraging these deleterious variants, researchers have successfully identified numerous ASD-associated genes ( Fu et al., 2022 , Satterstrom et al., 2020 ), advancing gene discovery. However, WES is inherently limited to protein-coding regions, 1.5% of the genome, leaving the vast majority of the noncoding genome unexplored. The development of large-scale whole-genome sequencing (WGS) datasets with high coverage has addressed these limitations by enabling genome-wide analysis of both coding and noncoding regions. WGS has identified rare noncoding variants in regulatory elements, such as enhancers and promoters ( An et al., 2018 , Kim et al., 2024d , Werling et al., 2018 ), as well as short tandem repeats (STRs), implicated in ASD risk ( Kim et al., 2024a , Trost et al., 2020 ). Beyond rare variants, WGS has also advanced the study of polygenic scores (PS), which quantify the cumulative effect of multiple common variants associated with ASD ( Grove et al., 2019 ). By integrating these diverse genetic factors, WGS provides a more comprehensive understanding of ASD liability ( Kim et al., 2024c ), bridging the gap between rare high-impact mutations and common polygenic influences on ASD risk. In this review, we summarize key findings from large-scale genomic studies, focusing on insights derived from WES and WGS datasets. We explore advancements in ASD gene discovery, deeper insights into genetic liability, and approaches to maximize clinical applications. By synthesizing recent progress in ASD genetics, we highlight the evolving landscape of ASD research and its biological implications for risk prediction, diagnosis, and therapeutic strategies. LARGE-SCALE GENOMIC DATASETS FOR ASD STUDY The advent of next-generation sequencing, particularly WES, has significantly advanced our understanding of ASD genetics. Early WES studies identified the critical role of de novo PTVs in ASD ( Iossifov et al., 2012 , Neale et al., 2012 , O'Roak et al., 2012 , Sanders et al., 2012 ), providing the first large-scale genetic insights into the disorder. To further refine de novo association analyses and improve gene discovery, the Autism Sequencing Consortium was established ( Buxbaum et al., 2012 ). This consortium facilitated the collection of large WES datasets and pioneered the development of statistical model, transmission and de novo association (TADA) ( He et al., 2013 ). TADA is a Bayesian framework that identifies genes with a higher mutation burden than expected by incorporating de novo PTVs and missense variants within a mutational model. The first application of TADA to the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) ( Fischbach and Lord, 2010 ) WES dataset led to the identification of 33 ASD-associated genes (False discovery rate, FDR ≤ 0.1) ( De Rubeis et al., 2014 ). Subsequent efforts focused on integrating previously published WES datasets with SSC WES data, culminating in the identification of 65 ASD risk genes (FDR ≤ 0.1) ( Sanders et al., 2015 ). These efforts continued over the years, resulting in a large-scale analysis of 35,584 WES samples in 2020. Expanding TADA to include rare inherited variants, this study identified 102 ASD-associated genes (FDR ≤ 0.1) ( Fig. 1 A) ( Satterstrom et al., 2020 ). Fu et al (2022) further expanded this dataset by incorporating WES data from the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research (SPARK) cohort ( SPARK, 2018 ), bringing the total to 63,237 individuals. TADA was further extended to incorporate CNVs, leading to the identification of 72 ASD genes (FDR ≤ 0.001) ( Fig. 1 A) ( Fu et al., 2022 ). The Autism Speaks MSSNG initiated large-scale WGS efforts ( Yuen et al., 2017 , Yuen et al., 2016 ), followed by similar efforts from SSC ( An et al., 2018 , Turner et al., 2017 , Werling et al., 2018 , Zhou et al., 2019 ) and Autism Genetic Resource Exchange ( Ruzzo et al., 2019 ). Trost et al. (2022) consolidated the WGS datasets of MSSNG, SSC, and SPARK and identified 53 ASD risk genes (FDR ≤ 0.001) by analyzing 20,517 samples. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses further confirmed that many of these risk genes show consistent enrichment in neuronal lineages, reinforcing their neurodevelopmental relevance ( Kim et al., 2024b ) ( Fig. 1 A). As genomic research advances toward clinical applications, ensuring greater ancestral diversity has become increasingly critical ( Popejoy and Fullerton, 2016 ). However, over 90% of participants in SSC, SPARK, and MSSNG studies are of European ancestry, raising concerns about the broader applicability of findings. To address this gap, new cohorts have emerged, expanding ASD genomic research beyond European population. The Chinese ASD cohort analyzed WES data from 1141 families, identifying 22 ASD genes, including novel gene SLC35G1 followed by functional validation in a mouse model (FDR ≤ 0.1) ( Wang et al., 2023 ). More recently, Kim et al. applied a sex-stratified TADA analysis to 4885 females with ASD and 19,160 males with ASD, incorporating the Korean ASD cohort with SSC, and SPARK cohorts. They identified 98 genes enriched exclusively in females and 461 enriched exclusively in males with ASD (FDR ≤ 0.05) ( Kim et al., 2024c ) ( Fig. 1 A). Beyond East Asia, ASD genomic research has expanded into admixed populations. The Genomics of Autism in Latin American Ancestries Consortium has assembled 15,427 individuals, including 14,152 WES and 207 WGS datasets ( Avila et al., 2025 ). This effort identified 61 ASD-associated genes, some of which have not been previously reported (FDR ≤ 0.1) ( Fig. 1 A). FUNCTIONAL CHARACTERISTICS OF ASD RISK GENES Building upon gene discovery efforts from large-scale genomic studies, significant advances have been made in uncovering the functional consequences of ASD risk genes at the molecular and cellular levels. Early genomic investigations of ASD identified recurrent de novo CNVs in synaptic genes such as NRXN1 , NLGN3 , SHANK2/3 , and SYNGAP1 ( Marshall et al., 2008 , Pinto et al., 2010 , Sanders et al., 2011 , Szatmari et al., 2007 ). Subsequent large-scale exome sequencing revealed a significant excess of de novo PTVs in genes governing neuronal communication (NC) and gene expression regulation (GER) ( De Rubeis et al., 2014 , Satterstrom et al., 2020 ). Network analyses of prenatal human brain transcriptomes showed that high-confidence de novo ASD genes coexpress in layer 5/6 cortical projection neurons of the midfetal prefrontal cortex ( Willsey et al., 2013 ). In parallel, Parikshak et al. (2013) identified ASD gene enrichment in developmental coexpression modules associated with transcriptional regulation during early corticogenesis and synaptic function during late fetal development. Coexpression network analysis further demonstrated that a synaptic gene module enriched for ASD susceptibility genes is consistently suppressed in ASD, while an immune-glial module is upregulated ( Parikshak et al., 2016 , Voineagu et al., 2011 ). Single-cell RNA-seq recapitulated transcriptional disruptions in excitatory neurons and microglia associated with ASD ( Velmeshev et al., 2019 ). The convergence of ASD risk on NC and GER pathways during early neurodevelopment was further replicated with rare inherited variants ( Kim and An, 2020 ). GER-associated ASD genes largely regulate early transcriptional programs that shape cortical development. For example, ARID1B is a component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex and modulates chromatin accessibility in deep-layer excitatory neurons and interneurons ( Fazel Darbandi et al., 2024 ). FOXP1 , which regulates neuronal gene expression as well, converges with ARID1B at shared genomic targets in the fetal cortex ( Fazel Darbandi et al., 2024 ). Another transcription factor, TBR1 , has also been shown to participate in the same midgestational transcriptional network regulating neuronal gene expression ( Fazel Darbandi et al., 2024 ). NC-related genes operate later, influencing axon targeting, synaptic organization, and intracellular signaling. SHANK3 encodes a postsynaptic scaffold protein that anchors glutamate receptors and other signaling molecules, and its haploinsufficiency results in ASD phenotypes ( Qin et al., 2018 ). PTEN loss of function induces synaptic overgrowth and abnormal circuitry via mTOR dysregulation ( Huang et al., 2016 ). CNTNAP2 affects synaptic vesicle cycling and mitochondrial dynamics, converging on mTOR- and ubiquitin-linked pathways ( Jang et al., 2023 ). TRIO activation governs actin remodeling through Rac1 activation, and BRAF, a RAS-MAPK component, modulates neuronal differentiation and dendritic arborization ( Kang et al., 2025 , Sadybekov et al., 2017 ). Single-cell transcriptomic studies refine these temporal and cellular distinctions. GER genes are enriched in neural progenitors and early postmitotic neurons, whereas NC genes peak in maturing excitatory neurons ( Satterstrom et al., 2020 ), consistent with the previous finding ( Parikshak et al., 2013 ). Comparing ASD and developmental disorder (DD) cohorts revealed ASD-predominant genes (eg, FOXP1 , DYRK1A ) are most abundant in maturing neurons, and DD-predominant genes (eg, KMT2A , ARID1B ) are the highest in progenitors, indicating that the timing and target cell types of gene disruption shape clinical outcomes ( Fu et al., 2022 ). Trajectory-based analyses visualize these temporal shifts across excitatory and inhibitory lineages ( Kim et al., 2024b ) ( Fig. 1 B and C). A critical next step is to determine whether these temporally staged disruptions can be functionally modeled in a controlled experimental system. Traditional approaches have relied on animal models to characterize molecular and cellular effects, but recent advances in patient-derived cortical organoids have expanded the experimental toolkit, enabling the modeling of early corticogenesis in vitro. Organoids carrying SYNGAP1 , FMR1 , or 22q11.2 deletions reproduce gene-specific defects in neurogenesis and cortical patterning ( Birtele et al., 2023 , Kang et al., 2021 , Khan et al., 2020 ). CRISPR screens targeting 36 ASD genes in isogenic organoids have revealed convergent impairments in neurogenesis ( Li et al., 2023 ). Moreover, knockout of high-confidence genes such as CHD8, ARID1B, SHANK3, and PTEN induces lineage-specific and circuit-level abnormalities, while perturbation of chromatin regulators (MECP2 , SUV420H1 , and CTCF ) and transcription factors ( GLI3, TP53 , and PAX6 ) disrupts cortical lamination, progenitor proliferation, and synaptic maturation ( Fleck et al., 2023 , Lindenhofer et al., 2024 , Paulsen et al., 2022 , Villa et al., 2022 , Wang et al., 2022 , Zhang et al., 2024 ). While these models have provided rich mechanistic insights into individual genes, a broader survey of 185 ASD-associated genes curated by Fu et al. revealed that only a subset has been experimentally examined. Specifically, 58 of 72 high-confidence genes (FDR ≤ 0.001) and 79 of the remaining 113 genes (FDR ≤ 0.05) have reported functional studies in systems such as mouse, induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC), or organoids ( Fig. 2 ), indicating that a substantial fraction of ASD candidate genes remain untested in experimental contexts. RISK NONCODING VARIANTS OF ASD Noncoding regions of the genome play a critical role in gene regulation, particularly during brain development, by housing key regulatory elements such as promoters, enhancers, untranslated regions (UTRs), and transcription factor–binding sites. The advent of WGS has enabled researchers to analyze noncoding rare and DNVs that were previously undetectable with microarray or WES. Given the strong association between DNVs and ASD, early investigations focused on identifying noncoding DNVs enriched in ASD cases. Yuen et al. (2016) conducted one of the first WGS studies in ASD, analyzing 200 ASD trios from the MSSNG project and comparing them to 250 control trios from a Dutch cohort. They found a significant enrichment of noncoding DNVs in 5' and 3' UTRs, exon-skipping junctions, and DNase I hypersensitive sites. Turner et al. (2017) extended this by analyzing 516 ASD families in the SSC, reporting enrichment of DNVs in 3′ UTRs of ASD probands compared with unaffected siblings. Further regulatory annotation using tools such as GERP and ENCODE ( Davydov et al., 2010 , Kundaje et al., 2015 ) revealed that these variants frequently resided in evolutionarily conserved elements and active regulatory regions such as transcription start sites and strong enhancers in embryonic stem cells. Given the importance of functional annotations in interpreting noncoding signals, Werling et al. (2018) introduced category-wide association studies, a framework to test the de novo noncoding associations across all combinations of functional annotations. While the initial analysis of 519 SSC families did not identify a statistically significant category, a follow-up analysis with 1902 families found enriched DNVs in conserved promoter regions ( An et al., 2018 ). More refined analyses incorporating single-cell regulatory maps later revealed significant enrichment in inhibitory neurons and transcription factor– binding sites in conserved region ( Kim et al., 2024d ). Similarly, Zhou et al. (2019) applied deep-learning-based annotation to 1790 SSC families and found that noncoding DNVs in transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory elements were enriched in ASD probands. Ruzzo et al. (2019) analyzed 2308 individuals from Autism Genetic Resource Exchange families and found limited enrichment of rare noncoding variants in promoters at the genome-wide level but found recurrent deletions affecting the promoters of NR3C2 and DLG2 . Building on these efforts, Kim et al. (2022) explored the spatial regulatory consequences of noncoding mutations by integrating WGS data from 242 Korean ASD families along with replication in 2419 additional families from SSC and MSSNG. By leveraging chromatin conformation data from developing human brains, they identified noncoding ASD risk variants that significantly disrupt enhancer-promoter interactions. These disrupted chromatin loops were enriched in neurodevelopmental genes and associated with transcriptional dysregulation in cortical tissue, underscoring the critical role of 3D genome architecture in ASD pathogenesis. Another significant advancement in the noncoding association research is the identification of STRs as contributors to ASD risk. STRs constitute approximately 6.8% of the human genome and influence regulatory sequences and gene expression ( Hannan, 2018 ). Trost et al. (2020) analyzed WGS data from 17,231 individuals from ASD families and identified 2588 loci where rare STRs were enriched in ASD cases compared with unaffected siblings. These rare STRs collectively explained 2.6% of ASD risk and were associated with lower IQ and adaptive ability. These expansions were found in neuromuscular genes, including DMPK and FXN , as well as new ASD-related loci such as FGF14 and CACNB1 . Another study by Mitra et al. (2021) developed a novel algorithm to detect de novo STRs in 6548 ASD family members and found a genome-wide excess of de novo STRs in ASD probands. This led to a total 4% contribution of STRs to ASD risk when combined with rare inherited STRs. A more recent Korean cohort study by Kim et al. (2024a) integrated postmortem single-cell RNA and ATAC-seq data, identifying STR expansions in early cortical layer–specific neurodevelopmental genes. These expansions were predicted by deep-learning models ( Chen et al., 2022 ) to disrupt enhancer/promoter activity and were associated with more severe ASD phenotypes and reduced adaptability. Consistent with the earlier findings ( Trost et al., 2020 ), rare STRs were associated with more severe ASD phenotypes and diminished adaptability, with expansions occurring in DMPK . As evidence of noncoding variant contributions to ASD risk continues to grow, experimental efforts have accelerated to validate their neurodevelopmental impacts ( Table 1 ). Initial validation efforts focused on noncoding DNVs. Plassmeyer et al. (2023) assessed 997 de novo 5′ UTR variants found in ASD patients ( An et al., 2018 ) using massively parallel reporter assays (MPRA) and demonstrated cell-type-specific translational effects. Notably, mutations in LRRC4 and ZNF644 were found to significantly alter translational activity in neurons. Nakamura et al. (2024) analyzed de novo promoter variants from 19,227 individuals in ASD families and, while no overall enrichment was observed, found enrichment in ASD-associated topologically associating domains. Validation using iPSC models further confirmed transcriptomic alterations within these topologically associating domains. Rare inherited noncoding variants have also been functionally probed. Shin et al. (2024b) analyzed &gt;16,600 ASD family members and focused on 3 categories of noncoding regulatory regions: human accelerated regions, experimentally validated neural VISTA enhancers, and conserved regions predicted to act as neural enhancers. They confirmed that rare inherited variants in these regions were significantly more frequent in ASD cases. Additionally, screening 1649 ASD-associated variants with synthesized MPRA identified 12 variants that significantly affect enhancer activity. More recently, the potential role of common variants has also gained interest. Rummel et al. (2023) functionally characterized common noncoding variants from schizophrenia GWAS through a 3-step experimental pipeline. They screened over 30,000 candidate SNPs using MPRA in iPSC-derived neural progenitors, followed by CRISPR interference to perturb 620 enhancer elements in human and mouse neurons. CROP-seq analysis revealed cell-type-specific transcriptional effects, including a variant (rs324017) near LRP1 that altered neuronal excitability. LRP1 has also been implicated in ASD through multiple independent WES and WGS studies, where de novo and rare inherited variants were found to be enriched in ASD probands ( Torrico et al., 2019 , Tuncay et al., 2023 ). Lee et al. (2025) performed MPRA on 17,841 SNPs from cross-disorder GWAS datasets of 8 psychiatric disorders, including ASD, identifying 683 expression-modulating variants. These pleiotropic variants altered chromatin accessibility across neuronal cell types and modified transcription factor–binding motifs involved in highly connected protein-protein interaction networks. CRISPR perturbation experiments further validated the functional impact of these variants on neuronal protein networks. Kosicki et al. (2024) conducted a similar MPRA study on GWAS SNPs of 4 psychiatric disorders, including ASD, but no strong MPRA activity was observed. Instead, they found MPRA effects in 777 out of 23,266 synthetic variants, many of which influenced transgenic assay activity and exhibited additional ectopic effects. UNDERSTANDING POLYGENIC BURDEN IN ASD Common inherited variants (minor allele frequency ≥0.01) account for most of the genetic liability for ASD, despite having smaller individual effect sizes compared with rare inherited and DNVs. Multiple GWAS have been conducted over the years to identify ASD-related common variants using array-based genotyping, with early studies ( Anney et al., 2010 , Wang et al., 2009 , Weiss et al., 2009 ). These studies identified several loci associated with ASD, including rs4307059 in 5p14.1 ( Wang et al., 2009 ); rs10513025, rs10513026, and rs16883317 in 5p15.2 ( Weiss et al., 2009 ); rs4141463 in 20p12.1 and rs1718101 in 7q35 ( Anney et al., 2010 ), but lacked the statistical power to detect genome-wide significant associations. This led to the formation of the ASD Working Group of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, which coordinated international efforts to aggregate and analyze independent genotyping datasets. A landmark study by Grove et al. (2019) leveraged a large Danish population resource, significantly expanding sample sizes (18,381 ASD cases and 27,969 controls) and leading to the identification of 5 genome-wide significant loci. They calculated PS for ASD and other ASD-associated traits and revealed differences in genetic load across ASD subtypes, suggesting distinct underlying genetic architectures for different clinical presentations of ASD ( Grove et al., 2019 ). Beyond identifying ASD-associated loci, researchers have sought to understand how PS interacts with high-effect-size rare and DNVs. Weiner et al. (2017) introduced the polygenic transmission disequilibrium test (pTDT) to examine this relationship. Their study showed that individuals carrying de novo CNVs overinherited PS from their parents (pTDT &gt; 0), suggesting that common and rare variants contribute additively to ASD risk. More recently, Weiner et al refined this approach by stratifying genomic regions to pinpoint those with the strongest ASD associations. They identified the 33-Mb p-arm of chromosome 16 (16p) as harboring the greatest excess of ASD pTDT ( Weiner et al., 2022 ). Notably, this region includes the well-established ASD-associated 16p11.2 CNV, providing compelling evidence for the convergence of common and rare genetic influences on ASD. While these findings support an additive contribution of common and rare variants, other studies suggest a more complex interplay between these genetic factors. Warrier et al. (2022) compared PS between individuals carrying de novo PTVs or missense variants and noncarriers. They found that individuals with DNVs exhibited lower PS, suggesting a compensatory effect between common and rare genetic risk factors. Antaki et al. (2022) further confirmed an anticorrelation between rare variant score, quantified by the number of de novo and rare inherited PTV/missense, and PS. This pattern supports a liability threshold model of ASD, which proposes that cumulative genetic burden must exceed a diagnostic threshold for ASD to manifest ( Fig. 3 A). In this framework, high-effect variants alone may be sufficient to trigger ASD. Well-established examples include de novo PTV in SCN2A ( Ben-Shalom et al., 2017 ) and deletions at 16p11.2 ( Fu et al., 2022 , Niarchou et al., 2019 ). In contrast, moderate-effect rare variants such as PTV in NAV3 ( Zhou et al., 2022 ) and deletions at 16p12.1 ( Girirajan et al., 2010 ) typically have moderate risk. They often require additional genetic burden, either in the form of a second or multiple variants or elevated PS ( Girirajan et al., 2010 , Wilfert et al., 2021 ). Common inherited variants contribute to liability through the accumulation of many small-effect alleles. A total genetic liability sufficient to meet the diagnostic threshold can therefore be achieved through different combinations of rare and common variants ( Fig. 3 A). Individuals with a high burden of rare, high-impact variants may require less contribution from polygenic burden, whereas those without such variants must accumulate greater polygenic burden, leading to the inverse relationship. This model also provides a framework to understand the wide phenotypic spectrum of ASD. The manifestation of ASD symptoms varies widely, with differences in cognitive ability, language development, and co-occurring developmental conditions. This heterogeneity complicates genetic analyses, as different subgroups may have distinct genetic architectures. Within the liability threshold framework, phenotypic differences can be interpreted as resulting from both the amount and type of genetic burden. A greater number of small-effect variants, the presence of high-impact rare variants, or their combination may push individuals past the diagnostic threshold to different extents ( Fig. 3 A). For example, rare DNVs are more strongly associated with global developmental comorbidities. On the other hand, polygenic burden is more closely tied to core ASD traits, including social communication deficits ( Antaki et al., 2022 , Warrier et al., 2022 ). Sex is another critical dimension of ASD heterogeneity. ASD is approximately 4 times more prevalent in males than in females. Early studies identified sex differences in genetic burden, with females with ASD exhibiting a higher load of rare, high-impact variants, including CNVs ( Jacquemont et al., 2014 , Sanders et al., 2015 ) and de novo PTVs ( Satterstrom et al., 2020 ). In parallel, recent transcriptomic analyses of over 270 midfetal cortical samples identified 101 consistently sex-differentially expressed genes ( Kissel and Werling, 2022 ). While these differentially expressed genes did not overlap significantly with known ASD risk genes, coexpression network analysis revealed a male-specific immune-enriched module, pointing to possible sex-specific modulation of neuroimmune pathways during cortical development ( Kissel and Werling, 2022 ). These findings suggest that sex differences in ASD may involve distinct downstream molecular mechanisms, potentially acting through immune signaling or cell-type-specific programs. Higher genetic burden in female ASD than male ASD supports the female protective effect, which posits that females require a greater genetic risk to surpass the threshold for ASD diagnosis compared with males ( Werling, 2016 ) ( Fig. 3 B). Based on this hypothesis, one might expect females with ASD to also have higher PS compared with males. However, studies examining sex differences in PS have yielded mixed results. Some studies found that females with ASD had higher PS than males ( Antaki et al., 2022 ), while others reported no significant differences ( Warrier et al., 2022 , Weiner et al., 2017 , Wigdor et al., 2022 ). These inconsistencies suggest the possibility of an alternative liability model, which proposes that sex differences in ASD risk may arise not only from higher diagnostic thresholds in females but also from greater variance in genetic liability among males ( Dougherty et al., 2022 , Sandin et al., 2024 ) ( Fig. 3 B). In ASD cases without intellectual disability, females tend to have higher PS than males ( Warrier et al., 2022 , Wigdor et al., 2022 ), further complicating the understanding of ASD heterogeneity. A recent study by Kim et al. (2024c) highlighted that the phenotypic differences between males and females within cohorts can confound evaluations of sex differences in genetic burden. Together, these observations reinforce that ASD liability is both sex- and phenotype-dependent, requiring further studies on ASD heterogeneity. MAXIMIZING NEAR-TERM CLINICAL OPPORTUNITIES Major advances in ASD genomic research have opened up promising clinical applications in 3 key areas: the development of gene-based therapeutics, the clinical interpretation of genetic variants, and the genetic diagnosis. Among these, gene-based therapeutics represent one of the most significant opportunities for clinical translation, with several early successes already reported in ASD-associated monogenic disorders ( Table 2 ). In Angelman syndrome, caused by loss of maternal UBE3A , therapeutic strategies target reactivation of the normally silent paternal allele. CRISPR-Cas9 disruption of the UBE3A-ATS antisense transcript in mouse models led to sustained paternal allele reactivation and behavioral improvement ( Wolter et al., 2020 ), while antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) suppressed UBE3A-ATS in human neurons and nonhuman primates, restoring UBE3A expression ( Dindot et al., 2023 ). In Rett syndrome (RTT), driven by mutations in MECP2 , delivery of a truncated MeCP2 protein via adeno-associated virus extended survival and improved motor function in mice ( Tillotson et al., 2017 ). Carrette et al. (2018) took a different approach by combining ASOs with a DNA methylation inhibitor (5-Aza-dC), partially reactivating MECP2 on the inactive X chromosome with minimal toxicity in mice. More recently, targeted RNA editing via adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 2 (ADAR2) precisely corrected a pathogenic MECP2 point mutation, improving symptoms in RTT mouse models ( Sinnamon et al., 2022 ). Fragile X syndrome is caused by transcriptional silencing of FMR1 , resulting in loss of FMRP. This loss leads to exaggerated synaptic protein synthesis and network hyperexcitability. These pathophysiological changes motivated efforts to inhibit metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5), a key regulator of synaptic plasticity. Although the mGluR5 antagonist mavoglurant showed limited effects overall, fully methylated patients demonstrated modest improvements in maladaptive behaviors ( Berry-Kravis et al., 2016 ). More recently, combined ASO and DNA demethylation therapy corrected aberrant splicing of FMR1 and restored FMRP expression in patient-derived cells ( Shah et al., 2023 ). SCN2A is one of major risk genes for ASD and associated with a broad range of syndromic symptoms: gain-of-function mutations often lead to early-onset epilepsy, whereas loss-of-function variants typically result in ASD, intellectual disability, and language impairments ( Ben-Shalom et al., 2017 , Sanders et al., 2018 ). ASO-mediated knockdown of SCN2A reduced seizures and extended survival in mouse models ( Li et al., 2021 ), while CRISPR-based transcriptional activation rescued neuronal excitability and synaptic function in loss-of-function contexts ( Tamura et al., 2022 ). These examples underscore the remarkable potential of gene-based therapeutics for ASD and related neurodevelopmental disorders. Notably, ASO-based therapies have already been approved for clinical use in spinal muscular atrophy ( Finkel et al., 2017 ), demonstrating the feasibility of RNA-targeted precision medicine. ASOs offer multiple mechanisms of action, including suppression of nonsense-mediated decay, exon skipping to restore in-frame transcripts, correction of splicing defects, and enhancement of translation via upstream open-reading frame inhibition ( Kim and Krainer, 2023 ). This modularity and versatility make ASOs a particularly attractive therapeutic platform. However, several challenges remain in translating gene-based therapies to the clinic ( Devinsky et al., 2025 ). ASO treatments and RNA editing require repeated administration due to their transient effects. Achieving precise dosing and timing is also critical, as even small deviations can compromise efficacy or cause toxicity. Delivery methods—often dependent on adeno-associated virus vectors—face limitations such as restricted cargo capacity, poor penetration of the blood-brain barrier, and risks of off-target effects or genomic instability. These hurdles highlight the importance of continued innovation in delivery systems, safety engineering, and timing strategies as gene-based therapies move toward clinical translation. In parallel with gene-targeted therapeutic development, the clinical interpretation of genetic variants has become a central focus in ASD research. While early efforts largely focused on rare coding variants, recent studies have expanded to include noncoding elements such as enhancers, promoters, and UTR regions that harbor ASD-associated variants. A growing body of evidence highlights spliceosomal small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) as clinically relevant noncoding loci. Pathogenic variants in RNU4-2 , which encodes the U4 snRNA of the major spliceosome, constitute one of the most common monogenic causes of neurodevelopmental disorders ( Greene et al., 2024 ). More recently, de novo mutations in RNU2-2 encode U2-2 snRNA and have been reported in 25 unrelated individuals presenting with neurodevelopmental delay, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and autistic features ( Greene et al., 2025 ). Robust expression of RNU2-2 transcripts and their incorporation into the major spliceosome further support their functional significance. Beyond these rare snRNA loci, the broader relevance of splicing variants in ASD has become increasingly evident. De novo splicing mutations are estimated to account for approximately 1.4% of ASD cases and 3.1% of DD cases ( Sanders et al., 2020 ). Notably, noncanonical splice-site variants have been recurrently identified in well-established NDD risk genes such as ARID1B , SCN2A , SHANK3 , and SYNGAP1 ( Li et al., 2024 ). These variants can disrupt normal splicing and lead to pathogenic outcomes through multiple mechanisms, including frameshift-induced nonsense-mediated mRNA decay or the production of aberrant protein isoforms. However, the timing and efficiency of how these transcripts are recognized and degraded remain incompletely understood. A recent study revealed that nonsense-mediated mRNA decay of transcripts encoding signal peptides can be spatially regulated and occur primarily after their targeting to the endoplasmic reticulum ( Shin et al., 2024a ), highlighting the need to consider cellular context when interpreting the impact of splicing-altered transcripts in ASD. Another emerging direction for clinical application is the use of predictive models trained on genomic data to estimate individual-level liability for ASD. These models range from traditional PS to more flexible machine learning and deep-learning approaches that incorporate diverse genomic features. Among them, PS provides a simple and scalable framework to quantify the cumulative effects of common variants. However, their predictive power for ASD remains limited—explaining only about 2% of liability variance ( Antaki et al., 2022 , Grove et al., 2019 ). This limited performance likely reflects both the genetic heterogeneity of ASD and the modest sample sizes of current ASD GWAS. To improve accuracy, recent studies have explored multi-PS models that incorporate genetically correlated traits. For example, integrating multiple psychiatric phenotypes increased the explained variance by up to 9-fold and improved differentiation between ADHD and ASD-ADHD mixed cases ( Albiñana et al., 2023 ). In parallel, PS refinement within high-risk genetic groups offers another potential application. In schizophrenia, PS stratification among 22q11.2 deletion carriers revealed meaningful variation in risk—from 9% in the lowest PS decile to 33% in the highest ( Davies et al., 2020 ). A similar strategy could enhance ASD risk assessment by combining PS with rare variant burden, especially in individuals carrying known pathogenic CNVs. Recent studies further demonstrate the utility of integrative prediction models that combine genetic and developmental features. A 2024 study applied a hybrid model integrating early developmental milestones with PS, rare CNVs, and de novo mutations to predict intellectual disability among children with ASD ( Bourque et al., 2025 ). Using over 5600 individuals from SPARK, SSC, and MSSNG cohorts, the model achieved an AUROC of ∼0.65 and a positive predictive value of 55%, identifying 10% of ID cases with high precision. Notably, PS improved the model’s negative predictive value when added to developmental features alone. Another recent approach, STAR-NN, used deep learning on WES data to jointly model polygenic and rare variant burdens, achieving an AUC of 0.73 in ASD prediction ( Wu et al., 2024 ). While clinical utility remains limited, the study illustrates the potential of neural network models to enhance genomic risk prediction. CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES Over the past decade, advancements in genomic research have significantly deepened our understanding of the complex genetic architecture underlying ASD ( Fig. 4 ). Both rare high-impact variants and polygenic contributions from common variants have been identified as key contributors to ASD liability. Large-scale sequencing efforts and international consortia have enabled the discovery of a growing number of ASD risk genes, spanning protein-coding and noncoding regions of the genome. These resources have also illuminated the interplay between rare and common variants, enhancing our capacity to explore gene function, convergence, and clinical relevance. Together, these findings have laid a foundation for early detection and intervention strategies that may optimize developmental outcomes for individuals with ASD. Despite these major advances, several key challenges remain in translating genetic discovery into biological understanding and clinical application. Although numerous genes have been robustly linked to ASD, their precise contributions to ASD pathophysiology remain difficult to resolve. While a growing number of ASD risk genes have been functionally investigated using animal models, iPSCs, and organoid systems, many remain uncharacterized at the experimental level ( Fig. 2 ). This highlights an ongoing gap between gene discovery and biological insight and emphasizes the importance of scalable and systematic approaches to evaluate ASD-linked genes across neurodevelopmental contexts. Moreover, although convergence analyses have highlighted expression in midfetal cortical neurons and roles in gene regulation and synaptic function, these patterns may reflect broad neurodevelopmental programs rather than ASD-specific mechanisms ( Fu et al., 2022 ). This calls for improved convergence analysis frameworks that leverage spatiotemporally resolved single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data to identify shared patterns of ASD gene activity ( Binder and Gordon, 2023 ). While MPRAs have enabled high-throughput testing of regulatory activity ( Koesterich et al., 2023 , Plassmeyer et al., 2023 ), 3′UTRs remain largely unexplored in functional studies, especially in neurodevelopmental contexts, despite their critical roles in mRNA stability, translation, and localization ( Hong and Jeong, 2023 ). Furthermore, these assays are typically conducted outside native chromatin contexts and focus primarily on enhancer-like effects. Recently, large-scale perturbation platforms have made gene and variant perturbations more efficient and scalable ( Yun et al., 2024 ). Yet, most approaches still assess their effects in isolation. This limits the ability to capture pathway-level convergence and combinatorial effects, where certain phenotypes arise only when multiple moderate-effect variants act together ( Lee and Kim, 2024 ). Bridging this combinatorial genotype-to-phenotype gap would require integrating functional data with neuroimaging, behavioral profiling, and longitudinal clinical records. These approaches are critical not only for mechanistic understanding but also for informing prognosis and personalized intervention. Nevertheless, functionally testing all possible variants and their combinatorial effects remains prohibitively time- and resource-intensive. To overcome this limitation, emerging deep-learning models such as Enformer ( Avsec et al., 2021 ) and Sei ( Chen et al., 2022 ) and large-scale perturbation simulators like scGPT ( Cui et al., 2024 ), CFGen ( Palma et al., 2024 ), and Geneformer ( Theodoris et al., 2023 ) offer promising approaches to simulate gene perturbations and forecast downstream cellular responses in specific contexts. In parallel, translating PS into clinical application has proven difficult. PS currently explains only a small fraction of ASD liability, and its accuracy is limited by the lack of ancestral and phenotypic diversity in existing GWAS datasets. Broader recruitment of diverse and deeply phenotyped cohorts will be essential to improve generalizability and equity in risk prediction. Another underexplored axis of variation lies in somatic mosaicism. While studies in schizophrenia have shown that somatic mutations in the developing brain can affect regulatory elements and contribute to disease ( An and Kim, 2024 , Maury et al., 2024 ), similar analyses in ASD are scarce. Systematic profiling of brain-specific somatic mutations, particularly those arising during prenatal neurodevelopment, may reveal novel mechanisms underlying ASD onset and heterogeneity. Although the heritability of ASD is high, environmental exposures and gene-environment interactions are increasingly recognized as important contributors to ASD risk. Prenatal factors such as maternal immune activation, toxin exposure, or metabolic stress have been implicated in altering neurodevelopmental trajectories ( Choi et al., 2016 , Herrera et al., 2024 , Jiang et al., 2022 ). Epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling, may mediate these effects and interact with inherited genetic variation ( Herrera et al., 2024 , Jiang et al., 2022 ). Yet, few studies have systematically examined how genetic and environmental factors converge to shape ASD liability. Recent work in rare neurodevelopmental conditions has shown that nontransmitted parental alleles, particularly those related to cognitive traits, can influence risk in offspring, suggesting indirect genetic effects via the prenatal or family environment ( Huang et al., 2024 ). These findings underscore the need to model both genetic and environmental contributions. To better link genetic variation to function, future research must also move beyond single-omics analyses. Most ASD studies have examined only one layer of molecular data, such as genomic sequence or gene expression, limiting insight into how mutations propagate through molecular systems. Recent advances in deep learning provide new tools to bridge this divide, such as AlphaFold ( Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2021 ), AlphaMissense ( Cheng et al., 2023 ), and ESM-based language models ( Brandes et al., 2023 ). Combining such predictive models with diverse molecular datasets, such as epigenomic profiles, proteomic measurements, chromatin accessibility maps, and spatial transcriptomic data, will be essential to fully characterize variant effects and uncover convergent biological mechanisms in ASD. In summary, this review highlights the emerging findings from large-scale ASD genomic studies and synthesizes the diverse genetic risk factors and their varying effect sizes and biological mechanisms. The rapid progress in ASD gene discovery has paved the way for early detection and intervention, which may maximize a child's cognitive and functional potential. However, to translate these findings into clinical practice, large-scale population studies with well-curated, longitudinal phenotype data are needed. A deeper understanding of genotype-phenotype relationships will be essential to fully harness the power of genetic discoveries for precision medicine in ASD.
