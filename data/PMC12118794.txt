Abstract Background: Many psychiatric disorders present with symptoms involving disordered sleep. The overlay of sleep and psychiatric disorders, though ubiquitous, remains less well understood. The evolution of diagnostic manuals for sleep and psychiatric disorders has also complicated our understanding of this intersection. Aim: Hence, this current review was conducted to explore the progress of Indian research on sleep disorders in patients with mental illness. Methods: In order to cover the wide spectrum of our scope of review, it was decided to conduct a narrative review. The search was conducted in PubMed and Google Scholar, and the recruited studies were all research work done in India on sleep disorders in patients with mental illness. Results: The review could identify substantial volume of research published from India on sleep disorders in patients with mental illness. The prominent sleep disorders that were addressed included restless legs syndrome, insomnia, hypersomnia, parasomnia, and sleep‐related breathing disorders. The studies were mostly clinic‐based and observational in nature. Conclusion: To conclude, the relationship of psychiatric disorders and sleep disorders has been in the forefront of research in the India. There is a high prevalence of sleep disorders in Indian patients with psychiatric ailments. However, it was found that the research attempts have been largely clustered toward certain selected disorders and there is a need for conducting more studies using more rigorous designs. INTRODUCTION Association between sleep and psychiatric disorders is long known. Development of sleep medicine over the years has uncovered several sleep disorders which differ with regard to the clinical presentation, pathophysiology, management, and prognosis.[ 1 ] Data suggest that sleep symptoms increase the risk for depression, bipolar disorder, and psychosis, especially those that fall in an established category of sleep disorders.[ 2 ] Insomnia has been reported to increase the risk for incipient depression, anxiety disorders, psychosis, and alcohol use disorders.[ 3 ] Lumping them all in the category of “disturbed sleep” leads to incomplete remission or persistence of psychiatric symptoms or worsening of the course of illness, while the treatment of sleep disorders improves the psychiatric disorder as well.[ 4 ] Untreated sleep disorders, especially insomnia, parasomnia, and sleep-apnea, increase the risk of suicide among patients having psychiatric disorders.[ 5 ] Indian medical professionals have also contributed to the existing literature by uncovering the link between sleep disorders and psychiatric disorders. However, the last review of Indian studies on sleep disorders was published more than a decade back.[ 6 ] This current paper is an attempt to compile the Indian research that has reported an association between sleep disorders and psychiatric disorders and review the recent updates. METHODOLOGY Since the topic the review was very broad, the authors convened about deciding the methodology of the review. The meeting was attended by all the authors that included certified sleep experts and mental health professionals. In the meeting, it was decided to choose the pragmatic option of conducting a narrative review to effectively depict the vast area of research conducted. The search was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar. The search terms included (but were not limited to) ‘Sleep’, ‘Insomnia’, ‘Restless legs syndrome’, ‘Hypersomnia’, ‘Parasomnia’, ‘Sleep related breathing disorders’, ‘Sleep Apnoea’, ‘Mental Disorders’, ‘Psychiatric Disorders’, ‘Psychosis’, ‘Depression’, ‘Mania’, ‘Anxiety disorders’, and ‘Substance use disorders’. Appropriate Boolean operators were used in the search, and where necessary, the search was refined using the appropriate Medical Subject Headings (MESH). Only studies that were conducted in India were included in this review. Disagreements, if any, were resolved by mutual discussions. RESULTS Restless legs syndrome Restless legs syndrome (RLS) is a sleep disorder that is characterized by an urge to move the legs which is often but not always associated with dysesthesia.[ 1 ] Symptoms often appear, or, if present during the day, worsen in the evening.[ 1 ] Rest worsens the symptoms, and movement or massage brings at least partial relief in the symptoms, till the time manoeuver is continued.[ 1 ] RLS must be differentiated from its mimics, for example, habitual foot tapping, arthralgia, myalgia, varicose veins, leg edema, and positional discomfort to name a few.[ 1 ] The prevalence of RLS in the Indian adult population is lower (2.3%–2.5%) than that of western literature (5–10%).[ 1 , 7 , 8 ] However, RLS is rarely reported by the patients themselves and in Indian context, they frequently present with complaints of insomnia, depression, chronic fatigue, and somatic symptoms disorders.[ 9 ] Furthermore, while assessment of RLS is made using the questionnaires, patients with anxiety, somatic symptoms disorder, and chronic insomnia often mistake their symptoms for RLS, leading to false positive diagnosis.[ 10 ] This suggests that clinical diagnosis is the mainstay for diagnosis of RLS. Indian studies that have assessed co-occurrence of RLS with psychiatric disorders are few in number. The prevalence of RLS has been estimated to be around 32% among patients presenting with depression, without any age and gender difference.[ 11 ] Furthermore, the total duration of depressive disorder and the number of depressive episodes were also comparable between patients with and without RLS, suggesting that RLS does not affect the course of depressive disorder.[ 11 ] Nearly similar prevalence (28%) of RLS has been found among adult patients presenting with somatoform pain disorder.[ 12 ] However, as opposed to depression, RLS in these patients was associated with longer duration, continuous course, and complaints of sleepiness during the day.[ 12 ] However, a recent study has reported very high prevalence of RLS among patients with depression (67%), anxiety disorders (50%), and somatic symptoms disorder (48%).[ 13 ] They found that female gender and use of benzodiazepines were associated with RLS in the multivariate analysis.[ 13 ] RLS has also been examined among patients with opioid use disorder.[ 14 , 15 ] A small case series reported that RLS appeared on the second day of withdrawal.[ 15 ] However, patients with and without RLS were comparable with regard to the demographic data and dose of buprenorphine required for controlling the withdrawal.[ 15 ] Similar to the findings in the study of somatoform pain disorder, these patients also had daytime sleepiness which was associated with delayed sleep at night and waking up late in the morning.[ 15 ] We could find only one study of RLS among the children. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) has also been found to be associated with RLS.[ 16 ] This study reported that 30% children with ADHD had RLS compared to 7% of the control population.[ 16 ] Insomnia in psychiatric disorders Out of the various sleep conditions that have been studied from India, there has been a fair representation of research on insomnia in various psychiatric disorders. The studies can be summarized in the following manner. Insomnia in psychiatric disorders in general In a study conducted in a tertiary care center in Amritsar, Mondal et al. [ 17 ] evaluated the prevalence of sleep disorders, specifically insomnia, in a sample of 500 outpatients with various psychiatric disorders recruited purposively. It was found that the prevalence of insomnia in this sample was 78.2% and 29.2% of them had moderate to severe insomnia. The authors also found that the chances of insomnia were higher if the diagnosis was depression and progressively lesser for psychosis, mania, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. Insomnia in psychotic disorders The studies on insomnia and sleep quality in patients with psychotic disorders have mostly been conducted in those having schizophrenia. In a study conducted in patients with schizophrenia, recruited from a tertiary care center in Telangana, India, it was found that both positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia were associated with poor sleep quality.[ 18 ] In another study conducted in Kolkata, India, it was found that sleep parameters were deranged in 60 medication naïve patients with schizophrenia as compared 30 normal controls.[ 19 ] The study further showed that the parameters were more deranged in patients with prominent positive symptoms. In another study by Yella et al. ,[ 20 ] 200 patients with severe mental illnesses reported of substantial insomnia in this group which was also positively associated with the magnitude of cognitive dysfunction in this sample. This finding was also supported by the findings of the study by Chaurasia et al ., who found the sleep quality of a sample of 100 patients of schizophrenia to be poor.[ 21 ] In another study by Ransing et al. ,[ 22 ] the sleep parameters were compared between patients of schizophrenia and major depressive disorder. It showed that the though the total time in bed was lesser in the schizophrenia group, the sleep onset latency was more and the sleep efficiency was less in the schizophrenia group. Subsequent randomized controlled trials have been done to find out the effects of various interventions in the improvement of the sleep parameters in patients with schizophrenia. Pant et al. ,[ 23 ] in their study, evaluated whether cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBTi) was beneficial in the improvement of sleep parameters in patients with schizophrenia and whether that translated into improvement in their cognitive performance. The study showed that CBTi led to improvement in the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), AIIMS Comprehensive Neuropsychological Battery (AIIMS NCB), Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory, Warwick-Edinburg Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS), and Global Assessment Functioning (GAF). In another study published from the same center, CBTi was found to improve the measures on insomnia severity index along with PSQI scores after 4 and 8 weeks in patients with schizophrenia.[ 24 ] Kumar et al. [ 25 ] studied the effects of melatonin in the resolution of insomnia in patients with schizophrenia and found that melatonin, at a modal dose of 3 mg, was beneficial as an add-on therapy in improving sleep onset latency, morning freshness, and daytime functioning. Insomnia in depression and anxiety disorders In the Indian context, interestingly, several studies have been conducted where the researchers have ventured to study the prevalence of insomnia in patients suffering from anxiety and depression. This was possibly due to the availability of psychometric tools that allow to study both the groups of patients together. In a study conducted in a community setting of Kolkata, it was found that the prevalence of insomnia was 42% with significant correlation of depression, anxiety, and stress with severity of insomnia.[ 26 ] Several studies have been conducted with a similar approach and methodology but in different subsets of patients. They include nurses,[ 27 ] students,[ 28 , 29 , 30 ] and teachers.[ 31 ] The results of all the studies showed a common trend of statistically significant correlation of insomnia with depression and anxiety. However, since the studies employed a cross-sectional design, no causal direction could be attributed to the findings. Similar studies have also been conducted in geriatric respondents where there have been reports of substantial burden of insomnia and depression in the respondents.[ 32 , 33 ] It was noteworthy that in both the studies conducted in community settings at geographically distant areas of the country, the afflicted patients tended to have a higher burden of other medical comorbidity and at a higher predisposition to self-medicate with sedative-hypnotic drugs. Over the past decade, the overlap of insomnia with depressive and anxiety disorders has also been studied in the context of specific disorders. In a study by Tomar et al .,[ 34 ] the prevalence of depression and insomnia was studied in patients with a history of traumatic brain injury. It was found that 84% of this sample had depression and the co-occurrence of insomnia was higher in this proportion of respondents. Similar focus has also been given to the prevalence of insomnia in patients with chronic kidney disease[ 35 ] or undergoing hemodialysis.[ 36 ] The studies established that the prevalence of depression and insomnia was high in this group and tended to be more severe with worsening status of the sociodemographic and clinical parameters. A few studies were conducted to find out the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on the anxiety and depressive symptoms.[ 37 , 38 , 39 ] The studies generally reported that subthreshold and frank symptoms of insomnia were highly prevalent among the respondents during the nationwide lockdown and it was associated with generalized anxiety as well as fear in relation to COVID-19 pandemic. The complex relationship between insomnia and depression has been a matter of interest among the Indian researchers in the past decade. Gupta and Lahan, in their study, tried to investigate the relationship of primary and secondary insomnia with depression. It is noteworthy that this study was conducted using the DSM IV TR criteria, and hence, a distinction between primary and secondary insomnia was used. The study found that primary insomnia tended to follow the onset of depressive symptoms, whereas secondary insomnia tended to appear before the onset of depressive symptoms.[ 40 ] However, with the advent of DSM 5, this question was re-evaluated under the light of the newer classificatory system. It was found that though there is a significant overlap of insomnia and depression, the clinical management should be independently targeted toward both the entities.[ 41 ] Gupta, in his study, further established that cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is an evidence-based treatment modality for patients presenting with insomnia and depression, and it should include problem-solving techniques as one of the essential components.[ 42 ] Muthuraman et al .,[ 43 ] in their study on a related theme, established that insomnia in patients with anxiety and depression is correlated with severity of psychiatric symptoms; the study also interestingly reported that maladaptive cognitions arising out of the illness can influence the sleep quality of the patients. Muhammad et al .[ 44 ] used the data of about 10,911 patients from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India 2017–18 (LASI) to suggest that treatment of insomnia can lead to the reduction in the development of depressive disorders among older adults. Insomnia in bipolar disorder There were two major themes that arose from the studies conducted on the interface of insomnia and bipolar disorder. First, it was established that insomnia was correlated to various cognitive deficits in patients with bipolar disorder. A study by Rajitha et al .,[ 45 ] showed that sleep efficiency, REM sleep percentage, and sleep latency were significant predictors of cognitive performance. The study also reported distinct patterns of the effect of insomnia on the cognitive functions of patients with bipolar I and bipolar II disorder. Similarly, another study by Yella et al. [ 20 ] also reinforced this finding by establishing an inverse correlation between the severity of insomnia with cognitive functioning in patients with bipolar disorder. The other major theme that arose was the significant effect of insomnia even in remitted patients of bipolar disorder. Vangal et al .[ 46 ] studied the sleep quality of patients with bipolar disorder and found that the quality of sleep was poorer in this group as compared to healthy controls. The differences were also present in subjective sleep assessment and total duration of sleep and were associated with significantly poorer quality of life. Karthick et al .[ 47 ] studied the quality of sleep in remitted patients of bipolar I disorder specifically and found that a significant proportion of patients with bipolar disorder who are in remission have sleep problems that seem to be independent of core symptoms of depression. The study by Grover et al .[ 48 ] also found that insomnia was a classical residual symptom in euthymic patients of bipolar disorder in their period of remission. Insomnia in childhood developmental disorders The volume of studies conducted on this theme has been relatively sparse in the Indian context. Ganaie conducted a study to assess the sleep patterns of children with various development disorders (including autism and intellectual disability). The study reported that sleep patterns were disrupted for patients with these disorders and was associated with problem behavior in this group.[ 49 ] Another study by Tyagi et al. [ 50 ] specifically looked into the sleep patterns in children with autism spectrum disorder and compared them with normally developing children. The commonly disrupted sleep parameters included sleep wake transition disorders, followed by disorder of initiation and maintenance. This study also established an association of hyperactivity, sensory issues, and poor motor skills to sleep problems. Joseph et al. [ 16 ] studied the sleep parameters of children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and compared them with their typically developing counterparts and found that the subjective sleep problems and parameters as obtained in polysomnography (characterized by significantly more time spent in sleep stage N3 and higher latency period for ADHD group) were deranged for the ADHD group. Another study from Kerala also showed about 70% of their sample of children with ADHD having reported sleep disorders including disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep and sleep–wake transition disorders.[ 51 ] The study also reported an association of the sleep disorders with problem behavior. Parasomnias and psychiatry Parasomnias are the disorders of complex movements occurring during sleep with little or no recall of the same. Some other sleep-related movement disorders have also been incorporated in parasomnias for this review. Prevalence of parasomnias in psychiatric disorders Parasomnias, as compared to insomnia, have been researched far less among psychiatric disorders. Vyjayanthi S[ 52 ] described a case series of three adolescents to young adults in 2017, where it was found that the patients developed sleep walking, automatic texting in sleep, and sleep bruxism after a traumatic period, which responded well to trauma-focused psychotherapy and benzodiazepines. A prevalence study of various sleep disorders in 2018,[ 17 ] among psychiatric outpatients, revealed a percentage of 4.8 having parasomnia. The majority of the psychiatric disorders found were depressive and psychotic disorders. The type of parasomnias present was however not classified in this study. In 2022, Prabhu D et al .[ 53 ] found out an increasing rate of bruxism and tooth wear in women with higher levels of psychological stress in both working and nonworking women. Although this study did not mention a certain psychiatric disorder, it highlighted the detrimental effects of stress on a type of sleep-related moment disorder like bruxism. Childhood psychiatric disorders and parasomnias A case-control study of childhood ADHD[ 54 ] revealed significantly more sleep problems in the ADHD group. Among the parasomnias, 35% of ADHD group had disturbances, with three children having restless movements during sleep, two having sleep-talking, and one each having teeth grinding and enuresis. Only one child in the control group had enuresis. The higher occurrence of RLS and sleep-related breathing disorders in the ADHD group compelled the authors to think whether such sleep problems could give rise to inattention and hyperactivity, thus mimicking ADHD. A study of 150 undergraduate medical students[ 55 ] showed a predominance of intermediate circadian chronotype (58%), followed by evening and morning chronotypes. Parasomnias were found to be most common among the evening phenotype. Nightmares and act-out dreams were the most common parasomnias found. Evening-oriented students had more difficulties in decision-making, becoming exhausted more easily, and a trend toward elevated vulnerability of feeling worthless than other chronotypes. A common thread could thus be perceived among evening chronotype binding psychological burden and parasomnias. Similarly, in the case of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), parasomnias were more commonly found in the children with autism compared to controls, as seen by Aathira R et al .[ 56 ] Polysomnography of such cases and controls also revealed reduced sleep efficiency and decreased REM and slow wave sleep (SWS) duration in the children with ASD, implying poorer sleep quality as well. Another study in 2019[ 50 ] had similar findings of higher occurrence of parasomnias [Disorders of arousal (DA) and sleep-wake transition disorders (SWTD)] among ASD compared to controls. Around 26% of ASD had DA, and 62% had SWTD (which encompasses hyping jerks, rhythmic movement disorders, bruxism, etc.). However, they also found that this difference in occurrence between cases and controls gradually vanishes as the age advances. A high prevalence of parasomnias (60%) was also showed by Samanta et al .[ 57 ] in 2020, among ASD children aged 2–6 years. They also opined about maternal age and birthweight of the child to be inversely related and caffeine intake of mothers to be directly related to parasomnia. Medication-related parasomnias Some studies have focused on the relation of psychotropic agents with parasomnias. Pandey et al .[ 58 ] studied the persons developing REM behavioral disorders (RBDs) after Clomipramine therapy and found 12.2% of patients developing RBD after Clomipramine. When compared to those who did not develop RBD, they found higher dosages and longer duration of treatment in those who developed RBD. Also, younger patients were more likely to develop RBD with Clomipramine. Avinash et al .[ 59 ] found out a high prevalence of parasomnias in 112 patients on various antidepressant and antipsychotic medications. Rhythmic feet movements were present in 82.1% of them, sleep terrors and nightmares were present in 32.1% each, and the other parasomnias comprised lower numbers. They also found that most of the parasomnias were less commonly present in those patients who are using SSRIs compared to non-SSRIs. Sleep-related breathing disorders in psychiatric disorders Sleep-related breathing disorders are a group of disorders having abnormal breathing during sleep. It includes obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), central sleep apnea (CSA), sleep-related hypoventilation disorders, sleep-related hypoxemia disorder, and isolated symptoms and variants like snoring and catathrenia. Among SRBD, we could find studies only on OSA in psychiatric disorders in India. OSA is a disorder where recurrent upper airway narrowing found leading to obstruction in airflow during sleep. This airflow obstruction causes snoring, snorting, apneas, nocturia, frequent arousals, or awakenings from sleep. In the daytime, it can present as nonrefreshing sleep, morning head heaviness, headache, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness. The prevalence of depression among OSA patients was found between 11% and 25.8% in different studies,[ 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 ] while the prevalence of anxiety in OSA patients was found between 7% and 11.5%.[ 61 , 63 ] Depression and anxiety were diagnosed by MINI-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI-Plus), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D), and the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale[ 63 ] and by HADS (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale) by Kant et al. in Lucknow.[ 61 ] The association of second-generation antipsychotics and OSA and metabolic syndrome had also been assessed. Saxena et al .[ 64 ] studied 119 major depressive disorder (MDD) adult (18–60-year-old) patients who were on at least 2 months on antidepressants. They found that 34% had a high risk of developing OSA, assessed by the Berlin questionnaire (BQ), and 19% had metabolic syndrome (MS), assessed by Adult Treatment Panel (ATP) III criteria. The patients having MS were significantly more exposed to antidepressants (by total fluoxetine units), had more duration of treatment, and had greater risk of developing OSA. A higher OSA risk denoted greater likelihood to cause MS in patients suffering from MDD. Similarly, Rohatgi et al. also studied participants suffering from schizophrenia spectrum disorder/bipolar disorder/major depressive disorder and taking second-generation antipsychotics. They found that 25.6% of patients had MS, and 20.9% had higher risk of OSA. Corollary was that presence of OSA, greater waist circumference, and higher fasting plasma glucose levels increased the risk of metabolic syndrome.[ 65 , 66 ] High risk of OSA created seven times higher likelihood of contributing to MS.[ 66 ] The risk of OSA (by BQ) in bipolar disorder patients was studied by Varadharajan et al .[ 67 ] and Wani et al .,[ 68 ] and they found 20% and 68.8%, respectively. Patients with high risk for OSA had higher BMI with comorbid physical illness and in those who were on a combination of valproate and lithium in comparison to valproate alone. Interestingly, higher risk of OSA was found in males by Wani et al. but in females by Varadharajan et al . A possible explanation could be because the mean waist circumference in females was higher than that of males in the participants studied by Varadharajan et al ., which increases the risk of OSA. The prevalence of OSA in schizophrenia was studied by Rijal et al .[ 69 ] They studied 200 adult schizophrenia spectrum disorder patients who were on clozapine for more than 1 year. BQ was used to assess the risk of OSA and Calgary depression scale for schizophrenia (CDSS) for depression. They found that 16.5% and 36% had a high risk for OSA at a body mass index (BMI) cutoff of 30 kg/m 2 and 25 kg/m 2 , respectively. High risk of OSA was associated with the presence of depression (48.6% vs 26.6%), diabetes mellitus (18% vs 7.8%), use of higher total chlorpromazine equivalent dose, the total number of medications, and poorer quality of life. Stress levels in OSA patients were assessed by Shyamsukha et al .[ 70 ] among 20 Level 1 PSG-confirmed OSA patients in Maharashtra by Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) questionnaire, and they found 40% had moderate and 60% had severe stress. Stress was higher in females than in males. The effect of OSA on cognitive functions was also studied. Cognitive impairment (Montreal Cognitive assessment score &lt;26) was found in 58.3% of patients and was positively associated with severity of OSA.[ 71 ] In another study, 46.7% patients of OSA had mild and 21.7% had major cognitive impairment as assessed by Addenbrook’s Cognitive Examination (ACE-III).[ 62 ] As far as the effect on specific cognitive domain is concerned, Haldar et al. [ 72 ] assessed the association of OSA and sleep quality with cognitive function in 6795 individuals of 50 or more age from a population-based cohort of middle-aged and older adults in New Delhi, India (LoCARPoN cohort). Cognitive functions were assessed by standard neuropsychological battery, OSA symptoms by BQ, and sleep quality by Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. They found that OSA symptoms were negatively associated with cognitive domains of information processing, memory, and general cognitive factor (G-factor) in full model. Stratified analysis by age group showed significant adverse effects of OSA symptoms on cognition for middle-aged people (50–60 years), but not in later age groups. Poor sleep quality was also associated with lower cognitive scores for G-factor, memory, and executive domains, but not with information domain. Similarly, Sharma et al .[ 73 ] studied the effect of OSA on neurocognitive function and included 50 patients (25–65 years) with PSG-proven severe OSA and 25 controls in the study. The cognition was assessed by standardized battery of neurocognitive tests. They found that the patients with severe OSA had significantly compromised performance on tests of alertness, working memory, response inhibition, problem solving, and executive function. However, when adjustment for delayed information processing was done, the difference in executive function between the groups disappeared. Although the test scores did not correlate with arousal index, apnea-hypopnea index, or Epworth sleepiness score, the %age of time spent below 90% O 2 saturation showed a weak correlation with the number of Stroop errors, number of trials required, and errors of perseveration on Wisconsin card sorting test. Hypersomnia and psychiatric disorders Hypersomnia is defined as an inability to stay awake and alert during the major waking episode of the day leading to unintended lapses into drowsiness and sleep in some other forms; sleepiness is associated with large increases in total daily amount of sleep without any genuine feeling of restoration.[ 74 , 75 ] The terms hypersomnia and hypersomnolence are often used interchangeably in the literature, but hypersomnia refers to specific disorder such as idiopathic hypersomnia (ICSD-3). The DSM-5 introduced the concept of “hypersomnolence disorder”, which had characteristics of excessive daytime or night-time sleep and/or impaired vigilance and/or sleep inertia, but not explained by narcolepsy, disturbed night-time sleep, circadian rhythm disorder, or an associated substance use and also not entirely explained by a medical or mental disorder. In our review, the Indian research in this field can be summarized as follows: Klein Levine syndrome Klein Levine syndrome (KLS) is a rare sleep disorder. A number of cases have been reported from India. Patients of KLS present with recurrent hypersomnia and associated cognitive and psychiatric features. The first reported case from India could be found in 1970.[ 76 ] Some of the cases may present without psychiatric symptoms.[ 77 ] However, many of them present with psychiatric symptomatology as described in Indian literature. Additional symptoms may be mistaken for depression;[ 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 ] for example, fatigue, restlessness, irritability, and episodic low mood have to be forcefully wake up even for daily activities.[ 83 ] These patients may also present with transient psychotic symptoms like referential thinking, fearfulness, and hallucinatory behavior.[ 79 , 84 , 85 , 86 ] Some patients may experience derealization.[ 79 , 80 ] Loss of social inhibition may compel one to consider other diagnoses.[ 85 ] Cognitive symptoms, namely, difficulty in reading, writing, simple calculations, and memory lapses, have also been reported.[ 81 , 87 ] Since hypersomnia is common among patients having bipolar depression and atypical depression, these episodes are difficult to be differentiated from bipolar disorder.[ 79 , 88 ] Another reason for difficulty in differentiating is attributed to response to lithium and carbamazepine in both the disorders.[ 79 , 80 , 81 , 83 , 85 , 88 ] Idiopathic hypersomnia A few cases of idiopathic hypersomnia have also been described from India. An adolescent boy was reported having multiple episodes of sleepiness in a day; however, naps were nonrefreshing as in idiopathic hypersomnia.[ 89 ] He did not have other symptoms of narcolepsy.[ 89 ] In another study, 38 cases of idiopathic hypersomnia were reported across 5 years period.[ 90 ] Around a quarter of them present with behavioral symptoms.[ 90 ] Narcolepsy Narcolepsy is rare in the Indian population.[ 91 ] The first case was reported in 2009.[ 92 ] Reports from sleep centers describe having seen only four cases and 20 cases in a period of 4 years, 5 years, and 7 years, respectively.[ 90 , 91 , 93 ] However, this figure should be considered cautiously as this may be attributed to poor reporting and missed diagnosis, besides lower prevalence.[ 94 ] Psychiatric symptoms are reported by around two thirds of the patients.[ 90 ] Behavioral and emotional symptoms include irritability, depression, mood swings, and poor scholastic performance.[ 95 , 96 ] Ravindren et al. [ 97 ] reported development of narcolepsy type 2 symptoms after suffering from obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) in a child with Wolfram syndrome.[ 97 ] Association of narcolepsy and OCD has also been reported after thalamo-striatal stroke and streptococcal infection.[ 98 ] However, cases may not have any psychiatric symptoms.[ 93 , 99 , 100 ] A case of narcolepsy was reported to have inflicted injury to the self, though the cause for inflicting injury could not be determined.[ 101 ] Substance use disorders and sleep disorders Literature review of the past 10 years at the interface of sleep and addiction reveals epidemiological, community-based, and biological studies involving both chemicals and behavioral addictions. Increased sleep problems among adolescents with substance use have been reported in community-based studies across the length and breadth of the country like among adolescents in schools of Manipur.[ 102 ] In a study among college students, excessive daytime sleepiness was associated with coffee/tea consumption and smoking.[ 103 ] Among the community-based research studies which relate alcohol with sleep, prominent are studies in the middle-aged and elderly. In the Longitudinal Cognition and Aging Research on Population of the National Capital Region (LoCARPoN) cohort, smoking, alcohol usage, and other psychosocial and metabolic factors correlated with poor sleep quality and obstructive sleep apnea.[ 104 ] Apart from this, sleep at the population level has been studied in different parts of the country, in both rural and urban settings. In Orissa (Sambalpur), among 1047 males and 945 females totalling 1992 individuals who were assessed with sociodemographic proforma and PSQI, it was found that 30.5% had poor sleep quality and this was associated with drinking habits of alcohol.[ 105 ] Other studies in Orissa have associated alcoholism with sleep problems with an adjusted odds ratio of 4.5.[ 106 ] Such studies have been done in urban slum dwellers in India as well; for example, in Bengaluru, poor quality of sleep has been associated with alcohol and tobacco use.[ 107 ] In another population-based study in Dehradun among substance dependence, only tobacco dependence was associated with insomnia after adjusting for other symptoms.[ 108 ] In the middle-aged group, as per the secondary data analysis of the Longitudinal Ageing study in India, the findings were equivocal. For example, whereas one study revealed that poor sleep quality was associated with higher incidence of benign prostatic hyperplasia with significant interaction effect of alcohol, another was contradictory.[ 109 , 110 ] However, other studies did not find any significant association between substance use like tobacco and alcohol and sleep problems.[ 111 ] In line were studies done in a South Indian cohort with PSG-proven OSA, which showed that exposure to smoking and alcohol showed no association with OSA.[ 112 ] In special groups like drivers, substance use and sleep problems have also been studied. For example, among night bus drivers, to ward off excessive sleepiness, more than 50% drivers had excessive sleepiness and usage of tobacco was their usual coping strategy.[ 113 ] Among 383 driver victims presenting to the emergency with road traffic accidents, sleep and substance use assessments revealed that more than half were having alcohol usage (57.7%) and among illicit drugs, the most common was cannabis.[ 114 ] Similar studies among drivers have been done in other parts of the country like Tamil Nadu.[ 115 ] In miners, alcohol use and low sleep duration were associated with increased absenteeism.[ 116 ] Among doctors and private school teachers, sleep, smoking, and alcohol intake were interrelated.[ 117 , 118 ] In an interesting study among female medical students, sleep problems, substance use, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) were interlinked.[ 119 ] Among caregivers of alcohol dependence, qualitative research has revealed sleep deprivation.[ 120 ] Alcohol and sleep inter-relation has also been studied in the context of comorbidities. In a study on patients with chronic liver disease where 54.2% had AUD and 45% had TUD, poor sleep quality was reported in 37.4% of the patients, which is therefore more than the population prevalence ( P &lt; 0.001). In another interesting study comparing the relapse rates in AUD between patients presenting to psychiatry versus gastroenterology OPD in a tertiary care setup in South India, it was found that poor sleep quality triggers relapse more in gastroenterology OPD; however, the significance of this needs to be understood in the wider population. Similar studies have found association between sleep quality and relapse rates.[ 121 ] In factor analysis of alcohol withdrawal scale, sleep wake disturbances have been identified to be an important component. Studies have shown that AUD has lower prevalence of restless leg syndrome of 2.4%, whereas in comorbid chronic liver disease, the prevalence is high.[ 122 , 123 ] In an interesting case report, it has been shown that alcohol withdrawal may present with REM sleep behavioral disorder.[ 124 ] There are also biological studies in India at the sleep–alcohol interface. On recently detoxified individuals with alcohol dependence, results on conventional sleep staging showed that patients had significantly lesser sleep efficiency, total sleep time, and longer sleep onset latency, while after multiple adjustments, spectral power analysis revealed significant differences only during REM sleep, specifically in high-frequency bands. Hence, the study concluded that both conventional PSG and power spectral analysis may have roles in understanding the effects of alcohol on sleep.[ 125 ] Apart from alcohol, both subjective and objective aspects of opioid and sleep interface have been studied. In an observation cross-sectional study on opioid-dependent males stabilized on buprenorphine, nearly 63% participants had PSQI score more than 5, suggesting sleep problems.[ 126 ] In recently detoxified 15 opioid-dependent patients, it was shown that patients had decreased total sleep time, sleep efficiency and stage N1 sleep, prolonged sleep latency, and increased limb movement latency in comparison to age, education, and handedness matched controls.[ 127 ] In a double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel randomized trial, 51 patients with opioid dependence started on trazodone 50 mg to 150 mg for sleep problems were found to have improved sleep disturbances in various components like quality, efficiency, and duration of sleep.[ 128 ] Among behavioral addiction research studies in India, studies at the interface of gambling and sleep are rare, except a study which looked at effects of sleep deprivation and test–retest gap in the Iowa Gambling Task in 40 healthy volunteers.[ 128 ] Internet addiction including gaming and their impact on sleep was researched from the pre-Covid era and intensified in the Covid era, when it has been studied among adolescents, students, and professionals across India from north, south, east, and northeast India, though a national survey is lacking in this regard.[ 129 , 130 , 131 ] Most of the studies are cross-sectional descriptive exploratory school/college or web-based studies with a mixed methodology design, and all of the studies showed significant correlation between Internet addiction, poor sleep quality, and mental health problems like anxiety and depression.[ 132 ] A meta-analysis involving 11 Indian studies showed that smartphone addiction is positively correlated with poor sleep quality (r = 0.17,-.31). An intervention involving yoga module has also been developed for smartphone addiction that considers sleep as an outcome.[ 133 ] DISCUSSION The objective of the current review was to look into the progress of research in the interface of sleep and psychiatric disorders in India. There were some striking observations while conducting this review. First, there has been a substantial volume of research that has been conducted in this field. If we compare the previous article that was published in this theme,[ 6 ] there has been a substantial increase in the volume of research that we could review. Second, we felt that there was a correlation in the volume of research that was conducted to the prevalence of the various sleep disorders. We could observe that the number of Indian studies was higher for disorders like insomnia or OSA, whereas the number of studies was relatively lesser for disorders of hypersomnia. Certain disorders like central disorders of hypersomnolence remain far less studied in India in the context of psychiatric disorders. Paucity of data on certain disorders and ample data on other disorders limited the systematic review approach. Insomnia or Sleep Initiation and Maintenance Disorders has been one of the most researched entities in the interface of sleep medicine and other psychiatric disorders. The research has extended to various psychiatric disorders like depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, psychotic disorders, and bipolar disorders. The research was also conducted in special groups like people with mental illness with other medical comorbidity (like patients with chronic renal failure) and in other special groups like teachers and students at universities. Overall, the pattern of the research findings has been uniform, which showed that there is significant co-occurrence of Sleep Initiation and Maintenance Disorders in these groups. The findings have been uniform despite use of various kinds of instruments used to study insomnia. Parasomnias have been researched widely with respect to psychiatric disorders in the global stage. Nightmares, sleepwalking, sleep paralysis, and RBDs have been found most in such disorders. Mood disorders, psychotic disorders, and anxiety disorders were commonly assessed. In most of the publications, a debate was seen discussing whether the parasomnias were a result of psychotropic medications or had a naturalistic propensity with psychiatric disorders.[ 134 , 135 ] Another aspect which has been studied extensively globally is the prevalence of parasomnias in childhood autism and ADHD.[ 136 ] Indian studies have lagged behind in delivering any comprehensive research (original articles and reviews) exclusively on parasomnias in psychiatric disorders, whereas a relatively better research has been done on the aspect of parasomnias in childhood. The number of reports on OSA and psychiatric disorders worldwide is very high (n = 14800 since 2024) on Google Scholar. But most of them are studies of OSA unrelated to psychiatric disorders. Global authors have assessed the prevalence of depression in the adult and pediatric population[ 137 ] and reported 28% prevalence among children having or at high risk of OSA. Pathophysiology of depression in OSA was also studied widely by international authors.[ 138 , 139 ] The outcome of co-occurrence of depression and OSA was also assessed.[ 140 , 141 ] Li et al .[ 142 ] have developed a machine learning model to screen for depression in adults with OSAS. The health care utilization cost of occult OSA in older adults with comorbid depression has been assessed by Wickwire et al .[ 143 ] A number of reports (n = 5850 since 2024) are available showing association between psychiatric disorders and hypersomnia from across the globe on Google Scholar. Global literature has addressed prevalence, pathophysiology, and predictor as response to treatment of psychiatric disorders, particularly depression.[ 144 , 145 , 146 , 147 , 148 , 149 ] Among these, Billard and Jasussent (2024) showed that nearly three fourth of patients with KLS show cognitive impairment and other psychiatric symptoms. Similarly, search for RLS after removing geographical areas resulted in 1070 results since 2024. This search showed systematic reviews and metanalyses of depression and anxiety with RLS.[ 150 , 151 ] Other explored areas were recognition of emotion expression by patients having RLS and feelings of alexithymia and anger in RLS.[ 152 , 153 ] The nature of the studies that we reviewed mostly were clinic-based studies and were observational in nature, with the common study designs including retrospective and prospective observations of different cohorts and following up. The common research questions that were tapped into with these approaches included the prevalence of various disorders, the rates of co-occurrences of various sleep disorders in various psychiatric disorders and vice-versa, and the comorbidities of these disorders. There were also a few clinical trials that have been conducted; however, they were largely in insomnia and OSA. Admittedly, the relatively lesser prevalence of the other sleep disorders may have hindered the possibility of conducted clinical trials. However, there also remains a virgin area of conducting research and multicentric studies could be planned in these areas. Most of the studies that were reviewed used questionnaire-based tools for the evaluation of the quality and severity of the disorders. Most of these tools have very good psychometric properties, have been translated to vernacular languages, and have been extensively used in Indian context. However, polysomnography- or actigraphy-based studies were relatively less frequently conducted and mostly have been in the context of sleep-related breathing disorders. Further studies should be conducted using more reliable tools to monitor sleep disorders like polysomnography. This should also act as evidence for the need of upscaling the sleep-related research infrastructure in the country. There are certain limitations in this study. First, while searching for literature, a spectrum of psychiatric disorders were glanced along with an array of sleep disorders. Since the area of research was quite large and diversified, a systematic review on the topic was difficult. A narrative review could however fulfil the purpose. Table 1 shows the volume of search done for this particular review. Second, for the same reasons, risk of bias could not be assessed in the reviewed literature. CONCLUSION To conclude, it can be said that the overlay between psychiatric disorders and sleep disorders has been in the forefront of research in India. The review substantiated that there is high prevalence of sleep disorders in Indian patients with psychiatric disorders. However, it was found that the research attempts have been largely clustered toward certain selected disorders and there was still a number of disorders that need more attention. It must be reiterated that there is a need for conducting more studies using designs that are higher up in the research hierarchy. Conflicts of interest There are no conflicts of interest.
