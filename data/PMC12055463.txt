For commercial re-use, please contact journals.permissions@oup.com Abstract With growing interest in causal inference and machine learning among epidemiologists, there is increasing discussion of causal discovery algorithms for guiding covariate selection. We present a case study of novice application of causal discovery tools and attempt to validate the results against a well-established causal relationship. As a case study, we attempted causal discovery of relationships relevant to the effect of adherence on mortality in the placebo arm of the Coronary Drug Project (CDP) data set. We used 4 algorithms available as existing software implementations and varied several model inputs. We identified 15 adjustment sets from 17 model parameterizations. When applied to a baseline covariate adjustment analysis, these 15 adjustment sets returned effect estimates with similar magnitude and direction of bias as prior published results. When using methods to control for time-varying confounding, there was generally more residual bias than compared to expert-selected adjustment sets. Although causal discovery algorithms can perform on par with expert knowledge, we do not recommend novice use of causal discovery without the input of experts in causal discovery. Expert support is recommended to aid in choosing the algorithm, selecting input parameters, assessing underlying assumptions, and finalizing selection of the adjustment variables. Introduction Graphical models are a useful and increasingly important aid for thinking about causal effect estimation in scientific research. “Causal graph” is a broad term for graphs or diagrams that provide explanation about relationships between variables and serve as a theoretical structure on which an etiologic study can be conceptualized. 1 Causal directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) have become widely used in epidemiology, statistics, and computer science for depicting causal relationships because of the incorporation of a mathematic framework linking these graphs to the joint probability distributions of the variables encoded in them. 2 , 3 Directed acyclic graphs are graphical representations of variables (nodes) and the relationships between them (edges, or arrows); a causal DAG is one in which the graph represents causal relationships between variables, and which includes all common causes of all variables on the graph. Unlike other graphical approaches, such as structural equation modeling (SEM), 4 causal DAGs are typically designed around a specific cause and effect relationship, and are nonparametric in nature. 4 The presence and direction of arrows on a DAG provides information about the direction of cause-and-effect relationships, but importantly, the absence of an arrow between 2 variables is the strong assumption encoded by the graphical model. 5 - 7 While no official best practices for creating DAGs exists, most literature stresses the importance of synthesizing expert knowledge to generate the best DAG representing the current state of evidence. 7 , 8 Directed acyclic graphs generated in this way can aid in identifying confounding factors and bias, identifying variables that must be measured and controlled, avoiding pitfalls in confounder selection, planning data collection, and analysis and communication of results. 1 , 5 , 9 - 12 However, there have also been theoretical developments aimed at allowing the use of machine learning to create a data-driven DAG with no or minimal expert input, called causal learning or causal discovery. Causal discovery algorithms attempt to generate causal graphs with structures consistent with the conditional independence relationships found in the data, exploiting the properties of confounders and colliders to attempt to orient the direction of relationships. 3 , 13 The graphs created in this way are often more similar in construction to SEMs, since they are not typically tied to a specific cause-and-effect relationship. However, many researchers foresee the use of these tools to allow automated generation of plausible causal inference models directly from high-dimensional data. 14 , 15 Indeed, a number of examples of causal discovery algorithms used to inform causal effect estimation exist in the literature, 16 - 18 despite concerns from some methodologists about the impact of “double dipping” on a single data set for constructing the graph and estimating the effect of interest. 19 There exists a large literature on the design and performance of various so-called causal learning or causal discovery algorithms. However, there has in general remained a methodological separation between causal discovery and causal inference—that is, between the generation of causal graphs from the data and the use of these graphs to help with valid effect estimation. In particular, very few examples exist using real-world data to benchmark the performance of causal discovery algorithms in situations where the underlying causal structure is known with a high degree of confidence. 20 The objective of the present article is to apply causal discovery algorithms following package instructions, as would a naïve user, and validate the resulting data-driven DAGs against a known confounded effect: the effect of placebo adherence on 5-year mortality in the Coronary Drug Project (CDP) trial. 21 - 23 While the exact confounding structure for this relationship is of course unknown, there is a widespread scientific consensus that the true value of this causal effect is null and there is strong evidence in support of the existence of confounding for this effect by both baseline and post-baseline covariates. 21 - 23 In addition, there is strong evidence that the available measured covariates, together with appropriate causal inference techniques, can successfully remove this confounding, suggesting that available data should provide at least 1 minimally sufficient adjustment set for the causal discovery algorithm to find. 21 - 23 Methods Data set and covariates We used the Coronary Drug Project (CDP) data set, available from NHLBI BioLincc. 24 The CDP was a 6-arm double-blind placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial for assessing potential treatments to reduce premature mortality in men with a history of myocardial infarction. 25 We restricted our analyses to individuals assigned to the placebo arm ( n = 2630), since it has previously been demonstrated that the relationship between adherence and mortality in this group is heavily confounded. We specified the 39 baseline covariates used in the original 1980 CDP study, plus the original version of cumulative adherence and mortality. 21 , 25 In addition, we included the outcome variable mortality during the trial (yes/no), as well as the exposure variable cumulative adherence ≥80% vs &lt;80% on average over the trial duration. 21 , 25 Additional details of the data are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1 . The available implementations of causal discovery algorithms which we considered included default settings with the assumptions that all variables be of the same data type (ie, either all categorical or all continuous), that categorical variables start at 0, and that the data do not include multiple time points of the same variables. While algorithm settings can be varied, these data simplifications were reasonable for the Coronary Drug Project data analysis because in the original 1980 placebo arm analyses , 21 most of these same simplifications had been made; that article assessed relationship between a binary measure of cumulative adherence and the cumulative incidence of death, with all covariates either dichotomous or having only a small number of categorical levels ( Supplementary Table 1 ). We recoded the risk group variable to match algorithmic requirements (original levels: 1, 2; new levels: 0, 1). We further restricted to complete cases ( n = 2413) within the placebo arm (ie, individuals with no missingness for any of the 39 baseline covariates, mortality within 5 years, or the exposure of interest, cumulative 5-year adherence to placebo). 21 - 23 Algorithms We initially selected the Peter-Clark (PC) algorithm, which assumes there are no unmeasured confounders affecting the data, but for which there is a simple implementation in the R software language. 26 - 28 The existence of the R package pcalg suggested to us that the PC algorithm would be the most likely algorithm chosen by novice users of causal discovery methods. Full details of the algorithm are beyond the scope of the current article, but briefly, the PC algorithm uses an iterative multistep process to identify a complete, but not necessarily fully directed or acyclic, graph (known as a completed partially directed acyclic graph or cpDAG), which summarizes the relationships in the inputted data set. The first step connects all variables in the data set to all other variables with directionless edges and then tests every edge conditional on each other nonedge variable in the data set. Conditional on each other variable, edges for which the null hypothesis of no association fails to be rejected at the prespecified alpha level are omitted from the graph; those for which the null hypothesis is rejected are retained. Once all edges have been checked, the algorithm then locates all connected triplets of variables and attempts to assign directionality to the connecting edges. Directionality is estimated by conditioning sequentially on each node in the triplet and exploiting the difference between colliders (conditioning creates association) and confounders/mediators (conditioning removes association). Again, edges are removed if the association fails to reject the null hypothesis at the prespecified alpha level. This process is iterated until no new edges can be removed or oriented. We then extended our analyses to also assess 3 additional algorithms: the Fast Causal Inference (FCI), Fast Greedy Causal Search (FGES), and Greedy Relaxed Sparsest Permutation (GRaSP) algorithms. The FCI algorithm uses a test-based approach to determine edges, whereas the FGES and GRaSP algorithms use score-based approaches. 20 , 28 Software We used existing implementations of these algorithms in the Tetrad software package 29 and in R using the package pcalg (version 2.7). 27 Table 1 summarizes the model inputs and software variations that we assessed (additional details in Supplementary Table 2 ). In R, we ran the algorithm using default settings for discrete data and set the initial alpha level to 0.01. We also compared the results using 4 additional prespecified alpha levels: 0.001, 0.05, 0.10, and 0.20. A larger alpha level is expected to result in more truly null edges being included in the DAG due to a larger number of false positive rejections of the null hypothesis. In Tetrad, we initially encountered difficulties obtaining results. However, these difficulties were ultimately resolved by ensuring all variables were categorical and that the discrete option was specified in the data input menu. We used the graphical user interface (GUI) version of Tetrad 7.1.2-2 and implemented the algorithms according to the Tetrad manual instructions. Briefly, for the PC algorithm and FCI algorithm, we used χ 2 tests with alpha level 0.01. For the FGES algorithm, we used a discrete Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) test, with maximum network degree of 40. Note that when running the model with no prior knowledge, we were required to reduce the network degree to 10 because of long computation times. For the GRaSP algorithm, we used χ 2 tests and the Bayesian Dirichlet equivalent uniform (BDEU) score with an alpha level of 0.01. We also compared results with the GRaSP algorithm using the BIC test. For all algorithms, we first ran the models on the data set with no additional prior knowledge. We then added 2 types of prior knowledge, sorting the variables into either 3 or 4 tiers of time points. In the 3-tier knowledge models, we specified that all covariates happened before adherence and that adherence happened before death. In the 4-tier knowledge models, we additionally specified that age and race happened before any other covariates. In this way, we aimed to ensure that adherence preceded death, while maximizing the possible covariates that could be selected as potential confounders. For all models, we first ran a single iteration and then ran the models with 100 bootstrap samples. Selecting adjustment sets For each model, we plotted the resulting cpDAGs using the automated causal graphics tool cStructure. 30 We were unable to identify best practices in the literature for selecting adjustment sets from bootstrapped results. In the absence of alternative guidance, we chose to graph all links detected in at least 1 bootstrap sample. When this resulted in an acyclic graph, we used cStructure to identify minimally sufficient adjustment sets for the causal effect of adherence on death. When the resulting graph was cyclic, we attempted to resolve the cycles by simplification. If this was not possible, we instead selected as the adjustment set all detected causes of adherence or death since the use of all causes of either adherence or death should both control for confounding and minimize the possibility of induced collider bias. 31 We also created a second adjustment set excluding any potential adherence-mortality causal intermediates from the previous list. Statistical analyses We summarized the results of the causal discovery algorithms by drawing each estimated graph and qualitatively by describing key features of the output. We also summarized the results of the bootstrap ensembles by plotting pairwise matrices indicating the proportion of bootstrap samples from each model detecting any edge between each pair of variables. For comparison, we also plotted pairwise correlation coefficients between all pairs of variables in the CDP data set using Pearson correlation (proc corr in SAS). To evaluate the performance of the adjustment sets, we estimated the effect of binary cumulative adherence (≥80% vs &lt;80%) on the cumulative incidence of mortality over 5 years among CDP trial participants assigned to placebo, adjusting for each adjustment set identified by any of the causal learning models. The statistical analysis we used for estimating the causal effect has been reported in detail elsewhere, and we reused software that our team previously developed for these analyses. The SAS code is available at https://github.com/eleanormurray/CDP-analysis2024 , and additional details are reproduced in Supplementary Appendix 1e . Briefly, we used logistic regression to model the outcome of mortality. We report unadjusted results and results adjusted for baseline covariates only and for baseline and time-varying covariates. When adjusting for time-varying covariates, we use inverse probability weighting to model the relationship between baseline and time-varying covariates and adherence over time, where the time-varying covariates are any baseline variables with measures available postrandomization. Both baseline-only, and baseline and time-varying covariate adjusted analyses are standardized across baseline covariates to ensure maximum comparability between analyses. To maximize comparability between the analyses, we used a data set excluding any observations with missingness for any covariates in any of the adjustment sets. P value functions were computed using the pvaluefunction package in R. 32 , 33 Results Figure 1 shows the P values for the pairwise correlation matrix for all covariates including adherence and death (for correlation coefficients see Supplementary Figure 1 ). Overall, there were 397 pairwise correlations with P values at or below 0.05 out of 820 pairwise comparisons. Applying a Bonferroni correction to reduce the alpha value to 0.00006 resulted in 183 statistically significant pairwise correlations ( Supplementary Figure 2 ). Adherence and death each show significant correlations with many covariates including each other. These results further support the conclusion of prior analyses of these data 21 that the relationship between adherence and mortality in these placebo arm data is likely confounded. When causal discovery algorithms were run without bootstrap samples, none of the algorithms detected any bias pathways between adherence and mortality, and none of the algorithms identified any variables as parent nodes of adherence ( Table 2 ; Supplementary Table 3 ). Supplementary Figure 2 displays cpDAGs from all algorithms when run with no bootstraps. Ten of the algorithms returned cpDAGs with direct edges from adherence to death (4 of 8 PC algorithm models, 2 of 3 FGES models, and 4 of 4 GRaSP models), while 3 returned indirect paths (1 of 3 FGES models, 2 of 4 GRaSP models). Only 5 of the models identified any variables caused by adherence other than mortality: FGES model 1 identified adherence as a cause of NY Heart Association functional class, diastolic blood pressure, cholesterol, triglycerides, and fasting glucose; and GRaSP models 1 and 2 identified adherence as a cause of every other variable. The variables most frequently identified as causes of mortality across the algorithms, without bootstraps, were ST segment depression on ECG (12 models), history of cardiomegaly (8 models), high/low number of prior heart attacks (2 models), and NY Heart Association functional class (2 models). Intermittent claudication and diuretic use were each identified by one model. Four of the 6 models that did not identify ST segment depression as a cause of mortality instead identified it as a consequence of mortality. Likewise, 2 models identified cardiomegaly as a consequence of death (including one model, PC 2, which identified the relationship with death as nondirected). When accounting for relationships between covariates, ST segment depression, diuretics use, antihypertensive medication use, and congestive heart failure were each identified as adherence-mortality intermediates by 2 models. The use of time-ordering improved the interpretability of the graphs from all 4 algorithms, particularly in terms of the covariate-covariate relationships. When no prior information was provided to the algorithm, several models returned “forbidden” relationships, including the 4 models that identified consequences of mortality. In addition, all models that did not include rules specifying that age and race occur prior to the other covariates resulted in at least one of these variables being identified as a consequence of one or more other variable. Explicitly specifying that these variables can only be causes of other variables in the model resulted in correct temporal ordering for all of the 4-tier models. The FGES and GRaSP algorithms allowed computation of scoring functions when not running bootstrap samples ( Table 2 ). The BIC results worsened (lower values generally indicate better model fit) across FGES models as more prior information was added and also suggested that the GRaSP model had fit better than the FGES models. In comparison, the BDEU score, for which higher values are generally taken to indicate better model fit, were similar across the 3 GRaSP models, showing a slight improvement in model fit as more prior information was added. After running the models with 100 bootstrap samples, we were able to determine potential adjustment sets. First, we graphed each set of bootstrap ensemble output selecting the highest probability direction for each arrow and assuming that all arrows identified in at least one sample were potentially correct. For all models, this resulted in graphs that were cyclic and could not be resolved into clear adjustment sets. We attempted to iteratively simplify these graphs following these steps: (1) remove nodes with no descendants; (2) replace nodes with one descendant with an arrow from any parents to the single descendant; and (3) remove arrows with an ensemble probability below a specified threshold. At each iteration, the threshold was increased as follows: from 1% to 5% by 1 percentage point; then from 5% to 20% by 5 percentage points; and from 20% to 50% by 10 percentage points. At each step, we checked the graph for cycles and for potential adherence adjustment sets. In all cases, all potential bias paths between adherence and death were removed before the simplified graph was acyclic. After reducing the graph complexity by including only those edges detected in at least 50% of the bootstrap samples, none of the ensemble results implied any adjustment sets. Rather than conclude that there were no appropriate adjustment sets, we instead selected for each ensemble all covariates identified as potential causes of either mortality or adherence in at least one bootstrap sample, ignoring the potential covariate-covariate relationships ( Table 3 ). We then created a second adjustment set by restricting this list to remove any variables identified in the corresponding model without bootstraps as a potential causal intermediate—this second type of adjustment set was only applicable to 3 models (FGES 1, GRaSP 1, and GRaSP 2). We used these adjustment sets to estimate the effect of adherence to placebo on mortality over 5 years in the CDP data set ( Table 4 ). This effect is widely agreed to be truly null, but the unadjusted adherence-mortality comparison in the placebo arm suggests a 5-year risk difference of 11 percentage points. In prior CDP analyses implementing causal inference methods to adjust for nonadherence and loss to follow-up, analyses adjusting for baseline covariates reduced the estimated 5-year risk difference to 7 percentage points (95% confidence interval [CI], 2.1-11.2). 23 Using the adjustment sets we identified from the causal discovery algorithms to replicate this analysis, we found that the estimated 5-year risk difference was very similar across all adjustment sets and input parameters. The estimated values ranged from a low of 6.4 percentage points (95% CI, 2.0-10.8) using the GRaSP 1a and GRaSP 2a adjustment sets to a high of 7.9 percentage points (95% CI, 3.6-12.3) using the FGES 1b adjustment set. Although these results are biased away from the suspected true null value, the magnitude and direction of the bias reduction relative to the unadjusted analysis and of the remaining residual bias match closely to the original covariate list created by the CDP trial team. Adherence in the CDP trial occurred over a period of up to 5 years, and thus adjustment for time-varying adherence-covariate confounding has previously been shown to be necessary to obtain a null effect estimate. 22 , 23 When we used the baseline and follow-up visit values of the covariates from our causal discovery algorithm adjustment sets, the estimated 5-year risk difference was reduced to between 2.9 percentage points (95% CI, −1.7 to 7.6) using the GRaSP 1a and GRaSP 2a adjustment sets, and 5.4 percentage points (95% CI, 1.1-9.7) using the FGES 1b adjustment set. Using the original CDP trial team adjustment set with this same analytic approach, the estimated 5-year risk difference was previously reported as a slightly lower 2.6 percentage points (95% CI, −2.2 to 7.5). 23 Figure 2 displays the P value functions 32 , 33 for these analyses; while the original CDP team’s covariate set shows the most compatibility with the hypothesized true null risk difference of 0, several of the GRaSP algorithms perform nearly as well (see Supplementary Figure 4 for P value functions for baseline covariate adjustment). On the other hand, many of the other algorithms’ covariate sets show almost no compatibility with the null hypothesis of a risk difference equal to 0. Discussion The goal of this study was to assess the feasibility of a novice user implementing causal discovery algorithms to construct a plausible set of DAGs with which to design an analytic plan for estimating a causal effect. Previous implementations of these algorithms have generally relied on simulated data, for which all variables are known, or have been applied to new data sets where other attempts at creating the DAG have not previously been made. Here, we apply 4 established causal discovery algorithms to a data set with a well-known pattern of confounding for the relationship between adherence and mortality—the Coronary Drug Project trial. In this trial, we have a strong prior that the true causal effect of adherence on mortality among placebo arm participants is null, and substantial evidence of this effect is heavily confounded. 21 - 23 Although we were not able to identify clear adjustment sets from any of the algorithm outputs initially, we were able to select adjustment sets after running the models with bootstraps. Importantly, the bootstrap ensemble results gave cyclic graphs from which adjustment set selection was not immediately obvious. The presence of cycles prohibited the use of automated tools for selecting adjustment sets. Instead, we selected all causes of the exposure or the outcome for our adjustment sets. In several cases, this included variables which in the initial models had been included as causal intermediates between adherence and death. To avoid conditioning on mediators, we created a second adjustment set excluding these variables where relevant. This resulted in 15 unique adjustment sets for the 12 models we ran using bootstraps, mirroring previous research that suggests different causal discovery algorithms will return different graphs. 26 , 28 , 34 Qualitatively, we found that the model results were more representative of our knowledge about the world when more tiers of knowledge were used to construct these models. That is, when the models were specified without prior knowledge, there were occasionally child nodes resulting from death. Similarly, when models were specified with minimal prior knowledge, we found that age and race were often identified as consequences of other covariates. When we specified 4 tiers of time-ordering (age and race, all other covariates, adherence, then death), these inappropriate relationship patterns no longer occurred. On the other hand, even with the addition of 4-tiers of time-ordering, several algorithms continued to suggest a direct causal relationship between adherence and mortality, which contradicts scientific consensus about this relationship. In terms of quantitative comparisons between algorithm results, only 2 of the algorithms provided global model scores, and only when the algorithms were run without bootstraps. For these algorithms, the pattern of model scores showed only minimal quantitative difference between algorithms of the same type as model inputs were differed, with the largest difference in score observed when changing algorithm type. When applying the adjustment sets selected from our causal discovery results to previously reported analytic approaches for estimating the 5-year risk difference in mortality comparing placebo adherers and placebo nonadherers in the CDP trial, we found varying success in removing known biases from the data. In general, the causal discovery adjustment sets performed approximately as well as the original expert-identified adjustment set when only baseline covariate values were used. In all cases, this resulted in a roughly 36% reduction of bias existing in the unadjusted relationship but not a total removal of this bias. When using both baseline and time-varying covariate analyses, there was a reduction of between 50% and almost 75% of the bias observed in the unadjusted relationship; but in all cases, the analyses using the adjustment sets from causal discovery resulted in more residual bias than the original list of covariates was used. Interestingly, the pattern of decreasing BIC scores correlated with the bias reduction comparing FGES model variants and GRaSP model 1, whereas the pattern of increasing BDEU scores used for the 3 additional GRaSP models did not correlate with the bias reduction in these models. Overall, we found that when the true value of the causal effect is known with a high degree of certainty, existing implementations of causal discovery algorithms may be able to partially substitute for expert knowledge in the selection of adjustment covariates. Even when complex analytic plans are required, this case study provides some evidence that causal discovery algorithms may be sufficiently developed to allow researchers to obtain effect estimates of comparable magnitude and direction as expert selected covariate lists. However, the use of causal discovery algorithms is not straightforward. We encountered a number of challenges in using the causal discovery implementations. We also obtained a wide range of point estimates when varying the algorithm input parameters. In a setting where the true effect estimate was unknown, interpreting the results of the 15 analyses we present would be highly subjective. The implementations we used also have a number of limitations. One challenge is that extensions of causal discovery to the setting of time-varying covariates and exposures are not yet widely accessible. On the other hand, syntheses of expert knowledge to create DAGs often assume that time-varying covariates share patterns of exposure-outcome relationships over time. We opted to mirror this by providing the algorithms with baseline covariate values only. Despite this, we still found it difficult to interpret the resulting graphs without providing at least some time-ordering information to the algorithm. In particular, the output seemed more reasonable when the algorithms were provided the knowledge that time-invariant covariates (such as race) occurred earliest in the temporal order. Nonetheless, the best performing algorithm, in terms of the covariate set which resulted in the smallest residual bias when estimating the effect of placebo adherence on mortality, was one in which the time-ordering of covariates was not specified. Use of these causal discovery algorithms also required several potentially subjective parameter decisions—or the user must decide to use default software parameters, as we did here for most inputs. For example, the PC, FCI, and GRaSP algorithms required setting an alpha value for determining the existence of relationships in the data, while the FGES algorithm required specifying the maximum degree of the model (ie, maximum amount of connectivity between variables). Understanding how the results may vary across the full range of parameter inputs is beyond the scope of this article but will be important for ensuring principled selection of model inputs. In addition, these algorithms generally required that the data type of all variables is the same. This is not a requirement of other methods that epidemiologists are accustomed to using, and many epidemiologic data sets will include a mixture of binary, ordinal, nominal, and continuous variables. For our case study, it was reasonable to dichotomize the covariates and exposure, since this is what had been done in the original 1980 CDP adherence analysis. However, in many cases, decisions about data cleaning will be arbitrary and the consequences for causal discovery output can be unclear. Finally, our choice of algorithms and software was also subjective. Since our goal was to emulate novice use, we attempted to select the simplest implementation. Other implementations exist, such as the bnlearn package in R, 35 and based on our findings, it is likely that the resulting covariate sets would have differed had we used these. Conclusion Using existing causal discovery software, we were able to approximately replicate previous findings of a confounded relationship between adherence to placebo and mortality in the CDP trial, as well as previous findings of a null relationship after adjusting for time-varying confounding-adherence feedback. However, varying the parameter inputs and algorithm type resulted in a range of unique adjustment sets, only half of which provided any evidence of compatibility of the data with a null effect. As a result, it is not clear that the use of causal discovery algorithms provides any less subjectivity in terms of causal assumptions than the use of expert knowledge to generate causal graphs. We recommend that researchers without detailed knowledge of causal discovery algorithms not attempt to use these without the aid of an expert in causal discovery. Absent this expert support, the use of subject matter experts will likely provide more transparency about the assumptions required for causal effect estimation. In our case study, the use of subject matter experts also resulted in the best estimate of the “true” causal effect.
