Abstract In 2023, the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) introduced a provision regarding preserved ratio-impaired spirometry (PRISm), a presumed pre-stage of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), into the COPD guidelines. However, further research in this area is needed. Our study aimed to investigate the epigenetic differences between PRISm and COPD. EWAS ( n = 572) and RNA-sequencing ( n = 60) were performed on blood samples from the COPD registry, and EWAS was replicated in the KoGES cohort data ( n = 98). Our findings revealed significant epigenetic differences between patients with PRISm and COPD. 39,980 CpG-sites displayed differential methylation between PRISm and COPD. Seven gene regions—EEF1A2, EMP2, EPCAM, MTSS1L, ARHGEF10, HYDIN, and FADS2 were not only differentially methylated but also exhibited differential expression. The consistency of differential methylation of CpG sites in five genes, excluding ARHGEF10 and MTSS1L, was replicated in the KoGES study, affirming the distinction between COPD and PRISm. Our research identified seven gene regions as critical contributors related to the modulation of gene expression, including CpG sites that differentiate COPD from PRISm. These results highlight the significance of DNA methylation changes in distinguishing PRISm from COPD and shed light on potential mechanisms by which methylation alterations impact lung function. Introduction Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), an ailment influenced by genetics and environmental factors like smoking and air pollution, is a chronic inflammatory condition that leads to gradual airflow restriction. In 2019, COPD affected 18.6% of Korean adult males aged 40 and above (Cho 2021 ). Some individuals with PRISm are prone to developing airflow problems over time, exhibiting respiratory symptoms, lung structural abnormalities, or physiological irregularities (including reduced lung capacity, gas entrapment, hyperinflation, or rapid FEV1 decline) without classic irreversible airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC ≥ 0.7 post-bronchodilation) (Wan, et al. 2014 ; Amin and Mohsen 2020 ). PRISm can evolve into COPD, the third leading cause of global mortality (Amin and Mohsen 2020 ). Despite its rising prevalence in Korea (11–12%), PRISm remains underdiagnosed and underestimated (Lee et al. 2015 ). Recent epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) have pinpointed specific CpG sites with abnormal DNA methylation linked to COPD (Imboden 2019 ). Methylation, a stable and inheritable epigenetic modification mechanism, can modulate gene expression without altering DNA sequences and can change in response to environmental triggers like smoking. Profiling methylation sites related to environmental influences holds promise for COPD diagnosis and management (He et al. 2020 ). Furthermore, a 2023 GOLD report underscores the significance of PRISm and pre-COPD in COPD diagnosis and management (Amin and Mohsen 2020 ; Alvar et al. 2023 ). Gene expression dysregulation is common in various human diseases, including COPD. In COPD, abnormal mRNAs and non-coding RNAs have significant roles in the initiation and progression of the disease. The application of next-generation sequencing has allowed us to identify differentially expressed RNA molecules, highlighting their importance in COPD. The transcriptome, which encompasses all transcripts and their respective quantities, is vital for understanding the molecular composition of cells and tissues. It also assists in deciphering how the genome functions in diverse physiological and pathological situations (Yeo, et al. 2018 ; Wang et al. 2009 ). Our research, which focuses on finding epigenetic factors contributing to the distinction between PRISm and COPD, is a significant step towards a deeper understanding of these conditions. We consider methylation and gene expression in our quest to discover genes. We conducted this analysis within the Korean COPD Subgroup Study—National Institutes of Health (KOCOSS-NIH) registry to elucidate the underlying epigenetic mechanisms driving the pathologies of PRISm and COPD. Our investigation assessed genome-wide variations in DNA methylation and correlated these variations with transcriptome data. Additionally, to validate these epigenomic-wide findings, we cross-referenced them with the KoGES study cohort to confirm the significance of the identified methylation changes. Methods Study design and subjects Data were obtained from the Korean COPD Subgroup Study (KOCOSS-NIH; 2016–2020), an ongoing prospective multicenter registry that recruited patients with COPD from 54 referral hospitals in the Republic of Korea. The inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥ 40 years, and (2) COPD diagnosed by pulmonologists based on respiratory symptoms and spirometry confirmed fixed airflow limitation (post-bronchodilator FEV 1 /FVC) &lt; 70). (3) Patients with FEV 1 /FVC ≥ 70%, but FEV 1 &lt; 80% are classified as PRISm. Using G*power, we confirmed that 138 participants were required, assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.3, a two-sided test, a type 1 error of 0.05, and power of 0.95 (Faul et al. 2007 ). Ethical considerations The study complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of each participating medical center. All data were collected anonymously, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was also approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korea National Institute of Health (KNIH; IRB No. 2020–03–06- P- A) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical data collection A total of 1,341 patients with COPD admitted to the referral hospitals between January 2016 and December 2020 were enrolled in this study. Data on the sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history and amount smoked, and spirometer values such as post-bronchodilator FEV 1 , FVC, FEV 1 /FVC of the patient were collected. In addition, the information on the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), St George ’ s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ), and Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scoring of symptom severity, treatment drug, and other clinical information, including deterioration within a year, were also collected. COPD was defined as FEV1/FVC &lt; 70%, PRISm was defined as FEV1/FVC ≥ 70 &amp; FEV1 &lt; 80%, and normal was defined as FEV1/FVC ≥ 70 &amp; FEV1 ≥ 80. After group classification according to lung function, it was confirmed that females were only in PRISm and Normal. Females were excluded from all analyses to make the analysis accurate and comparable to COPD. Our replication study used data from the 5th period of the Korean Genomics and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), conducted between 2009 and 2010 at the National Biobank, Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KBN-2023–028). Within this study, subjects from the KoGES cohort were categorized into three groups based on lung function and clinical data: Normal ( n = 4,079), PRISm ( n = 117), and COPD ( n = 175). People without lung function were excluded, the classification was divided into the same conditions as the KOCOSS-NIH criteria, and females were excluded for the same analysis. DNA methylation DNA samples were collected from 660 patients and converted using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, Tustin, CA, USA) before being added to the Infinium Methylation EPIC BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). DNA methylation data were obtained four times from 2019 to 2021 and normalized using the minfi and limma package in R (4.2.1). Detailed conditions for DNA methylation progression are described in Text S1. The analysis identified 660 samples, and CpG sites were retained after excluding those with a detection p- value of 0.05 or more, an indicator of methylation reliability. Filtering was also performed for samples that did not satisfy the detection p- value of &lt; 0.05, and all samples met the criteria in this analysis (Chen et al. 2013 ). Additionally, we adjusted the DNA methylation data using ReFACTor algorithm to account for the potential confounding effects of cell type heterogeneity (Rahmani et al. 2016 ). To correct for batch effects after applying EWAS model, we applied bacon (Iterson et al. 2017 ). Furthermore, we replicated the KoGES Ansan and Ansung DNA methylation data ( n = 729) obtained from the 850 K platform of the bioresources available at the National Biobank, Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KBN-2023–028). RNA sequencing RNA was collected from the blood of patients ( n = 82) admitted to the hospital from 2018 to 2020 using the PAXgene® Blood RNA Tube. RNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq standard mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and RNA was purified using the PAXgene Blood RNA Kit IVD (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer ’ s instructions. RNA sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq platform. DEG analysis was performed using edgeR and DESeq2 R package. Statistical analysis This study conveyed continuous variables by presenting means with standard deviation (SD), and numerical counts and respective percentages represented categorical variables. The Independent-samples t-test or ANOVA compared continuous variables. The Pearson ’ s Chi-squared test was done to compare categorical variables. Tests of normality were performed and determined by Shapiro–Wilk ’ s p- value. The criterion used to establish statistical significance was defined as P &lt; 0.05. False discovery rate (FDR) was adjusted for P- value correction, and statistical significance was accepted when FDR &lt; 0.05. For the KOCOSS-NIH and KoGES analysis, age, BMI, and smoking status were corrected as covariates to regression. Results Baseline characteristics In the DNA methylation data, notable demographic and clinical differences were observed between the patients with COPD and those with PRISm. Specifically, the mean age of COPD patients (78.6 ± 3.3 years) was significantly higher than that of PRISm patients (66.3 ± 6.2 years). BMI exhibited similar trends in both groups, and most individuals in the COPD and PRISm groups resided in metropolitan areas. More than half of the COPD group ( n = 92/127, 72.4%) and PRISm group ( n = 288/445, 64.7%) had ever smoked. Additionally, FEV1 was significantly higher in the COPD group than in the PRISm group (53.7 ± 15.7 vs. 65.6 ± 18.9; P = 7.491e-10). Conversely, FEV1/FVC was significantly higher in the PRISm group than in the COPD group (73.1 ± 2.3 vs. 68.1 ± 1.2; P &lt; 2.2e-16). However, FEF25-75% was significantly higher in the COPD group than in the PRISm group (25.6 ± 12.5 vs. 33.6 ± 17.1; P = 2.554e-06) (Table 1 ). For the RNA sequencing dataset, all subjects ( n = 60) were male, and substantial age differences were noted between COPD patients (79.3 ± 3.6 years) and PRISm patients (66.7 ± 6.4 years). Regarding smoking history, 81.2% of COPD patients were former smokers, 75.0% of PRISm patients had a history of smoking, and 20.5% were current smokers in the PRISm group. Interestingly, none of the COPD patients were current smokers, and this difference approached statistical significance ( P = 0.046). Additionally, FEV1 (58.3 ± 15.0 vs. 75.2 ± 19.6), FEV1/FVC (72.9 ± 2.3 vs. 67.9 ± 1.5), and FEF25–75% (28.3 ± 14.2 vs. 54.9 ± 33.7) showed similar trends as in the DNA methylation dataset, with distinct differences between the two groups (Table 1 ). Following comparative analyses matched for gender, age, and smoking history – key factors contributing to COPD – no significant differences remained between PRISm and COPD groups. In the KOCOSS-NIH, we used lung function-related measures to group subjects into normal, prism, and COPD. Then, we examined the statistical significance of the differences between each group. Clinical information for all KOCOSS-NIH subjects and males only (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), DNA methylation subjects (Supplementary Table 3), and RNA sequencing subjects is provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table 4). Characteristics of cohorts and pulmonary function differences among KoGES Based on FEV1/FVC and FEV1 ratio, 98 subjects were classified into PRISm and COPD. PRISm subjects were much younger than COPD subjects, the same as the KOCOSS-NIH registry, but the average age of PRISm subjects was younger than that of KOCOSS-NIH. There was no statistical difference between the two groups in terms of BMI, area of residence, and smoking history, but there was a statistically significant in the smoking duration. Significant differences were observed between the two groups concerning lung function indices, including FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, and especially FEF25-75%. Like KOCOSS-NIH, the PRISm group also showed FEV1, FVC, and FEF25-75% values (Table 2 ). Clinical information for all KoGES Ansan and Ansung and DNA methylation subjects is provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary Tables 5–7). Identification of differentially methylated positions (DMPs) EWAS was analyzed separately in the following groups: 1)COPD/PRISm, 2) COPD/Normal, 3) PRISm/Normal. 666,409 probes were compared for PRISm and COPD, COPD and normal, and normal and PRISm. First, 39,980 probes for genes showing significant differential methylation between PRISm and COPD showed significant FDR, p- value &lt; 0.05 (Supplementary Table 8). There were no significant probes showing differences in PRISm compared to normal. 3,978 probes were significant in COPD compared to normal (Supplementary Table 9). All methylation polymorphism analyses were adjusted for BMI and smoking status. Supplementary Table 10 presented the top 10 CpG sites after adjusting for BMI, smoking status, and cell-type proportion. Supplementary Table 11–13 shows overlapping CpG sites among the CpG sites organized in Table 3 by group. Identification of DEGs The study conducted a differential expression analysis between PRISm and COPD, revealing 26 genes exhibiting statistical significance, with FDR and P- values below 0.05. The genes with the most considerable log2 fold change value were miRNAs, with AL050316.1 downregulated, and AC007365.2 upregulated. In addition, the TRAV40 gene, known as the T cell receptor, was upregulated, while genes related to immunoglobulin, IGLV4-60, and IGLV9-49, were downregulated, but IGHD2-2 was upregulated. The TSPAN7 gene, which has been reported to have a high correlation with lung disease, was also confirmed to be downregulated (Supplementary Table 14). Correlation between differential methylation and expression of genes Our study analyzed specific genes that exhibited differential methylation and expression patterns. We also assessed the overall correlation between the extent of methylation differences and expression levels. In DMPs and DEGs results, seven genes of the 39,980 CpG sites and 26 DEGs site genes that met the FDR and p- value &lt; 0.05 criteria were confirmed to overlap (Fig. 1 ). Among the genes examined, we pinpointed EEF1A2, ARHGEF10, EMP2, EPCAM, MTSS1L, FADS2, and HYDIN as specific genes displaying differential methylation and expression. Therefore, we conducted a comparative analysis of methylation values focusing on these seven genes and conducted replication in the KoGES cohort. KoGES also conducted DMPs analysis under the same conditions, and a total of 259 probes satisfied with the FDR and p- value &lt; 0.05 conditions in COPD compared to PRISm (Supplementary Table 15), two probes in PRISm compared to normal (Supplementary Table 16), and COPD compared to normal was 47,879 (Supplementary Table 17). In particular, the significant CpG site detected in KOCOSS-NIH, considering BMI, smoking status, cell type heterogeneity, and batch effect was only one, cg21259808 of HYDIN, and cg03074225 and cg26186239 of EEF1A2, cg06458358 of ARHGEF10, cg08093537 of FADS2, cg00451635, cg07406359, cg06713374 of EMP2 showed marginal significance (Table 3 ). In addition, cg23433995, cg15792957 of EPCAM, cg08573648 of MTSS1L, and cg07406359 of EMP2 were CpG sites that passed dmrff (Table 3 ). We also compiled previous studies from the EWAS catalog of relevant CpG sites as references (Table 3 ). For ARHGEF10, all CpG sites exhibited hypomethylation, and the direction of methylation change at cg27134322 showed opposite results in KoGES compared to KOCOSS-NIH (Table 3 ). Notably, RNA expression was upregulated, showing a 1.63-fold increase in the COPD group compared to PRISm, and this difference was statistically significant ( P = 0.006; Table 4 ). EPCAM, MTSS1L, HYDIN , and FADS2 generally displayed hypomethylation in the KOCOSS-NIH dataset, and their RNA expression tended to decrease. Specifically, for EPCAM and MTSS1L , it was observed that CpG sites were generally hypomethylated (Table 3 ). Regarding expression changes, EPCAM decreased by approximately −2.5-fold, while MTSS1L decreased by approximately −1.15-fold. Conversely, EEF1A2 and EMP2 generally displayed hypermethylation in their CpG sites, accompanied by decreased RNA expression levels. Specifically, EEF1A2 exhibited a −1.9-fold decrease in expression, while EMP2 showed an −0.8-fold decrease (Table 4 ). Seven genes in the KoGES cohort were identified as showing similar patterns. Additionally, because there were subjects with normal lung function in the KoGES cohort, differences in seven genes were summarized in comparisons between people with normal lung function and COPD subjects and between people with normal lung function and PRISm subjects (Supplementary Table 18). In addition, considering that COPD disease is greatly influenced by environmental factors such as smoking, we also conducted a comparison of DNA methylation between KOCOSS-NIH and KoGES in non-smokers with COPD/Normal (Supplementary Table 19). DNA methylation was compared between KOCOSS-NIH and KoGES without smoking information (Supplementary Table 20). Discussion Our study identified essential epigenetic markers pinpointing CpG regions likely to impact COPD more than PRISm significantly. Although previously well-established COPD-associated genes were not identified in our study, significant methylation changes associated with COPD were observed. Importantly, we can cross-reference with RNA sequencing results to identify the functional impact of methylation-induced changes. Further validation in the KoGES cohort, representing a general population, confirmed the consistency of our findings between PRISm and COPD groups, thereby reinforcing the significance of our results. Smoking is a crucial etiological factor in COPD, and it is worth highlighting that smoking can trigger cellular stress and has been associated with various DNA methylation patterns. This connection between smoking, cellular stress, and DNA methylation patterns plays a significant role in understanding the development and progression of COPD (Eriksson Strom et al. 2022 ). DNA methylation profiles in peripheral blood have emerged as potential biomarkers for assessing disease risk, and these profiles hold promise for translation into clinical applications. Previous studies on DNA methylation, particularly in blood samples, have reported hypomethylation in regions associated with COPD, such as SERPINA1, FUT7 , DIP2C , and the rare variant rs140198372 of SERPINA12 (He et al. 2020 ). In addition to COPD, DNA methylation analysis has also been reported for PRISm and COPD in a paper published by A. Domingo-Relloso et al. In this paper, grouping was conducted into ‘ Airflow limitation (FEV1/FVC &lt; 70) ’ and ‘ restriction (FEV1/FVC &gt; 70, FVC &lt; 80%) ‘ . Next, DNA methylation analysis was performed in blood, and FEV1, FEV1/FVC, and airflow limitation were analyzed as outcome variables, and duplicate genes were organized. They selected EGFR, MAPK1, and PRPF8 genes. There were no genes that overlapped with our results. To clarify the reason for this result, we defined the PRISm and COPD groups by FEV1/FVC ratio and FEV1 and selected genes by comparing DMPs and DEGs between each group, so it appears that previously reported genes did not show significance (Domingo-Relloso et al. 2022 ). Referring to these results, we also conducted a study to identify epigenetic CpG sites using peripheral blood samples. This study identified seven genes as epigenetic site regions, including those exhibiting differential DNA methylation patterns and gene expression changes (DEGs). These seven genes— EEF1A2, EMP2, EPCAM, MTSS1L, ARHGEF10, HYDIN , and FADS2 —are pivotal in transitioning from PRISm to COPD. Furthermore, these genes were recognized as DMPs and DEGs closely associated with COPD and the progression of lung-related diseases. Specifically, we observed CpG sites in COPD patients, cg11878698 and cg03074225 located in the body region of the EEF1A2 gene were hypermethylated, but cg26186239 located in the 3 ’ UTR was hypomethylated. The CpG sites selected in ARHGEF10 and HYDIN were hypomethylated, and the CpG sites located in the 5 ’ UTR region of the EPCAM gene were confirmed to be hypomethylated. In the EMP2 gene, all CpG sites in the TSS1500 region were hypermethylated, and CpG sites in the 5 ’ UTR were hypomethylated. EEF1A is involved in translational processes and displays non-canonical functions. EEF1A also plays a role in viral infections, interacting with viral proteins to enhance viral replication, reduce apoptosis, and increase cell transformation (Wefers, et al. 2022 ). Viral infections are a common and significant factor in COPD exacerbations, responsible for 50–66% of cases. Approximately 25% of exacerbations involve viral and bacterial infections, often leading to greater severity (Frickmann et al. 2012 ). While not universally confirmed, the connection between COPD and viral infections suggests a plausible link. EEF1A2 is a previously unreported gene in open databases, potentially holding novel insights for COPD research. EMP2 , a member of the tetraspan superfamily of membrane proteins, is thought to regulate cell adhesion and signaling (Lin et al. 2020 ; Wadehra et al. 2003 ). It has been implicated in various cellular functions in cancer cells. EMP2 is highly expressed in the lungs and may affect polymorphonuclear leukocyte entry into the airspace. Notably, EMP2 has been negatively correlated with other COPD-related genes in genome-wide association studies (GWAS), explaining its decreased expression in COPD patients (Lin et al. 2020 ). Indeed, while the exact relationship between the DEGs and the specific CpG sites may be challenging to elucidate, their combined findings suggest a consistent pattern of reduced expression of the EMP2 gene in individuals with COPD. EPCAM , an evolutionarily conserved type 1 transmembrane glycoprotein, is expressed in epithelial cells of various tissues, including the lungs. Its primary function is the regulation of cell–cell interactions and the modulation of cell adhesion molecules. EPCAM has also been identified as a biomarker for stem-/progenitor-like cells in the human lung (Vries et al. 2022 ; Huang et al. 2018 ; Wang et al. 2020 ). While EPCAM displayed differential methylation patterns based on gender, age, and smoking, it did not reach statistical significance in our analysis. ARHGEF10 plays a crucial role in cell signaling events as a member of the Rho GTPase family, which regulates the actin cytoskeleton, influencing various cellular processes such as morphological changes, migration, and cytokinesis across different cell types (Khan et al. 2021 ). It has been widely expressed in various tissues, notably high spinal cord, and dorsal root ganglion expression levels. ARHGEF10 is involved in neuronal growth and axonal guidance processes and has implications for human hypomyelination (Niftullayev and Lamarche-Vane 2019 ). Our findings align with previous reports, as reduced expression of ARHGEF10 has also been documented in individuals with COPD (Singh et al. 2014 ). FADS2 is primarily associated with cholesterol, sphingomyelin, phospholipids, and fatty acid metabolism, with implications in respiratory diseases like asthma, COPD, and pneumonia. Abnormal FADS2 expression disrupts cell membrane phospholipid balance, affecting membrane fluidity and signal transmission, leading to proinflammatory factor production and arachidonic acid metabolite generation. Increased glucose uptake in adipose tissues has also been associated with FADS2 expression. As metabolic syndrome worsens inflammatory conditions, there may be an association between decreased FADS2 expression and COPD progression (Li et al. 2020 ; Fujii, et al. 2021 ). HYDIN is mainly studied in lung-related diseases, particularly in the context of respiratory infections. Mutations in HYDIN can cause primary ciliary dyskinesia, a genetic disease characterized by chronic respiratory infections, highlighting its potential importance in COPD, where respiratory infections play a critical role (Xu et al. 2022 ; Cindrić et al. 2020 ). HYDIN, relatively unexplored in previous research, emerged as a significant gene in our analysis. This gene suggests that HYDIN may have disease specificity or relevance to COPD beyond the infection-induced COPD reported in previous studies (Xu et al. 2022 ; Cindrić et al. 2020 ). MTSS1L has been linked to neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (Bhattacharya et al. 2021 ). While its precise role remains unclear, our findings suggest its association with COPD, especially considering the measurement of DNA methylation expression in children with the disease and the reported connection between bronchopulmonary dysplasia and COPD (Priyadarshini et al. 2020 ). It revealed correlations with various factors related to COPD, including negative regulation of gonadotropin secretion, steroid catabolic processes, and positive regulation of tau-protein kinase activity (Laghi et al. 2009 ). These findings align with previous reports of associations between COPD and endocrine hormone disorders, steroid metabolism, and the potential involvement of tau-protein kinase activity. Glucocorticoids and TNF-α have been linked to gonadotropins, suggesting a connection between these factors and COPD. Steroid catabolism, specific to COPD, can reflect the disease ’ s severity, and tau-protein kinase activity may be associated with COPD, particularly considering the involvement of transcription factor EB (TFEB), known to be linked to COPD (Bodas et al. 2017 ; Creutzberg and Casaburi 2003 ; Bodas and Vij 2017 ). Continuous exposure to harmful substances in cigarette smoke or e-cigarette/nicotine vapor induces inflammatory oxidative stress in the airways, leading to irreversible lung damage (Creutzberg and Casaburi 2003 ). This may explain the correlation between disease aggravation through smoking or external stress and the identified gene functions. Given the increasing importance of PRISm, as defined by the GOLD criteria, and its association with COPD, additional studies focusing explicitly on PRISm are warranted. According to W. Sara Renata Alex et al., approximately one-third of PRISm patients progress to COPD (Alex, et al. 2020 ). PRISm patients experience varying degrees of lung function decline over time. While some maintain PRISm status, others transition to normal lung function or develop COPD. Unlike COPD, PRISm is characterized by a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests, with preserved FEV1/FVC ratios. PRISm patients tend to be younger, female and have less smoking history compared to COPD patients. Lung parenchymal diseases, rather than airway diseases, are common in PRISm, such as tuberculous traces, bronchiectasis, and mild interstitial lung disease. Although PRISm patients transitioning to COPD have risk factors similar to those for COPD, such as old age, smoking, and asthma history, they exhibit a slower decline in lung function compared to COPD-only patients but faster than normal individuals. The presence of multiple COPD risk factors in PRISm patients, along with severe emphysema and small airway dysfunction, contributes to their progression toward COPD. Comparing PRISm and COPD, identifying genes that show differences, and investigating genetic factors related to COPD can help reveal the differences, relationships, and associations between COPD and PRISm. Additionally, comprehensive studies investigating the mechanisms underlying symptom progression associated with the up or down-regulation of these genes are essential. The present study has significant implications in investigating the mechanisms underlying symptom progression associated with the up or down-regulation of the gene. This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, it included relatively small sample size, with only 572 selected DNA methylation and 60 RNA sequencing data from the KOCOSS-NIH registry and 98 selected DNA methylation patients from the KoGES cohort for the replication study, potentially introducing bias. The DNA methylation data was also derived from whole blood samples, which may not fully capture the tissue-specific DNA methylation patterns relevant to lung diseases such as COPD and PRISm. Studies demonstrating a relationship between DNA methylation and lung disease derived from blood samples have been reported despite this (Domingo-Relloso et al. 2022 ; Mulder et al. 2021 ; Robert, et al. 2023 ). The key strength of this study lies in its comprehensive observational data sourced from the KOCOSS-NIH registry, which specifically targeted Koreans for COPD research. Moreover, the genes identified through analysis of this registry exhibited consistent patterns when evaluated in the KoGES cohort, enhancing the robustness of the gene selection approach. However, this study was conducted in Koreans and has racial limitations. In addition, EWAS and RNA sequencing analyses were conducted in some patients in KOCOSS-NIH, and DNA methylation data were produced from 2019 to 2021 to refine the data comprehensively. It ’ s important to note that the number of patients included in the analysis is small. However, the genes selected from the KOCOSS-NIH registry showed similar patterns in KoGES, a cohort of different characteristics, confirming that these genes are associated with COPD specific to Koreans. This study ’ s findings could have a significant impact on future research and the understanding of COPD. Conclusion Our study pinpointed ARHGEF10, HYDIN, FADS2, EMP2, EEF1A2, EPCAM , and MTSS1L as potential candidate genes associated with COPD versus PRISm. These genes exhibited predominantly hypermethylation in differential DNA methylation patterns, and their corresponding differential gene expression patterns showcased decreased expression. These findings shed light on the pathogenic mechanisms underlying the relationship between PRISm and COPD, suggesting potential avenues for novel therapeutic strategies.
