Introduction Anemia is a major public health issue that significantly affects health, as well as social and economic development worldwide. Among adolescent girls, the prevalence of anemia is higher due to increased demands for blood volume expansion associated with the onset of menstruation and the rapid growth during adolescence. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of anemia and its associated factors among adolescent girls in East and West African countries. Methods All published and unpublished studies from East and West Africa available in PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Hinari, and Google Scholar were screened based on their titles, abstracts, study design, and full-text review before inclusion in the meta-analysis. Data were extracted after assessing the quality of evidence using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool for cross-sectional studies. Microsoft Excel was used for data extraction, and STATA version 14 was employed for statistical analysis. A forest plot was used to display the pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West Africa. Heterogeneity was assessed using random-effects models, subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test and funnel plot analysis. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to present the factors associated with anemia among adolescent girls. Results A total of twenty-nine studies involving 12,905 participants were included in the analysis. The pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls was 32.88% [95% CI: 24.62%, 41.14%], based on a random-effects model. The meta-analysis also identified significant associations between anemia and certain factors. Adolescent girls living in rural areas were more likely to have anemia compared to those in urban areas (OR = 4.19; 95% CI: 1.42, 12.41; I² = 76.2%, p = 0.006). Additionally, girls who had not heard about anemia had significantly higher odds of being anemic (OR = 12.25; 95% CI: 4.59, 32.64; I² = 81.1%, p &lt; 0.01). Conclusion The pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West African countries represents a moderate public health concern. Adolescent girls living in rural areas and those who had not heard about anemia were significantly more likely to develop the condition. Addressing this nutritional challenge requires strong intersectoral collaboration between the health and education sectors to promote awareness, prevention, and effective interventions targeting adolescent girls. While this stage offers significant opportunities for growth, it also presents various risks to health and well-being [ 1 ]. Iron requirements increase in adolescent girls due to the greater demands for blood volume expansion associated with the onset of menstruation and the rapid growth during adolescence [ 2 , 3 ]. This imbalance between iron requirements and intake makes anemia more common among adolescent girls [ 2 – 4 ]. Anemia among adolescent girls is defined as a hemoglobin level below 12.5 g/dL and is further classified as mild (11.0–11.9 g/dL), moderate (8.0–10.9 g/dL), and severe (below 8.0 g/dL) [ 5 ]. Anemia occurs at all life stages of the human being but is more prevalent in women and children [ 6 ]. Over two billion people, or roughly a quarter of the world’s population, are thought to have anemia, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) report [ 7 ] and in sub-Saharan Africa, it is a major potential problem for adolescent girls [ 3 , 8 ]. Various studies have identified multiple factors contributing to anemia among adolescent girls. These include low dietary diversity, prolonged menstrual bleeding, history of malaria infection, hookworm infestation, large family size, low altitude residence, and household food insecurity [ 2 , 3 , 9 , 10 ]. The high prevalence of anemia during adolescence may also be linked to the rapid physical growth experienced in this period, which increases the need for hematinic nutrients such as iron, folate, and vitamin B12 [ 11 ]. Adolescent anemia has major impacts on growth, cognition, work performance, and then throughout the reproductive years of life [ 3 , 12 ]. Adolescent girls’ school performance, attendance, and physical labor capacity may be impacted by anemia, particularly when it is linked to severe micronutrient deficiencies [ 10 ]. Additionally, because she has not yet reached her full growth, the baby and mother are vying for resources, and being more chance of unwanted pregnancy, an adolescent girl who gets pregnant is at a higher risk of developing anemia [ 9 , 10 ]. Therefore, the occurrence of pregnancy during adolescence with anemia increases not only maternal morbidity and mortality but also the incidence of poor maternal birth outcomes such as stillbirth, low birth weight, and prematurity and also has a negative impact on infant iron status [ 10 , 12 ]. To initiate effective interventions that improve adolescent girls’ nutritional status and prevent various risks during adolescence, pregnancy, childbirth, and beyond, it is imperative to ascertain the prevalence of anemia and its associated factors. However, there is limited evidence on the pooled magnitude and determinants of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West African countries. Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence of anemia and its associated factors among adolescent girls in these regions. Methods Protocol and registration The methods for this systematic review were developed according to recommendations from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statements [ 13 ]. The protocol has been registred in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) and given ID no. CRD42023405418. Research questions What is the pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West African countries? What are the significant factors associated with anemia among adolescent girls in East and West African countries? Study design and search strategy A systematic review and meta-analysis of both published and unpublished studies were conducted to assess the pooled prevalence of anemia and its associated factors among adolescent girls in East and West African countries. Electronic databases used for the literature search included PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Hinari. Additionally, Google Scholar and general Google searches were employed to identify relevant studies. A snowballing technique and manual searching were also utilized to retrieve additional literature. The reference lists of the selected articles were also screened to identify any additional studies that could be included in the review. A manual search (snowballing technique) was also conducted; however, no additional articles were found beyond those identified through the aforementioned databases and websites. The following MeSH terms and keywords were used in combination with Boolean operators: (“Anemia“[MeSH] OR “Anaemia” OR “Iron-Deficiency Anemia”) AND (“Adolescent“[MeSH] OR “Adolescent Girls” OR “Teenage Girls”) AND (“Africa, Eastern“[MeSH] OR “East Africa” OR “Africa, Western“[MeSH] OR “West Africa”) AND (“Prevalence” OR “Epidemiology” OR “Associated Factors” OR “Determinants”). Eligibility criteria of the articles All available published, peer-reviewed data, abstracts, and grey literature conducted between January 1995 to December 2020, written in English, were included in the screening process. A literature search was conducted from January 28, 2021 to January 30, 2021. The review included all descriptive and analytical cross-sectional studies from East and West African countries that reported the prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls aged 10–19 years. Studies were excluded if they focused on non-adolescent populations, male adolescents, or if they did not report the prevalence of anemia based on hemoglobin levels among adolescent girls. Risk of bias and quality assessment The quality and bias risk of the included studies were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI) critical assessment checklist. This instrument is well known and suitable for use in cross-sectional and prevalence research as well as systematic reviews of observational studies. The suitability of the sample frame, sampling process, sample size adequacy, precise description and measurement of outcomes, measurement method reliability, and response rate management are all evaluated by the JBI criteria. Each study was evaluated individually by each author using the JBI tool [ 14 , 15 ]. A study in which a score greater than five was included in the review. Data extraction Three authors (BTA, DGB, and DC) independently retrieved data from the included studies using a standardized extraction procedure documented in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Each author independently assessed the relevance of each study based on the title and abstract, using predefined inclusion criteria. For the first objective, estimating the prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West African countries the data extraction tool captured the following information: author and year of publication, study design, study setting, country, study population, sample size, and the reported prevalence of anemia. For the second objective, identifying factors associated with anemia among adolescent girls data were extracted on adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) for variables such as age, body mass index (BMI), residence, maternal education level, awareness of anemia, menstrual status, malaria infection, family size, and intestinal parasite infection. Heterogeneity test and publication bias The heterogeneity test of included studies was assessed by using the I 2 statistics. The p -value for I 2 statistics less than 0.05 were used to determine the presence of heterogeneity. Low, moderate, and high heterogeneity was assigned to I 2 test statistics results of 25, 50, and 75% respectively [ 16 , 17 ]. The random effect analyses were performed since the studies are highly heterogeneous. Furthermore, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and Meta-regression analysis were performed to determine the source of heterogenity. The publication bias was assessed using the Egger regression statistical and funnel plot test [ 18 ]. Statistical methods and analysis Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using STATA version 14. A forest plot was used to present the pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West African countries. To account for heterogeneity among the included studies, a random-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis, following an initial assessment using a fixed-effects model [ 16 ]. Moreover, subgroup analyses were conducted by different study characteristics such as sub-regions of Africa (East, West), specific countries, study settings (community-based, school-based, and refugees), and the publication year (before 2015 or In and after 2015). Further, sensitivity analysis was performed to identify which study will more contribute to the heterogeneity among studies by “in and out” of the single study which has a deviant estimate at a time. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to identify the sources of heterogeneity among studies. It was conducted using sample size. The impacts of different factors on adolescent anemia were presented using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Results Study selection Review articles were screened using their titles, abstracts, and full paper reviews before including in the meta-analysis. Articles that were duplicates from all database searches were removed before screening the rest articles. The searches identified 820 potentially relevant citations. From the total of 820 downloaded items, 123 were duplicates, and consequently, authors reviewed the remaining 697 articles by title and abstract. Based on the criteria 660 articles were excluded and the full text of the rest 37 articles was examined. Five articles were removed because of including males and not specified the outcome variables based on sex and another three articles include the non-adolescent age group. Finally, 29 articles were included based on close agreement between more than two reviewers (Fig. 1 ). Characteristics of included studies Twenty-nine studies involving 12,095 participants were included in the prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Among the total 29 included studies, 20 (68.97%) studies were published after and in 2015. All the included studies were cross-sectional surveys, of which 7 (24.14%) were community-based, 19 (65.52%) were school-based and 2 (6.9%) were on refuges. Overall, a total number of 12,905 study participants who are female adolescents (10–19 years old) were included. Eight sub-Saharan African countries were included in this study. These are two studies from Benin, fifteen from Ethiopia, three from Ghana, three from Kenya, two from Nigeria, one from Tanzania, one from Uganda, and one from Sudan (Table 1 ). The pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in Sub-Saharan Africa The overall pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls was 32.88% [95% CI: 24.62%, 41.14%], with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 99.2%, p &lt; 0.001), as determined using the random-effects model. Random-effects analysis was applied to all models in an attempt to address and reduce heterogeneity, aiming to lower the I² value. The highest weight among studies observed from the studies conducted by Gebreyesus, S.H., et al. [ 33 ], Abdelrahim, I.I., et al. [ 36 ] and Gosdin, L. et al. [ 32 ] (Fig. 2 ). Subgroup analysis of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West Africa To further address the high heterogeneity observed in the meta-analysis, subgroup analyses were conducted based on study setting, year of publication, region in sub-Saharan Africa, and specific countries. Based on the study setting, the pooled prevalence of anemia among school-based adolescent girls was 33.39% [95% CI: 21.58%, 45.20%], with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 99.5%, p &lt; 0.001). Among community-based adolescent girls, the prevalence was 33.5% [95% CI: 23.94%, 43.08%], with I² = 97.1% ( p &lt; 0.001), while among refugee adolescent girls, it was 34.0% [95% CI: 22.23%, 76.90%], with I² = 98.2% ( p &lt; 0.001). When analyzed by region, the pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in Eastern Africa was 27.87% [95% CI: 17.27%, 38.48%], with I² = 99.4%, whereas in Western Africa, it was significantly higher at 50.77% [95% CI: 25.04%, 41.91%], with I² = 99.3%, both with p &lt; 0.001. Subgroup analysis by specific countries showed considerable variation. In Benin, the pooled prevalence was 48.0% [95% CI: 35.44%, 59.87%], with the lowest heterogeneity observed (I² = 42.6%). In Ethiopia, it was 22.69% [95% CI: 18.16%, 26.72%] with I² = 94.5%, while in Kenya it was 31.0% [95% CI: 15.81%, 46.42%], with I² = 97.1%. In Nigeria, the prevalence reached 52.0% [95% CI: 28.81%, 75.26%] with I² = 96.3%, and in Ghana, it was 50.97% [95% CI: 16.03%, 85.90%] with I² = 99.1%. Each of these subgroup analyses showed high heterogeneity with p &lt; 0.001. For Tanzania, Uganda, and Sudan, only a single study was available per country, thus no pooled estimates were computed. Subgroup analysis by year of publication also revealed differences. Studies published before 2015 showed a higher pooled prevalence of 49.56% [95% CI: 22.23%, 76.90%], with I² = 99.6% ( p &lt; 0.001). In contrast, studies published in or after 2015 reported a lower prevalence of 26.71% [95% CI: 22.77%, 30.65%], with I² = 95.6% ( p &lt; 0.001) (Table 2 ). Although subgroup analyses were conducted based on study setting, year of publication, and region, significant heterogeneity persisted within subgroups. Sensitivity analysis and Meta-regression analysis Sensitivity analyses were performed to identify studies contributing most to the heterogeneity by systematically including and excluding individual studies with deviant estimates. As a result, five studies with estimates substantially different from the pooled value were excluded. After removing these studies, the pooled prevalence of anemia was 26% [95% CI: 22%, 29%], with heterogeneity remaining high (I² = 94.4%, p &lt; 0.001) (Fig. 3 ). Meta-Regression To examine the effects of numerous variables on the prevalence of anemia among adolescents girls worldwide, a meta-regression analysis was carried out. The meta-regression analysis, which examined the impact of sample size, revealed a significant p-value of 0.00.This suggests that the variation in sample sizes among the included studies was a significant source of heterogeneity in the pooled results of this study (Table 3 ). Publication bias or small study biases Eggers test and funnel plot Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test, which indicated no significant publication bias ( p = 0.41). Additionally, Egger’s funnel plot demonstrated a nearly symmetrical distribution, supporting the absence of publication bias (Fig. 4 ). Factors associated with adolescent girl anemia among East and West Africa From all independent variables in random pooled odds ratio analysis, factors such as residence, and heard about anemia have an association with anemia among adolescent girls. Four studies assessed the association between residence and adolescent girls anemia and all have significant associations [ 21 , 23 , 27 , 33 ]. The pooled odds of developing anemia among adolescent girls living in rural areas of East and West Africa are three times higher than among those living in urban areas [OR = 3.18, 95% CI: 1.96–4.39]. There is a considerable statistically significant heterogeneity among studies (I 2 = 81.2%, P -value &lt; 0.01), but there is no small study effect (Egger’s P -value = 0.068). Two studies analyzed the association between an adolescent girls who heard about anemia with developing anemia [ 24 , 33 ]. Adolescent girls who didn’t hear about Anemia have 12.25 times more likely to develop anemia than those who heard about anemia [OR = 12.25, 95% CI: 4.59, 32.64]. There is a considerable statistically significant heterogeneity among studies (I 2 = 81.1%, P -value &lt; 0.01), but no small study effect (Egger’s P -value = 0.317) (Table 4 ). Discussion This study is attempt to assess the pooled prevalence of anemia and associated factors among adolescent girls in East and West African countries using the available published and unpublished studies. The results of this study will have implications for future research, policymakers, and practice. The results of this study highlight the necessity of context- and region-specific approaches to address anemia in teenage girls in sub-Saharan Africa. Improving dietary iron intake, treating intestinal parasites and malaria, improving menstrual health management, and fortifying school-based health initiatives should be the main goals of interventions. Since adolescents frequently receive less attention than pregnant women and children under five, policymakers should give adolescent nutrition top priority in national health agendas. Based on this the pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West Africa was 32.88% [95%CI: 24.62%, 41.14%] with I 2 = 99.2% and p -value &lt; 0.001 which is a moderate public health problem according to the WHO classification [ 44 , 45 ]. This study finding is in line with a study in Nepal (31%) [ 46 ]. However, it is higher than a study conducted in low socioeconomic countries which was 19.3% for the lowest wealth quintile and 13.05% for the highest wealth quintile [ 47 ]. On the other hand, it is lower than studies conducted in south India (45.2%) [ 48 ], Tamil Nadu India (48.63%) [ 49 ], Urban slum India 90.1% [ 50 ], and North Gaza Strip (49.6%) [ 51 ]. This might be due to study populations with dissimilar local circumstances. The subgroup analysis revealed that regional analysis revealed marked disparities, with West Africa exhibiting the highest pooled prevalence at 50.77%, compared to 27.87% in East Africa. These findings may reflect differences in dietary patterns, access to health services, and socio-economic factors. A significant temporal pattern emerged from the year of publication subgroup analysis. Prior to 2015, studies had a higher pooled prevalence of 49.56%, whereas those published after 2015 had a lower pooled prevalence of 26.71%. This could indicate that attempts to prevent and control anemia have made some headway in the past 10 years, perhaps as a result of better availability to iron supplements, more school health activities, or better dietary programs. Significant heterogeneity remained within subgroups despite subgroup analysis based on study setting, year of publication, and area. This implies that the observed variability among research may not be entirely explained by the factors examined. The remaining heterogeneity can have been caused by other unmeasured factors, such as differences in demographic characteristics, measuring instruments, or data collection techniques. Consequently, care should be taken when interpreting the pooled estimates. In this study, the pooled odds of developing anemia among rural residence adolescent girls in East and West Africa are higher than the rural residents. This is in line with studies in India which show that rural girls were more anemic [ 48 , 52 ]. Another study in India also revealed that the prevalence of severe anemia was higher (5.7%) in rural area as compared to their counterpart urban area (3.7%) [ 53 ]. This might be because rural girls might lack nutrition knowledge and might face food insecurities. In this finding, adolescent girls who didn’t hear about anemia were more likely to develop anemia than those who heard something about anemia. This is similar to a study in Delhi India which showed that among those who heard about the term anemia, 83.3% considered that anemia is a health problem and tried to prevent it [ 54 ]. Therefore propagation of comprehensive knowledge about anemia and nutritional knowledge regarding diet and supplements rich food in iron should be mandatory [ 54 ]. Strengths and limitations of the study This is a pooled estimate among East and West African Countries. All studies were incorporated from all search databases. International tools and guidelines also peer-reviewed articles are included to enhance the quality of data. All methods of heterogeneity treatment have been used to treat the heterogeneity effect. Unfortunately, the effect is still not resolved. Therefore, there are unexplained variables that make the studies to be different. We only included studies in the English language. Moreover, even if almost all studies used &lt; 12 g/dl for anemia cut points, some studies [ 43 ] used different cut points for anemia for different age groups under adolescent age. This review included only cross-sectional studies, which limits the ability to determine causal relationships between anemia and its associated factors. While appropriate for estimating prevalence, these studies cannot establish temporal direction. Future longitudinal research is needed to confirm these associations. Conclusion The pooled prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls in East and West African countries is a moderate public health problem. Adolescent girls living in rural residences and adolescent girls who didn’t hear about anemia were more likely to be develop anemia. To improve the prevailing nutritional problem, there must be inter-sectorial collaboration among the health sectors and education sectors in providing nutritional education and counseling based on menses status, living in rural and education status of their mothers. Adolescent girls had better be screened periodically for anemia and nutritional supplementation for those who need it.
