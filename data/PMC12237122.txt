The work cannot be changed in any way or used commercially without permission from the journal. http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0 Abstract Hypertension (HTN) is a significant public health concern affecting individuals across all age groups, including those with and without disabilities. Among children and adolescents, particularly those with intellectual disabilities, the risk of HTN is heightened due to factors such as obesity, low physical activity, and comorbid conditions. Regular blood pressure (BP) monitoring is essential, considering the challenges in measurement accuracy among children with intellectual disabilities. Beyond traditional lifestyle modifications, individualized dietary interventions and structured physical activity programs play a fundamental role in HTN prevention and management. Additionally, optimizing sleep quality and addressing comorbidities are essential for improving long-term health outcomes. The updated recommendations emphasize a broader specialist involvement, including endocrinologists, nephrologists, cardiologists, and rehabilitation specialists, to ensure comprehensive care. The integration of these approaches, along with appropriate pharmacological strategies whenever necessary, is crucial for achieving health benefit. This article provides practical guidance for primary care providers, specialists, and caregivers, advocating for a collaborative, patient-centered approach to reducing cardiovascular risks and enhancing the quality of life for children with intellectual disabilities. INTRODUCTION Pediatric hypertension (HTN) is a growing public health concern, affecting 4% of children under 19 globally, with 9.6% having high normal blood pressure (BP) [ 1 ]. HTN, a modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease, doubles mortality risk from ischemic heart disease and stroke with every 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic increase [ 2 ]. HTN often persists from childhood to adulthood, causing cardiac and vascular changes that increase the risk of future cardiovascular diseases [ 3 ]. Individuals with intellectual disability face a disproportionate burden of HTN [ 4 – 7 ], driven by higher rates of obesity [ 8 ] and low physical activity [ 9 ]. Contributing factors include also limited access to healthy food, sedentary behavior, transportation barriers, and inaccessible community spaces, all of which reduce opportunities for regular exercise [ 8 ]. Early identification and treatment of pediatric HTN are vital for preventing these outcomes, particularly in high-risk groups such as those with obesity, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease. A 2019 study estimated that approximately 107 million people (2% of the global population) have intellectual disability, with higher prevalence in regions of lower socioeconomic development and a marked decline with age [ 5 ]. This decline reflects significantly higher mortality rates among individuals with intellectual disability, with a five-fold reduction in prevalence for those aged 70+ compared to ages 0–9 [ 5 ], corroborating prior studies on elevated mortality in this group [ 6 , 7 ] Among pediatric populations, prevalence varies widely, from 0.22% (2007–2008, USA) to 1.55% (1996, USA). The Global Burden of Disease Study estimated pediatric intellectual disability prevalence at 3.1%, with lower rates (1.5%) in high-income countries, highlighting higher prevalence in low-income and middle-income countries [ 10 ]. Managing HTN in individuals with intellectual disability requires a multidisciplinary approach, combining dietary guidance, lifestyle changes, and medical therapies. This article highlights the need for integrated strategies to enhance health outcomes and reduce mortality through targeted interventions. METHODOLOGY A comprehensive review of existing literature was undertaken in June–July 2024, using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Science Direct databases. The search strategy incorporated a combination of relevant keywords ‘intellectual disability’, ‘blood pressure’, ‘children’, ‘adolescents’, ‘hypertension’, ‘high blood pressure’, ‘cardiovascular disease’, ‘Down syndrome’, ‘Williams–Beuren syndrome’, ‘Neurofibromatosis’, ‘Prader–Willi’, ‘diet’, ‘physical activity’, ‘sleep’, ‘epidemiology’, ‘obesity’, ‘weight reduction’, ‘treatment’, ‘recommendation’ to identify the studies. Studies published between 2000 and 2024 were prioritized based on relevance. Titles and abstracts were screened to exclude unrelated articles, followed by an assessment of selected studies by all authors to ensure alignment with the research objectives. DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION OF HYPERTENSION HTN in children is defined based on BP percentiles adjusted for sex, age, and height. According to the 2016 European Society of Hypertension (ESH) Guidelines and Joint Statement between HyperChildNET and the European Academy of Pediatrics, HTN is diagnosed in children and adolescents aged 0–15 years when SBP and/or DBP is persistently at least 95th percentile for sex, age, and height on at least three separate occasions. High-normal BP is defined as BP values at least 90th to less than 95th percentile. HTN is further classified as grade 1 (95th to 99th percentile +5 mmHg) and grade 2 (&gt;99th percentile +5 mmHg). For adolescents aged 16 and older, BP categories are defined by absolute thresholds. High-normal BP ranges from 130 to 139/85 to 89 mmHg, while HTN is diagnosed at at least 140/90 mmHg. It is further classified into stages: stage 1 (140–159/90–99 mmHg), stage 2 (160–179/100–109 mmHg), and stage 3 (≥180/110 mmHg). HTN subtypes include isolated systolic HTN, defined as SBP at least 95th percentile with DBP less than 90th percentile in children, or SBP at least 140 mmHg with DBP less than 90 mmHg in those aged 16 and older. White coat HTN occurs when BP is elevated only in office measurements but normal in ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM), while masked HTN presents as normal office BP but elevated ABPM values. Sustained HTN refers to persistently high BP in both office and ambulatory settings [ 11 , 12 ]. If weight and height are below the third percentile, BP percentiles should be based primarily on height, or arm span if height measurement is difficult. ABPM is feasible from 120 cm but may be challenging because of cooperation, discomfort, and movement artifacts. When ABPM is not possible, multiple office BP measurements, as suggested by Ardissino et al. [ 13 ], may be an alternative. IDENTIFICATION OF HYPERTENSION IN INTELLECTUALLY DISABLED CHILDREN HTN in the general pediatric population should be identified using office BP measurement, starting at age 3 during well child visits, or earlier for children with risk factors [ 11 ]. Several challenges exist in measuring BP in children with intellectual disability, including the conical shape of the arm (proximal arm circumference larger than distal) and low cooperation during measurement. No evidence-based guidelines specify how often BP should be measured in children with intellectual disability. Based on expert opinion, it is recommended to: measure BP at least as frequently as in the general pediatric population, starting at age 3 during well child visits, or earlier for those with risk factors such as obesity, medications affecting BP, or low physical activity; and measure BP more frequently in children with intellectual disability and conditions with higher HTN prevalence, such as Williams–Beuren syndrome or Turner syndrome (Table 1 ) [ 14 ]. The following methods are recommended for identifying HTN in children with intellectual disability: office BP measurement for all children with intellectual disability, and 24-h ABPM for high-risk cases of nocturnal or masked HTN [ 15 ]. Elevated BP in children, including those with intellectual disability, can lead to HTN-mediated organ damage like left ventricular hypertrophy and, if untreated, increases the risk of premature cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Vigilant BP monitoring and early detection are strongly recommended in this population. EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS FOR ARTERIAL HYPERTENSION IN THE PEDIATRIC POPULATION WITH INTELLECTUAL DISABILITIES Among individuals with intellectual disability, HTN prevalence varies widely, ranging from 11.7 to 31.4% [ 18 – 21 ] Sun et al. reported HTN rates of 34.8% in girls and 29.9% in boys with intellectual disability, with no significant sex differences. HTN affected 30% of those with mild and 37.3% with moderate intellectual disability, again showing no statistical difference between groups [ 18 ]. In contrast, Lin et al. found higher HTN prevalence in boys (13.5%) than girls (8.8%) and observed a nonsignificant trend where greater intellectual disability severity was linked to lower HTN prevalence: 13.8% in mild and 9.2% in profound intellectual disability [ 20 ]. Conversely, Wyszyńska et al. [ 19 ] reported a significant correlation between greater severity of intellectual disability and increased HTN prevalence: 13.3% in mild, 23.3% in moderate, and 24% in severe intellectual disability. Secondary HTN in children often results from underlying medical conditions, primarily renal, endocrine, vascular, genetic, oncologic, and neurologic disorders. Renal diseases, including chronic kidney disease, glomerulonephritis, and polycystic kidney disease, are among the most common causes. Endocrine disorders such as Cushing syndrome, diabetes mellitus, and thyroid dysfunction, along with vascular abnormalities like aortic coarctation and renal artery stenosis, significantly contribute to HTN. Genetic syndromes associated with an increased risk of HTN include Williams–Beuren, Prader–Willi, Turner, and Alagille syndromes. Additionally, oncologic conditions, such as Wilms tumor and pheochromocytoma, neurologic disorders leading to increased intracranial pressure, and sleep disorders, including obstructive sleep apnea, have been implicated in HTN development [ 22 ]. Children with Williams-Beuren syndrome often present with cardiovascular abnormalities affecting over 80% of patients, leading to significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [ 23 ]. Supravalvular aortic stenosis occurs in approximately two-thirds of patients, and systemic HTN is diagnosed in about 30% of children and adolescents during office visits [ 14 ]. However, studies using 24-h ABPM have identified HTN in 40–70% of patients, often in the absence of symptoms or evident aortic or renovascular narrowing [ 24 – 26 ]. Cardiovascular complications remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in this syndrome, with vascular stenoses being the most common early-life threat [ 14 ]. Supravalvar aortic stenosis and peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis are the most frequent lesions, along with potential coronary artery stenoses. Approximately one-third of patients require cardiovascular interventions, with a heightened risk of sudden cardiac death, particularly during procedures [ 27 ]. Down syndrome, the most common chromosomal abnormality, occurring in 16 per 10 000 live births [ 28 ], is frequently associated with congenital heart disease and pulmonary HTN, which are often exacerbated by obesity and sleep apnea [ 29 ]. However, studies suggest a lower prevalence of HTN in children, adolescents [ 30 ], and adults [ 31 ] with Down syndrome. A multicenter study found that only 0.5% of 2661 BP recordings indicated prehypertension, and 0.4% indicated HTN [ 32 ]. The underlying reasons for these differences remain unclear but may involve baseline sympathetic dysautonomia, evidenced by reduced heart rate and BP responses in tilt-table testing [ 33 ]. On the other side, congenital kidney and urinary tract anomalies in children with Down syndrome increase their risk of HTN and chronic kidney disease [ 34 ]. Prader–Willi syndrome, characterized by severe obesity due to uncontrollable appetite, raises HTN risk, with young adults showing HTN rates of 30.8% compared to 16.1% in controls [ 35 ]. Other genetic conditions, such as Bardet–Biedl and Cohen syndromes, are also associated with an increased risk of obesity and HTN. In contrast, boys with Klinefelter syndrome generally do not exhibit an increased risk of HTN, despite a higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome components [ 36 , 37 ]. Children and adolescents with intellectual disability tend to have lower physical activity levels, increased sedentary behavior, and poorer dietary habits, often struggling to adopt healthy lifestyle choices [ 38 , 39 ]. They also face higher rates of overweight and obesity compared to their peers, driven by medications, often required to manage their symptoms [ 40 ]. Table 2 presents the most frequently used drugs, their effects on BP, and their interactions with antihypertensive drugs. Sun et al. found that children with both intellectual disability and obesity were nearly three times more likely to develop HTN than their peers with intellectual disability but without obesity [odds ratio (OR): 2.77], highlighting obesity as the most significant risk factor. Interestingly, paternal college education and certain occupations (e.g. clerks, sales representatives) were also associated with an increased risk of HTN [ 16 ]. Wyszyńska et al. further emphasized that obesity, more than 2 h of daily screen time, and low physical activity levels are key risk factors for HTN. Boys and adolescents aged 14–18 were particularly vulnerable, with obesity identified as the primary contributor to elevated BP [ 41 ]. LIFESTYLE MODIFICATION STRATEGIES FOR THE TREATMENT OF HYPERTENSION IN CHILDREN AND ADOLESCENTS WITH INTELLECTUAL DISABILITIES Nutrition strategies Individuals with intellectual disabilities face a higher prevalence of nutrition-related issues, particularly chronic constipation. Key dietary factors contributing to these challenges include low fruit and vegetable intake [ 42 ], high soft drink consumption [ 43 ], frequent snacking [ 44 ], and selective eating patterns, especially in those with autistic traits [ 45 ]. Additionally, digestive and swallowing difficulties can further limit dietary variety and nutrient intake. Limited caregiver support for healthy eating may impact nutritional status. These factors highlight the need for targeted dietary strategies to support optimal health in this population. Research indicates that among children and adolescents with disabilities, intakes of vitamin E, vitamin B1, folate, potassium, calcium, and iron are often inadequate, while intake of macronutrients (proteins, carbohydrates) and some micronutrients, including vitamins A, B2, B6, B12, C, phosphate, zinc, sodium, and vitamin D, are commonly above recommended levels. While excess water-soluble vitamins such as vitamin C and B vitamins are excreted, imbalances in other nutrients may have implications for health [ 46 ]. The Mediterranean diet, along with other balanced dietary patterns, has been associated with health benefits, including for children and adolescents with disabilities [ 47 ]. Participants who adhered to a high-quality Mediterranean diet demonstrated higher energy and nutrient intakes, as well as a greater likelihood of meeting nutrient requirements [ 48 ]. A Spanish study found that high adherence to this diet, combined with regular napping, was associated with lower BP [ 49 ]. A meta-analysis showed that healthy dietary patterns reduce BP in adolescents [ 50 ]. A study of 1068 children aged 5–7 found that adherence to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet and a low genetic risk score for HTN were linked to lower BP [ 51 ]. Pérez-Gimeno et al. found that high adherence to the Mediterranean diet is linked to lower BP in adolescents, with significant interactions between diet score and a genetic risk score for HTN, affecting SBP ( ß = 0.02) and DBP (ß = 0.02) z scores. These findings highlight the Mediterranean diet's potential to reduce HTN risks through genetic–diet interactions [ 52 ]. The Lancet highlighted the day of persons with disabilities [ 39 ], emphasizing health challenges from societal marginalization. Individuals with intellectual disabilities often lack access to dietitians and face limited emphasis on nonpharmacological HTN management. The American Association for the Advancement of Science and Human Rights Coalition [ 53 ] underscores the underrepresentation of individuals with intellectual disabilities in research. Human rights policies advocate for proper nutrition, stressing the role of medical and social care providers. Effective nutritional strategies can enhance BP control and overall health in this vulnerable group. Physical activity strategies Individuals with intellectual disability face cognitive impairments that impact communication, social interaction, and physical activity, limiting their participation in recreational and community events. Consequently, children with intellectual disability often struggle to achieve the same physical activity levels as their typically developing peers [ 9 ]. The latest WHO guidelines recommend that children and adolescents aged 5–17 with disabilities engage in at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily, including 3 days of vigorous-intensity activities and exercises to improve muscle and bone strength, with most activity being aerobic [ 54 ] Physical activity provides numerous health benefits, including better physical fitness, cardiometabolic health (lower BP, improved lipid levels, and glucose regulation), stronger bones, enhanced cognitive function, improved mental health, and reduced body fat. It also improves cognitive function in conditions like attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and enhances physical function in children with intellectual disability [ 54 , 55 ]. Despite these benefits, children with intellectual disability engage in significantly less physical activity than their peers [ 8 , 41 , 56 ], highlighting the need for targeted interventions to promote physical engagement in this population. Physical activity interventions mainly target typically developing children, leaving a gap for those with intellectual disabilities. McGarty et al. 's [ 57 ] meta-analysis revealed these programs are largely ineffective for children with intellectual disabilities, complicating the development of useful recommendations [ 56 ]. Many interventions rely on strategies for the general population, failing to address the specific needs of individuals with intellectual disabilities, who may require more time to form habits due to rigid routines. Behavior change theories are often ineffective, requiring abstraction and cognitive abilities beyond their capacity, as well as additional social support [ 58 ]. The lack of tailored research worsens health disparities for this group. Future studies should prioritize population-specific, evidence-based interventions to improve physical activity and address these inequities (Tables 3 and 4 ). Weight reduction To date, there are no clear guidelines on how to reduce body weight in children with specific intellectual disability [ 59 ]. Current general weight management strategies for patients with obesity include lifestyle changes that can be self-managed or administered under professional care, antiobesity medications, and bariatric surgery. Each of these methods has its limitations when applied to children and adolescents with intellectual disability. The existing data indicate that weight loss in youth with intellectual disability can be encouraged through multifactorial interventions that incorporate physical activity [ 60 – 62 ]. Currently, in addition to lifestyle changes, the most recommended dietary intervention for the treatment and prevention of obesity in children and adolescents with intellectual disability is caloric restriction [ 63 ]. One long-term study showed that a well balanced, low-energy diet (∼30% fat, 45% carbohydrates, 25% protein, and at least 20 g of fiber per day) reduced weight gain and reduced excess fat mass in individuals with Prader–Willi syndrome [ 64 ]. Portion sizes should be controlled based on an individual's weight management and exercise level. One approach is to use the person's fist size as a guideline for measuring portions of complex carbohydrates and protein, while limiting healthy fats and fruit portions to what can fit flat in the person's hand. Nonstarchy vegetables, however, are not restricted [ 65 ]. It is recommended that the entire family follow the same well balanced, healthy diet and drink plain water with meals. There is clear evidence of effective action of antiobesity drugs from the group of glucagon-like peptide-1 analogues in patients with Prader–Willi syndrome [ 66 ]. In the case of bariatric surgery, the activities are very limited. The main factor limiting this form of therapy is the inability to obtain informed consent. A necessary condition for making a decision about surgery is the patient's full awareness of the irreversibility and consequences of the surgery. This is a particularly difficult issue for children and adolescents with intellectual disability [ 67 ]. Sleep Lack of sufficient nighttime sleep and poor sleep quality are significant risk factors for obesity, HTN, and cardiovascular disease [ 68 ]. Sleep disorders in individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders are often chronic, persisting into adolescence or adulthood [ 69 ]. They face a higher risk of specific sleep disorders, including insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness, and sleep-related breathing issues, compared to their typically developing peers [ 70 , 71 ]. Factors such as co-sleeping with parents and a family history of sleep issues contribute to further increase this risk [ 71 ] autonomic nervous system, which is crucial for maintaining the correct daily rhythm of BP [ 72 ]. Children with intellectual disability experience sleep disorders more frequently than their typically developing peers. For example, sleep problems affect up to 74% of children and adolescents with Down syndrome [ 73 ]. The cause of sleep disorders in individuals with intellectual disability is complex and involves various medical conditions, including gastroesophageal reflux, epileptic seizures, poor sleep hygiene, environmental factors, medication use, anxiety, restless legs syndrome, and obstructive sleep apnea [ 74 ]. In some patients, sleep disturbances are a phenotypic feature of a specific disorder or genetic condition. Understanding the characteristic patterns of these disorders can aid in diagnosing the syndrome itself and its consequences for the cardiovascular system [ 75 ]. Awareness of specific sleep disorders in individuals with intellectual disability is essential for accurate diagnosis, effective treatment, and the prevention of HTN. After excluding medical contributors and other primary sleep disorders (e.g., epilepsy, gastroesophageal reflux, iron deficiency, obstructive sleep apnea, and periodic limb movements), parent based-education, and behavioral interventions are the first-line treatment of insomnia [ 68 ]. If these methods prove ineffective, pharmacotherapy may be considered, with drug selection depending on the type of sleep disorder. For circadian rhythm disorders, melatonin is the preferred treatment. In cases of primary insomnia characterized by delayed sleep onset and/or frequent nocturnal awakenings, diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine, niaprazine, zolpidem, or trazodone may be used. For sleep disorders associated with epilepsy, restless legs syndrome, or resistant sleep-onset insomnia, clonazepam or gabapentin can be considered. In patients with ADHD or disruptive behavior disorders, clonidine is the first-choice medication [ 69 ]. Effective treatment of sleep disorders not only improves the functioning of patients with mental disabilities but also reduces their risk of developing heart and vascular diseases. PHARMACOLOGICAL THERAPY The pharmacological treatment of HTN in children and adolescents with intellectual disabilities and comorbidities requires an individualized approach. While lifestyle modifications remain the first-line strategy, pharmacological interventions become necessary when nonpharmacological measures are insufficient or when HTN is symptomatic, secondary, or associated with target organ damage [ 76 , 77 ]. The selection of antihypertensive agents should be guided by the child's underlying conditions, potential drug interactions, and established treatment guidelines. The 2016 ESH Guidelines for the Management of High Blood Pressure in Children and Adolescents provide a comprehensive framework for pharmacological treatment, recommending a personalized, stepwise approach [ 12 ]. The choice of antihypertensive medications should consider factors such as renal protection in children with diabetes, seizure thresholds in those with epilepsy, and potential cardiac effects in those with congenital heart defects. Beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) each have specific indications based on the clinical profile of the patient [ 78 , 79 ]. Rimmer et al. [ 40 ] emphasize the importance of addressing obesity and related conditions in adolescents with intellectual and developmental disabilities, while Hampl et al. [ 59 ] recommend combining lifestyle interventions with pharmacological treatments, such as orlistat and antihypertensive medications. In the context of Prader–Willi syndrome, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogues, including liraglutide and semaglutide, have been explored as potential treatments. These agents may provide benefits by targeting both obesity and associated HTN through mechanisms that influence appetite regulation and BP control [ 63 , 80 ]. The use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists in children with intellectual disabilities remains largely unexplored in scientific literature. However, a case study on an adolescent with autism spectrum disorder and moderate intellectual disability reported that liraglutide reduced obsessive food cravings, binge eating, weight gain, and behavioral issues, suggesting a potential role for GLP-1 signaling in managing food-related behavioral problems in this population [ 81 ]. Given the high prevalence of obesity among children with intellectual disabilities, these agents may be a valuable therapeutic option. However, further research is needed to evaluate their long-term safety, tolerability, and efficacy in individuals with intellectual disabilities, particularly those with complex metabolic and behavioral challenges. Additionally, the presence of psychiatric conditions, which are common in children with intellectual disabilities, must also be considered in HTN management. Medications such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and stimulants for ADHD can interact with antihypertensive drugs, requiring careful monitoring and dose adjustments [ 82 ]. Various psychiatric disorders, including anxiety, depression, ADHD, and conduct disorders, are associated with increased cardiovascular risk through mechanisms, such as autonomic dysfunction, inflammation, and metabolic abnormalities [ 82 ]. Other co-existing conditions, such as hyperlipidemia and thyroid disorders, frequently accompany HTN in this population. The use of statins for managing hyperlipidemia and the optimization of thyroid hormone levels in children with hypothyroidism are crucial for overall cardiovascular risk reduction [ 18 , 83 ]. Given the complexity of treating HTN in children with intellectual disabilities and comorbidities, clinicians should refer to the 2016 ESH Guidelines for specific pharmacological recommendations, including drug selection and dosing strategies. These guidelines emphasize a personalized, multidisciplinary approach, starting with a single antihypertensive agent and titrating as needed while considering the child's overall health status and risk factors [ 12 ]. Summary Implementing lifestyle modification strategies, particularly those focused on nutrition and physical activity is critical for managing HTN in children and adolescents with intellectual disability, who frequently encounter difficulties in making healthful dietary choices due to cognitive limitations. To support healthier eating habits, structured education is essential, with the Mediterranean diet identified as a beneficial option that can positively impact BP and overall health. A tailored treatment plan for HTN should also account for the individual's specific needs, comorbidities, and medication adherence, with roles for nutritionists in creating user-friendly meal plans, physical therapists promoting exercise, and family support for lifestyle changes and medication compliance. Effectively addressing HTN in children with intellectual disabilities requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary strategy, integrating expertise from various specialties and active involvement of caregivers and the community. Preventive measures Preventive strategies include early identification and management of the primary risk factors and underlying conditions contributing to HTN; dietary interventions: dieticians play a central role in formulating balanced, low-sodium diets rich in potassium, fiber, and essential nutrients, tailored to the specific needs of children with intellectual disabilities. These interventions should consider individual dietary preferences, challenges, and any metabolic disorders; physical activity: physical activity specialists/physiotherapist should design adaptive exercise programs promoting aerobic and strength-based activities that suit the child's physical abilities; family and community education: engage families, caregivers, and schools to foster a supportive environment for implementing healthy lifestyles. Education on reducing sedentary behaviors, limiting screen time, and ensuring active family involvement is vital. Diagnosis and monitoring Diagnosis and monitoring involve regular screening: general practitioner, pediatricians or trained nurses should perform annual BP checks from age 3 during well child visits, or earlier for children with risk factors (e.g. those with obesity, family history of HTN, coexisting diseases, physical inactivity); advanced diagnostics: involve cardiologists, nephrologists or endocrinologists for specialized diagnostics like ABPM. When ABPM is not feasible, multiple office or home BP measurements may serve as an alternative; screening for secondary HTN requires a multidisciplinary approach to ensure accurate diagnosis and effective management. Endocrinologists play a key role, but other specialists such as nephrologists, cardiologists, primary care physicians, and radiologists should also be involved, depending on the underlying condition. Management and treatment Management and treatment approaches include lifestyle interventions: collaborate with dieticians and physiotherapists to implement and monitor effective weight management and increased physical activity. Physiotherapists should address physical or motor challenges that might limit activity levels, ensuring children with intellectual disabilities participate in suitable and enjoyable physical activities; pharmacological therapy: in severe cases or when lifestyle changes are insufficient, involve pediatric cardiologists and nephrologists to initiate pharmacological treatment. Medications should be selected based on the child's specific needs and closely monitored for efficacy and side effects; multidisciplinary team, including endocrinologists, nephrologists, and cardiologists, should be engaged when HTN is linked to conditions like Cushing's syndrome, hyperaldosteronism, or renal abnormalities; psychological support: children with intellectual disabilities may face unique challenges adhering to treatments. Psychologists should provide behavioral support to enhance compliance and manage anxiety or stress related to medical care. Collaboration and specialist support Collaborative care should include multidisciplinary specialist centers: these centers should offer pediatric, cardiologic, nephrologic, endocrinologic, dietary, and psychological expertise to address the multifaceted nature of HTN. Such collaboration ensures comprehensive care, including HTN-mediated organ damage assessments and individualized management plans; role of schools and caregivers: train school staff to support physical activity and dietary interventions. Caregivers should receive education on recognizing symptoms, ensuring adherence to treatment plans, and providing emotional support.
