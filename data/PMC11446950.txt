Abstract Migraine, a complex disorder, is characterized by recurrent headache episodes. The production of melatonin in the pineal gland, which is crucial for controlling circadian rhythms and sleep–wake cycles, is altered in various conditions, including neurological disorders such as migraine. Recent studies underscore the significance of serum melatonin levels in patients with chronic and episodic migraine, the focus of this study. This case‒control study, conducted from September 2017 to June 2020 in Tehran, Iran, selected potential participants aged 18–65 years from a headache clinic at Sina Hospital (affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences). Both episodic migraine and chronic migraine were diagnosed following the diagnostic criteria in the International Classification of Headache Disorders’ third edition. Melatonin levels were measured according to the instructions of the ELISA kits. There were significant differences in the frequency of headache days and the duration of abortive medication usage between the two groups (P value &lt; 0.001). Besides, analysis revealed significantly lower serum melatonin levels in patients with episodic ((80.45–45.06) 72.83) and chronic migraine ((154.34–63.34) 70.38, P value &lt; 0.001) than in healthy controls (281.25–160.86) 280). Although no considerable differences were found between episodic and chronic migraine patients, the current study demonstrated that serum melatonin levels were substantially greater in healthy controls than in patients with migraine. Introduction Both clinically and economically, migraine, a prevalent disease, substantially impacts individuals and society. Despite available data on its population effects, migraine often remains undiagnosed and, consequently, undertreated 1 . Migraine is a persistent headache lasting 4–72 h that typically affects one side. The pain, usually pulsating, ranges from moderate to severe in intensity. Physical activity often exacerbates headaches, and photophobia and phonophobia are also common 2 . The 2016 Global Burden Disease study estimated that approximately one billion people worldwide experienced migraine in 2016. Migraine was more prevalent in women aged 15–49 years 3 . Migraine can be classified into two types: episodic migraine and chronic migraine. The primary distinction between these types lies in the frequency of monthly headache days. Episodic migraine is characterized by less than 15 headache days per month, while chronic migraine involves experiencing 15 or more headache days per month for at least 3 months 4 , 5 . The causes of migraine are believed to be influenced by a combination of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors that contribute to the diverse range of symptoms observed in patients. These causes, combined with an individual’s life history, ultimately contribute to the observed clinical heterogeneity 6 . The mechanisms underlying the pathophysiology of migraine are still not fully understood, and various theories have been proposed to explain its pathogenesis. These theories include activation of vascular dysfunction, the trigeminovascular pathway, neuroinflammation, and cortical spreading depression. Additionally, the activity of certain factors, such as nitric oxide and neuropeptides, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide, is thought to play a role 6 – 10 . The absence of suitable biomarkers also hinders the creation of more effective therapeutic/preventive approaches. Ultimately, biomarkers have the potential to assist clinicians in accurately diagnosing and treating migraine 6 . There is a growing body of research indicating that the relationship between headache disorders and melatonin secretion is noteworthy. Melatonin has been identified as a considerable factor in migraine-related causal mechanisms. Its significance in headache disorders may also have implications for treatment options 11 . Research has studied the level of serum melatonin levels in migraine patients 12 – 16 . According to Murialdo et al., subjects with migraine exhibit substantially lower urinary excretion of melatonin than healthy individuals 15 . In another study by Kozak et al., it was reported that in comparison to healthy controls, melatonin levels were considerably lower among migraine patients 12 . In contrast, according to some studies, serum melatonin levels of patients with episodic migraine are higher than those of healthy controls 17 , 18 . There are controversies regarding this issue that prompted us to conduct this survey. Additionally, our objective was to examine the difference in serum melatonin levels between chronic and episodic migraine in a sample of Iranian patients. Given the substantial impact of melatonin on the underlying causes of migraine and the limited studies examining the effectiveness of melatonin in chronic migraine and episodic migraine, our research aimed to investigate the role and alterations of melatonin in chronic migraine and episodic migraine. Results Headache characteristics in patients with migraine The headache characteristics of patients with migraine are shown in Table 1 . There were no notable differences in age, sex or body mass index profile across the groups. Patients who experienced episodic migraine reported an average of 8.78 headache days per month (with an average severity of 7.37), with abortive medications being utilized for approximately 6 days. Conversely, individuals in the chronic migraine group experienced headaches approximately 25.74 days per month (with an average severity of 7.42) and resorted to abortive medications for approximately 14.42 days. A significant difference was noted in the frequency of headache days and the duration of abortive medication usage between the two groups (P value &lt; 0.001, Table 1 ). The only difference observed among individuals with migraine regarding their medical background was the greater consumption of vitamin B2 and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in patients experiencing episodic migraine (Table 2 ). Although there were no considerable differences between patients with episodic migraine and those with chronic migraine, analysis revealed significantly lower serum melatonin levels in patients with episodic and chronic migraine than in healthy controls (P value &lt; 0.001, Table 3 ). No considerable linear associations were detected between serum melatonin levels and headache characteristics, including headache duration, headache severity, or attack duration (Table 4 ). Discussion Despite no considerable differences between episodic migraine patients and chronic migraine patients, this study demonstrated that serum melatonin levels were substantially greater in healthy controls than in patients with migraine. Research has shown that serum melatonin levels are lower in migraine patients than in individuals without migraine 12 – 15 , especially in those with chronic migraine 16 . Moreover, migraine attacks further decrease melatonin levels 13 – 15 . Murialdo et al. reported that subjects with migraine exhibit substantially lower urinary excretion of melatonin than healthy individuals 15 . In a case‒control study conducted by Kozak et al., melatonin levels were compared between patients with migraine and sex- and age-matched control subjects. It was observed that in comparison to healthy controls, melatonin levels were significantly lower among migraine patients 12 . Decreased levels of melatonin may be the cause of sleep disturbances and insomnia commonly reported by patients with chronic migraine 19 . In contrast, some studies have shown that the serum melatonin levels of patients with episodic migraine are greater than those of healthy controls 17 , 18 . Considering headache characteristics, we did not observe any correlation between serum melatonin levels and headache characteristics, such as headache duration, headache severity, or attack duration. In contrast, Zduńska et al. reported that melatonin levels were positively correlated with the frequency and duration of migraine attacks, suggesting that melatonin could be involved in migraine pathophysiology 17 . Moreover, melatonin, a promising treatment for migraine, has been the subject of various clinical trials. Notably, an open-label trial investigating melatonin for migraine prophylaxis demonstrated a considerable reduction in attacks for 64.7% of migraine patients 20 . Alstadhaug et al. showed that melatonin is superior to placebo in reducing migraine attacks. However, this study did not reveal a substantial effect on sleep quality 21 . Bougea et al. conducted a pilot study on forty-nine patients, 37 of whom had migraine and 12 of whom had chronic tension-type headache. The reports indicated that taking 4 mg of oral melatonin four times a day for six months resulted in promising results in terms of reducing headache frequency and improving daily quality of life in both groups 22 . Gonçalves et al. Conducted a study in which 196 participants were randomly assigned to placebo, 25 mg amitriptyline or 3 mg melatonin and followed for 3 months. It was observed that melatonin intake for 3 mg per day is superior to placebo for migraine prevention, more tolerable than amitriptyline and as effective as amitriptyline 25 mg 23 . Alstadhaug et al. demonstrated the superiority of melatonin over placebo in reducing the frequency of migraine attacks. However, this study did not show a considerable effect on sleep quality 23 . The mechanism behind the change in melatonin levels in migraine patients is not fully understood. Melatonin has been shown to exhibit anti-inflammatory effects through various pathways, so regulates the body’s response to pain and inflammation, in addition to various other effects. Melatonin may help to reduce CGRP and other proinflammatory mediators, which are relevant in migraine pathophysiology so melatonin may reduce the severity of migraine attacks 24 , 25 . The trigeminal ganglion is responsible for releasing neuropeptides such as substance P and CGRP, which can cause migraine attacks 26 , 27 . CGRP is a biomarker indicating the presence of trigeminal inflammation, and its role in vasodilation during a migraine attack has been documented. Melatonin can inhibit the release of CGRP, which plays an essential role in regulating blood flow in the brain 24 , 28 . So melatonin reduction has the potential to cause migraine. Ansari et al. conducted a study that involved 12 patients with menstrual migraine and 12 healthy subjects who were age- and sex-matched. Melatonin reduced CGRP mRNA expression by inducing nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) enzyme activity. Moreover, melatonin synthesis in the body was decreased by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), leading to potential harmful effects of chronic NSAIDs use, such as naproxen, in migraine patients 29 . Melatonin decreases tissue damage caused by inflammation through various functions. One of its key mechanisms of action involves its ability to scavenge harmful free radicals, thereby minimizing macromolecular damage in various organs 11 , 28 , 30 , 31 . Melatonin has additional mechanisms to mitigate the effects of inflammation. It prevents the translocation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-kappa B) to the nucleus and its binding to DNA, thereby decreasing the upregulation of a variety of proinflammatory cytokines 11 , 32 , 33 . Moreover, melatonin inhibits the production of adhesion molecules that facilitate the attachment of leukocytes to endothelial cells. This inhibition results in the suppression of transendothelial cell migration and edema, which are key factors that contribute to tissue damage 11 , 32 . Melatonin also suppresses the enzyme nitric oxide synthase and modulates serotonin and dopamine secretions, which are involved in the pathophysiology of migraine 24 , 28 . Melatonin has antioxidant properties. It substantially increases the levels of antioxidant enzymes such as catalase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione peroxidase while inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome 34 . Melatonin has been shown to suppress Th1 and Th17 cells, prompting Treg cells, and inhibiting the ROS/TXNIP/HIF-1α pathway 35 , 36 . Moreover, melatonin has been shown to reduce inflammation and pelvic pain by activating the Sirt1 pathway and downregulating the NLRP3 inflammasome 35 . Additionally, melatonin has been found to inhibit tumor growth and reverse enzalutamide resistance in animal models of castration-resistant prostate cancer by regulating lipid accumulation and CES1 expression, ultimately impacting CGRP levels 29 . Furthermore, in a study on hypoxic-ischemic brain injury, melatonin treatment post injury was shown to interact with the CGRP-related plasticity-related protein plppr5, affecting cognitive and motor functions 37 . It is possible that the increase in melatonin levels, which has been shown in other studies, is a way to compensate for the increased oxidative stress and inflammation associated with migraine attacks 17 , 18 . The antioxidant effect of melatonin protects the brain from damage caused by toxic molecules. It helps maintain brain structural and functional integrity by acting as a membrane stabilizer. It can prevent migraine attacks by controlling neural pathways and neurotransmitters, for example, inhibiting dopamine release, limiting nitric oxide synthesis, and antagonizing excitotoxicity. Melatonin also exhibits analgesic effects via the release of endorphins. Thus, the anxiolytic and antidepressant properties of melatonin can also help patients with migraine experience pain 25 . The main point of this study is to compare serum melatonin levels in chronic and episodic migraine. The current study was not free of limitations. We did not gather data about dietary habits, physical activity, insomnia, and other environmental factors, this could influence the results. Besides, blood samples for melatonin measurement were collected at a single time point, which might not accurately reflect the fluctuations in melatonin levels over time. Melatonin levels exhibit diurnal variation, and single-time-point measurements may not capture these variations effectively. It is not appropriate to select the control group from hospital staff and patient companions. Because these people probably have an irregular sleep profile due to the hospital environment, and this affects the melatonin level. This may affect the results. Future studies are suggested to investigate serum melatonin levels and the effect of supplementation in menstrual related migraine and in cluster headaches. Conclusion The present study indicated that serum melatonin levels are lower in patients with chronic and episodic migraine than in healthy controls. Interestingly, we did not observe any considerable differences in the serum melatonin level between episodic and chronic migraine patients, which needs further clarification in future studies. Further research is needed to fully understand the role of melatonin in the pathogenesis of migraine and to determine whether melatonin supplementation may be a useful therapeutic strategy for the treatment of migraine. Methods Study design This case‒control study, conducted from September 2017 to June 2020 in Tehran, Iran, recruited participants through advertisements, primarily posted in a headache clinic and its vicinity, as well as throughout the hospital. The study protocol, approved by the National Institute for Medical Research Development (NIMAD) with grant number 957537, was also approved by the NIMAD ethical committee with ID: IR.NIMAD.REC.1396.054. All participants independently provided their written informed consent after a thorough explanation of the research process. All procedures were conducted according to the latest version of the Helsinki Declaration. Potential participants underwent a medical evaluation by a clinical neurologist specializing in headache disorders to confirm the diagnosis of both episodic migraine and chronic migraine, following the diagnostic criteria outlined in the third edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-III) 38 , 39 . The episodic migraine and chronic migraine patients selected from a headache clinic at Sina Hospital (affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences) were considered case groups. Additionally, an age- and sex-matched control group consisting of individuals from the general population (such as patient companions and hospital staff) who did not experience headaches was also included. Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: aged between 18 and 65 years, had a body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 35 kg/m 2 , were not breastfeeding or pregnant and had no medical history of systemic diseases (e.g., cardiovascular, endocrinological, or infectious diseases) or other chronic neurological diseases such as multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, or epilepsy. Moreover, having migraine (with or without aura) for at least 6 months and ruling out the medication overuse headache (MOH) diagnosis was other inclusion criteria for the patient groups. Exclusion criteria included not meeting the above conditions or refusal to complete the questionnaires. NSAIDS and Triptans were the medications used by patients who had chronic headache. Demographic and clinical assessments Initially, participants underwent interviews to provide demographic and clinical information. Participants’ weight and height were assessed using a Seca Clara 803 digital scale (accuracy of 0.01 g; Seca GmbH &amp; Co. KG., Hamburg, Germany) and a Seca 216 wall-mounted stadiometer (accurate to 0.1 cm without shoes; Seca GmbH &amp; Co. KG., Hamburg, Germany), and BMI was calculated as weight (in kg)/(height (in meters)) 2 . Peripheral venous blood samples were collected from controls. Headache diaries and visual analog scale Subsequently, patients completed the headache diary form. The diaries recorded various aspects related to the duration (time elapsed from headache onset until the cessation of headache, whether naturally or through abortive medications), severity, frequency (number of headache days), and time of discontinuation of migraine attacks. Additionally, the diaries recorded information about the number and type of analgesics used and the triggering factors for the headaches, such as menstruation and light, over a 30-day period. The intensity of head pain was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS), a 10-cm measurement tool where the left side indicates the absence of pain, and the right side represents the maximum level of pain. Blood sample collection and biochemical assessments A 10-millilitre blood sample was taken, between 9 and 11 AM, from each episodic migraine patient enrolled in the study during the second visit, which was conducted approximately 30 days after the first visit and at least 72 h after the participant’s last headache attack. This collection aimed to obtain more accurate information regarding the interictal phase of migraine. For chronic migraine patients, as the duration of the headache extends beyond 15 days each month, it becomes challenging to acquire blood samples during the interictal phase. The blood samples were categorized into 18 microtubes, which were stored in freezers at a temperature of − 80 °C, while 10 Microtubes were stored in freezers at a temperature of − 20 °C. We did not store the samples in freezers for more than 1 year. In addition, a portion of the blood sample was separated and collected in the form of dried blood spots on WhatmanTM 903 protein saver cards (manufactured by Global Life Sciences Solutions USA LLC, located in Marlborough, Massachusetts). The cards were subjected to temporary storage at room temperature in the dark for a period of three hours, after which they were transferred to a refrigerator set at 4 °C. During the sampling process, individuals were asked whether they were currently experiencing a headache. All the serum samples were collected from the laboratory at Sina Hospital for biochemical studies. Serum levels of melatonin were then assessed utilizing commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from the Bioassay Technology Laboratory (Shanghai Korain Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and Crystal Day Biotech Co. (Shanghai Crystal Day Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The levels of these biomarkers were measured in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturers of the ELISA kits. All assays were conducted in triplicate. The intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variant were found to be within the commonly accepted ranges determined by regulatory bodies, with values of less than 8% and 10%, respectively 40 . Statistical analysis Data analysis in this study was conducted with SPSS software (v. 27). The normality of the quantitative variables in each group was assessed using different methods, such as skewness, histograms, Q‒Q plots, stem-and-leaf displays, box plots, and the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. Quantitative variables were compared between groups using one-way analysis of variance. The comparison of quantitative data between the episodic and chronic migraine groups was performed using an independent two-sample t test. For qualitative variables, the chi-squared test was used to compare differences between groups. Due to the lack of a normal distribution, logarithms of headache and attack, duration, as well as the melatonin SQRT, were generated and used to calculate P values. To evaluate the linear correlation between quantitative variables and serum melatonin levels, a generalized linear model was used. All hypothesis was two-tail and P values less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
