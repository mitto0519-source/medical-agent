This study examined adolescent exposure to influencer marketing and the use of dating apps by adolescents to establish possible relationships to body dissatisfaction (BD) and unhealthy weight-control behaviors (UWCBs). A total of 3121 high-school students were recruited from 36 schools in Taiwan. These students completed a self-administered questionnaire during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2022. Multivariate analysis revealed that adolescents who use dating apps, who have higher levels of exposure to influencer marketing of weight-control products, and who could be considered either overweight or obese are more likely to experience BD. Furthermore, adolescents who use dating apps, who have higher levels of exposure to influencer marketing related to weight-control products and experience BD are more likely to use weight-control products and engage in UWCBs. In conclusion, both the adolescent use of dating apps and exposure to influencer marketing are related to BD as well as to the purchase and use of weight-control products and to UWCBs. Introduction Social media have taken the world by storm, and this includes Taiwan. One study shows that 70% of Taiwan adolescents are social media users. [ 1 ] Adolescence is a period of personal and social identity formation, [ 2 ] and we now know that much of this development is now likely to be influenced by social media. Another study has revealed the degree to which adolescents are vulnerable to the adverse effects of social media. [ 3 ] Empirical studies have associated the use of social media (the use of highly visual platforms in particular) with increasing adolescent concerns surrounding body image, [ 4 , 5 ] which includes ramifications such as body dissatisfaction (BD), [ 6 ] eating disorders, [ 7 , 8 ] and depression. [ 3 , 9 , 10 ] Social media has created a “perfect storm” that exacerbates girls’ body image concerns, and in turn, BD, depressive symptoms, and disordered eating. [ 11 ] Negative body image increases the likelihood of engaging in disordered eating behaviors that include dieting, binge eating, fasting, calorie counting, and self-induced vomiting [ 12 ] with numerous serious long-term health consequences. [ 13 ] In these studies, adolescents have also often been unknowingly affected by algorithms that bias the content they are shown on social media, which exacerbates an adolescents negative body image and creates mental health problems. [ 14 ] Social media influencers have become a new source of advertising for children and teens. These influencers are persuasive to their younger followers. [ 15 ] One study showed that more than one-third of adolescents intentionally seek out influencer accounts when looking for product information. [ 16 ] One review study has revealed the negative effects that digital marketing exerts on intentional use and the actual consumption of unhealthy commodities. [ 17 ] According to the Federal Trade Commission, there are many fraudulent advertisements for weight-loss products. [ 18 ] Most weight-loss products on the internet are advertised according to algorithmic rules that target adolescents and obese people who are the most vulnerable consumers of this type of advertising. [ 19 ] Public health researchers have called for increased policy and research attention to marketing effects and the extension to adolescents and young adults. [ 17 ] A study in Brazil showed that 80% of adolescents were dissatisfied with their body image. [ 20 ] The tripartite influence model (i.e., media, parents, and peers) [ 21 ] and the dual psychological processes (i.e., internalization of appearance ideals and appearance comparisons) [ 22 ] provide frameworks to guide understanding of factors relevant to the development of BD. [ 6 ] Social media sites allow opportunities for users to share and view visual content, while frequent engagement with social media content may prompt negative behaviors and attitudes about society’s ideal appearance. [ 23 ] Empirical evidence supports the identification of a positive relationship between social media use and BD in pre to late adolescent boys and girls. [ 24 , 25 ] Meta-analytic work into media exposure reinforces appearance-ideal internalization and illustrates how this affects all ages of both genders, although adolescents are the most at risk. [ 26 ] Unhealthy weight-control behaviors (UWCBs) among adolescents are emerging problems. According to the Youth Risk Behavior Survey in the United States, 22% of girls and 10% of boys have engaged in UWCBs. [ 27 ] Studies have positively associated the use of dating apps with BD, [ 28 , 29 ] eating disorder symptoms, [ 30 , 31 ] and UWCBs. [ 31 , 32 ] Dating apps often contain commercial ads and user profiles depicting images that convey societally accepted image ideals for men and women that promote excessive concerns about body image and UWCBs. [ 31 , 33 ] One study compared dating app users with nonusers and found that, regardless of gender, users reported significantly lower levels of satisfaction with their face and body and higher levels of internalization, appearance comparisons, and body shame and surveillance. [ 29 ] Another study associated the use of Tinder with increased levels of distress, including sociocultural pressures, internalization of appearance ideals, and body image concerns. [ 34 ] However, these research findings were mainly for college students and adults. Thus, the present study explored the relationships between dating apps and UWCBs among adolescents. In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown, people were sedentary and indoors, and a lack of outdoor activity may have led to weight gain, which could influence individual body-image perception. [ 35 ] The lockdown also increased the amount of time online and viewership of social media. [ 36 ] In Taiwan, companies use influencers to endorse weight-control products, and experts are concerned that children and adolescents are more susceptible to influencer marketing. To date, relatively few studies [ 37 – 39 ] have been conducted in Asian societies to examine the relationships between social media use and body image among adolescents. Therefore, the present study explored the impact that exposure to influencer marketing and dating apps exerted on BD and on the weight-control behaviors of adolescents during the COVID-19 pandemic. Based on the theoretical perspectives and literature reviews mentioned above, we proposed the following hypotheses: The influencer marketing of weight-control products and the use of dating apps would have a positive relationship with BD among adolescents. The influencer marketing of weight-control products and the use of dating apps would have a positive relationship with usage of weight-control products among adolescents. The influencer marketing of weight-control products and the use of dating apps would have a positive relationship with UWCB among adolescents. 2. Materials and methods 2.1. Participants This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2022 during the initiation of measures intended to reduce the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. A probability-proportionate-to-size sampling method was used to systematically draw a random sample of schools, while the sampling frame was a list of schools and high-school student enrollments from the Ministry of Education 2021 academic year data. The participating schools in urban areas included Taipei City, New Taipei City, and Kaohsiung City, while the participating schools in rural areas included Pingtung County, Yilan County, and Hualien County. There are 257,305 high-school students in these 6 cities/counties. Using a 95% confidence level and a 3% confidence interval, it is estimated that at least 1067 participants would be needed to fulfill the requirements of a representative sample; this study garnered 1356 participants from urban areas and 1765 from rural areas. A total 36 high schools from both urban and rural areas in Taiwan agreed to participate in this study. The number of students and parents who agreed to participate met the inclusion criteria, but special-needs students were excluded. During the COVID-19 pandemic a no-visitor policy was implemented in schools. Therefore, we collaborated with teachers from each sample school. These teachers were tasked with introducing the survey to the students and facilitating the distribution of consent forms and questionnaires in their classrooms. A total of 3251 high-school students between the ages of 15 and 18 participated by completing self-administered questionnaires. After excluding incomplete responses, a total of 3121 valid questionnaires were collected (boys: 1472; girls: 1649; urban: 1356; rural: 1765) for an overall response rate of approximately 89%. 2.2. Materials A self-administered questionnaire was developed based on previous references. [ 40 – 43 ] A group of 6 experts were invited to evaluate the questionnaire’s content validity. After discussions with these experts, we modified the descriptions of items and removed inappropriate items based on their advice. The questionnaires were distributed to students in their classrooms. A pretest survey was conducted to interpret the students’ responses to the survey and evaluate the reliability of the scales in the questionnaire. Students were assured that their information would be kept confidential and anonymous. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at National Taiwan Normal University (202012HS010). 2.3. Measures 2.3.1. Purchase and use of weight-control products This study defined the use of weight-control products, which encompass a spectrum of items, including meal replacements. Aspects such as the purchase and usage of weigh-control products were assessed through 2 items. Students were asked “How often do you purchase weight-control products (i.e., meal replacement)?” and “How often do you use weight-control products (i.e. meal replacement)?” Response options for each item included “never,” “ever (ever before a year),” “seldom (a few times within a year),” “sometimes (a few times within a month),” and “usually (a few times within a week).” If students answered “a few times within a year” or more frequently, they were classified as engaging in the purchase and usage of weight-control products. 2.3.2. UWCBs This study used terms from a prior study [ 43 ] and defined unhealthy weight-control behaviors as continuous fasting for 24 hours and the use laxatives or self-induced vomiting. UWCBs were measured using 2 items. Students were asked “How often do you engage in continuous fasting for 24 hours?” and “How often do you use laxatives or self-induce vomiting?” Response options for each item included “never,” “ever (ever before a year),” “seldom (a few times within a year),” “sometimes (a few times within a month),” and “usually (a few times within a week).” If students answered “a few times within a year” or more frequently, they were categorized as exhibiting UWCBs. 2.3.3. Use of dating apps The use of dating apps was measured with 1 item. Students were asked “How often do you download and use dating apps?” Response options included “never,” “ever (ever before a year),” “seldom (a few times within a year),” “sometimes (a few times within a month),” and “usually (a few times within a week).” If students answered “a few times within a year” or more frequently, they were classified as engaging in the use of dating apps. 2.3.4. Exposure to influencer marketing Exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products was measured using 1 item. Students were asked “During the past year, how often did you see influencers promote weight control products?” Response options were rated on a 5-point scale: “never” (scoring 1), “seldom” (scoring 2), “sometimes” (scoring 3), “usual” (scoring 4), and “always” (scoring 5). Higher scores indicated greater levels of exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products. 2.3.5. BD BD was measured using 1 item. Students were asked “How satisfied are you with your body shape or size?” Response options included “very unsatisfied,” “unsatisfied,” “neutral,” “satisfied,” and “very satisfied.” If students answered, “very unsatisfied” or “unsatisfied,” they were classified as experiencing BD. 2.3.6. Demographic variables Demographic information included gender (male vs female), area (rural vs urban), and body mass index (BMI) (underweight, normal, overweight, or obese). Participants reported their height and weight information. The BMI values were calculated and classified as underweight, normal, overweight, or obese based on the Ministry of Health and Welfare recommended BMI values for children and adolescents. [ 44 ] 2.4. Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4. For all variables, percentages were calculated, and Chi-squared tests were conducted to analyze gender differences. Logistic regression models were conducted to examine the relationships between the use of dating apps, online influencer marketing exposure, BMI, and BD. In addition, multiple logistic regression models were conducted to examine the use of dating apps, online influencer marketing exposure, BMI, and BD, and to show how these factors related to the purchase and usage of weight-control products as well as to the adoption of UWCBs. 3. Results A total of 3251 high-school students of ages that ranged between 15 and 18 years participated by completing self-administered questionnaires. After excluding incomplete responses, a total of 3121 valid questionnaires were collected (boys: 1472; girls: 1649; urban: 1356; rural: 1765) for an overall response rate of approximately 89%. 3.1. Adolescents’ BMI, body image, and weight-control behaviors Overall, 18% of adolescents reported using dating apps in the past year. The distribution of body mass index among adolescent participants included 52.2% classified as normal weight, 24.6% as underweight, 11.5% as overweight, and 11.6% as obese. Approximately 37% of adolescents reported experiencing BD. About 3% of adolescents reported ever having engaged in continuous fasting for 24 hours, and 2% reported using laxatives or ever engaging in self-induced vomiting (Table 1 ). 3.2. Exposure to influencer marketing and use of weight-control products The mean score of exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products was 2.45, with 46% of adolescents reporting sometimes, often, or always being exposed to the influencer marketing of weight-control products. In addition, about 4% of adolescents reported purchasing and using weight-control products in the past year (Table 1 ). 3.3. Factors related to BD The simple logistic regression results showed that gender, dating app usage, exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products, and BMI were related to BD. In addition, multiple logistic regression results indicated that students who used dating apps had greater levels of exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products, and those who were considered overweight or obese were more likely to experience BD (Table 2 ). 3.4. Factors related to the purchase and usage of weight-control products Multiple logistic regression results indicated that adolescents who used dating apps, had greater levels of exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products, and those who experienced BD were more likely to purchase and use weight-control products (Table 3 ). 3.5. Factors related to UWCBs Multiple logistic regression results indicated that students using dating apps, who had greater levels of exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products, and who experienced BD, were more likely to engage in continuous fasting for 24 hours. In addition, students using dating apps and who had higher levels of exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products were more likely to use laxatives or engage in self-induced vomiting (Table 4 ). 4. Discussion This study examined the relationships between exposure to influencer marketing, dating app usage, BD, and weight-control behaviors among adolescents. The findings indicated that a third of adolescents were dissatisfied with their bodies, and that adolescent exposure to the influencer marketing of weight-control products increased the risk of BD. Our results approximate those of previous studies [ 45 – 48 ] that associate adolescents’ BD with media exposure to thin-ideal images and promote appearance comparisons with social media influencers. In a similar manner, another study associated BD and the drive for thinness in adolescents with the frequency of comparing one’s own physical appearance with that of influencers on social media. [ 49 ] On the other hand, 1 study associated daily exposure to body-positive images with the attainment of a positive mood and body satisfaction. [ 50 ] These types of body-positive images are known to improve the acceptance of real bodies by reducing upward comparisons and decreasing concerns with appearance. [ 51 ] The results of those studies show why the body-positive movement should be considered when developing interventions to improve the body image of adolescents. Our findings indicate that most (about 75%) adolescents have seen influencers introducing weight-control products on the internet, and this exposure to influencer marketing was positively associated with the purchase and usage of weight-control products. Many prior studies have shown that the exposure of children and adolescents to influencers’ marketing is positively associated with the intake of fast food, sugar-sweetened beverages, and snacks. [ 52 , 53 ] The present research is the first study in Taiwan to reveal the prevalence of adolescent exposure to influencer marketing of weight-control products and to quantify the positive association of such marketing with the purchase and usage of these products. More research is needed to understand the impact that influencer marketing exerts on children and adolescents in order to develop appropriate policy actions. The World Health Organization has advocated global policies and international cooperation to monitor and regulate the cross-border digital marketing of unhealthy products to minors. [ 54 ] Our study indicates that approximately one-fifth of adolescents use dating apps, which is positively associated with BD and UWCBs. Prior studies have also demonstrated a positive association between the use of dating apps and BD among adults. [ 28 ] Another study found that users of Tinder reported significantly lower levels of satisfaction with their face and body, as well as higher levels of internalization, appearance comparisons, body shame, and surveillance compared with nonusers. [ 29 ] Furthermore, the results of 1 study of adults has shown that compared with nonusers of dating apps, adults who are users are more likely to engage in unhealthy weight-control behavior. [ 31 ] Another study has reported that lifetime dating app users report greater daily urges for binge eating/purging and negative moods. [ 32 ] However, the literature on how dating apps affect UWCBs in adolescents is limited, and longitudinal and representative research is needed. A study found that social media literacy programs are a useful approach to preventing the risk of eating disorders in adolescents. [ 55 ] Schools could develop social media literacy programs to enhance students’ social media literacy to resist digital marketing. In addition, in the present study about one-fourth of the adolescent participants could be classified as either overweight or obese, which increases the likelihood of experiencing BD. Students with BD were more susceptible to influencers’ marketing of weight-control products and were more likely to purchase and use weight-control products and engage in UWCBs. Prior studies have shown that mass media and BMI are predictors of BD in both genders. [ 56 , 57 ] At least 1 study has connected a negative body association with factors such as browsing on Instagram, being an adolescent female, and having a higher BMI. [ 47 ] Adolescents who experience BD are known to be more likely to engage in UWCBs. [ 20 , 58 , 59 ] These results confirm the importance of implementing obesity-prevention programs for adolescents. School obesity-prevention programs could include digital media literacy programs to enhance adolescents’ competence for resisting influencers’ marketing of weight-control products. Additionally, governments should regulate influencers’ marketing of unhealthy products to minors. This study had several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the ability to infer causal relationships. Longitudinal research is necessary to examine the causal pathways between dating app use, exposure to online influencer marketing, BD, and weight-control behaviors. Second, the use of self-reported data for influencer marketing exposure, height, weight, and UWCBs may be subject to recall bias. Future studies should consider incorporating objective measures for height and weight to enhance validity. Third, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses, potentially leading to underreporting or exaggeration of BD and UWCBs. However, confidentiality was emphasized in this study. Fourth, important contextual factors such as cultural norms, family dynamics, and mental health conditions were not assessed. Future research should investigate the moderating roles of parental monitoring, peer influence, and media literacy. Finally, while the study measured exposure to influencer marketing of weight-control products, it did not differentiate between social media platforms (e.g., Instagram, TikTok, YouTube) or specify the nature of the marketing messages. Qualitative analyses of influencer content may offer deeper insights into which marketing strategies are most impactful on adolescents. Despite these limitations, the study’s large sample size remains a key strength, offering valuable insights into the associations between dating app use, influencer marketing exposure, BD, and weight-control behaviors among adolescents. 5. Conclusions The findings of the present study indicated that about two-third of participate adolescents have been exposed to the influencer marketing of weight-control products, and that at least a third of adolescents experienced BD. About 4% of the participating adolescents reported purchasing and using weight-control products in the past year, while 3% of the participating adolescents reported UWCBs such as fasting, laxative use, and self-induced vomiting. This study confirms that exposure to influencer marketing of weight-control products and the use of dating apps were risk factors for BD that encourage the purchase and use of weight-control products, which could lead to the development of UWCBs among adolescents. Participating adolescents were particularly susceptible to influencer marketing and to the risks associated with dating apps. Governments should regulate influencer marketing and restrict minors’ access to dating apps. The implementation of digital media literacy and e-health literacy is crucial for enhancing an adolescent’s ability to resist digital marketing influence and to develop a healthy body image. Future research should focus on long-term effects, cultural influences, and intervention programs that promote digital literacy and critical thinking to help adolescents resist harmful marketing messages.
