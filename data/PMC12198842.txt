Abstract ABSTRACT: Introduction Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) affects 1–5% of the paediatric population, including 55–90% of children with Down syndrome (DS), and has been associated with negative effects on neurocognitive development, cardiovascular health, immune development and quality of life. In-lab attended polysomnography (PSG) is currently the gold standard for the diagnosis of OSA in children, but it poses challenges due to the burden on families and limited testing facilities. Home sleep apnoea testing (HSAT), an unattended sleep test done at home, is an accepted alternative for adults but lacks sufficient evidence to be used clinically for the evaluation of OSA in children. HSAT may be especially beneficial for children with DS or others with sensory issues or those who struggle with sleeping in a laboratory setting overnight. Methods and analysis This single-centre trial compares HSAT to PSG for the diagnosis of OSA in children, including those with DS. The trial will enrol 317 children 5–12 years old, including approximately 100 with DS. The primary outcome is the diagnostic accuracy of HSAT compared with PSG for OSA evaluated through ROC. Secondary outcomes include the agreement between HSAT and PSG for therapeutic decision-making and comparison of preference and acceptability of HSAT versus PSG. This trial seeks to evaluate HSAT as an alternative diagnostic tool for paediatric OSA, potentially expanding testing options for clinicians and families. Ethics and dissemination This study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (#21–0 19 533). Informed consent will be obtained from all participants, and no identifiable data will be reported. Trial registration number NCT05382754 . STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY Sample size is large compared with other studies of home sleep apnoea testing (HSAT) in children and intends to include a diverse paediatric cohort, including children with Down syndrome. Trial includes key outcomes omitted from many previous paediatric HSAT studies, including parent and child-reported acceptability and preference as well as the impact of HSAT on therapeutic decision-making for obstructive sleep apnoea in children. There is a lack of standardised reporting for HSAT in children, making it difficult to compare results across different studies. Full polysomnography, including electroencephalogram, is used, which may limit the ability to scale up HSAT using this methodology in some resource-limited areas. Introduction Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a syndrome characterised by recurrent episodes of complete or partial upper airway obstruction during sleep, which disrupts normal breathing patterns and negatively impacts sleep quality and gas exchange. 1 OSA affects approximately 1–5% of children, 2 3 and even mild-moderate OSA can lead to cardiovascular, metabolic and neurobehavioural consequences which can be reversed with appropriate treatment, including adenotonsillectomy or positive airway pressure. 4 Children with Down syndrome (DS) are at a significantly higher risk of developing OSA, with approximately 50–75% of this population being affected by the condition. 5 6 OSA in children with DS can exacerbate existing comorbidities such as cognitive impairment and cardiovascular issues, and untreated OSA may further contribute to developmental delays and poor behavioural outcomes. 7 The gold standard for diagnosing OSA is in-laboratory polysomnography (PSG). PSG is a standardised comprehensive diagnostic test that records sleep through electroencephalogram (EEG) and electro-oculogram (EOG), along with various cardiorespiratory parameters including airflow, respiratory effort, ECG, and gas exchange (oxyhaemoglobin saturation and end-tidal CO 2 ), electromyogram (EMG) to detect movement and audiovisual recording. 8 Because of its ability to quantify the number of discrete respiratory events that occur during sleep, PSG is the gold standard for the assessment of OSA in children. 9 OSA is determined in children by calculation of the obstructive apnoea hypopnoea index (OAHI), which is the sum of obstructive and mixed apnoeas and obstructive hypopnoeas divided by hours of sleep. Although the American Academy of Sleep Medicine diagnostic criteria involves an OAHI greater than 1 event/hour, 10 we decided to use an OAHI greater than 2 /hour that is commonly used in large paediatric OSA studies as this is closer to the threshold typically used for clinical care. 4 9 11 Despite its diagnostic accuracy, PSG has significant limitations. It is labour-intensive, requires that a technologist monitors the child overnight in a sleep laboratory, accompanied by a caregiver. As a result, PSG is expensive, and high copayments may be a barrier for some patients. Sleep laboratories that are equipped to study children, especially those with complex medical needs who are at high risk for OSA, are limited in the USA and may not be available at all in many locations and may cause delay in diagnosis or missed diagnosis for children. Poor sleep quality and quantity may occur while sleeping in a foreign environment, and it is unclear how closely in-laboratory sleep mimics sleep in the home environment. 12 Home sleep apnoea testing (HSAT) is an unattended OSA evaluation that takes place in the patient’s home environment. In many adults, HSAT is an acceptable alternative to PSG for evaluating OSA if the raw data can be reviewed and interpreted by an appropriate physician. 13 14 In multiple randomised trials, there was not a significant difference between functional or treatment outcomes in adults evaluated by HSAT compared with PSG. 15 , 18 These results contributed to the acceptance of HSAT as a widely used clinical diagnostic tool in adults. However, the accuracy and acceptability of HSAT in paediatric populations remain uncertain. There is a critical need for alternatives to PSG for the evaluation of OSA in children. Even with an increasing number of sleep laboratories capable of evaluating children, the demand for paediatric OSA evaluation far exceeds the availability of testing with PSG. Increasing objective testing for OSA with a highly accurate alternative as described in this proposal could allow for more targeted treatment. Indeed, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine has recently identified the need to study HSAT in the paediatric population, recognising its potential to increase accessibility and reduce diagnostic wait times. 19 With the continued advancement of consumer wearables to monitor sleep, 20 amplified by the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on at-home medical care, many parents may prefer and increasingly expect the availability of testing outside of a hospital setting. 21 This study’s main objective is to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of HSAT compared with PSG for determining OSA status among children referred clinically for the evaluation of OSA. Secondary objectives include determining the agreement between HSAT and PSG for therapeutic decision-making and assessing the child/parent-reported acceptability of HSAT and preference of HSAT among children and their parents compared with PSG. If HSAT is found to be accurate and acceptable to families compared with PSG and has similar impact on therapeutic decision-making, it could provide an alternative that would allow for expanded sleep apnoea evaluation for children. Methods and analysis Study design The Home Apnoea Testing in CHildren trial is a comparative effectiveness trial aimed at evaluating the efficacy of HSAT compared with PSG for the diagnosis of OSA in children, including children with DS. Eligible participants will include children clinically referred for diagnostic PSG at the Sleep Laboratory in the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) for the evaluation of OSA. Written or electronic informed consent and assent, if applicable, will be obtained from the parent or legally authorised representative of eligible participants (refer to online supplemental material ). Enrolled participants will undergo baseline assessment including medical record review, anthropometric measurements and symptom questionnaires. All participants will complete both PSG and HSAT and will be randomised 1:1 stratified by DS status to either (1) HSAT followed by PSG within 1 week or (2) PSG followed by HSAT within 1 week. Families will complete acceptability and preference questionnaires after both OSA testing modalities ( figure 1 ). Participants will undergo treatment per usual clinical care based on clinical PSG. The study procedures, recruitment and data collection will be conducted at CHOP, beginning in 2023. Trial completion is anticipated to be 2027. The University of Miami will be involved in the study as a relying site, limited to management and interpretation of deidentified data. Patient and public involvement statement Feedback from families undergoing clinical care at the CHOP Sleep Center who have struggled with in-lab PSG, including children with medical and developmental comorbidities, was both the inspiration for this trial and involved in the study design and outcomes. Specifically, parents and children involved in our preliminary study evaluating the use of HSAT in youth with DS had a positive experience with HSAT, 22 and this trial seeks to obtain more definitive data in a larger cohort to determine if this technology could be clinically applicable as an alternative for these families. Study participants and eligibility criteria The study will enrol 317 children aged 5–12 years, who meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria listed in table 1 . We expect that 100 of the enrolled children will have DS. The CHOP Sleep Laboratory schedule will be reviewed to identify potentially eligible participants. Additionally, recruitment may occur from the CHOP Sleep Clinic or Trisomy 21 Program when patients are scheduled for diagnostic PSG. No recruitment will occur from advertisement or beyond patients clinically scheduled for diagnostic PSG. Each subject’s participation will last approximately 1 month. Randomisation At the baseline visit after consent, participants will be randomised (1:1 for the order of HSAT and PSG) using a predetermined schema in Stata (Statacorp, College Station, Texas, USA) established by the trial statistician DS and completed by the unblinded study coordinator. The investigators interpreting the HSAT and outcomes assessor will be blinded to the order of the two sleep tests. Measures Demographics At baseline, we will collect demographics and medical history, including information on age, sex, comorbidities, previous treatments and any prior diagnoses. In addition, caregivers will complete the 22-item Paediatric Sleep Questionnaire sleep-related breathing disorders scale. 23 This validated instrument asks about snoring and observed apnoea as well as daytime symptoms including sleepiness and hyperactivity. Caregivers will also complete the 15-item PROMIS (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System) Sleep Disturbance parent report, which includes questions about difficulty falling and staying asleep and night awakenings. 24 Home sleep apnoea testing Participants will undergo HSAT in their home environment, after setup at the CHOP Sleep Laboratory by a certified sleep technologist. HSAT will be conducted using the Nox A1S monitor (Nox Medical, Reykjavik, Iceland), which is an FDA 510(k)-cleared device that has been in use since 2015, and over 1000 devices are currently being used worldwide both clinically and for a variety of research studies. HSAT recording will include EEG (leads at C3-A2, C4-A1, F3-A2, F4-A1, O1-A2 and O2-A1), bilateral EOG, ECG, submental EMG, airflow (nasal pressure transduction) with thermistor, oxyhaemoglobin saturation (SpO2, Bluetooth pulse oximeter- WristOx2 Nonin Model 3150), chest and abdominal respiratory effort (inductance plethysmography belts) and accelerometry. Participants will come to the CHOP Sleep Laboratory in the afternoon on the night of the HSAT. Families will also be asked to watch a web-based video about the HSAT before the visit to prepare participants for the setup ( https://youtu.be/vU7st6lBbno ). Study staff will configure the device to begin recording at the child’s typical bedtime on a school weekday and to stop recording the following morning following a typical wake time. A sleep technologist will apply the EEG, EOG, ECG and EMG sensors and chest and abdominal plethysmography belts to the participants. Parents will be instructed by the technologist on how to apply the pulse oximeter and airflow cannula, how to troubleshoot leads that may dislodge and how to detach the applied sensors at the conclusion of the study. The study team helpline will be provided to the parents for use if there are questions either at setup or throughout the night of the HSAT. Participants will return home fitted with the device and will be instructed to go to bed at their usual bedtime as well as continue their current medications. In the morning after the overnight HSAT, parents will detach the sensors from the participants and a courier service will return the device to the CHOP Sleep Laboratory. The disposable components will be discarded, and the HSAT device and its accessories will be cleaned according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data will be downloaded onto a secure research drive with only the participant’s study identification number and will be scored by a sleep technologist and reviewed by the study physicians who will be blinded to the randomisation order and results of the clinical polysomnogram. Participants will have an opportunity to repeat the test if the HSAT data show less than 50% of total sleep time without EEG, direct or indirect measure of airflow or pulse oximetry. HSAT signal quality is defined as the proportion of recording time with a signal that is reliable for scoring, typically when the lead is in proper position on the participant. In-lab PSG The in-lab PSG will be conducted as part of clinical care. Participants will report to the sleep laboratory by 19:00 and remain in a quiet dark room until the study ends the following morning at 6:30 AM, and children are accompanied by a parent/guardian throughout the night. PSG will be conducted using a Polysmith system (Nihon Kohden, Irvine, California, USA) to record the following parameters: EEG (leads at C3A2, C4A1, F3A2, F4A1, O1A2, O2A1); bilateral EOGs; submental and tibial EMGs; chest and abdominal wall motion using respiratory inductance plethysmography (Natus, Middleton, Wisconsin, USA); heart rate by ECG; arterial oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry (Nihon Kohden, Irvine, California, USA); end-tidal carbon dioxide measured at the nose by infrared capnometry (Smith Medical, Dublin, Ohio, USA); airflow using a three-pronged thermistor (Pro-Tech Services, Mukilteo, Washington, USA) and nasal pressure (Pro-Tech Services, Inc., Walnut Cove, North Carolina, USA). Participants are continuously observed by a PSG technician in a dark room. Audio/video will be recorded with the use of an infrared video camera and microphone. Both HSAT and in-lab PSG will be scored using the current American Academy of Sleep Medicine paediatric standards. 8 25 Therapeutic decision questionnaire Three off-site investigators who are experienced clinical paediatric sleep physicians will receive a set of deidentified participant clinical information. This will include medical history, physical exam findings and reported symptoms. The off-site investigators will also be provided with results of either the HSAT only or PSG only and will provide their recommended therapeutic decision (conservative treatment with watchful waiting vs active treatment with adenotonsillectomy or positive airway pressure). Results will be consolidated and analysed. Participants will undergo clinical treatment, which is not part of this research protocol, based solely on PSG. Acceptability and preference questionnaires A 5-point Likert scale questionnaire assessing the acceptability of HSAT in the home environment will be completed by caregivers the morning after the HSAT. Questions will include to what degree the test was appealing, met the caregiver’s approval, was liked and was welcomed by the family. Additional questions will include how frequently the caregiver needed to intervene to replace the different sensors, whether they contacted the sleep lab for assistance and whether the child’s sleep was typical or atypical compared with a usual night. Following completion of both HSAT and PSG, both caregivers and children will complete a brief preference questionnaire which includes questions about which test was easier, which was preferred and which represented a more typical night’s sleep. Caregivers and children can also leave a comment about their experience with both sleep studies. Data safety monitoring board An external data and safety monitoring board (DSMB) has been convened by the principal investigators (PIs) and approved by the NIH (National Institutes of Health) programme officer and includes experts in paediatric OSA, DS, biostatistics and clinical trials (some overlapping). The DSMB has convened to review the final protocol and DSMB Charter before study initiation and will meet periodically, and not less frequently than annually either in person or via teleconference and will provide reports to the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. The DSMB Chair will receive all reports of serious adverse and unexpected adverse events and treatment failures in real time. The DSMB will also receive interim reports on a twice-annual basis. CMC and IET will be responsible for assembling materials/reports for the DSMB and NIH and maintaining all regulatory files across the duration of the study. The PIs will be primarily responsible for overseeing the safety of the trial. Safety monitoring will include careful assessment and appropriate reporting of adverse events as indicated below and will also include a regular assessment of the number and type of serious adverse events. Any serious adverse event that might reasonably be due to the study participation will be reported to the local IRB and NIH within 24 hours of its occurrence. Outcomes Primary outcomes The primary outcome of this study is the diagnostic accuracy of HSAT compared with PSG for OSA. The in-laboratory PSG will be considered the reference variable. The primary outcome variable will be OSA diagnosis, defined as OAHI &gt;2 events/hour. Level II HSAT will be used to evaluate for OSA in the home environment with caregiver supervision. Also, overnight PSG in the sleep laboratory with continuous monitoring by clinical staff will be completed. Secondary outcomes The secondary outcomes will include the agreement between HSAT and PSG for therapeutic decision-making and comparison of preference and acceptability of HSAT versus PSG. Data analysis All statistical analyses will be conducted with two-sided tests of hypotheses, and a p value &lt;0.05 will be the criterion for statistical significance. Descriptive analyses will include computation of means (with 95% CI), SD, medians, IQRs of continuous variables and tabulation of categorical variables. Tests of normal distribution will be performed to determine the extent of skewness, and transformation methods (eg, logarithmic) may be used to normalise the distribution of seriously skewed variables. Frequency counts and percentages (and their respective 95% CI) will be estimated based on the Clopper-Pearson interval. Confidence or fiducial limits illustrated in the case of the binomial will be used for summarising categorical variables (eg, sex, demographic data). Table 2 summarises the overview of the different tests used in the study. For the primary outcome, evaluating the diagnostic performance of HSAT compared with PSG, non-parametric receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analysis with stratification by DS status will be used with PSG as the reference method and the HSAT as the one to be assessed ( table 2 ). Furthermore, HSAT estimated OAHI will be assessed as a continuous outcome in comparison to the PSG reference and using non-parametric ROC analysis. Sensitivity and specificity of HSAT for OSA will be calculated. Additional analyses will include comparisons of AHI, SpO2 nadir and arousal index, between PSG and HSAT using, depending on data normality, paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed rank test, Pearson or Spearman correlation, Bland-Altman plot and regression of the differences on the ‘true’ value from the in-lab PSG. Logistic regression will be used to assess the association between therapeutic decision (conservative vs active treatment) between PSG and HSAT, adjusted (confounded) by randomisation arm and DS status. Due to the crossover design of the study (matched observations), we do not anticipate any major impact of confounding by other unforeseen factors. The random effects will be set on the level of clinical reviewer. To adjust for multiple comparisons that may result in a Type I error, the least significant difference correction will be applied. For the preference and acceptability questionnaires, descriptive statistics will include the Likert scores from HSAT acceptability and proportion and percentages (with their respective 95% CI) of parents and children who preferred each test. To assess the possible agreement/disagreement between children and parents on the preference for type II HSAT or PSG, the McNemar-Bowker test will be used. Sample size and power will be calculated for both primary and secondary outcomes. In the primary outcome, we anticipate the proportion of eligible participants who have OSA (OAHI &gt;2/hour) to be approximately 50%. Using a test for one ROC curve implementation in commercial power analysis software (PASS 16, NCSS, Kaysville, Utah, USA) and assuming that 50% of participants will have an OAHI &gt;2, a sample size of 285 participants will achieve 90% power to detect a difference of 0.10 between the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for the null hypothesis (AUC ROC=0.7) and the AUC for the alternative hypothesis (AUC ROC=0.8) using a two-sided z-test at a significance level of 0.05. Assuming 10% of participants will have an inadequate HSAT, we plan to enrol 317 participants in this study. It is expected that approximately 100 subjects will have DS. For the secondary outcome, which includes the therapeutic decision-making, we anticipate using equivalence tests for one proportion test implementation in commercial power analysis software (PASS 16, NCSS, Kaysville, Utah, USA). Sample size calculations indicate that 224 participants will achieve 90% power to detect an equivalence difference of 0.1, using a one-sided exact test with a significance level of 0.05. These results assume a baseline proportion of 30% of children who will require clinical treatment. Discussion This comparative effectiveness trial aims to evaluate the efficacy of HSAT compared with overnight in-laboratory PSG for the diagnosis of OSA in children. PSG is the recommended clinical test for paediatric OSA but has a high burden of care for families, and testing facilities are limited. HSAT offers a convenient and potentially lower-cost alternative to in-lab PSG for diagnosing sleep apnoea in children. One of the main advantages of HSAT compared with PSG is that HSAT captures sleep parameters in a familiar environment, potentially leading to a more accurate representation of typical sleep, including duration, position and environment. 22 In contrast, sleep laboratories use hospital beds, are free of environmental allergens and the study must end at a set time, which may not match the child’s typical sleep time. The HSAT equipment used in this study includes most of the channels used in in-lab PSG, and all the channels used to score sleep and apnoeas and hypopnoeas. EEG data are crucial for differentiating sleep stages and identifying and classifying arousals as spontaneous, respiratory or associated with movement. Without EEG data, HSAT could result in respiratory events being scored when the patient is not actually asleep, and hypopnoeas and central apnoeas that are scored due to their association with microarousals cannot be scored. Both of these scenarios could affect the accuracy of the AHI and OSA presence and severity. This is especially important for children with DS, behavioural issues or sensory sensitivities, who may experience frequent sleep disruptions unrelated to sleep-disordered breathing. Other measures, such as respiratory channels and pulse oximetry, are similar between HSAT and PSG. If HSAT demonstrates good diagnostic accuracy for OSA in children compared with PSG and shows similar results for therapeutic decision-making, it could provide significant indirect benefits to society. OSA is prevalent in children, and the availability of HSAT could expand access to testing for children who might otherwise face barriers to in-lab PSG, such as logistical challenges or the burden on the families. Understanding HSAT’s diagnostic accuracy and acceptability will be important and essential to determine whether it could be used as an alternative to PSG in the clinical assessment of OSA in children, particularly when PSG is not available or would be challenging for families, including those with children with DS or other special needs. Ethics and dissemination Risk assessment Participation in this study involves only risk that is no more than minimal risk. There are not significant risks associated with HSAT. The Nox A1S has fewer wires than standard PSG equipment, but patients might still get tangled, requiring parental intervention to monitor the child. Sensitive skin could react to sensor adhesives; parents will be instructed on repositioning if needed and removing adhesives once the study is done, as well as cleaning the skin. Topical hypoallergenic barrier creams can treat skin irritation. Any incidental findings from HSAT will be corroborated with clinical PSG data. Considering the breach of confidentiality during filling out the questionnaire, the study staff will take all necessary precautions to ensure that there are no risks associated with completion of the questionnaires. This study has been approved by the IRB at the CHOP (#21–0 19 533). The study is registered on a publicly accessible database ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration number: NCT05382754 ). We will obtain written or electronic informed consent from all participants. The study principal investigator, co-investigators, clinical research coordinators, staff scientist and statistician will have access to the collected data. Study findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals and at scientific conferences. No report or publication will contain any identifiable data that could lead to the participants’ identification.
