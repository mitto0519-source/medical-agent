Abstract ABSTRACT Objectives To investigate the association of sarcopenia, exposure to medium-term to long-term ambient particulate matter 2.5 µm and 10 µm (PM 2.5 and PM 10 ) pollution and cognitive function in a community-dwelling cohort of middle-aged and older adults in South Korea. Design A community-based prospective cohort study. Setting In the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES). Participants The participants were drawn from the seventh follow-up visit conducted between 2015 and 2016 in the KoGES community-based Ansung cohort who had participated in an ageing substudy. Outcome measures Cognitive function was evaluated by the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE) and decreased cognitive function was defined as a K-MMSE score of 23 or less. Results Of the 2274 participants (mean age 70.1 years, 58.3% women and mean annual PM 2.5 and PM 10 levels of 30.7 and 52.2 μg/m 3 , respectively), 8.7% (n=197) were sarcopenic, 35.8% (n=814) were possible sarcopenic and 55.5% (n=1263) were non-sarcopenic. The predictors of sarcopenia included body mass index, cognitive function, age, marital status, hypertension and physical activity. Exposure to PM 2.5 and PM 10 for an average duration of 1 month to 3 years was not selected as a predictor of sarcopenia. Participants with sarcopenia were associated with lower cognitive scores (β=−1.51, p&lt;0.0001) and decreased cognitive function compared with those without sarcopenia (OR 2.34, 95% CI 1.56 to 3.52). Exposure to medium-term and long-term PM 2.5 or PM 10 was not associated with sarcopenia. The effect modification of PM exposure on the association between sarcopenia and cognitive function was generally not detected. Conclusions In this community-based observational cohort study of KoGES participants aged 50 years and older, sarcopenia was associated with decreased cognitive function. However, medium-term to long-term exposure to PM 2.5 or PM 10 was not associated with sarcopenia and did not modify the relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive function. STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY This study used a broad range of particulate matter (PM) exposure levels, with an average duration ranging from 1 month to 3 years. Individual PM exposure levels were determined based on each participant’s residential address, allowing for more precise exposure measurement. This study explored the association of sarcopenia, medium-term to long-term exposure to ambient PM 2.5 and PM 10 pollution and cognitive function, which are rarely investigated together. A limitation of the study is that it relied on residential address data for PM exposure, which may not account for participants’ time spent in other environments. Background South Korea has seen an increase in its elderly population, with individuals aged 65 years and older comprising 18.4% of the population in 2023. This percentage is projected to rise significantly, reaching 46.4% by 2070. 1 As the elderly population grows and life expectancy extends, age-related health issues, particularly those leading to disabilities, such as sarcopenia and cognitive impairment, have garnered substantial interest from academia, government and the health industry. These conditions are preventable, which offers opportunities for disability prevention. 2 3 Sarcopenia, a geriatric syndrome that occurs with advancing age, is characterised by a loss in skeletal muscle mass and strength, and reduced physical performance. 4 5 It is crucial to prevent or postpone the onset of sarcopenia, as sarcopenia increases the risk of developing deleterious diseases and conditions, such as cognitive deterioration, 6 metabolic risk factors, 7 osteoporotic hip, 8 fragility fractures 9 and cardiovascular diseases. 10 These conditions, once developed, result in substantial personal, social and economic burdens. 11 12 Notably, the onset period and advancement of sarcopenia vary from person to person, suggesting that different factors, such as dietary patterns, lifestyle factors and exposure to environmental toxicants, could accelerate or decelerate the initiation and progression of sarcopenia. 13 Accordingly, identifying the modifiable risk factors for sarcopenia, as well as taking into consideration these factors while investigating sarcopenia, is important. Ambient air pollutants pose a substantial threat to human health due to their toxic substances being inhaled into the lungs and reaching various organs, thereby causing morbidity and mortality. 14 15 Among the various types of air pollutants, airborne particulate matter (PM), which consists of a heterogeneous combination of particles of different sizes and types, has been extensively studied for its adverse health-related effects. 16 Access to clean air is a human right; however, this necessity has been compromised by factors, such as industrialisation, urbanisation and inadequate regulations. 17 18 A comprehensive understanding of the detrimental effects of air pollutants on human health is essential for developing effective policies that regulate the extent of ambient air pollution and ultimately diminish its destructive health risks. Given the growing elderly population and the prevalence of air pollution in South Korea, investigating these modifiable factors, such as air pollution and sarcopenia, can provide valuable insights for promoting better health outcomes in this demographic. Empirical studies have demonstrated that exposure to air pollution is associated with systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which are known to contribute to muscle degradation and cognitive impairment. 19 20 Our previous study documented the associations between ambient PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposures during the short-term, medium-term and long-term and decreased cognitive function in middle-aged to elderly adults in South Korea. 21 Additionally, we found that PM exposure is associated with increased levels of inflammatory and oxidative biomarkers, such as serum C reactive protein, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10 and tumour necrosis factor-α, as well as urinary levels of 8-hydroxy-2A-deoxyguanosine. 22 23 Notably, these biomarkers are elevated in individuals with sarcopenia and cognitive impairment. 24 , 27 Despite these connections, there is a lack of research specifically examining the interplay between sarcopenia, cognitive function and air pollution in vulnerable elderly populations. The notion that sarcopenia mediates the relationship between air pollution exposure and cognitive decline suggests muscle health as the key link. Specifically, air pollution initiates a range of mechanisms, such as systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which accelerate muscle degradation and lead to sarcopenia. Sarcopenia, characterised by a loss of muscle mass and function, is associated with several mechanisms that can lead to cognitive impairment. 28 , 30 Chronic inflammation in sarcopenia could contribute to neuroinflammation, affecting brain health. Oxidative stress, common in sarcopenia, damages neurons, while insulin resistance impairs the brain’s ability to use glucose effectively, leading to cognitive decline. 31 Additionally, sarcopenia often results in physical inactivity, which reduces blood flow to the brain and limits neurogenesis, both of which are vital for maintaining cognitive function. Poor vascular health associated with sarcopenia could reduce cerebral perfusion. 32 33 Recognising sarcopenia as a mediator highlights how air pollution might influence cognitive decline through muscle health deterioration. Moreover, air pollution might also modify the relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive function, potentially exacerbating cognitive decline in those already suffering from sarcopenia. 19 20 We hypothesised that more advanced forms of sarcopenia are associated with decreased cognitive function. Based on our previous finding that prolonged exposure to high levels of ambient PM 2.5 and PM 10 is associated with decreased cognitive function, 21 we sought to identify the extent of the harmful effects of air pollution on cognitive scores by hypothesising that exposure to ambient air pollution, including PM 2.5 and PM 10 , affects the relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive function as an effect modifier. We further hypothesised that sarcopenia mediates the relationship between ambient air pollution and decreased cognitive function. Accordingly, the aims of the current study were as follows: (1) to explore the prevalence of sarcopenia and identify the predictors of sarcopenia, (2) to investigate the relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive function and (3) to examine the association of sarcopenia, exposure to ambient PM 2.5 and PM 10 pollution and cognitive function in a community-dwelling cohort of middle-aged and older women and men in South Korea. Our objectives are grounded in the need to better understand the pathways through which environmental factors, particularly air pollution, may influence age-related health outcomes, including sarcopenia and cognitive decline, and to identify potential intervention strategies that could mitigate these effects. Methods Study population The Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) is a South Korean government-funded consortium project consisting of six prospective gene-environment models or population-based cohort studies initiated in the early 2000s by the Korea National Research Institute of Health, Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Ministry of Health and Welfare of Korea. The aim of establishing the KoGES was to reduce the burden of disease and improve the quality of life of South Koreans by investigating public health issues. 34 The participants of this study were drawn from the seventh follow-up (eighth examination) visit conducted between 2015 and 2016 to the KoGES community-based Ansung cohort who had participated in an ageing substudy. The Ansung cohort study was conducted in a rural region of South Korea to investigate the influence of rural community characteristics on public health and health-related outcomes. The seventh follow-up visit to the Ansung cohort was chosen because it provided the most recent and largest dataset on sarcopenia, air pollution (PM 2.5 and PM 10 ) and cognitive function scores in the KoGES database. Detailed information regarding the background, design and methodology of the KoGES Ansung study has been documented previously. 34 Online supplemental figure S1 shows the flow chart of the study participants included in this study. Of the 3235 KoGES Ansung cohort participants in the seventh follow-up examination, 2285 had participated in the ageing substudy. Of these, 11 participants were excluded due to missing covariates, including marital status (n=1), physical activity (n=8), alcohol consumption (n=1) and cerebrovascular disease (n=1), resulting in a total of 2274 participants available for this investigation. Sarcopenia assessment Of the various definitions of sarcopenia provided by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), 5 35 the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP), 4 Newman et al , 36 Foundation of the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) 37 and International Working Group on Sarcopenia (IWGS), 38 the current study implemented the AWGS 2019 criteria, which was evaluated as the most suitable diagnostic criteria for the current study population of South Korean adults. We used the bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) scale (InBody 3.0, Biospace, Seoul, South Korea) to assess muscle mass, a method that is both non-invasive and widely validated in clinical and epidemiological studies. Muscle strength was measured using a handgrip dynamometer, and physical performance was evaluated using the short physical performance battery (SPPB), which includes balance tests, a sit-to-stand test and a gait speed assessment. These instruments are well-established in the literature and provide reliable data for sarcopenia classification. Muscle mass index (MMI) was calculated as the total muscle mass assessed via BIA divided by height squared (kg/m 2 ). Sex-specific and age-specific (50–64.9 years, 65–75 years and &gt;75 years) 25th percentile was used as a cut-off for classifying high (≥25th percentile) and low (&lt;25th percentile) muscle mass. For women, high muscle mass was defined by an MMI of ≥14.63 kg/m 2 for 50–64.9 years, ≥14.37 kg/m 2 for 65–75 years and ≥13.83 kg/m 2 for &gt;75 years. For men, high muscle mass was defined by an MMI of ≥16.95 kg/m 2 for 50–64.9 years, ≥16.01 kg/m 2 for 65–75 years and ≥15.00 kg/m 2 for &gt;75 years. A handgrip strength of &lt;18 kg for women and &lt;28 kg for men was used as the cut-off for defining low muscle strength. The SPPB consisted of three distinct components: balance tests, a sit-to-stand test and a gait speed assessment. Each component was scored on a 0–4 ordinal scale, with the total score ranging from 0 (indicating poor performance) to 12 (indicating excellent performance). 39 The balance tests assessed the ability to maintain stability in three progressively challenging positions: side-by-side stand, semi-tandem stand and tandem stand, each held for 10 s. The sit-to-stand test involved timing the individual as they stood up and sat down five times from a standard-height chair. The gait speed test measured the time it took for the individual to walk 4 m at their usual pace, recorded over two trials with the faster time taken. In our study, these standardised procedures were carefully followed to ensure the reliability and validity of the SPPB scores. An SPPB score of 9 or less was chosen as the cut-off for classifying low physical performance, while a score &gt;9 was considered to indicate high physical performance. Sarcopenia in participants was defined by low MMI, low muscle strength and low physical performance, as well as participants who had low MMI, with either low muscle strength or low physical performance. Possible sarcopenia was defined by the presence of any one of the following conditions: low MMI, low muscle strength or low physical performance. Participants with low muscle strength and low physical performance, but high MMI, were included in the possible sarcopenia group. The non-sarcopenic group included participants with high MMI, high muscle strength and high physical performance ( table 1 ). Cognitive function assessment Cognitive function was evaluated by the previously validated Korean edition of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE), a widely accepted instrument for screening cognitive impairment and evaluating mental status in clinical and research settings. 40 41 The K-MMSE consists of seven domains that assess cognitive function—orientation, memory and registration, attention and calculation, recall and language. The total K-MMSE score is 30; higher scores represent better cognitive function, and lower scores indicate poorer cognitive performance. Decreased cognitive function was defined as a K-MMSE score of 23 or less. Normal cognitive function was defined as a K-MMSE score of 24 or higher. Ambient particulate matters The average exposure levels to PM 2.5 and PM 10 during the 1, 3 and 6 months (medium-term exposure), as well as 1, 2 and 3 years (long-term exposure) prior to the study visit, were assessed for each participant. We used the Community Multiscale Air Quality model for assessing the ambient air pollution level and meteorological data (eg, humidity and temperature) using a grid of 1 km 2 (1 km×1 km) for air pollution assessment and 81 km 2 (9 km×9 km) for meteorological data assessment. More detailed information regarding the formulation of the air pollution data has been described elsewhere. 42 , 44 Potential covariates Trained staff collected information from the KoGES Ansung participants regarding their demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, medical history, health-related lifestyle behaviours (eg, smoking status and alcohol consumption) and the use of medications using standardised questionnaires and procedures. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from data on the participants’ weight (kg) and height (m 2 ). Participants with a BMI of 18.5 kg/m 2 or less were defined as underweight, those with a BMI of 18.5 kg/m 2 or higher and &lt;23 kg/m 2 were defined as normal weight, those with a BMI of 23 kg/m 2 or higher and &lt;25 kg/m 2 were defined as overweight and those with a BMI of 25 kg/m 2 or higher were defined as obese. Diabetes mellitus was defined by the presence of any one or more of the following conditions: fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, haemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus or the use of insulin or oral hypoglycaemic medications. Hypertension in participants was defined by the presence of any of the following conditions: systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, a diagnosis of hypertension or the use of antihypertensive medication. Hyperlipidemia was defined by either high cholesterol, high triglycerides, low high-density lipoprotein, a diagnosis of hyperlipidemia or taking lipid-lowering medications. Cerebrovascular disease was defined by the presence of at least one of the following conditions: myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease, based on responses from self-reported questionnaires. Statistical analysis The data were summarised descriptively to present the demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, medical history and health-related lifestyle behaviours of the study participants. Means and SDs for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables were used to describe the characteristics of the overall participants, as well as for the participants grouped according to the sarcopenia classification (non-sarcopenic, possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia). Additionally, differences in the characteristics among the sarcopenia groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, with Scheffé’s method for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 test. The distribution of ambient air pollution data was summarised according to the average duration of exposure (from 1 month to 3 years) and the classification of sarcopenia. One-way ANOVA tests were conducted to examine the differences in average PM exposure levels in the different sarcopenia groups, followed by post hoc analysis using Scheffé’s test to assess pairwise differences. To identify the factors associated with sarcopenia, the following stepwise selection specifications were used: select variables to enter the model at the significance level of 0.1 or smaller and drop the variables from the model at the significance level of &gt;0.05. The following variables were included in the model: age in years, sex (women or men), BMI group (underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese), educational level (below elementary school, elementary school, middle and high school and college and above), marital status (married, widowed, separated, divorced or others, eg, single, living together), physical activity (yes or no to whether the study participants regularly exercised to make their bodies sweat at least once a week), smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption (never, former, moderate and heavy), diabetes mellitus (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), hyperlipidemia (yes or no), cerebrovascular disease (yes or no), prior diagnosis of cancer (yes or no) and PM exposure levels (average 1-month to 3-year exposure to PM 2.5 and PM 10 ). Of note, a prior diagnosis of cancer was included because existing literature suggests a relationship between cancer and muscle-related changes, potentially arising from factors, such as cancer treatments or the psychological impact of the disease. 45 46 For the primary analysis, the associations between sarcopenia (and components of sarcopenia, including muscle mass, muscle strength and physical performance) and cognitive function were assessed using a multivariable-adjusted linear regression model for continuous K-MMSE scores and a multivariable-adjusted logistic regression model for dichotomous K-MMSE outcomes (normal vs decreased cognitive function). The relationship between PM exposure and sarcopenia (and components of sarcopenia) was further assessed using multivariable-adjusted linear regression models with continuous PM 2.5 and PM 10 levels and tertiles of PM 2.5 and PM 10 levels as independent variables. For models using the continuous PM level as a dependent variable, the effect size was based on a one-unit increase in each PM level. For models using the tertiles of the PM levels as dependent variables, the lowest PM exposure group (tertile 1) served as a reference for comparing the association with the intermediate (tertile 2) and highest (tertile 3) PM exposure groups. All models were adjusted for the potential covariates included in the stepwise selection model described above. In addition, the relative temperature in °C, relative humidity in % and season of study visits to the participants were adjusted for models that involved PM exposure due to the relationship with air pollutants. 47 48 For the models exploring the association between sarcopenia and cognitive function, PM exposure levels were not included as a covariate. We further sought to identify the effect modifiers by including a term for the interaction between sarcopenia and the cognitive score based on the multivariable-adjusted linear model. Each of the following variables was tested for their potential interaction effect: sex (women, men), age group (50–64.9 years, 65–75 years, &gt;75 years), BMI group (underweight, normal weight, overweight, obese), alcohol consumption (former, current, never), physical activity (yes or no), smoking status (former, current, never), hypertension (yes or no), hyperlipidemia (yes or no), cerebrovascular disease (yes or no), prior diagnosis of cancer (yes or no) and PM exposure. Furthermore, we performed stratified subgroup analyses only for variables that showed a significant effect modification (p&lt;0.05). If the association between PM exposure and sarcopenia were found to be statistically significant, statistical mediation models adjusted for the aforementioned covariates would be constructed to assess the mediating role of sarcopenia in the association between PM exposure and cognitive function. Of note, all multivariable-adjusted linear regression analysis models using sarcopenia as the outcome used a dichotomous classification of sarcopenia, that is, the sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic (participants with possible sarcopenia and those without sarcopenia). A two-sided p&lt;0.05 was deemed statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS software (V.9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Patient and public involvement No patients or members of the public were involved in the study. Results The prevalence of sarcopenia was the following: 35.8% (n=814) for possible sarcopenia and 8.7% (n=197) for sarcopenia ( table 1 ). Of the 2274 participants, 55.5% (n=1263) were classified into the non-sarcopenic group. Table 2 describes the demographic, physical, clinical and lifestyle characteristics of the 2274 study participants. The mean age (SD) of the 2274 participants was 70.1 (7.2) years, ranging from 53 to 84 years. Of them, 58.3% were women, the mean BMI (SD) was 24.4 (3.4) kg/m 2 and 41.4% of the participants were classified as obese (ie, BMI of 25 kg/m 2 or higher). Approximately three-quarters of the participants were married (n=1720; 75.6%) and 33.0% (n=751) had completed middle-school or high-school education. The prevalence rate was highest for hypertension (58.2%), followed by that for hyperlipidemia (44.9%) and diabetes mellitus (23.1%). Cerebrovascular disease was the least prevalent disease (7.8%), followed by cancer (12.8%). A total of 693 out of 2274 (30.5%) participants had decreased cognitive function with a K-MMSE score of 23 or less ( table 2 ). As shown in table 2 , all continuous variables, including age, BMI, MMSE score, hand grip strength, MMI, SPPB and its component tests (balance, sit-to-stand and gait speed), exhibited statistically significant differences among the three sarcopenia groups (p for comparison &lt;0.0001). Pairwise comparisons confirmed that each group was significantly different from the others. Additionally, all categorical characteristics showed statistically significant differences among the sarcopenia classifications (p for comparison ≤0.03), except for diabetes (p=0.68), hypertension (p=0.48) and cancer (p=0.10). The mean age (SD) was 68.2 (6.7) years in the non-sarcopenic group, 71.9 (7.1) years in the possible sarcopenia group and 75.1 (6.3) years in the sarcopenic group, indicating that the sarcopenic group had the oldest participants, followed by the possible sarcopenia group, with the non-sarcopenic group being the youngest. The mean BMI (±SD) was the highest in the non-sarcopenic group (25.5±2.9 kg/m²), followed by the possible sarcopenia group (23.6±3.4 kg/m²) and the sarcopenia group (21.1±2.9 kg/m²). The sarcopenia group had the highest proportion of participants who were underweight (18.8%) compared with the other groups. The proportion of married participants ranged from 61.4% to 81.8% across the different classifications of sarcopenia, with the highest proportion observed in the non-sarcopenic group and the lowest in the sarcopenia group (p for comparison &lt;0.0001). The proportion of participants with decreased cognitive function varied significantly among the groups. More specifically, decreased cognitive function was observed in 55.8% (110/197) of participants in the sarcopenia group, 39.8% (324/814) in the possible sarcopenia group and 20.5% (259/1263) in the non-sarcopenic group, indicating a higher prevalence of cognitive decline in those with sarcopenia. The distributions of PM 2.5 and PM 10 levels according to the classification of sarcopenia are shown in online supplemental table 1 . The average exposure concentrations of PM 2.5 did not statistically differ across the sarcopenic groups (p values ranged from 0.07 to 0.74), except for 2-year exposure levels (p=0.03). For PM 10 , different levels of exposure across various sarcopenic groups were detected; the possible sarcopenia group had higher PM 10 exposure levels than the non-sarcopenic group during the average 3-month, 6-month, 1-year, 2-year and 3-year exposure periods. In addition, the average 1-year to 3-year exposure levels of PM 10 for the sarcopenia group were higher than those in the non-sarcopenic group. Stepwise selection regression analyses identified a subset of variables that were predictive of sarcopenia ( table 3 ). Predictors of sarcopenia selected by this procedure included the following: BMI category, cognitive function assessed by the K-MMSE, age group, marital status, hypertension and physical activity. The adjusted OR obtained by the fitted multivariable-adjusted logistic regression model showed that underweight participants had higher odds of being sarcopenic than normal weight participants (OR 4.18; 95% CI 2.48 to 7.05). Compared with that in the normal weight group, the overweight or obese group had lower odds of being sarcopenic (OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.50 for overweight vs normal weight participants; OR 0.10, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.17 for obese vs normal weight). Decreased cognitive function, older age, unmarried status, hypertension and absence of regular exercise were associated with higher odds of being sarcopenic. This stepwise procedure did not select PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposure for any duration. The results of the multivariable-adjusted regression analysis exploring the association between sarcopenia and cognitive function are presented in table 4 . Participants classified as possible sarcopenic and sarcopenic had lower cognitive scores as opposed to participants who were non-sarcopenic (β=−0.79, SD=0.15, p&lt;0.0001 for possible sarcopenia vs no sarcopenia; ß=−1.51, SD=0.26, p&lt;0.0001 for sarcopenia vs no sarcopenia). Consistently, the possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia groups had higher odds of having decreased cognitive function compared with the no sarcopenia group (OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.92 for possible sarcopenia vs no sarcopenia; OR 2.34, 95% CI 1.56 to 3.52 for sarcopenia vs no sarcopenia). Regarding the defining components of sarcopenia, muscle mass assessed via MMI was not associated with cognitive function (OR 1.17, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.57). In contrast, low muscle strength evaluated by handgrip strength (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.84) and low physical performance assessed by SPPB (OR 2.33, 95% CI 1.75 to 3.11) were related to decreased cognitive function. A significant effect modification was found in different age groups for the association between sarcopenia and cognitive scores (p&lt;0.0001), and none of the other variables showed statistically significant interaction (p value s ranged from 0.19 to 0.94). The results of the subgroup analysis stratified by age group are shown in online supplemental table 2 . The odds of having decreased cognitive scores were higher in the possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia groups compared with the non-sarcopenic group in all age groups; however, the extent of the odds was greater for the youngest age group. Exposure to PM 2.5 and PM 10 for an average of 1–3 months was not associated with sarcopenia. In addition, no significant relationship was detected regardless of using the PM exposure level as a continuous variable or categorical variable (tertiles; low-exposure, medium-exposure and high-exposure groups) ( table 5 ). Regarding the defining components of sarcopenia, PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposures were not associated with muscle mass (p values ranged from 0.07 to 0.95) and physical performance (p values ranged from 0.06 to 0.98) ( online supplemental tables 3 and 4 ). One-month, 3-month and 1-year exposure to PM 2.5 and 6-month, 1-year and 2-year exposure to PM 10 were associated with muscle strength (OR ranging from 0.46 to 4.21, 95% CI 0.27 to 7.77). Of note, the direction of the association was inconsistent depending on the duration or type of PM exposure ( online supplemental file tables 3 and 4 ). The effect modification of air pollution in the association between sarcopenia and cognitive score and function was not detected (p value for interaction ranged from 0.06 to 0.94), except for PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposure for the 6-month period (PM 2.5 , p=0.049 for cognitive score and p=0.04 for cognitive function; for PM 10 , p=0.02 for cognitive score and p=0.04 for cognitive function). The results of the subgroup analysis stratified by tertiles of PM are shown in online supplemental table 5 for PM 2.5 and online supplemental table 6 for PM 10 . Regardless of PM exposure levels, the sarcopenia and possible sarcopenia groups were generally associated with decreased cognitive scores as compared with the non-sarcopenic group. Discussion In this community-based observational cohort study of KoGES participants over 50 years, we explored the prevalence and predictors of sarcopenia, and investigated the associations among sarcopenia, exposure to air pollutants and cognitive function. Our findings were fourfold. First, 8.7% of our study population had sarcopenia—according to the AWGS definition of low muscle mass with either low muscle strength, physical performance or both. Second, the factors associated with sarcopenia were BMI, cognitive function, age, marital status, hypertension and physical activity. Exposure to ambient air pollution, including PM 2.5 and PM 10 , for an average duration of 1 month to 3 years was not selected as a predictor of sarcopenia. Third, while sarcopenia and its defining components (ie, muscle strength and physical performance, but not muscle mass) were associated with decreased cognitive function, exposure to PM 2.5 or PM 10 was not associated with sarcopenia and its defining components (ie, muscle mass and physical performance, but not muscle strength). The role of air pollution as an effect modifier in the association between sarcopenia and cognitive function was generally not detected. Due to the lack of association between air pollution and sarcopenia, an investigation into the role of sarcopenia as a mediator of the relationship between air pollution and cognitive function was not performed. Fourth, significant interactions with age groups were observed in the association between sarcopenia and cognitive function. In contrast, effect modifications based on sex, BMI group, alcohol consumption, physical activity, smoking status, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cerebrovascular disease and prior diagnosis of cancer were not observed. These findings add to the growing body of literature concerning sarcopenia and cognitive function in the approaching era of a super-aged society, while also contextualising the role of air pollution. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association of sarcopenia, ambient PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposure for various durations and cognitive function in the vulnerable middle-aged to elderly population in South Korea. Assessing the prevalence of sarcopenia requires the evaluation of qualitative, quantitative and functional aspects of the muscle as a whole, as this geriatric syndrome is characterised by the loss of skeletal muscle mass, muscle strength and physical performance with advancing age. 4 5 However, the current literature lacks consensus on the definition and means of assessment by various working groups and studies, which creates obstacles in uniformly defining and screening this condition. Within the same population, the proportion of sarcopenia varied by over 30% depending on the application of different definitions, such as those from EWGSOP1, EWGSOP2, FNIH, IWGS or AWGS. 49 , 52 Furthermore, even within the same study sample and diagnostic algorithm, the prevalence of sarcopenia varied depending on the components used for sarcopenia classification. For example, a study of 2099 elderly Koreans (aged 70–84 years) observed a fluctuating range of sarcopenia prevalence (sarcopenia defined as low muscle mass and low muscle strength) between 7.9% and 18.4%, depending on whether height-adjusted squared appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) or non-adjusted ASM was used for determining low muscle mass and whether grip strength or the sit-to-stand test was used for assessing skeletal muscle strength according to the EWGSOP2 approach. 49 Accordingly, the absence of a single mutually agreed diagnostic criterion leads to difficulties in fairly comparing the prevalence of sarcopenia across different studies. A unified definition of sarcopenia, supported by scientific evidence related to its health risks, will allow for comparability among sarcopenia studies and, importantly, a clear translation of the implications from research to clinical settings. A recent meta-analysis of 151 studies (n=692 056, mean age of 68.5 years) reported that the prevalence of sarcopenia ranged between 8% and 36% for participants younger than 60 years and between 10% and 27% for those aged 60 years or older. 53 Another meta-analysis of 109 studies representing 58 cohorts from 26 countries documented a wide range of estimates from 9.9% to 40.4%. 54 The current study observed a prevalence of 8.7% (n=197/2274) among all participants, 3.0% (n=19/631) among those aged 50–64.9 years, 7.0% (n=64/920) among those aged 65–75 years and 15.8% (n=114/723) among those aged 75 years and older. These results were consistent with the trend of increasing prevalence of sarcopenia with advanced age observed in a meta-analysis that investigated sarcopenia prevalence based on age groups. 50 In terms of the factors associated with sarcopenia, our study identified variables that were previously well-established as predictors of sarcopenia, including BMI, 13 55 56 cognitive function, 56 age, 13 52 56 marital status, 52 56 hypertension 57 and physical activity. 52 56 A recent UK Biobank study of 396 283 participants (52.8% women, ages ranging from 38 to 73 years) provided four categories for assessing factors related to sarcopenia: sociodemographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, lifestyle factors and health status (eg, self-reported prevalent diseases). 13 The factors associated with sarcopenia that we identified fit into these four categories (age and marital status for sociodemographic factors, BMI (underweight) for anthropometric measurement, physical activity for lifestyle measures and cognitive function and hypertension for health status). 13 Interestingly, marital status emerged as a significant factor associated with sarcopenia. Marital status is likely to influence various aspects of health and lifestyle that contribute to muscle maintenance. Married individuals often experience greater social engagement and emotional support, which encourage participation in physical activities and healthier behaviours. 58 The increased material well-being and access to healthcare associated with marriage further promote nutritional support essential for muscle health. 59 Additionally, the protective effects of reduced stress, facilitated by the ‘buffering hypothesis’ of social support from a spouse, could mitigate the negative impact of chronic stress on muscle degradation. 60 Furthermore, marital status contributes to economic stability. 61 In turn, this stability facilitates better access to healthcare and healthier lifestyle options, both of which are crucial in reducing the risk of sarcopenia. In contrast, non-married individuals may face different social dynamics and lifestyle patterns, leading to fewer opportunities for social engagement and higher levels of stress, which can negatively affect muscle health. 62 63 Therefore, the combination of social, emotional and economic factors associated with being married could collectively be identified as a factor associated with sarcopenia, highlighting the importance of considering marital status in understanding and addressing this condition. Exposure to ambient air pollution has been associated with multiple adverse health outcomes, including modest transient changes in the respiratory tract, acute diseases and chronic diseases and mortality. 64 , 66 Research on acute and chronic exposure to air pollution supports this evidence, and more associations have been observed with long-term exposure to high levels of air pollution. Our latest study also reported an association between short-term and long-term exposure to high levels of PM 2.5 and PM 10 and decreased cognitive function. 21 Building on our recent study and based on accumulating findings from the literature on the association of PM exposure with cognitive function, 67 sarcopenia 68 or components of sarcopenia (eg, skeletal muscle mass and handgrip strength), 69 , 71 the current study sought to investigate whether: (1) air pollution is associated with sarcopenia, (2) exposures to air pollution affect the relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive function and (3) sarcopenia plays a role as a mediator linking the association between air pollution and cognitive function in our KoGES population. The current study included air pollution data as potential predictors of sarcopenia; however, the statistical model selected none of the PM measures (ie, average exposure to PM 2.5 and PM 10 for 1-month and 3-year intervals). In addition, PM exposure was not associated with sarcopenia and generally no effect modification of ambient air pollution was detected, suggesting a lack of their relatedness with sarcopenia and adding to the scarce current body of knowledge. Interpreting and comparing the findings of studies investigating the relationship between air pollution and sarcopenia is inherently challenging due to several factors. First, the varying methods used to assess PM exposure and differences in exposure durations (from short-term to long-term) make it difficult to draw direct comparisons across studies. Second, the considerable variation in PM exposure levels of study participants across studies makes it even more challenging to compare results directly. Third, sarcopenia is defined in various ways across the literature, adding to the difficulty of making direct comparisons. Fourth, the diversity in study designs and the characteristics of study participants introduces additional variability that must be considered. Finally, the use of differing statistical methods to evaluate the relationship between air pollution and sarcopenia adds another layer of complexity. For instance, the study by Shi et al . (2024) differed from the current study in several ways. They used different methods to assess PM levels, included only yearly PM exposure levels, and involved participants with higher levels of PM exposure. Additionally, they had a younger mean age of participants and adjusted for a distinct set of covariates in the multivariable regression models. Specifically, Shi et al explored the association between annual exposure to PM (PM 2.5 and PM 10 ) and the prevalence of sarcopenia among Chinese older adults (n=11 700, 52.5% women, mean age of 61.0 years). 72 Using satellite-based and ground-based estimates for PM exposure, with levels varying across different regions, and applying the AWGS criteria, the study did not find a significant association between a 10 µg/m 3 increment of annual levels of PM 2.5 or PM 10 with sarcopenia based on a model adjusted for multiple covariates (eg, age, sex, BMI, etc). The median PM explosure levels of the participants were 53.33 µg/m 3 for PM 2.5 and 85.75 µg/m 3 for PM 10 , which were higher than the exposure levels of our study sample. Despite these inherent differences, neither the study by Shi et al nor the current study found an association between PM 2.5 or PM 10 exposure levels and sarcopenia. Another recent prospective cohort study conducted by Cai et al differed from the current study in several aspects. It employed a different method to assess PM exposure, included yearly PM exposure levels, and reported lower levels of PM 2.5 exposure among the study participants. Additionally, it involved younger participants, applied a different guideline for defining sarcopenia and adjusted for a different set of covariates. In particular, Cai et al investigated the relationship between exposure to ambient air pollution and the risk of developing sarcopenia using data from the UK Biobank (n=303 031, 51.0% women, mean age of 56.1 years). 73 The concentrations of PM 2.5 was assessed via a land use regression model to estimate yearly exposure levels of air pollutants at the participants’ residential locations. Sarcopenia was determined by the EWGSOP2 guideline. The findings indicated a significant association between higher levels of PM 2.5 exposure and an increased risk of probable sarcopenia with HR of 1.23 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.44) based on a model adjusted for various covariates (eg, age, sex, ethnicity, traffic intensity, etc). Notably, the mean PM 2.5 exposure level was 9.97 µg/m 3 , which was lower than the exposure levels of our study sample. This study discussed that the detrimental effects of air pollution, potentially through mechanisms, such as mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation, may contribute to the onset and progression of sarcopenia. Similarly, a retrospective cohort study conducted by Chen et al , which had several notable differences from the current study, investigated the association between long-term exposure to air pollution and the risk of developing sarcopenia using data from the Taiwan National Health Research Database (n=286 044, 54.9% women, mean age of 41.0 years). 74 This study assessed the concentrations of PM 2.5 and PM 10 , using data from 74 air quality monitoring stations across Taiwan to estimate yearly exposure levels. Sarcopenia was identified based on medical records with the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 and ICD-10 coding. The findings indicated a significant association between higher levels of PM 2.5 (adjusted HR (aHR) 21.9, 95% CI 19.7 to 24.5) and PM 10 (aHR 15.6, 95% CI 14.1 to 17.4) and an increased risk of sarcopenia, with the highest risks observed in the quartile with the most severe pollution exposure. Notably, the mean annual PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposure levels in this study were 21.0 µg/m³ and 43.2 µg/m³, respectively, which were lower than the exposure levels in the current study. Taken together, the various methodological and participant differences across studies make direct comparisons with our findings challenging. These factors, combined with the potential for publication bias, where studies with significant results are more likely to be published, highlight the need for cautious interpretation of the existing literature. 75 Publication bias can distort the understanding of the true relationship between air pollution and sarcopenia, as studies showing no association may be under-represented. Therefore, while our findings contribute valuable insights, they should be interpreted in the context of these limitations and considered alongside the broader body of research with a critical perspective. We found a significant association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment even after adjusting for confounders. These findings are consistent with the majority of the results from the literature that have explored the association between sarcopenia or its constituent components (ie, muscle strength and muscle mass) and cognitive dysfunction, impairment or subproperties of cognitive function (eg, verbal fluency) assessed by various means, such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment and MMSE. 76 , 81 Our results also align with two previous systematic reviews and meta-analysis studies. Specifically, a meta-analysis study that included seven observational studies with 5994 participants (ages ranging from 63 to 84 years) reported an adjusted OR of 2.25 (95% CI 1.21 to 4.12) for the association between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment, 77 and another analysis that showed a meaningful association between the sarcopenia and cognitive impairment (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.70 to 2.97), which persisted independent of the study populations and the definitions of sarcopenia and cognitive impairment. 82 There are several possible explanations for our findings. The significant association between sarcopenia and decreased cognitive function may be related causally. The coexistence of these health conditions may also suggest that they have common risk factors (eg, dietary intake, socioeconomic status and degree of physical activity) and/or shared pathophysiological pathways not necessarily causally related. 83 Oxidative stress, inflammation and insulin resistance have previously been reported as predisposing factors for sarcopenia and are also associated with cognitive impairment. 84 , 88 Thus, a shared pathophysiological pathway may explain our findings. Additionally, previous findings have shown that exposure to air pollution leads to oxidation and inflammation, resulting in muscle dysfunction. 89 This finding supports the causality between exposure to air pollution and sarcopenia. However, the insignificant results between air pollution exposure and sarcopenia may not be due to the lack of causality but because the low level of exposure did not reach the threshold for provoking sarcopenia. With the confirmation of no effect modification of ambient air pollution and no association between air pollution and sarcopenia, it is possible that the degree of air pollution to which our participants were exposed was not excessive enough to pose a deleterious influence on sarcopenia and its association with cognitive function. The ambient PM level in South Korea was comparably lower in comparison with that reported in several other countries, given its rank on the list where public and validated PM 2.5 data for 117 countries are available. 90 South Korea ranked 52nd in this list, with a yearly average PM 2.5 that is four times smaller than the first-ranked nation (Bangladesh) and five times larger than the last-ranked country (New Caledonia). 90 However, the annual average PM 2.5 level of 30.7 µg/m 3 and PM 10 level of 52.2 µg/m 3 observed in this study exceeded the South Korean annual air quality standards of 5 µg/m 3 for PM 2.5 and 15 µg/m 3 for PM 10 . 91 Moreover, a recent study that used the UK Biobank study reported statistically significant results, where PM exposure levels were lower than the values observed in the current study. 68 Specifically, this UK Biobank study of 352 265 study samples with 8.2% (n=28 710) participants with probable sarcopenia and 91.8% (n=3 23 555) non-sarcopenic participants as determined by the EWGSOP2 criteria showed an association between PM exposure and sarcopenia with an OR of 1.20 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.21) for PM 2.5 (vs OR 1.19, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.36 for the current study) and an OR of 1.24 (95% CI 1.22 to 1.26) for PM 10 (vs OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.17), whereas our results were not significant despite the higher PM exposure levels. 68 It is noteworthy that compared with the current study, the UK Biobank study included participants with a broader age range of 37–73 years (vs 53–84 years for the present study), predominantly white participants (94.8%, n=334 120 for the UK Biobank vs all South Koreans), had a lower annual PM exposure level (9.97 µg/m 3 vs 30.7 µg/m 3 for PM 2.5 and 19.29 µg/m 3 vs 52.2 µg/m 3 for PM 10 for the UK Biobank vs the current study, respectively), and used the EWGSOP2 criteria with appendicular lean mass index (kg/m 2 ) for muscle mass assessment and walking pace for physical performance measurement (vs AWGS 2019 criteria, MMI and SPPB used in the current study). These different characteristics and factors, as well as differences in the sample size (n=323 555 vs n=2274), may have resulted in discordant results between the two studies. Future studies are warranted to identify the source of discordant results across different studies and, if applicable, the cut-off PM levels that cause harmful health effects, particularly sarcopenia and impaired cognitive function, in the elderly population. As the present study did not identify a significant relationship between ambient PM exposure and sarcopenia, we did not pursue a pathway analysis to examine the potential mediating effect of sarcopenia. Mediation analysis requires a statistically significant association between the independent variable (PM exposure) and the mediator (sarcopenia) to satisfy the necessary statistical assumptions. Since this critical assumption was not met, conducting a mediation analysis was not feasible, and therefore, detailed methods for such analysis were not included. 92 The strengths of this study include its well-defined, community-based cohort of both women and men, with a comprehensive list of covariates and varying durations of PM exposure. In addition, air quality data were based on each participant’s address, which provided more precise individual exposure levels. The limitations of the present study are worth nothing. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study design precludes the determination of causality of the identified associations. Second, our reliance on self-reported measures for several variables may introduce reporting biases. Third, the lack of objective measures for PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposure is a limitation, as this may affect the accuracy of the exposure assessment. Fourth, the study population consisted of South Korean adults over 50 years, all residing in the rural region of Ansung, with relatively homogenous demographics and age distribution. This homogeneity limits the generalisability of our results to urban populations or those with greater diversity in demographics, socioeconomic status and environmental conditions. Fifth, the lack of data on memory disorders among participants limited our ability to account for this condition in the study. However, individuals with significant memory disorders were likely less inclined to participate, potentially reducing the impact of this condition. Additionally, the current study adopted the AWGS 2019 definition with total muscle mass measurement to create the MMI and used sex-specific and age-specific cutoffs (using the 25th percentile for each group) due to the lack of ASM assessment from the BIA used in this study. The 25th percentile cut-off was chosen to include a broader range of individuals who may be at an earlier stage of muscle loss or at risk of sarcopenia. This approach allowed us to capture those who might not meet the more stringent 20th percentile cut-off but still face increased risks associated with muscle decline, making it particularly useful for early intervention strategies. Accordingly, this may limit the comparison of the prevalence of sarcopenia detected in our study with that seen in other studies. Finally, the current study used ambient PM levels from the residential areas of the participants; thus, PM exposure levels during indoor activities and outside of these residential areas were not considered. Future studies incorporating a broader range of air pollution exposure levels, objective measures such as personal air pollution monitors and unified methods for assessing sarcopenia, along with longitudinal study designs, are warranted to precisely explore the effect of air pollution on age-related health issues in vulnerable elderly populations. Conclusions In community-dwelling South Korean adults over 50 years of age, this study detected a sarcopenia prevalence of 8.7%. The predictors of sarcopenia included BMI, cognitive function, age, marital status, hypertension and physical activity, but not PM 2.5 and PM 10 exposure.
