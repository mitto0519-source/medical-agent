Abstract INTRODUCTION The clinical, radiological, and pathological features have not been well documented for the recently discovered autosomal‐dominant vacuolar tauopathy (VT) harboring the Valosin‐containing protein ( VCP) p.Asp395Gly variant. METHODS We investigated the clinical, neuropsychological, physiological, laboratory, and radiological data and neuropathological findings in five symptomatic VT cases who met the diagnostic criteria for frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Radiological data were also collected from two pre‐symptomatic carriers. RESULTS All participants had heterozygous c.1184A &gt; G, p.Asp395Gly in VCP . All symptomatic cases exhibited cognitive, behavioral, and/or language dysfunction indicative of FTD in their 30s to 50s. Neuroimaging studies revealed marked bilateral frontal neurodegeneration and occipital lobar diffusion abnormalities. Post mortem examination of three cases and brain biopsy of one case revealed abundant three‐ and four‐repeat tau deposition and neocortical microvacuolization. Radiological changes were not evident in two pre‐symptomatic carriers in their 20s. DISCUSSION This study reveals distinct clinical‐radiological‐pathological correlations in VT, expanding the spectrum of early‐onset frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD). Highlights We characterized the clinical, radiological, and pathological features of vacuolar tauopathy (VT). Five VT cases exhibited a behavioral syndrome, often with aphasic features, with marked frontal lobar atrophy and hypometabolism. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of VT cases revealed occipital lobar diffusion abnormalities. Their premorbid character was described as sociable with no neuropsychiatric prehistory, working as a successful manager. Their parent had a history of electroconvulsive therapy for depression and died of early‐onset dementia in their mid‐60s. This individual was first evaluated 1 year after the onset of behavioral changes, including decreased attention at work. No significant changes in personality or mood were noted at that time. Neurological physical examination and laboratory tests, including liver, renal, and thyroid function, and glucose tolerance, were normal. Neuropsychological examination showed errors in verbal repetition and abstract interpretation of proverbs. Baseline brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was interpreted as normal; in retrospect, subtle frontal volume loss was present (Figure 1A , left), and diffusion‐weighed images (DWIs) had subtle ribbon‐like hyperintensity involving posterior cerebral and insular cortices (Figure 1A , right). No specific diagnosis was rendered at that time. Within a year after presentation (P+1 year), they gradually developed confusion and anxiety about even simple tasks, for which lorazepam was prescribed. The patient subsequently was unable to maintain their business. One and a half years after the first visit (P+2), fluency of spontaneous speech became markedly reduced with impaired comprehension of simple instructions, loss of empathy, increased apathy, craving for sweets, disinhibition, and perseveration were noted, resulting in a diagnosis of FTD with both bvFTD and mixed PPA features. 6 , 7 Treatment with memantine had little effect on symptoms. Over the next 1.5 years (P+3), behavioral and language symptoms worsened with echolalic speech and mild rigidity, and they became dependent on caretakers to fulfill activities of daily living (ADL). Short‐term memory and visuospatial deficits emerged later in the disease and were observed with preserved orientation to person and place but not to time. Repeat brain MRIs showed diffuse and progressive frontal atrophy, more prominently involving the left hemisphere (Figure 1A , middle), with relative sparing of the bilateral medial temporal lobes (Figure S1 ). 18 F‐flortaucipir tau positron emission tomography (PET) studies 3 and 5 years after presentation revealed progressive tau deposition in a frontotemporal distribution (Figure 2A ). Muscle cramps were treated with clonazepam. At P+4, they had a single seizure episode, but a subsequent electroencephalogram was normal. They did not sleep well and were agitated, requiring treatment with quetiapine and trazodone. They entered hospice care and died in their late 50s (P+8), corresponding to an overall disease duration of 9 years. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : The authors used PubMed and Google Scholar for literature on recently discovered vacuolar tauopathy (VT) cases associated with VCP p.Asp395Gly. The published research has provided very limited knowledge of the clinical, radiological, and neuropathological features due to the extremely rare occurrence of this disease. Interpretation : We characterized the diverse clinical phenotypes and clinical‐radiological‐pathological correlations of VT based on five symptomatic cases, including two new post mortem autopsy analyses. All five symptomatic cases presented with behavioral and language symptoms indicative of frontotemporal dementia (FTD), with neuroimaging revealing marked frontal lobar atrophy and occipital lobar diffusion abnormalities. Post mortem examination of three cases and brain biopsy of one case revealed abundant tau deposition and neocortical microvacuolization. Radiological changes were not evident in two pre‐symptomatic carriers in their 20s. Future directions : Future research must confirm our findings with additional cases and include more longitudinal clinical and radiological data. 3.1.2 Case 2 This patient, the sibling of Case 1, is a right‐handed Caucasian individual. Their premorbid character was described as outgoing, and they ran a company for years. They had a concussion in their youth and continued medication and brain MRIs for relapsing‐remitting multiple sclerosis (MS) from their mid‐30s. Their child had Down's syndrome. By their early 40s (P‐2), they gradually became distracted and forgetful at work with difficulty in word‐finding. They were evaluated 2 years later (P) by a neurologist due to confusion with difficult tasks. Their mood was apathetic, anxious, and depressed, in part attributed to the deterioration of their business. Neuropsychological tests demonstrated impaired execution, learning, memory, visuospatial cognition, and language function, including naming. Neurological physical examination and routine cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing were normal. Brain MRI at the time of presentation revealed global volume loss with ventriculomegaly, but no distinct regional cortical atrophy, compared to a study obtained 7 years prior to presentation (Figure 1B ). Diffusion imaging at presentation did not show any distinct abnormalities. Venlafaxine was initiated for depression with limited efficacy. Over 3 years (P+3), there was no evidence of relapsing MS. Rather, cognitive and behavioral symptoms gradually worsened with severely impaired verbal fluency, loss of their business, social withdrawal, and overeating with weight gain. 18 F‐fluorodeoxyglucose ( 18 F‐FDG) PET revealed hypometabolism of bilateral frontal lobes (Figure 2B ), slightly more pronounced on the left side. Memantine was added on suspicion of FTD. Within 4 years after the first visit (P+4), they became more dependent on caretakers for their ADL, and they developed echolalic and stereotyped speech. A follow‐up MRI obtained 5 years after presentation revealed progressive brain atrophy, most prominently involving the frontal lobes (Figure 1B ). Diffusion imaging at this time showed subtle signal hyperintensity involving the occipital cortices (Figure 1B , right panels). The patient was frequently disinhibited and agitated, resulting in admission to a long‐term care facility, where they were treated with quetiapine, haloperidol, and lorazepam. They died in their early 50s (P+10) after an overall disease duration of 12 years. 3.1.3 Case 3 This patient, the sibling of Case 1 and Case 2, is a right‐handed Caucasian individual. They were characterized as a cautious person who worked as a public employee. They were medicated for depression. In their early 50s (P‐1), they became careless, impulsive, and wasteful resulting in the loss of their job. They showed a preference for sweets and gained 9 kg of weight. On their first visit the next year (P), they could not answer simple questions about their past medical history. Short‐term memory, executive function, and verbal function (repetition and comprehension) were impaired. Neurological physical examination, nerve conduction, and electromyography were normal. Laboratory tests revealed hyperandrogenism and glucose intolerance, with otherwise normal liver, renal, thyroid function, vitamin B12, syphilis, and routine CSF tests. Brain MRI showed mild frontal volume loss and mild periventricular white matter hyperintensities, more prominent in the frontal lobes (Figure 1C ). Within the next year (P+1), they became apathetic and perseverative with worsening executive function and were diagnosed with bvFTD. A follow‐up MRI showed progressive frontal atrophy and perhaps subtle cortical ribboning on diffusion imaging (Figure 1C ). They were admitted into a long‐term care facility 5 years after the onset (P+4) with occasional treatment with antipsychotics for behavioral symptoms. They died in their early 60s (P+11) after an overall disease duration of 12 years. 3.1.4 Case 4 A right‐handed Japanese individual in his 40s (P) visited a neurologist for poor work performance and lack of hygiene for 3 years, including an episode of neglecting to extinguish the fire of a gas apparatus. Their parent and uncle had developed early‐onset dementia in their 40s and died in their 50s. Their maternal grandparent also had dementia. Their speech was fluent without paraphasia. They had some difficulty understanding conversations. Neurological physical examination was generally normal except for increased left brachioradialis and bilateral patellar tendon reflex. Audiogram revealed hearing difficulty at 4000 and 8000 Hz. CSF showed a slightly increased level of protein (49 mg/dL) without pleocytosis. Liver, renal, thyroid, and adrenal blood tests and tests for syphilis were normal, and there was no glucose intolerance. Their Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 22 out of 30, and their Frontal Assessment Battery was 7 out of 18, indicating frontal cognitive dysfunction. The Standard Language Test for Aphasia revealed agraphia and acalculia. Brain MRI revealed bilateral atrophy of frontal more than temporal lobes, including hippocampi with bifrontal white matter FLAIR hyperintensity. There was also posterior cortical, insular, and cingulate ribbon‐like hyperintensity on diffusion imaging (Figure 1D , right). Technetium‐99 m ethyl cysteinate dimer ( 99m Tc‐ECD) single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) showed remarkable hypoperfusion of the frontal lobes (Figure 2C ). They were diagnosed with FTD with pyramidal tract signs, sensory neuropathy, and hearing difficulty. Follow‐up MRI showed mild progression of brain atrophy over 2.5 years and apparent resolution of the cortical ribbon diffusion abnormalities (Figure 1D , right). The patient remains alive 11 years after onset (P+8). 3.1.5 Case 5 This patient is a right‐handed Greek individual, married, with 12 years of education, who worked in a factory. Their parent died in their 50s from a behavioral dementia syndrome, and their grandparent developed early‐onset dementia in their late 40s. In their mid‐30s (P‐6), the patient was fired from work for suspected theft. They began to spend excessive money and became indifferent to the financial consequences for other family members. They had many quarrels with their spouse, who believed the patient had an extramarital relationship. Two years later (P‐4), they became indifferent to their children, apathetic, irritable, and neglectful of family needs and their own hygiene. Over the next 2 years, they exhibited strange behaviors, including taking a shower with their clothes on, cleaning clothes with dirty water, and hanging dirty clothes to dry. Four years after symptom onset (P‐2), cognitive issues such as memory difficulties and attention deficits became evident, and speech worsened. The family sought medical advice 6 years after symptom onset (P). 9 At the exam, the patient spoke quickly with syntactic errors and used incorrect words. They attributed their situation to “depression” because they moved to the suburbs. They also had memory difficulties and executive dysfunction. They scored 18 on the MMSE and had great deficiencies on social cognition tests. An MRΙ scan revealed mild bifrontal atrophy, patches of white matter signal abnormality in the frontal lobes on FLAIR images, and prominent parietooccipital cortical ribboning on DWI and FLAIR images (Figure 1E ). An 18 F‐FDG PET revealed frontal hypometabolism (Figure 2D ), and 99m Tc‐hexamethylene‐propylene amine oxime (HMPAO) SPECT showed mainly frontal but also temporal and parietal hypoperfusion (Figure 2E ). CSF tests were normal, including tau protein, phosphorylated tau, β‐amyloid, and 14‐3‐3 protein. An ECG and a muscle biopsy were normal. Memantine and a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) were prescribed, and their cognition seemed steady and their behavioral profile improved for a year (P+1). After that, their symptoms deteriorated rapidly. Two years later (P+3), they repeated the same words, laughed without reason, and could not understand anything but their name; their MMSE score was 4. They became bedridden 13 years after onset (P+7). The patient remains alive 22 years after onset (P+16). 3.2 Pre‐symptomatic carriers Both Carriers 1 and 2 are right‐handed and in their 20s without any neurological or psychiatric signs suggestive of neurodegenerative diseases. Neuroimaging did not show cerebral atrophy nor abnormal cortical diffusion restriction (Figure 1F,G ). 3.3 Genetic findings Targeted, exome, or whole‐genome sequencing of the seven cases revealed a heterozygosity for VCP c.1184A &gt; G, p.Asp395Gly ( NM_007126.5 ) in all seven cases. Cases 1, 2, and 5 were negative for other known neurodegenerative disease pathogenic variants including GRN , MAPT , C9orf72 , and PRNP . The pedigrees of the U.S. and Greek kindreds were previously reported in Darwich et al., (2020). 5 The pedigree for the Japanese kindred is shown in Figure 2F where VCP p.Asp395Gly was absent in their unaffected parent and sibling. 3.4 Pathological findings A brain autopsy was performed on Case 2 and a brain biopsy was obtained from Case 5, as described in Darwich et al., (2020). 5 Subsequently, brain autopsies were performed for Cases 1 and 3. The neuropathologic findings for these cases are summarized in Table 2 and Figure 3 . 3.4.1 Case 1 The autopsy was performed 24 h after death. The brain weighed 1215 g. Gross examination revealed bilateral and symmetrical frontal lobar atrophy (Figure 3A ). Atrophy of the subcortical nuclei, limbic regions, and brainstem was not apparent. The substantia nigra (SN) was well pigmented, and the locus coeruleus (LC) was moderately depigmented. There was no evidence of infarction, arteriosclerosis, hemorrhage, or tumor. Histologically, H&amp;E staining revealed a pronounced neuronal loss and gliosis in the frontal neocortex (Figure 3B , upper‐left), cingulate gyrus, and insular cortex, and to a lesser extent in limbic regions including the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala. Sparse free melanin was present with mild neuronal loss in the SN and LC. Other subcortical and brainstem regions were only mildly affected (Table 2 ). NFTs were readily evident on H&amp;E stain (Figure 3B , arrows) and were distributed mostly in the regions exhibiting neurodegeneration. Neocortical NFTs were often flame‐shaped like those in Alzheimer's disease (AD), with some globose‐shaped NFTs in the nucleus basalis of Meynert (nbM), amygdala, SN, and LC. The most striking feature of this case was a marked microvacuolization involving most prominently the occipital neocortex, although neuronal loss and gliosis were not readily evident there (Figure 3B , right column). Vacuoles appeared clear and devoid of storage material and mainly involved layers II and III, with relative sparing of the other cortical layers. The vacuoles were also found in the other gray matter regions, such as the insular cortex, ambient and parahippocampal gyri, and medial parts of the amygdala, with relatively few vacuoles in the frontal and parietal cortices. Immunostaining with the PHF‐1 antibody revealed abundant phosphorylated tau‐positive NFTs and neuropil threads in the frontal and parietal neocortices (Figure 3B , middle, arrows), amygdala, ambient gyrus, nbM, thalamus, SN, LC, and pontine nuclei, with moderate involvement of the temporal and insular cortex and hippocampal CA1‐2 regions. Rare tau inclusions were present in the putamen, globus pallidus, medulla, and cerebellum (Figure 3B ; Table 1 ). Neocortical tau deposits were seen in layers II, III, and V with a pronounced superficial preference. Pretangles were also seen but were not very frequent. Some neuronal cytoplasmic inclusions were complex with the pre‐tangle like staining of the perikarya admixed with unevenly rounded, dense/compact aggregates (Figure 3B , arrowhead). Glial tau inclusions were not evident. β‐amyloid staining revealed a mild to moderate burden of neocortical diffuse amyloid plaques in addition to scattered amyloid‐encrusted ghost tangles (Amyloid Thal phase 2, A1; CERAD 0, C0) (Figure 3E ). Immunostaining for phosphorylated α‐synuclein and TDP‐43 was negative throughout the brain. Hippocampal sclerosis was not seen, and mild arteriosclerosis was noted. 3.4.2 Case 2 The brain weighed 1090 g. Macroscopic observation revealed marked atrophy of the frontal lobe with milder atrophy of the temporal lobe. The pigmentation of SN and LC was normal and there was no evidence of infarction, arteriosclerosis, hemorrhage, or tumor. Multiple white matter foci exhibited grey discoloration up to 0.9 cm in the greatest dimension consistent with the clinical history of MS. Histologically, the overall findings including neurofibrillary tau pathology and vacuoles were similar to Case 1 with less extensive tau deposition in the basal ganglia and brainstem. H&amp;E staining showed severe neuronal loss and gliosis most pronounced in the frontal neocortex, followed by the cingulate gyrus, caudate nucleus, insular cortex, and LC (Figure 3C upper‐left). Temporal and parietal neocortices and limbic regions were mildly degenerated. The LC showed some free melanin (Figure 3C upper‐left). NFTs were found in the neocortical, limbic, and brainstem regions. Vacuoles were slightly more noticeable in temporal and parietal neocortices compared to Case 1 and were also found in the insular cortex, ambient gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, medial parts of the amygdala, and putamen. PHF‐1‐positive NFTs, pretangles, and neuropil threads were mainly found in the superficial layers of frontal, temporal, and parietal neocortices, cingulate gyrus, insular cortex, subiculum, amygdala, ambient gyrus, and LC. Neuronal cytoplasmic inclusions with pre‐tangle like staining of the perikarya admixed with dense/compact aggregates were present (Figure 3C upper‐right). Tauopathy was less severe in the basal ganglia and thalamus with only rare NFTs in the pons, medulla, cerebellum, and spinal cord (Figure 3C ). The accumulated tau was also positive for RD3 (three‐repeat tau), RD4 (four‐repeat tau), and GT‐38 (3R+4R specific epitope; Figure 3C ). Ghost tangles were mostly negative upon RD4 staining, while they were weakly stained with RD3. Glial tau inclusions were not evident. Gallyas silver staining showed a similar neuronal tau aggregate distribution (Figure 3C ). β‐amyloid, α‐synuclein, and TDP‐43 were negative throughout the brain. Hippocampal sclerosis was not seen and mild arteriosclerosis was noted. In addition, multiple demyelinating plaques were identified in the periventricular and subcortical white matter, highlighted by Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining with adjacent GFAP‐positive reactive astrocytes and CD68‐positive macrophages (Figure 3D ). Phosphorylated neurofilament confirmed the relative preservation of axons within the demyelinating plaques (data not shown). 3.4.3 Case 3 The brain weighed 938 g. Macroscopic observation revealed marked atrophy of the frontal and temporal lobes (Figure 3A ). The SN was well pigmented, and the LC was mildly depigmented. There was no macroscopic evidence of infarction, arteriosclerosis, hemorrhage, or tumor. Histologically, H&amp;E staining revealed neurodegeneration and vacuoles overall similarly distributed as in the other two cases, but the entorhinal region was more severely degenerated in this case (Table 2 ). The SN and LC showed some free melanin. PHF‐1‐positive NFTs, pretangles, and neuropil threads also exhibited similar distribution to the other cases, with most prominent in the superficial layers of frontotemporal cortices and limbic regions and less in the basal ganglia and brainstem regions, but also with very rare staining in the cerebellar Purkinje cell processes (Figure 3 lower‐left, Table 2 ). The hippocampal and entorhinal region tau pathology was more severe compared to the other autopsied cases. Some neuronal cytoplasmic tau inclusions presented a distinct compact appearance, which was most noticeable in the medial temporal lobe (Figure 3C ). β‐amyloid, α‐synuclein, and TDP‐43 were negative. Hippocampal sclerosis was not seen, and a small chronic infarct (1.1 mm) was noted in the putamen. Additionally, acute meningoencephalitis was present, affecting the cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord, consistent with acute bacterial meningitis. Scattered eosinophilic neurons were observed across the neocortical, hippocampal, brainstem, and cerebellar regions, consistent with acute anoxic/ischemic encephalopathy. 3.4.4 Case 5 A brain biopsy was performed 8 years after the onset of the disease, and abundant phosphorylated tau‐positive NFTs were revealed by PHF1 and Gallyas silver staining, as reported elsewhere. 5 The small biopsy sample precluded additional analyses. 4 DISCUSSION Here we present detailed clinical, radiological, and neuropathological features of five VT cases across three kindreds to better define the features that distinguish VT with the pathogenic c.1184A &gt; G, p.Asp395Gly VCP variant relative to other types of FTD. This includes descriptions of brain autopsies of Case 1 and Case 3, which overall support that VT is a novel disease entity characterized by abundant neurofibrillary degeneration and microvacuolization. These findings recapitulate previously reported brain autopsy and biopsy results for Cases 2 and 5. 5 Although the tau accumulation in VT brains was consistent with the impaired tau disaggregation due to p.Asp395Gly VCP in both in vitro and in vivo experimental models, 5 the mechanism underlying vacuole formation is still unknown. NFTs and neuropil threads were observed, microscopically similar to those seen in AD with immunoreactivity to RD3, RD4, and GT‐38. 10 , 11 The frontal predominance of tauopathy together with the sparsity of β‐amyloid deposits 12 somewhat resembles the “hippocampal‐sparing” subtype of AD but is distinct enough to allow the classification of VT as a unique neuropathologic entity, particularly given the preferential aggregation of tau affecting superficial neocortical layers. In addition to NFTs, close examination revealed occasional neuronal cytoplasmic tau inclusions with a more rounded, compact appearance. An additional case has recently been described that exhibits clinicopathological features described here. 13 , 14 The distribution and severity of tauopathy correlated well with the severe behavioral and language cognitive FTD syndromes seen in this entity. 6 Although genetic testing of affected parents in each kindred has not been conducted, the pattern of symptomatic individuals suggests that the disease follows an autosomal dominant inheritance. Several pathogenic variants within MAPT , PSEN1 , PSEN2 , APP , and LRRK2 have been reported to cause an autosomal‐dominant tauopathy. 15 The known pathogenic loci in MAPT were negative in Cases 1, 2, and 5, and our cases’ clinical and pathological features are inconsistent with the above variant cases due to the lack of evident onset of amnesia or Parkinsonism. Cases 1, 2, and 5 also tested negative for GRN and C9orf72 , the other major pathogenic variants of FTLD that accumulate phosphorylated TDP‐43. With regard to Case 2, comorbid MS can cause diffuse brain atrophy and cognitive decline 16 but this individual demonstrated progressive dementia in the absence of MS relapses. The asymmetric atrophy observed in this case is also atypical for MS‐related atrophy. Thus, the p.Asp395Gly VCP variant and resultant tauopathy appeared to be responsible for downstream FTD clinical phenotypes. We note that neurodegeneration/tau deposition seems to be largely inversely proportional to microvacuolization. The mechanism underlying this distributional pattern is unknown. DWI hyperintensities in several VT cases were observed, consisting of a ribbon‐like appearance along the posterior cortical line, which roughly matched the vacuole distribution in the autopsied cases. A similar finding has been a known biomarker for cortical vacuolization seen in prion disease. However, there was some variability in the extent of cortical hyperintensity on diffusion‐weighted imaging, perhaps due to the evolution of microvacuolization over the course of disease and/or due to technical differences between imaging sessions (scanner field strength and sequence parameters for diffusion imaging). Despite this variability, cortical ribboning may be an auxiliary finding in support of the diagnosis of VT. Additionally, there is relatively limited research on 18 F‐flortaucipir uptake in clinical FTD and pathologically confirmed FTLD‐tau cases. 17 , 18 The progressive increase of tracer uptake in a frontotemporal distribution in Case 1 suggests 18 F‐flortaucipir is able to detect tauopathy in some subtypes of FTLD‐tau. Our VT cases exhibited atypical/mixed FTD, which either fulfilled the bvFTD criteria fully (in Cases 1, 3, and 5) or partially (in Cases 2 and 4), 6 with relatively early visuospatial and/or memory deficits in Cases 2 and 3 (Table S2 ). Several of these cases were characterized by various language symptoms that spanned several PPA subcategories, suggesting different involvement of the dominant frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices. Case 1 had initial mild difficulty with repetition, followed a few years later by impaired fluency and word comprehension, leading to the diagnosis of mixed PPA. These symptoms suggest dysfunction of the left posterior perisylvian/inferior parietal, inferior frontal/superior temporal, and anterior temporal lobes, respectively, consistent with widespread tau deposition in the left frontal, temporal, and parietal regions (Figure 2A ). 19 , 20 , 21 Case 2 exhibited impaired naming and word comprehension with initially preserved fluency similar to semantic variant PPA (svPPA). 7 Although this suggests left temporal lobar dysfunction, this case exhibited a somewhat left‐dominant frontal hypometabolism (Figure 2B ). Case 4, in which language symptoms were relatively mild, appeared to develop symmetrical cortical atrophy (Figure 1D ) with mildly right‐worse‐than‐left frontal perfusion changes (Figure 2C ). Thus, VT can exhibit subtly different clinical FTD phenotypes, perhaps due to some heterogeneity in terms of laterality. Current pharmacologic interventions for FTD are generally limited to symptomatic measures. In Cases 1, 2, and 5, memantine, an anti‐dementia drug for AD, and/or SSRIs were prescribed, although their effect on their cognition was unclear. Their sedative effect on behavioral disturbances was insufficient, requiring the addition of antipsychotics in Cases 1 and 2. Psychiatric symptoms such as anxiety and concomitant/prodromal depression were also prominent among our cases, presumably due to neurodegeneration, 22 , 23 limiting the effectiveness of psychotropics. As VCP p.Asp395Gly is associated with a partial loss of VCP function, VCP activation represents a potential novel therapeutic for tauopathy and other neurodegenerative disease proteinopathies. 24 This study was conducted with a small number of cases. Thus, we cannot be certain of the prevalence or time course of the reported imaging and neuropathological findings in VT. In addition, imaging was performed with different protocols and scanners and at different stages of disease between cases, which could account for some variability in imaging findings. Therefore, it is critical to further confirm these findings with additional cases, including more longitudinal data. 5 CONCLUSIONS VT due to VCP p.Asp395Gly is a devastating early‐onset FTD clinical syndrome with unique radiologic and pathologic features. This report expands the spectrum of recognized subtypes of FTLD and highlights the need to identify genetic etiologies for cases with early‐onset FTD. Further study of additional cases harboring the same pathogenic VCP variant will help elucidate the clinicopathologic evolution of disease in VT in order to better inform efforts to develop mechanism‐based, disease‐modifying therapies.
