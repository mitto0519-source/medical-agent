To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Background Insulin response may significantly contribute to the formation of kidney stones. Diets can modulate the insulin response and we hypothesize that high insulinemic potential diets may increase the kidney stones risk. Methods Data were from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Diets were assessed by 24-hour dietary recall. Two empirical dietary indices for insulin resistance (EDIR) and hyperinsulinemia (EDIH) were used to reflect the dietary insulinemic potential. Diagnosis of kidney stones was based on self-report. Logistic regression was employed to calculate ORs and 95% CIs while adjusting for variables identified through a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Results Higher EDIR [OR Tertile 3 vs. Tertile 1 = 1.31 (95% CI: 1.13–1.53); OR Per−standard deviation increase = 1.11 (95% CI: 1.05–1.18); p trend = 0.001] and EDIH [OR Tertile 3 vs. Tertile 1 = 1.26 (95% CI: 1.08–1.47); OR Per−standard deviation increase = 1.10 (95% CI: 1.04–1.16); p trend = 0.001] scores were both positively associated with kidney stones. The conclusion remains unchanged in the sensitivity analysis after adjusting for potential mediating factors that were identified from the DAG, including BMI, hypertension, and diabetes. Subgroup analysis showed that results in most subgroups were consistent with the main analysis. Conclusions This study indicates that the insulinemic potential of diet may partly underlie the influence of dietary patterns on kidney stones, emphasizing the importance of avoiding dietary patterns with insulinemic potential. In the United States (US), approximately 11.0% of adults are affected by this condition [ 1 ]. If kidney stones are not treated promptly, they may lead to hydronephrosis and damage kidney function, ultimately resulting in renal dysfunction, which can severely affect the patient’s quality of life and overall health [ 3 ]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify modifiable risk factors for preventing kidney stones. Evidence suggests that insulin resistance and/or hyperinsulinemia may play significant roles in kidney stones formation [ 4 – 9 ], and diets can modulate insulin response [ 10 , 11 ]. Therefore, we hypothesized that a high insulinemic potential diet is associated with an increased risk of kidney stones. Recently, Tabung et al. [ 12 ] developed and validated two empirical dietary indices for insulin resistance (EDIR) and hyperinsulinemia (EDIH) to identify the foods most predictive of each. Higher scores on these two indices are associated with an increased risk of a multitude of health outcomes, including hypertension [ 13 ], diabetes [ 13 ], hepatic steatosis [ 14 ], or chronic kidney disease [ 15 ]. However, there are currently no studies assessing the relationship between these two dietary patterns and kidney stones. Furthermore, the two indices developed by Tabung et al. were constructed and validated in three cohort populations in the US, where most participants were Caucasian health professionals, which limits their generalizability to the overall US population [ 12 ]. Additionally, the baseline prevalence of kidney stones in these cohorts (4.5%) [ 16 ] was lower than the 8.8% prevalence reported in a large, nationally representative cross-sectional survey in the US, known as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [ 2 ]. Furthermore, factors such as body mass index (BMI), race, sex, and age influence the prevalence of kidney stones [ 1 , 2 ]. Therefore, we utilized the two indices previously reconstructed and validated within the NHANES [ 14 , 17 ] to assess the impact of EDIR and EDIH on the prevalence of kidney stones in the NHANES population. Methods Study population This study used data from the US NHANES 2007–2018. NHANES is a cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the noninstitutionalized US population. It enrolls a nationally representative sample of about 5,000 participants each year by a complex multistage probability sampling method. NHANES is a publicly available dataset, and details on the design and data collection methods can be found on the NHANES website: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm . The study protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board (Protocol #2005-06, #2011-17, and #2018-01). Each participant provided written informed consent before participation. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. A total of 59, 842 people participated in the NHANES from 2007 to 2018. Participants were excluded from the analysis if they were less than 20 years of age ( n = 25, 072), did not report whether ever had kidney stones ( n = 91), had no dietary data ( n = 3, 963), or had implausible energy intake (&lt; 600 kcal/day or &gt; 3500 kcal/day for women, &lt; 800 kcal/day or &gt; 4200 kcal/day for men, n = 1, 220) [ 18 , 19 ]. Finally, 29, 496 participants were included in this study (Fig. 1 ). Dietary assessment and dietary pattern indices For the NHANES 2007–2018, dietary intake was assessed by two 24-hour dietary recall interviews. The first interview was conducted in person at the Mobile Examination Center, while the second was conducted by telephone 3 to 10 days later. Participants with at least one recall were included in the current analysis. The Automated Multiple Pass Method was employed, a method that has been shown by many studies to reduce measurement error in dietary assessments, improve the accuracy of dietary measurements, and lessen the burden on populations. Details on the construction and validation of EDIH and EDIR were described in our previous articles [ 14 , 17 ]. We first defined 39 common food groups (Supplementary Table 1 ) based on the similarity in nutrient profiles and culinary usage. For EDIH, we first used the reduced rank regression (RRR) method, with fasting insulin and C-peptide as the response variables and these food groups as predictor variables. Then, the first factor (i.e., the identified RRR dietary pattern) identified by RRR was used as the dependent variable, while the 39 food groups served as independent variables, employing stepwise linear regression for further data reduction to identify the food components that best reflect the RRR dietary pattern. The statistically significant factors identified by the stepwise linear regression were the food categories of EDIH, and the regression coefficients of these factors served as the weights for these food categories. Finally, the EDIH score was calculated by summing the weighted contributions of the respective food groups. For EDIR, we directly applied stepwise linear regression for its construction because only the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) could be used in NHANES [ 14 , 17 ]. Similarly, the regression coefficients of the statistically significant food groups determined by stepwise linear regression were served as the weights of these food groups. Supplementary Table 2 presents the food components and weights of EDIR and EDIH [ 14 , 17 ]. EDIR encompassed 18 food components, with six (processed meat, red meat, and potatoes, etc.) being positively associated, while 12 (leafy green vegetables, dark yellow vegetables, and coffee, etc.) were inversely associated with HOMA-IR. EDIH included 13 food groups, with four (processed meat, potatoes, low-energy carbonated drinks, etc.) being positively associated, while nine (coffee, nuts, fruit juice, etc.) were inversely associated with fasting insulin and C-peptide. The ability of EDIR and EDIH to predict insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia has been validated [ 17 ]. Assessment of covariates A directed acyclic graph (DAG) drawn using DAGitty v3.1 was used to determine the minimal sufficient adjustment sets for estimating the total effect of EDIR and EDIH on kidney stones [ 20 ]. Supplementary Table 3 was the process of determining potential confounders. According to the DAG (Fig. 2 ), we should adjust for sex, age, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, family income, physical activity, smoking, total energy intake, water intake, dietary calcium, and protein intake. Data on demographic and lifestyle factors were collected through household interviews. Family income was evaluated using the income-to-poverty ratio. Physical activity was measured in terms of metabolic equivalent task hours per week (MET-h/week). BMI was calculated using the formula weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Hypertension was diagnosed based on self-reported assessment, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes was diagnosed based on self-reported assessment, a hemoglobin A1c level of 6.5% or higher, or a fasting glucose level of 126 mg/dL. Data on water intake, dietary calcium, and protein intake were obtained from 24-hour dietary recall interviews. Measurement of kidney stones NHANES utilized a CAPI system, administered by trained interviewers, to gather information on kidney stone history from participants at home. This survey targeted individuals aged 20 and older, resulting in the creation of the Kidney Conditions-Urology Files. We identified kidney stones from one question in these data files, where participants were asked, “Have you ever had kidney stones?” Those who answered “yes” were classified as having a history of kidney stones. During the period from 2007 to 2014 in NHANES, participants were subsequently asked “How many times have you passed a kidney stone?”. We considered those who reported passing a stone at least once as having symptomatic kidney stones [ 2 ], since self-reporting is valid for patients with symptomatic kidney stones [ 21 ]. Statistical analysis All statistical analyses in this study were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), accounting for the sampling weights, clustering, and stratification to ensure nationally representative estimates. Age-adjusted characteristics of participants were presented. Continuous variables were presented as means (standard deviation). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Chi-square test was used for categorical variables, and the analysis of variance was used for continuous variables. In addition, weighted percentages or means (with standard error) from a complex sampling analysis were estimated. Taylor series linearization method recommended by NHANES was used to estimate standard error. Additionally, the Rao-Scott F adjusted Chi-square statistic was utilized to test for differences in categorical variables. For the continuous variables, the Wald F test was used. Logistic regression models were used to assess the association between EDIH and EDIR and kidney stones. We presented odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on dietary scores analyzed both categorically as tertiles and continuously for each standard deviation increment. Three models were constructed: model 1 was unadjusted, model 2 was adjusted for age and sex, and model 3 was adjusted for covariates identified by the DAG. An indicator variable was created for missing data of each covariate. Tests for linear trends were performed by treating EDIH and EDIR as a continuous variable. The potential non-linear association between EDIH and EDIR and kidney stones was evaluated using restricted cubic splines with knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations stratified by the potential subgroups, including age (&lt; 65, ≥ 65 years), sex (female, male), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic white, others), marital status (married, others), education level (high school or below, more than high school), physical activity (light, moderate to vigorous), ratio of family income to poverty (&lt; 2.5, ≥ 2.5), smoking status (never smokers, ever smokers), and alcohol drinking (never drinkers, ever drinkers). Wald tests were used to assess the interaction between EDIH and EDIR and the subgroup. Additionally, several sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the stability of results. First, we further adjusted for BMI, hypertension, and diabetes, which were potential mediating variables in the DAG. Second, to avoid over-adjustment [ 14 ], alcohol consumption was not considered when drawing the DAG, as wine, beer, and/or liquor are components of EDIR and EDIH. To assess the impact of alcohol on the outcomes, we made additional adjustments for alcohol consumption. Third, we employed multiple imputation to handle missing variables, which generated 25 data sets. The results were combined according to the Rubin method [ 22 ]. Fourth, we excluded the participants with missing values to evaluate the impact of missing data on our findings. Fifth, we calculated propensity scores and applied four methods of propensity score analysis [ 23 ]: propensity score matching (PSM), inverse probability of treatment weighting, stratification, and adjustment, to assess the associations. To simplify the analysis, we first divided the EDIR and EDIH scores into quintiles. Then, we combined the first three quintiles into one reference group, and finally, the last two quintiles were combined into one “exposure” group. The logistic regression model was used to calculate the propensity score with covariates consistent with the model 3. For the propensity score stratification, we used the quintiles of the estimated propensity score. The propensity score adjustment was performed conducted solely by adjusting for propensity score, which was treated as a continuous covariate. The PSM was conducted using 1:1 nearest neighbor matching (caliper value = 0.02). The standardized mean difference was utilized to evaluate covariate balance, with a value below 0.1 indicating good covariate matching [ 24 ]. Sixth, the associations between EDIR and EDIH and symptomatic kidney stones were analyzed in NHANES from 2007 to 2014. Seventh, we further adjusted for work type and living environment (country of birth, food security, and housing characteristics). Eighth, a sensitivity analysis was conducted on participants with two complete dietary recalls. Finally, we tested whether the effect sizes of EDIH and EDIR scores on the prevalence of kidney stones would increase as the population was divided into more groups, thereby creating a greater disparity in EDIH and EDIR scores between the highest and lowest groups. To achieve this, we divided the population into five, ten, fifteen, and twenty equal-sized groups and examined the changes in OR for the highest group relative to the lowest group. P &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results Characteristics of participants Among the 29, 496 participants, the mean age was 49.88 ± 17.689 years. When these characteristics were standardized to the age distribution, the prevalence of kidney stones was 9.52%. All the variables across the tertiles of EDIR and EDIH were found to be significant, except for age within the tertiles of EDIR (Table 1 ). For the weighted data, the weighted prevalence of kidney stones was 9.71%. Most variables were significant across the tertiles of both EDIR (Supplementary Table 4 ) and EDIH (Supplementary Table 5 ). Overall, participants with higher EDIR and/or EDIH scores had a higher prevalence of kidney stones, diabetes, hypertension, a higher BMI, lower educational attainment, and a lower ratio of family income to poverty. EDIH and EDIR and kidney stones Table 2 indicates that higher scores of EDIR and EDIH were risk factors for kidney stones, regardless of whether in the unadjusted model 1, the basic adjusted model 2, or the model 3 that adjusted for all covariates identified by the DAG. In model 3, compared to the lowest EDIR tertile, individuals in the highest EDIR tertile had an OR of 1.31 (95% CI: 1.13–1.53), and for each standard deviation increase, the OR was 1.11 (95% CI: 1.05–1.18), with a p trend of 0.001. Compared to the lowest EDIH tertile, individuals in the highest EDIH tertile had an OR of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.08–1.47), and for each standard deviation increase, the OR was 1.10 (95% CI: 1.04–1.16), with a p trend of 0.001. When the population was divided into five, ten, fifteen, and twenty equal-sized groups, respectively, with the disparity between the EDIH and EDIR scores of the highest and lowest groups becoming increasingly larger, the OR values also gradually increased (Fig. 3 ). Additionally, restricted cubic spline regression, adjusting for the same potential confounders as in model 3, was performed, and no nonlinear association was found between EDIR ( p for non-linearity = 0.756) or EDIH ( p for non-linearity = 0.870) and kidney stones (Fig. 4 ). The results indicated an almost linear positive association between the prevalence of kidney stones and both EDIR and EDIH scores. Furthermore, the odds of kidney stones monotonically increased with increases in EDIR and EDIH scores. Sensitivity analysis When adjusting for BMI, hypertension, and diabetes individually or simultaneously, the conclusions remained unchanged (Table 3 ). Further adjustment for alcohol consumption, using multiple imputation to handle missing variables, excluding the participants with missing values, further adjustment for work type and living environment, or restricting the analysis to participants with complete two-day 24-hour dietary recalls did not change the results (Supplementary Tables 6 – 10 ). All results obtained from the four propensity score methods were statistically significant (Supplementary Tables 11 and Supplementary Table 12 ). For the PSM, the standardized mean difference of all covariates was &lt; 0.1 after matching (data not shown). The association between EDIR and EDIH and symptomatic kidney stones were also statistically significant (Supplementary Table 13 ). Subgroup analysis The subgroup analysis (Fig. 5 ) showed that results in most subgroups were consistent with the main analysis. We observed that the positive association between EDIR and kidney stones appeared to be stronger in males (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.05–1.23) compared to females (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 0.97–1.19), in individuals with education beyond high school (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.20) versus those with a high school education or less (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.98–1.23), and in ever drinkers (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05–1.21) compared to never drinkers (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.92–1.18). Additionally, the positive association between EDIH and kidney stones seemed more pronounced among married individuals (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.04–1.17) compared to others (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.98–1.21), in those engaging in moderate to vigorous activities (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.19) versus those with light activity (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.99–1.18), and in ever drinkers (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.05–1.18) compared to never drinkers (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.93–1.16). However, no significant interactions were detected, with all p -values for interaction exceeding 0.05 (Fig. 5 ). Discussion Our findings suggested that a higher dietary insulinemic potential, specifically indicated by higher EDIR and EDIH scores, was associated with an increased risk of kidney stones. Further adjustments for BMI, hypertension, and diabetes weakened the associations; however, the overall conclusion remained unchanged. Other sensitivity analyses also supported these positive associations. Several studies have examined associations of indicators for insulin resistance and/or hyperinsulinemia with kidney stone risk [ 4 – 9 , 25 ]. Weinberg et al. [ 5 ] reported that higher HOMA-IR and fasting plasma insulin were associated with a higher risk of kidney stones. Kim et al. [ 6 ] also found an 18% higher risk of kidney stones when comparing men in extreme HOMA-IR tertiles. Cameron et al. [ 7 ] reported that uric acid stone patients without diabetes had higher fasting serum insulin levels compared to normal volunteers. Additionally, Ando et al. [ 8 ] found that both HOMA-IR and serum insulin levels were associated with a history of kidney stones. A recent observational study found that hypercalciuric calcium kidney stone patients had higher levels of HOMA-IR and fasting insulin [ 26 ]. In addition, Shen et al. [ 4 , 25 ] reported that two additional indices of insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome-IR index and triglyceride glucose-body mass index, were associated with a higher prevalence of kidney stones. C-peptide is usually released simultaneously with insulin from β-cells. It has been used to assess hyperinsulinemia [ 12 ] and insulin resistance [ 27 , 28 ]. Furthermore, a recent study found that higher dietary insulin load was associated with higher prevalence of kidney stones [ 29 ]. In calcium kidney stones, approximately 80% are calcium oxalate, while calcium phosphate accounts for about 15%. The mechanisms underlying the formation of calcium kidney stones included low urine pH, hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria, hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, and low urine volume [ 30 ]. In the formation of uric acid stones, the mechanisms primarily included low urine pH, hyperuricosuria, and low urine volume [ 30 ]. Studies have shown that high levels of insulin resistance were associated with lower urinary citrate excretion [ 31 ], possibly due to alterations in the renal tubular transport processes of citrate, Na(+)-K(+), and H(+) [ 31 ]. Citrate is a known inhibitor of calcium stone formation. Additionally, insulin resistance was related to impaired generation of renal ammonium, which reduced ammonium production and urinary ammonium excretion, and insulin resistance levels were negatively correlated with urine pH [ 32 ]. These factors were associated with the formation of uric acid stones and/or calcium oxalate stones [ 32 ]. Furthermore, studies suggested that hyperinsulinemia may enhance renal sodium retention and decrease the urinary clearance of uric acid, which could also lead to a decrease in urine pH [ 33 ]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the impact of higher dietary insulinemic potential on kidney stones. However, there are several limitations in our study. First, using one or two 24-hour dietary recalls may not accurately reflect the usual dietary intake. Second, both diet and kidney stones were self-reported, which may be influenced by recall bias, self-report bias, or social desirability bias. Third, given the cross-sectional design of this study, it cannot establish causation. Fourth, the extrapolation of the study’s conclusions is limited because the subjects were from the US. Fifth, although the DAG was used to select relevant confounding variables, some important confounders, such as dietary oxalate, which may play a role in kidney stone formation, were not available in the NHANES database. Sixth, self-reporting of kidney stones may underestimate the prevalence of kidney stones, particularly by overlooking asymptomatic cases. This could affect the results of this study, as the dietary patterns of asymptomatic kidney stone patients may differ. Although the accuracy of self-reported kidney stones in NHANES is still unclear, two previous studies have validated its reliability among other populations [ 21 , 34 ]. Additionally, in the current study, similar associations were observed between EDIR and EDIH and symptomatic kidney stones. Nevertheless, self-reporting inevitably carries the risk of reporting bias; therefore, future research should utilize objective medical records to further investigate the accuracy and validity of self-reported kidney stones. Finally, information on the chemical composition and types of kidney stones was not available in the NHANES database, preventing further analysis. In summary, our findings suggest that the insulinemic potential of diet may partly underlie the influence of dietary patterns on the occurrence of kidney stones. However, further prospective cohort studies with accurate diagnostic methods for kidney stones are needed for in-depth investigation. Mechanistic studies are also necessary to validate the underlying pathways involved. Electronic supplementary material Below is the link to the electronic supplementary material.
