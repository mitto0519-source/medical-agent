ABSTRACT The DASS‐21 has been widely used in university student mental health research. However, studies on its cross‐cultural reliability and validity, particularly among Eastern student populations, remain limited. This study examined the DASS‐21's psychometric properties and measurement invariance across Australian, Chinese and Vietnamese students. Data were drawn from multiple surveys conducted between 2017 and 2021, comprising 2420 university students from Australia ( n = 1093), China ( n = 626) and Vietnam ( n = 701). Single‐group confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) supported the three‐factor model of the DASS‐21 with acceptable fit for each cultural group, requiring minor modifications for Chinese and Vietnamese data. Multiple‐group CFAs resulted in an 18‐item version (DASS‐18) that achieved cross‐group measurement invariance and acceptable internal consistency. Non‐parametric tests revealed the lowest scores for all the DASS‐18 results among Australian students, while Chinese students reported the highest level of anxiety and Vietnamese students reported the highest level of depression. The two Asian samples exhibited similar levels of stress and overall distress. In general, this study supports the measurement invariance of the DASS, with caution regarding culturally sensitive items. Implications for the DASS‐21's universality and recommendations for future research and multicultural psychological assessment are discussed. Introduction 1.1. The Problem For many young people, higher education can be a fruitful phase of life but also presents significant challenges. Among these, poor mental health stands out as one of the most influential negative outcomes, leading to issues at individual, institutional and societal levels (Ibrahim et al. 2013 ). Poor student mental health is associated with diminished academic performance (Zhang et al. 2024 ), working disabilities (Meckamalil et al. 2022 ), university withdrawal (Zając et al. 2024 ) and an increased risk of suicide (Sheldon et al. 2021 ). Therefore, university student mental health has garnered significant attention globally, prompting researchers worldwide to delve into this critical area. The 21‐item version of the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS‐21), developed by Lovibond and Lovibond ( 1995a , 1995b ) in Australia, is a widely used mental health screening instrument valued for its advantages in university settings (Cao et al. 2023 ). It consists of three subscales and a total score assessing depression, anxiety, stress and overall distress. The measure offers both theoretical and psychometric advantages, contributing to its widespread use in both general and clinical populations (Cowles and Medvedev 2022 ). Despite its popularity, further research is needed to assess the psychometric properties and validity of the DASS‐21 beyond its country of origin, particularly in Asian populations. Although the instrument has been translated into 55 languages (Psychology Foundation of Australia [PFA], n.d. ), its psychometric properties have not been thoroughly examined in Asian contexts. For instance, despite increasing interest in mental health, validation studies on the DASS‐21 in China and Vietnam remain limited, both generally and among university students (see Medvedev 2023 , for a list of validated translations). Moreover, its use in diverse cultural settings has raised concerns about its validity across different populations, as prior studies have produced inconsistent findings, potentially due to cultural variations in how these psychological states are experienced and interpreted (Medvedev 2023 ). Furthermore, recent research suggests that while the DASS‐21 may reliably assess overall distress across populations, its subscales may not be equally stable (Lightburn et al. 2023 ). These limitations highlight the need for further research to assess the cross‐cultural validity and generalisability of the DASS‐21, particularly in Asian contexts. To ensure the instrument's effectiveness across different cultural populations (e.g., Australian, Chinese and Vietnamese students) and facilitate meaningful cross‐cultural comparisons, it is suggested that researchers examine its measurement qualities and statistical invariance across the relevant cultures (Meredith 1993 ). The recommended and popular method for testing measurement invariance (MI) includes tests of configural, metric and scalar invariance (Fischer et al. 2025 ). Successful establishment of configural, metric and scalar invariance across cultures means it shows similar estimated parameters, strengths of factor loadings and group mean scores, respectively, among those cultural groups. For cross‐group comparison of psychological structures being measured, satisfactory metric invariance is the minimum requirement (Fischer et al. 2025 ). 1.2. The Present Study The primary objective of this research was to test the DASS‐21's psychometric properties and MI among university students from three Asia‐Pacific countries: Australia, China and Vietnam. The selection of university student groups from these countries allows an in‐depth investigation into the DASS‐21's cross‐cultural validity. Australian students, as representatives of the country where the DASS‐21 was developed, provide a reference point for how the scale functions in a WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic) society. Meanwhile, Chinese and Vietnamese students, from collectivist cultures shaped by Confucian values, offer a distinct contrast to the Australian context. While China and Vietnam share cultural similarities, differences in historical trajectories, modernisation and mental health policies may lead to variations in how students respond to the DASS‐21. The inclusion of both Chinese and Vietnamese students enables a more sophisticated examination of intra‐Asian variability beyond the traditional Eastern–Western dichotomy. Prior research suggests that cultural differences shape how depression, anxiety and stress are experienced and reported in these populations (Lightburn et al. 2023 ). In China and Vietnam, emotional distress is more likely to be somatised, with symptoms expressed through physical complaints such as headaches or fatigue rather than cognitive‐affective terms (Ryder et al. 2008 ). This tendency may influence the factor structure of the DASS‐21, potentially affecting its ability to capture psychological distress in Eastern populations. Additionally, academic pressure and expectations vary across cultures. Chinese and Vietnamese students face intense pressure to succeed academically due to familial and societal expectations (Thai et al. 2021 ; Zheng et al. 2023 ), whereas Australian students, within a more individualistic context, may experience stress linked to personal independence (Humphrey and Bliuc 2021 ). These differences highlight the importance of examining cross‐cultural MI before comparing mental health scores across student populations. Previous studies have examined the traditional three‐factor structure of the DASS‐21 (correlated depression, anxiety and stress; Lovibond and Lovibond 1995a , 1995b ) and the single‐factor structure (general negative affectivity) in both Western and Asian samples, yielding relatively inconsistent results regarding which model is more optimal (Cao et al. 2023 ). Therefore, this study will evaluate both the three‐factor and one‐factor models. Based on results from existing research involving student and non‐student populations, it is hypothesised that the instrument will demonstrate satisfactory psychometric properties across three cultural samples and achieve at least metric invariance for the three‐factor model across these groups. 2. Methods 2.1. Participants University students from Australia, China and Vietnam participated in the study. The Australian sample comprised data collected from three online studies involving students at an Australian university, conducted from 2017 to 2019. In total, there were 1093 participants (male = 379, female = 714, not specified = 2), with ages ranging from 17 to 30 years ( M = 20.15, SD = 2.69). The Chinese sample consisted of data from two online studies conducted between 2017 and 2019, with 626 students (female = 438, male = 174, not specified = 14) from three Chinese universities. Ages ranged from 17 to 23 years ( M = 19.07, SD = 0.90). The Vietnamese sample included data collected from 2018 to 2021 through an online study involving 701 students from three Vietnamese universities (female = 518, male = 174, not specified = 9). Ages ranged from 18 to 30 years ( M = 19.25, SD = 1.44). 2.2. Measures Each survey consisted of different sets of questionnaires exploring such issues as personality, relationships and psychological health. For the current study, only basic demographic and DASS‐21 data are reported. The DASS‐21 is a 21‐item version of the original 42‐item DASS scale (Lovibond and Lovibond 1995a , 1995b ) and evaluates the three domains of depression, anxiety and stress with three respective sets of seven items. The DASS‐21's reliability and validity have been established across Western and Asian cultures (Wang et al. 2016 ; Zanon et al. 2021 ), with Cronbach's α coefficients for the subscales ranging from 0.80 to 0.97. For the Vietnamese sample, a Vietnamese version of the DASS‐21 was developed. Items were translated into Vietnamese by a native speaker and back‐translated by an independent Vietnamese academic fluent in English. Differences between the original and translated items were discussed, and necessary revisions were made to ensure accuracy and comprehensibility. A similar process was employed to generate a Chinese version of the DASS‐21. The translation process involved local mental health professionals, who provided input on the cultural relevance and clarity of the items. 2.3. Procedures The survey was administered via the Qualtrics platform and promoted through visual advertisements, emails, Facebook posts and lecture presentations across all participating countries. Participants provided informed consent before completing the survey at their convenience. Recruitment methods were adapted to align with institutional and cultural contexts in each location. In Australia, participants were recruited through an online research participation system (SONA), where psychology students voluntarily enrolled and received course credit for their participation. In China and Vietnam, participants accessed the Qualtrics survey via a direct link provided in advertisements and were given the option to enter a prize draw upon completion of the survey as an incentive. The study received ethical approval from the University of Newcastle Human Research Ethics Committee and the Chinese Academy of Sciences. 2.4. Analysis Data were prepared and analysed with SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp. 2021 ), and then confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) and MI testing were conducted with Mplus version 8 (Muthén and Muthén 2017 ). The fit of one‐factor and three‐factor measurement models was evaluated separately for each group first. When the measurement models did not provide an acceptable fit, minor modifications were made to produce a parsimonious, best‐fitting solution. Then, MI testing was conducted across the samples. Because the DASS‐21 has only four response categories for each item, the weighted least square mean and variance adjusted estimator (WLSMV) was employed for all CFAs. For model fit evaluation, for each analysis, the chi‐square, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) are reported. Values greater than 0.95 for the CFI and TLI, and less than 0.06 for the RMSEA indicate acceptable model fit (Hu and Bentler 1999 ). We applied the commonly employed guide that differences in fit indices of &gt; 0.009 for the CFI and TLI indicate meaningful differences in model fit for nested models (Schermelleh‐Engel et al. 2003 ). Establishing MI between samples includes examining their factorial structures, followed by a series of steps of increasingly restrictive tests for invariance (Milfont and Fischer 2010 ). The first step evaluated evidence for configural invariance, the requirement of which is that the number of factors and the items that load onto each factor are similar across groups. The next step is the evaluation of metric invariance, which involves comparing the fit of the configural invariance model to the fit of a nested model in which the factor loadings are constrained equally across groups. The absence of meaningful decrements in fit between the configural model and the more constrained metric invariance model suggests metric invariance. Finally, scalar invariance was evaluated by adding constraints on item intercepts and comparing model fit to the MI model. As previously, if there is no meaningful decrement in fit from the metric model, then scalar invariance is supported. 3. Results 3.1. Measurement invariance of the DASS‐21 CFA results indicated that the one‐factor model did not produce an acceptable fit to the data for any of the three samples (Table 1 ). For the Australian data, an unmodified three‐factor model was an acceptable fit based on the RMSEA, CFI and TLI results. For the Chinese sample data, the initial three‐factor solution was a marginal fit, and two modifications were indicated. Item 13 was added as an indicator to the stress latent variable (∆CFI = 0.011). Then, the covariance between the residuals for items 1 and 12 was freed (∆CFI = 0.013) resulting in an acceptable fit (three‐factor model 2). For the Vietnamese sample, the unmodified three‐factor solution was a marginal fit. Based on modification indices, the covariance between the residuals for items 18 and 11 was freed (three‐factor mod 1). This resulted in a slight improvement in model fit (∆CFI = 0.011). A further modification freeing the covariance between items 4 and 19 was indicated, and a further small improvement in fit resulted (∆CFI = 0.004). The final model (three‐factor model 2) is an acceptable fit. To evaluate fit across the three samples, a multi‐group CFA was conducted that incorporated the model modifications from the above analyses (Table 2 ). The configural model was an acceptable fit indicating that the structure across the three samples is supported. However, the metric model fit deteriorated significantly based on ∆CFI (−0.020), indicating that the item weightings across the samples were not invariant. To determine which items were producing problematic weightings across the samples, the factor loadings from the unmodified CFAs for each sample were examined (Table 3 ). Items 2 and 7 from the Anxiety subscale were identified as having loadings of less than 0.5 for the Vietnamese and Chinese samples (item 2: 0.414 and 0.412; item 7: 0.421 and 0.470, respectively). Item 14 was also found to load only at 0.420 on the Stress scale for the Vietnamese sample. Based on these relatively low loadings, these three items were removed from the analyses, and a multi‐group invariance test was conducted on the remaining items (Table 4 ). The configural model was found to be an acceptable fit, and the metric model produced a minor deterioration in CFI (∆CFI &lt; 0.01), supporting metric non‐invariance. The scalar model compared to the metric model also produced a ∆CFI of less than 0.01, supporting scalar non‐invariance. Reliabilities for the 18‐item version of the DASS are presented in Table 5 . Because of violations in assumptions, parametric tests were not advisable. Thus, non‐parametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis) were conducted to test differences between national samples with respect to the subscales and total scores of the DASS‐18. Post hoc pairwise tests with Bonferroni adjustment ( p &lt; 0.019) were used to compare all pairs of national groups. Differences between genders within each country were examined using two‐sided, independent t ‐tests with equal variances not assumed. Results indicate significant differences across the samples with respect to Depression, H (2) = 102.544, p &lt; 0.001; Anxiety, H (2) = 37.925, p &lt; 0.001; Stress, H (2) = 58.980, p &lt; 0.001; and Total DASS‐18, H (2) = 69.508, p &lt; 0.001. For Depression, all groups were significantly different from each other, with Vietnamese students reporting the highest levels, followed by the Chinese, then Australian students. For Anxiety, all groups were significantly different from each other, with Chinese students reporting the highest levels, followed by the Vietnamese, then Australian students. For Stress, the Vietnamese and Chinese groups were not different from each other ( p = 0.131), but both were higher than the Australian students. Similarly, for the total DASS‐18 score, the Vietnamese and Chinese groups were not different ( p = 0.108), but both were higher than the Australians. The only gender differences were for Australian students, with females reporting higher levels of Anxiety, t (896.91) = 6.39, p &lt; 0.001; Stress, t (845.43) = 7.17, p &lt; 0.001; and Distress, t (808.69) = 4.65, p &lt; 0.001. 4. Discussion 4.1. Important Findings The present study investigated the DASS‐21's psychometric properties and MI among Australian, Chinese and Vietnamese university students. For each sample, the one‐factor model was not supported, while the three‐factor model, with minor modifications, produced an acceptable fit. A multi‐group CFA showed that satisfactory MI across the samples was not established. However, the removal of problematic items (items 2, 7 and 14) produced satisfactory invariance at the scalar level for an 18‐item version (DASS‐18) of the DASS‐21. Overall, the new version of the instrument produced acceptable internal consistencies globally and for each subscale. 4.2. Confirmatory Factor Analyses The single‐group CFA results for each sample were in line with previous findings on this topic. Specifically, the primary three‐factor model showed an acceptable fit to the Australian data without modifications. For the Chinese sample, a study of over 13,000 university students in mainland China also reported an acceptable fit for a three‐factor model but with no modifications (Lu et al. 2018 ). Similar findings were also shown in other studies by Chan et al. ( 2012 ) and Wang et al. ( 2016 ) but with some modifications. In those two studies, item 13 changed completely from Depression to the Stress subscale (Chan et al. 2012 ; Wang et al. 2016 ), while in the current study, it loaded onto both Depression and Stress scales for the Chinese data. For the Vietnamese sample, Lan et al. ( 2020 ) also supported a modified three‐factor model of the DASS‐21 for their university student sample. Interestingly, they also reported a well‐fitting 18‐item version of the DASS‐21 with item profile (items 2, 4 and 7 removed) somewhat similar to our final version of DASS‐18. Results from our multi‐group CFAs failed to support MI across the three samples, suggesting that the DASS‐21's use for cross‐cultural comparisons may be problematic and that certain revisions might be needed depending on the specific cultural groups involved. For example, individuals from Asian cultures like China and Vietnam tend to perceive and report somatic symptoms related to mental health differently from Westerners (Balaraman et al. 2023 ; Goodmann et al. 2021 ). For that reason, somatic‐related items (e.g., 2 and 7) might need to be modified or dropped to achieve meaningful comparisons across cultures. Thus, items describing individual's expression of mental health issues under social circumstances, like item 14, may face similar cross‐cultural problems. 4.3. Country Differences in DASS‐18 Results National group differences were examined for the DASS‐18, and the violation of assumptions around equality of covariances and homogeneity of variances limited our ability to employ parametric tests, further supporting difficulties in cross‐national comparisons. Non‐parametric tests supported differences between the student groups with respect to the DASS‐18 scores. While Vietnamese students reported the highest level of depressive symptoms, Chinese students reported the most severe anxiety symptoms. Chinese and Vietnamese students reported similar levels of stress and overall distress, while Australian students reported the lowest scores for all the DASS‐18 measures. The higher level of psychological distress among Chinese and Vietnamese students compared to Australian peers is consistent with previous studies, where Eastern samples often reported poorer mental health than that of Western groups (Bibi et al. 2020 ; Shorey et al. 2022 ). Previous findings on mental health among Chinese and Vietnamese samples, however, have been inconclusive. For example, recent studies reported higher levels of psychological distress among Chinese adults than Vietnamese participants (Park et al. 2020 ; Wang et al. 2021 ). Conversely, other researchers found a higher prevalence of mental distress among Vietnamese adults compared to their Chinese counterparts (Jang et al. 2019 ). In line with our findings, Zhang and Ta ( 2009 ) reported no significant differences in self‐rated overall distress between Chinese and Vietnamese adult samples. However, these studies either focused on Chinese‐American and Vietnamese‐American populations, did not use the DASS‐21, or employed it without examining its MI across groups. Little research has validated and directly compared DASS results between Chinese and Vietnamese students, making strict comparisons challenging due to differences in study design. Given that the present DASS‐18 version achieved scalar invariance, ensuring valid cross‐group comparisons, our findings help bridge a gap in direct comparisons between the studied populations and serve as an initial reference point for similar future research. The observed differences in depression, anxiety and stress scores among Australian, Chinese and Vietnamese students may reflect culturally specific ways of experiencing and expressing psychological distress. The higher anxiety scores among Chinese students are consistent with research linking academic pressure (Yang and Yang 2022 ) and parental expectations (Zheng et al. 2023 ) to elevated anxiety levels in China. Likewise, the higher depression scores among Vietnamese students may be associated with financial stress (Thang et al. 2022 ) and limited access to mental health resources (Nguyen et al. 2021 ). In contrast, Australian students reported the lowest distress levels across all subscales, possibly due to greater societal acceptance of mental health issues and more accessible support systems. Further research is needed to validate these explanations and explore additional contributing factors. 4.4. Gender Differences in DASS‐18 Results Interestingly, the only gender differences occurred within the Australian data. While both genders reported similar levels of depression, females reported more symptoms of anxiety and stress, thus contributing to a higher overall distress score. These gender differences in the three subscales are consistent with previous studies using the DASS‐21 with Western university students (Nankoo et al. 2019 ). The higher psychological distress among Australian female students likely reflects social and cultural influences on mental health (Kirkbride et al. 2024 ), suggesting their exposure to gender‐specific stressors (Pascoe et al. 2022 ). In the present study, Australian students ( M = 20.15, SD = 2.69) were significantly older than both Chinese ( M = 19.07, SD = 0.90, p &lt; 0.001) and Vietnamese students ( M = 19.25, SD = 1.44, p &lt; 0.001), while the age difference between Chinese and Vietnamese students was not significant ( p = 0.062). The interplay between gender and age‐related factors may have contributed to the higher distress levels observed among Australian female students, as they might be more exposed to gender‐specific stressors that become more pronounced with age, such as lower received social support (Knoll and Schwarzer 2002 ). 4.5. Implications for the DASS‐21's Universality One theoretical implication of modifying the DASS‐21 is the potential impact on the original theoretical integrity of its subscales. Items 2 and 7 (anxiety) and item 14 (stress) were removed due to low factor loadings and measurement non‐invariance across cultural groups. While this modification optimises the scale for cross‐cultural comparisons, it may subtly shift the theoretical basis of these measures. Specifically, the anxiety subscale no longer includes skeletal musculature effects (item 7: “I experienced trembling (eg, in the hands)” ), while the stress subscale no longer captures impatience (item 14: “I was intolerant of anything that kept me from getting on with what I was doing” ) and places less emphasis on autonomic arousal (item 2: “I was aware of dryness of my mouth” ) (see Lovibond and Lovibond 1995a , 1995b ). The retained items continue to reflect the core dimensions of anxiety and stress, including fear‐related symptoms and subjective and somatic experiences in the former, as well as persistent arousal and a low threshold for becoming upset in the latter (Cowles and Medvedev 2022 ), maintaining their theoretical coherence. That said, our findings suggest potential limitations in the universality of the original DASS‐21 across cultures, particularly in how certain symptoms manifest and are measured. Besides considering the original framework of the DASS‐21, examining findings from previous student‐focused DASS research offers valuable insights. While the present study's results on the DASS subscales (e.g., depression and anxiety levels in Chinese and Vietnamese groups) appear inconsistent with specific past studies, the overall trend in distress aligns with broader East–West patterns of mental health expression, with Chinese and Vietnamese students reporting similar distress levels, both higher than their Australian peers (Shorey et al. 2022 ; Zhang and Ta 2009 ). This pattern can be understood in light of recent evidence on the DASS‐21's cross‐cultural validity. For example, Lightburn et al. ( 2023 ) applied Generalisability Theory to assess the measure's reliability across cultures and time periods, finding that while the total DASS‐21 score remained highly stable, its subscales showed lower reliability under specific conditions. Their findings suggest that while overall distress measured by the DASS‐21 may be perceived and expressed more universally, its subscales and items may be more dynamic than previously assumed. This aligns with our findings that certain DASS‐21 items function differently across cultural groups, highlighting the need for further refinement to enhance the scale's applicability across diverse cultural and situational contexts. 4.6. Considerations for Multicultural Psychological Assessment In traditional psychological measures like the DASS‐21, verbal expression is a key source of psychological data (Boyd and Schwartz 2021 ). However, in collectivist cultures, distress often manifests through somatic symptoms (Ryder et al. 2008 ). Researchers and practitioners assessing mental health in these populations may need to explore how individuals perceive and report somatic responses to distress. Additionally, individuals from collectivist societies prioritise belonging, group identity and shared goals (Humphrey and Bliuc 2021 ), which can significantly shape mental health. Future research should examine whether incorporating culturally relevant distress indicators—such as loneliness and isolation, familial burden, relational conflicts, relation‐related guilt or social disconnection—enhances the validity of self‐report measures in these contexts. Qualitative or mixed‐methods research, along with clinical interviews, could help capture unique patterns in mental health perception and expression, clarifying how individuals interpret standard mental health items and whether these align with culturally specific expressions of distress. Regarding the cross‐cultural use of the DASS‐21 vs. DASS‐18, validation studies with relevant cultural groups should inform their selection and interpretation. Where such studies exist, researchers and practitioners may use the respective measures with caution. However, in the absence of validation, cross‐cultural application remains speculative and is not recommended. Our findings support the use of the DASS‐18 among students from Australia, China and Vietnam, but caution is still needed due to known cultural variations in the expression of depression, anxiety and stress. Test score interpretation should closely consider corresponding research findings and expert recommendations (e.g., Cowles and Medvedev 2022 ). 4.7. The Present Study's Limitations and Recommendations for Future Research There are several limitations to consider in the present study. Participants were volunteer university students from a limited number of universities and countries, restricting the generalisability of findings to non‐student populations, other cultural backgrounds, educational levels and age groups. Future research would benefit from extending the examination of the DASS beyond student populations (e.g., community members) to enhance its generalisability. Additionally, research should explore cultural variability by comparing subgroups within countries (e.g., gender differences) and incorporating a broader range of Eastern cultural backgrounds to refine the DASS for diverse populations. Additionally, this study encountered variations in recruitment procedures due to institutional and cultural contexts, such as offering course credit to Australian students while providing incentives to Chinese and Vietnamese students. These differences may influence participants' motivation and response patterns, potentially introducing bias. Standardising recruitment methods across groups could help mitigate these effects in future research. It is also noteworthy that the present study employed full MI testing and primarily relied on item modifications (e.g., item removal and cross‐loading) to achieve model fit. While this approach ensures a strict measurement model and facilitates mean comparisons across cultural groups, it is not always feasible (Luong and Flake 2023 ). Removing misfitting items may also alter the theoretical focus of the revised instrument, as previously discussed. An alternative is partial MI testing, which allows for some item variance while preserving the measure's theoretical integrity, though careful considerations are required (Luong and Flake 2023 ). Additionally, integrating other psychometric methods could strengthen the cross‐cultural validity of the DASS‐21. For instance, Item Response Theory (IRT) and Rasch modelling assess item difficulty and response categories (Medvedev et al. 2020 ), while Generalisability Theory (G‐theory) evaluates overall reliability and the generalisability of test scores across groups and time points (Lightburn et al. 2023 ). Nonetheless, this was still one of the first studies to examine differences across both Western and two different Asian samples with respect to the DASS‐21, while employing samples large enough and with sufficient statistical power to provide meaningful and valuable inferences about student mental health and practice in the three countries of focus. 5. Conclusion The results of this study suggest that caution should be employed when using the DASS‐21 in non‐Western populations and that modifications may need to be made to the scale for different cultural groups. We found removing several problematic items enhanced the ability to compare results across the three different national groups while retaining appropriate psychometric properties. These results are consistent with the broader concerns that have been raised about the appropriateness of simply translating scales and then applying them to different cultural groups without examining measurement differences with appropriate rigour. We encourage researchers and practitioners to critically assess assumptions about the comparability of psychological measures and their constructs to support meaningful cross‐cultural investigations and applications.
