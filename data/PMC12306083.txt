Background Air pollution exposure is a leading health risk mainly due to its detrimental respiratory and cardiovascular effects. Ambient air quality varies greatly across time and space, most anthropogenic pollutants being higher in cities than rural areas. Residents of rural areas who commute to cities for work are also exposed to the air pollution there. Therefore, exposure assessments that neglect population mobility produce biased estimates. Methods In this study, we quantify the effect of recurrent mobility on long-term air pollution exposure and its attributable mortality for the pollutants NO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} 2 , O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 , PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} 2.5 and PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{10}$$\end{document} 10 , for 584 districts of Catalonia (Spain) in 2022. We use anonymized phone-based mobility data to infer the dynamic distribution of the residents of each district among the different areas, considering only recurrent mobility. We also utilise finely-resolved air quality data for the four pollutants from the bias-corrected CALIOPE model, projected over the districts. We integrate dynamic population with the air quality to calculate dynamic exposure estimates, and compute the effect of mobility on long-term exposure with respect to the static estimates. We also calculate the mortality attributable to each pollutant and the effect of mobility. Results Considering the four pollutants, between 75.9% and 86.3% of the districts present significant effects of mobility on exposure. Rural areas surrounding cities display increased exposures to NO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} 2 , PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} 2.5 and PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{10}$$\end{document} 10 , and decreased exposures to O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 . The magnitude of these effects stays under 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document} μ g/m \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^3$$\end{document} 3 when considering the complete populations, but they increase up to 8.3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document} μ g/m \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^3$$\end{document} 3 of change when we focus on the mobile populations. However, the effects on attributable mortality are negligible. Conclusions Our work evidences the impact of cities on the air pollution exposure of people living far away from them, made possible by recurrent mobility. Our results show that correcting exposure profiles by mobility might not have a large impact at the population level when inter-area mobility is relatively low, but can be very significant for individuals and population segments with specific mobility habits, and as such should be taken into account for the design of public health policies. To make proper assessments of the health burden, we need accurate estimates of the air pollution exposure of populations. Exposure estimation requires both precise air quality estimates and knowledge of population presence. While significant improvements have been made for air quality models [ 7 , 8 ], population exposure often relies on the assumption that people are only exposed to the air pollution at their home location. Nevertheless, activities such as working or schooling usually require people to move or commute between different areas. In urban environments, air pollutant concentrations can vary greatly over space at the street scale [ 8 ], so incorporating mobility information is important to assess a more accurate people’s exposure. Several works that simulate movement trajectories of individuals have shown how much the exposure profiles can change when considering mobility [ 9 – 11 ]. Real mobility data can be obtained with different methods, from mobility surveys [ 12 ] to GPS tracking [ 13 ]. Surveys offer a coarse spatial resolution (postcode), and can be costly and non-representative [ 14 ]. On the other hand, GPS provides very precise spatial resolution, but it is very difficult to obtain GPS data representative of the whole population. A good alternative is to use mobile phone data from a network operator company [ 15 , 16 ]. People’s movements can be reconstructed from the interactions of their mobile phone terminals with the network, hence providing a spatial resolution corresponding with the antenna coverage areas (hundreds of meters in urban areas, and several kilometers in rural areas). The trip data can then be anonymized and aggregated at a certain spatiotemporal resolution, to avoid reidentification problems. This makes it possible to obtain representative mobility data at the population level. In this context, we contribute to the body of evidence of the effect of mobility on air pollution exposure from a multi-pollutant perspective, enabling comparisons across pollutants. In particular, we calculate the effect of recurrent mobility on long-term air pollution exposure for the year 2022, in Catalonia (Spain), for 7.76 million people living in 584 districts. For this, we leverage a public mobility dataset based on mobile phone data [ 25 ], which contains the aggregated trips taking place between districts; as well as air quality data for the pollutants NO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} 2 , O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 , PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} 2.5 and PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{10}$$\end{document} 10 . We obtain the effect by computing daily static and dynamic (mobility-informed) exposure estimates and taking the annual average difference. Furthermore, we evaluate the impact of recurrent mobility on the mortality burden. We do it by computing the mortality attributable to each pollutant using dose-response functions described in literature, and then calculating the effect as the difference between attributable mortality rates in the static and dynamic case. We highlight the large population size and geographical diversity of our study, as well as our capability to work with small areas and long-term exposures (one year). Our results provide valuable information on whether we should consider mobility to correct population exposures in future public health studies. We also provide a methodological framework to integrate mobility data with air quality data, which can be applied to other regions and pollutants. Methods Data Mobility data and area of study We obtained mobility data for the year 2022 from the ‘Mobility Study with Big Data’ of Spain’s Ministry of Transport and Sustainable Mobility (MITMS) [ 25 ]. This study is publishing open data of daily mobility in Spain from January 2022 onward. The data is generated from the geolocation over time of a sample of more than 13 million mobile phone terminals, provided by the telecommunications company Orange Spain. The geolocation data is based on Call Detail Records (CDRs), that is, interactions of the phones with the mobile phone network; and network probe data, i.e., recordings of every time a phone terminal connects to a different antenna. For each mobile phone in the sample, their records are pseudonymized to grant anonymity. Then, MITMS studies their behaviour over several weeks and they identify the place of habitual residence, the work/study location, as well as other frequent activities. This inference is based on lengths of stay, land-use data and sociodemographic data of the mobile phone owner. Once these key locations are identified, MITMS extracts the sequence of activities and trips of each user for the length of the study. They then expand the sample data to the total population. At this point, the anonymized data is held by the telephone company, and it is available at spatial resolution of telephone cells and a continuous temporal resolution. Before delivering the data, they aggregate it over space and time at a resolution that ensures that no individuals can be reidentified by data-fusion methods. In this case, data was aggregated per hour, and at a spatial resolution consisting of census tracts or aggregations of them. Therefore, this dataset consists of tables recording number of trips per hour stratified by the activities performed at the origin and destination mobility areas, as well as different sociodemographic indicators (see Supplementary Table S1 for the complete list). These indicators are not provided when there could be privacy problems, so we decided against doing an analysis stratified by age and sex because of the missing data. The data is available at different levels of spatial aggregation, and we chose to work with the finest one: districts. These districts, or mobility areas, are composed of census tracts or aggregations of them when it was determined necessary to guarantee the anonymity of the data. There are 3909 districts within Spain, and Portugal and France were included in the study as NUTS-3 zones. Trips occurring between other foreign locations are aggregated into one zone. We focused this study on the region of Catalonia, in northeastern Spain. The region is home to 7.76 million people as of 2021, with 3.30 million (42.56%) living in the Barcelona Metropolitan Area (BMA), an urban agglomeration consisting of Barcelona plus 35 other municipalities [ 26 ]. There are 584 districts in Catalonia, and Fig. 1 shows how the districts are smaller in urban areas, where the census tracts are more compact and the larger population makes it easier to upkeep privacy requirements; while the districts are larger for rural areas, where the opposite happens. For geographical reference, Fig. 1 highlights cities that define the main urban areas across the region. We also highlight the Air Quality Zones (AQZs; Supplementary Figure S1), which are delimited by the Surveillance and Air Control Service of the Government of Catalonia [ 27 ]. The AQZs group areas which tend to have similar air quality levels, and they are defined according to orography, climatology, population density and industrial and traffic emissions. [ 31 ]. Dynamic population computation The trip data was stratified according to the activities performed at origin and destination, which can be ‘work_study’ (work or study), ‘home’ or ‘other’ (combination of ‘frequent’ and ‘not_frequent’ activities). We defined four types of mobility with respect to trip types: Recurrent mobility : trips between ‘home’ and ‘work_study’ activities Work-related mobility : trips between ‘work_study’ and ‘other’ activities Home-related mobility : trips between ‘home’ and ‘other’ activities Other mobility : trips between ‘other’ and ‘other’ activities We are mainly interested in recurrent mobility, as the trips are referenced to a ‘home’ area, so we can keep track of where the residents of a certain area are located; and also because people going to either work or study are very likely to stay there for a long time, so we can assume they will be exposed to the air pollution of those locations. Trips taking place between ‘other’ activities are not helpful to assess long-term exposure to air pollutants, as we cannot know how long and how frequently people stay at those activities. Unfortunately, this means that by focusing on recurrent mobility, we are not able to account for the people going to work or going back home after other activities. Regardless of this, we filtered the recurrent mobility trips to compute the dynamic population. We also filtered the trips occurring between areas in the interior of Catalonia, as the fraction of trips occurring between Catalonia and the exterior was really small (1.32%). We defined dynamic population as the distribution of the residents of an area among the rest of the areas. Results Recurrent mobility patterns We filtered the 2022 mobility data for Catalonia (Catalonia being either origin or destination) and examined its composition. Trips in Catalonia were 17.43% of all trips in Spain. Of the Catalan trips, 98.68% were internal and only 1.32% took regions outside Catalonia. External trips were neglected for the exposure analysis and we filtered them out (note that we do not have air quality for the exterior territories). For the remaining trips, the dataset composition regarding the four mobility types is shown in Table 1 . Recurrent mobility was only 15.33% of all trips, so we applied the correction explained in Sect. 3.2 to add the work-related trips onto the recurrent ones and, in this way, avoid underestimating the real recurrent mobility. The correction resulted in an increase of the annual average mobile population fraction with median 1.09% (1st and 3rd quartiles [Q1, Q3]: 0.82%, 1.42%; Supplementary Figure S4). Here, we explore the dynamic population data we generated by inspecting the mobile population fractions, that is, the fractions of displaced residents for every area. First, we examine the average mobile population fraction for each hour of the day (Fig. 2 A). All areas share a pattern where the residents leave to work from 02:00 to 10:00, populations stay relatively constant from 10:00 to 12:00 and residents gradually return home from 12:00 onwards. The hour at which most areas have the most displaced residents is 12:00, with the median mobile population fraction being 10.63% (Q1, Q3: 9.16%, 11.90%). Furthermore, all mobile population fractions are 0% at 02:00, as this was our constraint to calculate the dynamic populations. However, by 01:00 the mobile population fractions are not back to 0%. This desynchronization is due to our constraints and because we could not account for travelers that go from home to work or vice versa by stopping at other activities along the way. Second, we study the daily maximum mobile population fractions of every area (Fig. 2 C). We observe drops in mobility during holiday periods, as can be the month of August (summer vacations), Easter (in April for 2022) or Christmas and New Year holidays during December and January. Otherwise, we observe relatively stable levels of mobility, with the average daily maximum mobile population fraction across areas being around 15% for weekdays. In addition, 2022 is a potentially anomalous year because of COVID-19, as the sixth and last wave of cases took place during January and February [ 50 ]. No lockdown was imposed, although people were encouraged to self-isolate. Nevertheless, we observe how the mobility patterns during January and February are quite similar to the rest of the year (Fig. 2 C). We also observe a weekly seasonal pattern where recurrent mobility drops during weekends, settling at about 5% (Fig. 2 B). Considering both weekdays and weekends together, the annual average of daily maximum mobile population fractions for each area has a median of 10.98% (Q1, Q3: 9.52%, 12.32%). Therefore, on average, we can expect that about 11% of the population of an area might have a different exposure to air pollution compared to the one in their area of residence. We show spatial patterns of the annual average of daily maximum mobile population fractions on Supplementary Figure S5. The areas with larger fractions of inter-area commuters are found around cities and urban areas. However, note that the places with the largest mobile population fractions are not the city centers themselves, but areas that can be relatively far away from them. In Supplementary Figure S6, we visualize the same annual averages of daily maximum mobile population fractions, but disaggregated by origin (area of residence) and destination (using arrows to represent the flows). This allows us to reveal connection structures between areas regarding their working population. A different way to explore the dynamic population data is by inspecting the transient to static population ratio. We calculated the transient population of each area by adding up all the people who are present there at a given time, irrespective of their area of residence. Then, we computed the ratio dividing by the census population of each area. Areas with a ratio greater than one denote higher activity than expected given their census population, while a ratio of less than one indicates that a large fraction of the population of the area performs their recurrent activity outside their residence area. The average transient to static population ratio by hour of the day (Supplementary Figure S7A) shows how at the busiest time of the day (12:00), some areas can have up to 50% more population present, while more than half of the areas experience a reduction in their effective population. We took the daily most extreme transient to static population ratios to build time series for each area. As we had seen for the mobile population fraction, the transient to static population ratio also exhibits a weekly seasonal pattern and is dependent on holidays, getting closer to one when mobility is less intense (Supplementary Figure S7B-S7C). Furthermore, we calculated the annual average of daily most extreme ratios and visualized it in space (Supplementary Figure S8). The map reveals that areas depleted of residents are spread throughout the territory. Hot spots for workers are found at population centers or at areas close to them. An important remark about these results is that they concern recurrent mobility, which, given our processing, accounted for about 23% of the trips. However, home-related mobility (trips between home and other activites) was about 60% of all trips (Table 1 ). We decided against using home-related mobility for the exposure correction, as we cannot keep track of whether people stay at the location of the ‘other’ type of activity or they travel somewhere else, and thus this would bias the spatial pattern of dynamic exposure. Given the great volume of home-related trips, we performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate how much our dynamic population would change if we were to consider home-related mobility. In particular, we computed the dynamic population using only home-related mobility and inspected the mobile population fractions as in Fig. 2 (Supplementary Figure S9). In Supplementary Figure S9A, we observe how more people leave home during the morning hours and more people return home during the evening, with a bimodal pattern where there is a global maximum of displaced people at 11:00 and a local maximum at 17:00. Mobility also drops during the weekends, although to a lower extent as with recurrent mobility (Supplementary Figure S9B). Considering only home-related trips, we reach higher daily maximum mobile population fractions, where the annual average for each area has a median of 18.39% (Q1, Q3: 16.09%, 20.08%; Supplementary Figure S9C). Therefore, this analysis shows that with recurrent mobility we are underestimating the total floating population, which might be two to three thimes larger than what we described earlier. Nevertheless, discarding the home-related trips is necessary to avoid biasing the spatial exposure pattern. Even so, we obtain significant average effects for the whole Catalan population for either increased or decreased exposure, determined by the pollutant concentration gradient with respect to city centres. while O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 presents a decreased exposure ( \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document} - 0.073 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document} μ g/m \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^3$$\end{document} 3 ) due to its lower concentration in cities. This decreased exposure to O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 caused by mobility has also been described by Reis et al. [ 24 ] and Yu et al. [ 19 ]. Interestingly, our population-weighted average effects are in the same order of magnitude as the ones reported by Reis et al. [ 24 ] for the population of the UK. In the background section we listed several works that evaluated the effect of mobility on air pollution exposure, but not many extrapolate what would be the impact on health outcomes. Gilardi et al. did a health burden assessment, although they report different metrics than we do [ 12 ]. In our study, we have quantified the effect of recurrent mobility on the mortality attributable to each pollutant, for each district and Catalonia as a whole. The change in exposure is modest at the population level, and as such, the impact on mortality is small. For all Catalonia, mobility explains up to two additional mortality counts for NO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} 2 , and less for the other pollutants. For each district, mobility shifts the attributable mortality rates per 100,000 people up to a maximum change of 0.42, 0.14 and 0.10 for NO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} 2 , PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} 2.5 and PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{10}$$\end{document} 10 , respectively; and up to a minimum change of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document} - 0.18 for O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 . That is, its effect is of less than one attributable death, over 100,000 people in all cases. According to our results, considering mobility to correct air pollution exposure profiles in studies done at the population level might not be necessary. If the inter-area mobility is not high enough, the change in the population-weighted average exposure is likely to be negligible. Considering this, it might be useful to do a pre-emptive analysis of the mobile population fraction of the population of study, as a high percentage of mobile people could make this correction really important. Conversely, correcting the exposure profiles of specific individuals could be vital to avoid biases in follow-up studies, as the gradient of air pollutant concentrations is very steep between cities and the countryside. Moreover, there are population subgroups which can have markedly different mobility habits, like age groups (children and the elderly are likely to move differently than active workers). Therefore, the mobility correction could be especially relevant for analyses stratified by age, sex and/or socioeconomic status. This work is subject to some limitations. First, our results are contingent on the mobility data we used. This dataset, generated from mobile phone data, makes it possible to study mobility at a high level of granularity, but it also faces some challenges regarding population representativity: the sample might not be large enough to perform inference at a very disaggregated level, all of the mobile phone terminals belong to the same network operator, there is no representation of children, and there is less representation of very old people [ 25 ]. Second, as the mobility data was not stratified by the area of residence of the travelers, we had to post-process this data under some constraints to infer the mobile population fractions of each area. These constraints (assuming all population is present at their area of residence at 02:00, and calculating the dynamic population iteratively in 24-hour cycles) also introduce some bias into our calculations. Using this dataset and this processing, we obtained an annual average daily maximum mobile population fraction of 11%. And third, given that we only consider trips between home and work, we are underestimating the total floating population. In our sensitivity analysis where we computed the dynamic population using only trips between home and other activities, we found out the total number of displaced people might be two to three times larger. Considering non-recurrent mobility can be especially important for studies on the short-term effects of air pollution exposure, as they require accurate and finely time-resolved exposure data. Nevertheless, we had to limit ourselves to mobility related to work so as to avoid biasing the spatial dynamic exposure pattern, as a work activity implies staying at that location for four hours or longer. The fourth limitation concerns the spatial resolution of the data used. Although a resolution of 1 km × 1 km is relatively high for air quality data, strong pollution gradients exist near emission sources. Increasing the resolution to a matter of meters could further highlight the differences between static and dynamic approaches. Indeed, some studies examining pedestrian exposure in urban centers suggest this direction [ 57 ]. In addition, our PM data consisted of daily averages instead of hourly values. This could help explain why the effect of mobility on exposure to PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} 2.5 and PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{10}$$\end{document} 10 is more attenuated in comparison to NO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} 2 and O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 . Another limitation is the assumption that the population is directly exposed to outdoor air pollution. In reality, people spend approximately 90% of their time indoors. While outdoor pollution levels are commonly used in such studies, this assumption may introduce significant uncertainty. A possible approach to adjust ambient air pollution to indoor values would be to scale down our exposure estimates (and thus the mobility effect estimates) by an infiltration ratio, as has been done in other studies [ 12 , 17 ]. However, the estimation of infiltration ratios has a large inherent uncertainty [ 58 ] and setting a fixed value for every microenvironment in our sample would be a great assumption. Lastly, our health assessment is limited to mortality, as there is a great body of evidence of the effect of air pollution exposure on all-cause mortality, and we think it is reasonable to extrapolate attributable mortality from the meta-analytic effects we considered. Nevertheless, air pollution exposure contributes to conditions which do not necessarily cause the death of the patient, and their effect on them might be greater than for mortality. For a different disease, like asthma, correcting exposure by mobility could be more relevant. Despite these limitations, we consider our case study is able to appropriately quantify the effect of recurrent mobility on long-term air pollution exposure at a fine spatial resolution. We highlight the reality that the residents of rural areas are more exposed to air pollution (except for O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 ) than we would expect without mobility. This result is especially relevant for people who decide to settle in the countryside to avoid pollution or because of lower housing prices, but who still commute to the cities for work regardless [ 59 ]. For future public health studies, we consider the mobility correction on exposure could be omitted for questions concerning population averages, but it should be included for studies at the individual level or for specific population segments. Conclusions Our research shows how recurrent mobility affects air pollution exposure and its attributable mortality on the long term (one year) for small areas in Catalonia (Spain), offering a multi-pollutant perspective. We find significant effects for a majority of areas, evidencing a pattern where rural areas in proximity to urban locations had an increased exposure to NO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} 2 , PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} 2.5 and PM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{10}$$\end{document} 10 , and a decreased exposure to O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document} 3 , formed by the regular commuting of their residents to the cities. The effects on exposure were small when averaged over the whole population of an area, but they reached high magnitudes when we focused on the mobile population. The small changes in population average exposure lead to negligible changes in attributable mortality. Altogether, this study shows how recurrent mobility has a moderate effect on population average exposure, and a potentially large effect on individual exposures, especially for the residents of areas close to urban agglomerations. Furthermore, the effect can be very significant for population segments with specific mobility habits, like children going to school or elderly people going to day centres. Therefore, correcting air pollution exposure profiles by mobility is especially relevant for future epidemiological studies stratified into population groups with different mobile behaviors, with age being a key factor. In addition, the mobility adjustment could have a greater impact for health outcomes more directly related to air pollution, like asthma. All in all, we provide information to researchers on whether they should correct their air pollution exposure profiles, which might be necessary depending on their case of study in public health.
