Adolescent athletes often face challenges that impact their mental health and sleep quality, including the need to adjust to irregular training schedules, intense training, and competitive pressures. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the prevalence of sleep and mental health problems and their relationships with perceived stress in Iranian adolescent athletes. The research design was a cross-sectional study. Adolescent athletes ( N = 579, aged 13 to 18 years) were selected from eight different team and individual sports, including wrestling, basketball, volleyball, karate, futsal, handball, and football. Three scales were used to collect data: the Cohen Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-14), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and General Health Questionnaire-28 (GHQ-28). The results showed high levels of sleep disturbance (54.2%), mental health problems (54.7%), and perceived stress (48.7%). The results also showed significant positive correlations between perceived stress and mental health symptoms ( r = 0.35) and between sleep disturbance and mental health symptoms ( r = 0.39). These findings suggest an association between sleep quality, perceived stress, and mental health in adolescent athletes. Sleep is essential for optimal bodily function, aligning with key strategies for physiological and psychological recovery [ 2 ]. However, the inherent psycho-physiological demands of sports can disrupt athletes’ sleep [ 3 ]. Late-night strenuous exercise, insufficient recovery time, overtraining, and jet lag are known to negatively impact sleep and performance [ 4 ]. Research has demonstrated that adolescent athletes, in particular, exhibit high rates of sleep restriction [ 5 ]. Epidemiological studies suggest that 40–60% of adolescents experience sleep problems [ 6 – 8 ], with approximately 45% prevalence specifically among adolescent athletes [ 9 ]. This population often fails to meet recommended sleep durations, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions [ 10 ]. Adolescent athletes in Iran may face unique challenges related to sleep and mental health due to cultural, educational, and sports system factors. High academic pressures, early school start times, lack of psychological support in sports settings, and limited access to mental health resources contribute to these difficulties. Furthermore, societal attitudes toward mental health and sport participation in some contexts may lead to underreporting of symptoms. However, this area remains understudied. Sleep plays an essential role in both cognitive and physical performance, particularly among adolescent athletes (ages 13–18), who are at a higher risk of sleep disturbances and associated cognitive, physical, and emotional symptoms [ 11 , 12 ]. Both adolescent and adult athletes with insufficient sleep are more prone to injuries and poorer performance [ 13 , 14 ], while extended sleep duration improves both health and performance [ 15 , 16 ]. Poor sleep quality has also been linked to increased emotional distress and symptoms of anxiety and depression [ 17 ]. Therefore, investigating how sleep quality relates to perceived stress and mental health symptoms in adolescent athletes is warranted. Many athletes are adolescents, and epidemiological data suggest that a significant proportion of this age group experience mental health symptoms [ 18 , 19 ]. Available data suggest that athletes may experience symptoms of conditions such as depression and anxiety at rates comparable to the general population [ 20 ]. The literature highlights a complex interplay between stress and mental health symptoms in athletes, with evidence suggesting that stress can negatively impact mental well-being [ 21 , 22 ]. Moreover, elevated stress may contribute to sleep difficulties through neuroendocrine and behavioral mechanisms [ 21 ]. Given the significant role of recovery in athletic development and performance, sleep remains a critical but often overlooked issue among adolescent athletes [ 14 ]. Psychological stress, negative affect, and worry further increase vulnerability to poor sleep [ 23 ], which can, in turn, worsen stress responses. This cycle is particularly relevant during periods of athletic performance development and competition. Additionally, research suggests associations between sleep deprivation and externalizing behaviors such as impulsivity and aggression [ 24 , 25 ]. While previous research has explored factors related to mental health symptoms such as anxiety, depression, and stress in adolescent populations, studies examining these relationships comprehensively in adolescent athletes are lacking, especially in the Iranian context. Research has often taken a fragmented approach, focusing on isolated variables like social media [ 26 ], physical activity [ 27 ], or screen time [ 28 ], without integrating them into a broader psychosocial model of adolescent athlete well-being. To address this gap, the present study considers sleep quality, perceived stress, and mental health symptoms in an integrated framework [ 29 ]. To date, no study has examined the prevalence of sleep problems, perceived stress, and mental health symptoms among Iranian adolescent athletes. This study aims to examine the prevalence of sleep disturbances, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and perceived stress. Additionally, it seeks to explore the relationships among sleep quality, perceived stress, and mental health symptoms, with a particular focus on the potential mediating role of sleep quality in the association between perceived stress and mental health outcomes. Based on prior research, the following hypotheses are proposed: (1) Poor sleep quality will be positively correlated with depressive and anxiety symptoms; (2) Perceived stress will be positively correlated with depressive and anxiety symptoms; and (3) Sleep quality will mediate the relationship between perceived stress and mental health symptoms. Method Procedure This cross-sectional study examined the relationship between perceived stress, sleep quality, and mental health in adolescent athletes (aged 13–18 years) participating in various individual and team sports (wrestling, basketball, volleyball, karate, futsal, handball, and football) at a competitive level. We defined a competitive level of athletic involvement as regular participation in structured training programs and competition at a minimum of a local level, potentially including regional or national competitions. This study was conducted in Tehran, Iran, during December 2023, an active competition season for these sports, where participants rated their sleep, mental health, and stress over the preceding month. Data was collected through online questionnaires distributed via social media and sports federations, with a final sample size of 579 participants after removing distorted surveys. A convenience and random sampling method was used. Ethical considerations were taken into account in this study, such that the goals of the study were explained to the adolescent athletes in an understandable way, participation in the research was completely voluntary and without any coercion, they were assured that the results of the surveys and tests would be completely confidential and the principle of confidentiality would be observed, the identity information of the subjects was not recorded and only codes were used to identify the data. Participants provided informed consent by checking a box at the beginning of the online survey, confirming their understanding of the study’s purpose, their rights as participants, and the voluntary nature of their involvement. For participants under 18, parental consent was also obtained with taking their phone numbers, which further ensured the ethical integrity of the study. The participants had the right to withdraw from the study at all stages of the research, including data collection, informed consent was obtained from all participants and they were asked to sign the prepared form. Instruments Demographic information questionnaire This tool is used to assess individual, social, and economic indicators (such as age, gender, type of sports, field of study, and family income). Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) A self-report questionnaire developed by Buysse and colleagues [ 30 ] was used to assess the sleep problems. This tool consists of 18 questions that are categorized into 7 components. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, with a total score range of 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate lower sleep quality. A score of 6 or higher indicates poor sleep quality [ 31 – 33 ]. The Persian version of the PSQI has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 [ 32 ]. In the present study the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the PSQI were 0.84, indicating adequate internal consistency of the scales. In the present study, any participant with a PSQI score above 6 was classified as having poor sleep quality. The percentage of participants meeting this criterion was calculated by dividing the number of participants with scores above 6 by the total sample size ( N = 579) and then multiplying by 100 to express it as a percentage. Perceived stress scale (PSS-14) Developed by Cohen and colleagues [ 34 ] to measure perceived stress. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Scores range from 0 to 56, with higher scores indicating higher stress. The Persian version of the PSS-14 has been validated [ 35 ]. In the present study the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the PSS-14 were 0.78. The PSS-14 is not a diagnostic instrument, and no predetermined cut-off points exist to classify varying degrees of perceived stress. For our analysis, the PSS-14 was operationalized in two ways: (1) as a categorical variable divided into quartiles, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference group, and (2) as a continuous variable. In this analysis, scores in the upper quartile of the PSS-14 distribution were considered as “high stress.” The percentage of participants with scores within this high-stress range was calculated similarly by dividing the number of participants meeting this criterion by the total sample size and multiplying by 100. General health questionnaire-28 (GHQ-28) GHQ-28 is used to assess mental health [ 36 ]. This tool is consisting of four subscales: somatic symptoms, anxiety and insomnia, social dysfunction, and severe depression. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale: 0 (not at all), 1 (no more than usual), 2 (rather more than usual), and 3 (much more than usual). The total score can range from 0 to 84, with higher scores indicating worse mental health. The Persian version of the GHQ-28 has been validated in Iran [ 37 ]. In the present study the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the GHQ-28 were 0.90, indicating adequate internal consistency reliability of the scales. For the present study, scores that met or exceeded a threshold of 24 were categorized as low mental health [ 38 ]. Again, the percentage was calculated by dividing the number of participants with scores above this threshold by the total number of participants and multiplying by 100. Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation) were used to summarize the data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to examine the correlation between stress levels, mental health, and sleep disturbances. Linear regression with the Enter method was used to analyze. The assumptions of the independent variable and multi collinearity were tested using the Durbin-Watson statistic and VIF. SPSS version 25 was used for all analyses. The level of significance p values was set at alpha &lt; 0.05. Results In total 579 adolescent athletes (mean age 16.55 years old) from various team sports (wrestling, basketball, volleyball, karate, futsal, handball, football, and swimming) were completed the study. The majority of the participants were under 17 and in high school (3rd grade). Nearly half of participants of adolescent athletes (48.7%) reported experiencing stress. The majority of adolescent athletes (54.2%) reported experiencing sleep disturbances. The majority of adolescent athletes (54.7%) reported experiencing low levels of mental health. The descriptive statistical of sample characteristics highlighted in Table 1 . As shown in Table 1 , the mean sleep quality score among adolescent athletes was 7.47 ± 3.14 on the PSQI. This mean score, which exceeds the PSQI threshold of 6, indicates a significant prevalence of sleep disturbances and low sleep quality in this group. The sleep quality scores ranged from 1 to 14. Additionally, the mean perceived stress score was 20.68 ± 8.27 on the PSS-14. This score indicates a relatively high level of perceived stress compared to other general populations, as suggested by Cohen et al. [ 35 ]. While the PSS-14 does not have fixed clinical cut-off points, this score reflects a high stress burden in this athletic population. The distribution of perceived stress scores, including quartiles as Q1: 12.51, Q2: 20.08, Q3: 29.95. Furthermore, the mean score for mental health, measured with the GHQ-28, was 50.36 ± 11.28. Based on the validated GHQ-28 scoring threshold of 24, this mean score of 50.36 indicates a low level of mental health among adolescent athletes. Table 2 shows the Pearson’s correlation coefficients between sleep quality dimensions, perceived stress and mental health dimensions. Results showed a positive and significant correlation between perceived stress and mental health problems ( r = 0.35, p &lt; 0.01). Similarly, sleep quality was significantly correlated with mental health problems ( r = 0.39, p &lt; 0.001). These findings indicate that higher perceived stress and poorer sleep quality are associated with increased mental health problems in adolescent athletes. Table 2 revealed significant correlations between specific components of sleep quality and mental health. Notably, sleep disturbance was positively correlated with anxiety and insomnia, and poor daytime performance was associated with increased somatic symptoms. These findings suggest that specific sleep quality issues are linked to particular mental health challenges in adolescent athletes. Based on Table 3 and the multiple regression coefficients, both perceived stress (PSS-14) and sleep quality (PSQI) were found to be significant predictors of mental health (GHQ-28). Specifically, perceived stress (PSS-14) significantly predicted mental health (beta = 0.55, p &lt; 0.001), and sleep quality (PSQI) significantly predicted mental health (beta = 0.37, p &lt; 0.001). The overall model, including both predictors, explained 39% of the variance in mental health scores (R-squared = 0.39). The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values in Table 3 indicate acceptable levels of multicollinearity, with both predictor variables having VIF values below 2. Based on Table 3 , the multiple regression results indicate that adolescent athletes with higher levels of sleep problems and perceived stress tend to report poorer mental health outcomes. Specifically, both sleep problems and perceived stress were significant predictors of lower mental health scores. Although improved sleep quality could potentially mitigate the negative impact of stress on mental health, a direct mediation effect of sleep quality in this relationship was not tested in the current analysis. The relationships between perceived stress, sleep quality, and mental health can be represented as: PSS -&gt;GHQ, and PSQI -&gt;GHQ. (see Fig. 1 ). To further investigate the potential moderating or mediating role of gender in our multivariate models, we conducted additional analyses. Specifically, we performed a multiple regression analysis with gender included as a moderator variable for the relationships between sleep quality, perceived stress, and mental health symptoms. The results of this analysis showed no significant interaction effects between gender and perceived stress on mental health and no significant interaction effects between gender and sleep quality on mental health. Furthermore, we included gender as a direct predictor in the regression model, along with perceived stress and PSQI. Gender was also not a significant predictor of mental health (beta = 0.03, t = 0.75, p = 0.45). Therefore, gender did not significantly influence the observed relationships. Discussion The results of the present study indicate that adolescent athletes in Tehran experience high levels of mental health problems, perceived stress, and sleep disturbances, which aligns with previous research [ 39 – 41 ]. Moreover, the results of our study are consistent with the findings of Daignault, Deslauriers-Varin, and Parent [ 42 ], who reported that more than one-third of adolescent athletes have some type of mental health problem. Their study emphasized that adolescent athletes are often exposed to psychological violence in competitive and professional sports settings, which can lead to low self-esteem, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, and psychological distress. While their study focused on professional contexts, its relevance here lies in the shared vulnerability of athletes to psychological stressors, which may extend to adolescent competitive environments. Importantly, the Routine Activities Theory (RAT), which posits that victimization is likely to occur when three elements converge: motivated offenders, suitable targets, and the absence of capable guardians [ 43 ]. Applied to adolescent athletes, this framework suggests that mental health challenges may emerge in environments where there is a lack of emotional support, insufficient mental health education, and increased interpersonal conflicts among athletes, coaches, and peers. Prior studies have shown that adolescent athletes can indeed be vulnerable to psychological stress and interpersonal conflict from peers and coaches, intensifying their mental health risks [ 42 ]. Understanding these contextual dynamics is essential for developing effective interventions to support this population. Our findings also align with broader epidemiological data showing the prevalence of mental health problems in adolescents typically ranges between 15.6% and 19.9% [ 44 ]. In line with this, Hrozanova, Moen, and Pallesen [ 3 ] demonstrated that perceived stress and emotional stress responses negatively impacted sleep quality in adolescent athletes. Although adolescent athletes may not be financially independent, indirect financial stress related to family burden (e.g., costs for training, travel, equipment, and competition fees) may contribute to their overall stress levels, potentially affecting their mental health. These financial pressures may escalate during breaks in training or competition when resources may be limited, adding to their psycho-emotional load. Although this mechanism requires further exploration, it may partly explain elevated stress and its detrimental impact on sleep and mental health in this population. According to cognitive theories, perceived stress may trigger cognitive-emotional processes that damage both mental and physical health, intensifying sleep disturbances [ 29 ]. Despite the possibility that regular physical activity might confer mental health benefits to athletes [ 45 ], current evidence is inconclusive regarding whether adolescent athletes are consistently at lower risk for specific mental disorders compared to their non-athlete peers [ 46 , 47 ]. Instead, athletes may face unique psychological vulnerabilities due to factors such as demanding training schedules, lack of control over daily routines, performance pressures, and sports-related injuries [ 48 , 49 ]. Research also points to increased risks for eating disorders, particularly in weight-class sports such as wrestling and boxing, as well as elevated risks for suicidal behaviors in elite athletes due to injury, early retirement, and related stressors [ 50 ]. Consistent with prior studies [ 51 , 52 ], we found that mental health problems were significantly associated with perceived stress. Stress can exacerbate various mental health concerns, including depression, anxiety, and sleep issues. Importantly, our findings suggest that sleep quality is closely linked to both perceived stress and mental health symptoms, echoing evidence that poor sleep may heighten susceptibility to psychological difficulties [ 53 , 54 ]. While the correlational nature of our data limits causal interpretation, it raises important questions regarding whether sleep mediates the relationship between perceived stress and mental health outcomes. Further mediation analyses in future studies could clarify these dynamics. Additionally, our study identified a significant positive correlation between sleep quality and mental health, supporting prior research [ 55 , 56 ]. Sleep is crucial not only for cognitive functioning [68, 69] but also for emotional regulation [ 57 , 58 ]. Promoting sleep hygiene practices, including regular sleep routines, a relaxing bedtime environment, and minimizing technology use before bed, should be considered essential elements of interventions aimed at improving adolescent athletes’ well-being. Given the observed associations between sleep, stress, and mental health, we recommend that routine health assessments for adolescent athletes include mental health screening protocols. Future research should explore evidence-based interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness strategies, and structured sleep hygiene programs tailored to athletes’ needs. Longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials will be particularly valuable in clarifying causal pathways and evaluating intervention efficacy. Limitations and future directions This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted exclusively on adolescent athletes in Tehran, limiting the generalizability of findings to other populations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships between sleep, stress, and mental health. Third, reliance on self-report measures may introduce reporting biases. Additionally, while we considered gender, future research should explore additional variables such as socioeconomic status, family dynamics, access to mental health resources, and pre-existing mental health diagnoses. Studies should also carefully consider the timing of assessments relative to athletes’ competition seasons or periods of intensive training, which may influence stress and mental health outcomes. Lastly, future research should focus on developing targeted interventions for adolescent athletes that address both stress management and sleep improvement. Conclusion The present study highlights the high prevalence of sleep disturbances, perceived stress, and mental health issues among adolescent athletes in Iran. The findings emphasize the interconnections between sleep quality, perceived stress, and mental health, suggesting that interventions targeting sleep improvement may enhance mental health outcomes in this vulnerable population. Future research should focus on longitudinal examinations of these relationships and explore tailored intervention programs within athletic settings.
