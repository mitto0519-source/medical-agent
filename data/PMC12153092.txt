Background Food insecurity, characterized by limited access to adequate and safe foods, is a prevalent global issue affecting millions. Anemia, often stemming from insufficient iron intake, is a common health concern, especially in children. Although the association between household food insecurity and anemia risk has been studied, findings remain inconclusive. This systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies aim to provide a clearer understanding of this relationship. Methods A comprehensive search of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar was conducted up to January 2024. Studies that reported odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) on the association between food insecurity and anemia risk in children and adolescents were included. Studies assessing food insecurity using validated tools, such as the Household Food Security Survey Module (HFSSM), were prioritized. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Results Twenty-five studies comprising 87,027 individuals were included. Household food insecurity was significantly associated with increased odds of anemia in both younger children (0–5 years) (OR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.13, 1.53) and older children/adolescents (6–18 years) (OR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.16, 1.83). Subgroup analyses suggested that food insecurity severity and geographic region influenced the strength of this association. Conclusions This study provides robust evidence that household food insecurity is associated with an increased risk of anemia in children and adolescents. Addressing food insecurity is critical to mitigating anemia risk, particularly among vulnerable populations. Future research should further explore how different measures of food insecurity, including child-specific food insecurity, impact anemia risk to inform targeted interventions. Food insecurity, characterized by limited or uncertain access to adequate and safe foods, represents a pervasive challenge affecting millions of individuals worldwide, with children and adolescents being among the most vulnerable [ 2 ]. In the United States, food insecurity affects approximately 12.8% of households, with households with children experiencing a higher prevalence of 15.4%, according to the latest USDA report (2023). Among households with children under the age of 6 years, the prevalence is 16.8%, highlighting the vulnerability of younger children [ 3 , 4 ]. Globally, it is estimated that approximately 2.3 billion people experience moderate to severe food insecurity, with children comprising a significant proportion of this population [ 5 ]. In low- and middle-income countries, where resources are often scarce and access to nutritious foods is limited, the prevalence of food insecurity among children and adolescents can be particularly high, posing significant threats to their health and well-being [ 6 ]. Food insecurity encompasses a wide range of situations, from consuming less preferred foods to missing meals or going without food entirely for a day [ 7 ]. The most noticeable and immediate impact of food insecurity is on dietary habits. Children facing food insecurity may be exposed to dietary patterns determined by their caregivers that increase their susceptibility to health issues related to insufficient nutrient intake, such as iron deficiency [ 8 – 11 ]. Anemia is a condition characterized by a decrease in the number of red blood cells or a lower-than-normal concentration of hemoglobin, which impairs oxygen delivery to tissues [ 12 ]. These include nutrient deficiencies, such as vitamin-deficiency anemia (e.g., deficiencies in vitamin A, vitamin B12, and folic acid) and iron-deficiency anemia (IDA); hereditary hemoglobinopathies, such as thalassemia minor; red cell production defects; and chronic diseases [ 13 ]. IDA, the most common form of anemia, occurs due to insufficient dietary iron intake or poor iron absorption [ 14 ]. The odds of anemia refers to the likelihood of developing anemia based on exposure to contributing factors, such as inadequate nutrient intake or underlying health conditions [ 15 ]. Iron is a vital nutrient, and a deficiency in iron during early stages of life has been linked to potentially irreversible adverse developmental consequences [ 16 ]. Animal products such as beef, pork, and poultry are rich in heme iron and offer the highest bioavailability of iron [ 17 ]. However, the cost of meat may pose a barrier to access for children experiencing food insecurity. Research utilizing data from the Continuing Survey of Food Intakes by Individuals found that children aged 1–5 years living in food-insecure households had lower iron intakes compared to those in food-secure households [ 8 ]. Increased levels of severity of food insecurity are associated with inadequate quantity and quality of diet, as well as elevated rates of neonatal, infant, and under-5 mortality [ 18 ]. Despite its significance, the association between food insecurity and the odds of anemia in children and adolescents has not been thoroughly delineated, and existing studies have reported controversial findings [ 19 , 20 ]. The present study endeavors to contribute to this growing body of knowledge by conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis focused on examining the association between food insecurity and the odds of anemia among children and adolescents. Through a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence, we aim to provide insights into the magnitude and nature of this relationship, while also exploring potential moderators and sources of heterogeneity. Methods Data source and search strategy The current study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Guidelines. This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [ 21 ](CRD42024500268). The systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar from inception to January 2024. The following MeSH and non-MeSH terms were used, with no language or date restrictions, to comprehensively capture studies on food insecurity and anemia: Terms related to food insecurity: (‘Food Supply’[Mesh], ‘Food Supply’[Title/Abstract], ‘Food Supplies’[Title/Abstract], ‘Food Insecurity’[Title/Abstract], ‘Food Insecurities’[Title/Abstract], ‘FI’[Title/Abstract], ‘Food security’[Title/Abstract], and ‘Food securities’[Title/Abstract]). Terms related to anemia: (‘Anemia’[Mesh], ‘Anemia’[Title/Abstract], ‘Anemias’[Title/Abstract], ‘Iron Deficiency Anemia’[Title/Abstract], ‘ Anaemia’[Title/Abstract], and ‘Iron Deficiency Anaemia’[Title/Abstract] ). We included ‘Food Supply’ as a search term due to its common use in some databases and studies to describe food security or insecurity. However, we ensured consistency by focusing on studies that specifically addressed validated definitions and measurements of food insecurity (e.g., HFSSM, HFIAS, ELCSA) in the context of anemia. In addition, the reference list of retrieved articles was hand-screened. Eligibility criteria Studies were eligible if they (i) were observational; (ii) were conducted among children and/or adolescents; and (iii) reported effect estimates in the form of odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) on the association between food insecurity and anemia risk. We included observational studies that reported effect estimates in the form of odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) on the association between food insecurity and anemia. Studies that originally reported risk ratios (RRs) were not excluded; instead, they were converted to ORs following the Cochrane Handbook guidelines (Sect. 6.4.1.1). This approach ensured that all relevant studies could be included while maintaining methodological consistency. Studies were excluded if: (i) they focused on populations other than children and adolescents; (ii) the necessary data for analysis, such as effect estimates on the association between food insecurity and anemia, was missing or incomplete; (iii) they were not original research, such as editorials, commentaries, conference papers, or reviews. Age groups in this study were classified as ‘children aged 0–5 years’ and ‘older children aged 6–18 years’ based on operational definitions used in prior studies. Adolescence typically begins at age 10, but for practical alignment with anemia thresholds, we used WHO’s 2024 guidelines, which provide cutoffs across these age categories. Study selection Two authors independently assessed the title and abstract of all obtained records. Then, in the second stage of evaluation, the full text of the eligible articles was retrieved and evaluated. Any disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus. Food insecurity was assessed using validated tools, including the 18-item Household Food Security Survey Module (HFSSM), the Escala Brasileira de Insegurança Alimentar (EBIA), and the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS). Each study’s methodology was reviewed to confirm the validity of the food security scale used. The level of food insecurity was categorized based on the definitions provided by the scales used in the included studies. The HFSSM classifies households as high food security, marginal food security, low food security, and very low food security. The ELCSA and EBIA scales categorize food insecurity into mild, moderate, or severe. We analyzed food insecurity severity levels as reported in each study, ensuring alignment with the specific scale used to assess food insecurity. Anemia was defined based on established cutoffs from multiple sources, including WHO (2024), CDC (1998), NHANES III, and other commonly used clinical guidelines. The following hemoglobin thresholds were used for classification: WHO (2024) Guidelines : Children 6–23 months: ≤10.5 g/dL Children 24–59 months: ≤11.0 g/dL Children 5–11 years: ≤11.5 g/dL Children 12–14 years: ≤12.0 g/dL Adolescents 14–18 years: ≤13.0 g/dL CDC (1998) and NHANES III Cutoffs (commonly used in U.S. studies): Age-specific hemoglobin thresholds that vary slightly from WHO standards Other Commonly Used Criteria : Domellof (2002) and USA JAMA (2009) thresholds For studies that assessed iron deficiency anemia (IDA), standardized definitions based on serum ferritin levels were used where available. We performed subgroup analyses to distinguish between overall anemia and iron deficiency anemia, considering the heterogeneity in anemia classification across studies. Exclusion criteria for anemia papers Given the complexity of anemia etiology, we carefully considered exclusion criteria for included studies. Data collection Two independent investigators extracted data from all of the eligible studies. The following information were extracted from each study: the first author’s name, study publication year, study country of origin, study design; sample size, participants’ gender, criteria for anemia status, level of food insecurity measurement, method of food insecurity assessment, relevant effect estimates, and any adjusted analyses. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer. Quality assessment The quality of included studies was assessed by two reviewers independently using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), a widely used tool for evaluating the quality of non-randomized studies, specifically cohort and case-control studies. The NOS assesses studies based on three broad categories: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and exposure/outcome assessment. The total score ranges from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating better quality [ 22 ]. A quality score was determined on the basis of three major domains: (i) selection of study groups (0–5 points), (ii) adequacy of adjustment for confounding (0–2 points) and (iii) ascertainment of the outcome of interest (0–3points) for a total score of 10 points. Studies scoring 9–10, 7–8 and 0–6 points were identified as high, moderate and low quality, respectively. Certainty assessment The certainty of the evidence was evaluated by using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach, which categorizes the quality of evidence as moderate, very low, low, and high [ 23 ]. Statistical analysis All statistical tests for this meta-analysis were performed with STATA (version 14.0; Stata Corporation, College Station, TX). To evaluate the relationship between food insecurity and anemia, the Odds Ratios (ORs) for anemia and IDA were pooled. To ensure consistency, we used standardized cutoffs to classify anemia, risk of anemia, and IDA. The following cutoffs were considered: In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we focused on anemia as the primary outcome, using ORs to quantify the association between food insecurity and anemia risk. While hemoglobin concentration could serve as a continuous supplementary outcome, we did not include it in our analysis due to the substantial heterogeneity in reporting across studies. Hemoglobin cutoffs varied based on different guidelines (e.g., WHO 2024, CDC 1998, NHANES III), and several studies did not report mean hemoglobin levels or provide sufficient data for standardization. Additionally, meta-analyzing continuous hemoglobin levels would require access to individual participant data or standardized mean differences, which were inconsistently reported across studies. We conducted separate meta-analyses for total anemia and IDA to account for their distinct etiologies. IDA is a subset of total anemia, and distinguishing between them provides more precise insights into the role of food insecurity in anemia development. Studies that explicitly reported IDA were analyzed separately from those that assessed overall anemia. If studies provided data for both anemia and IDA, we extracted and analyzed them independently. Subgroup analyses were performed using WHO (2024) guidelines to account for specific age-based hemoglobin cutoffs: children 6–23 months (≤ 10.5 g/dL), 24–59 months (≤ 11.0 g/dL), 5–11 years (≤ 11.5 g/dL), 12–14 years (≤ 12.0 g/dL), and 14–18 years (≤ 13.0 g/dL). This ensured that anemia classification aligns with age-specific standards. Also, we performed subgroup analysis based on sex (girl or both), level of FI (mild, moderate or severe), type of anemia (anemia, Iron deficiency anemia) or Region (Americas, Africa, or Asia). Food insecurity severity was categorized based on the definitions provided by the scales used in the included studies. While some scales, such as ELCSA, categorize severity as mild, moderate, or severe, others, like the HFSSM used in the United States, classify households as high food security, marginal food security, low food security, and very low food security. We analyzed food insecurity severity levels as reported in each study, ensuring alignment with the specific scale used to assess food insecurity. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and the I² statistic, which quantify variability attributable to heterogeneity rather than chance [ 24 ]. Between study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane’s Q test and I 2 statistic. The heterogeneity was considered significant if either the Q statistic had P &lt; 0·1 or I 2 &gt; 50%. The pooled estimates were calculated using the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model, a widely used approach for meta-analyses [ 25 ]. Publication bias, which occurs when studies with significant or favorable results are more likely to be published than those with null or negative findings, was assessed using Egger’s regression test and Begg’s test. These statistical tests evaluate asymmetry in the funnel plot, which can indicate the potential presence of bias [ 26 , 27 ]. A significance threshold of P &lt; 0.05 was used for both tests to determine the presence of publication bias. Given the observed heterogeneity across studies, we carefully examined whether differences in FI and anemia assessment methodologies contributed to variability in the results. Studies from African regions, Asia, and the US use different measurement tools, which may influence the reported associations. To mitigate this, we performed subgroup analyses based on FI assessment tools (HFSSM, HFIAS, EBIA, ELCSA) and anemia classification criteria (WHO, CDC, NHANES III). Studies that did not use validated tools or had inconsistent definitions were excluded to ensure methodological rigor. Sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out method, a technique in which one study is removed at a time from the meta-analysis to evaluate the impact of each study on the overall pooled estimate. This approach helps identify whether the results are unduly influenced by any single study. We conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of using different anemia cutoffs (e.g., WHO 2024 vs. CDC 1998) on the classification of anemic and non-anemic individuals. Variations in hemoglobin thresholds primarily affected younger children (6–23 months) due to more stringent WHO 2024 cutoffs [ 28 ]. Results Literature research The systematic search identified a total of 1539 records. After removing duplicate studies, 844 publications remained (Fig. 1 ). At this stage, two authors reviewed the titles and abstracts, and excluded 695 articles that did not meet study inclusion criteria. The remaining 69 records were reviewed in full text to confirm eligibility. Finally, 25 studies were included in the current systematic review and meta-analysis [ 29 – 53 ]. Characteristics of the included studies The general characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1 . Twenty-four studies used a cross-sectional design [ 29 – 43 , 45 – 53 ], whereas the other one used a longitudinal approach [ 44 ]. Studies were published between 2006 and 2022 and were conducted in Ethiopia [ 29 – 31 , 34 – 36 , 38 , 39 , 42 ], China [ 32 ], Mexico [ 33 , 41 , 48 ], Brazil [ 37 , 39 , 47 ], Pakistan [ 45 ], USA [ 40 , 44 , 51 – 53 ], India [ 43 ], Indonesia [ 49 ], and Nepal [ 50 ]. The risk ratios of 87,027 individuals in these studies were pooled for the meta-analysis. Out of these 25 studies, twenty-four studies reported their results as maximally adjusted ORs [ 29 – 39 , 41 – 53 ], whilst the other one did not adjust for any confounders [ 40 ]. Based on the Newcastle-Ottawa scale,13 studies ranked as high quality [ 29 , 31 , 33 – 35 , 44 – 46 , 48 , 49 , 51 , 52 ],and 14 had moderate [ 30 , 32 , 36 – 40 , 42 , 43 , 47 , 50 , 53 ] (Table 1 ). Food insecurity and odds of anemia among 0 to 5 years olds children Pooled analysis of effect sizes indicated a significant association between food insecurity and increased odds of anemia among 0 to 5 years olds children (OR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.13, 1.53) (see Fig. 2 ). However, there was a high heterogeneity across studies (I 2 = 82.6%; P &lt; 0.001). Subgroup analysis was carried out to determine possible sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis suggested that there was a significant association between food insecurity and odds of anemia among children 0–5 years for overall anemia (OR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.08, 1.53; I²=84.2%; P &lt; 0.001; n = 28), and iron deficiency anemia (OR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.09, 2.31; I²=58.7%; P = 0.046; n = 5). Also, subgroup analysis suggested that there was a significant association between food insecurity and odds of anemia among 0 to 5 years olds children for articles performed in the African (OR = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.39, 2.72; I 2 = 75.6%; P &lt; 0.001; n = 8), and America region (OR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.61; I 2 = 72.9%; P &lt; 0.001; n = 14) (vs. Asia) (Table 2 ). Other subgroup analysis revealed no significant differences between groups. Food insecurity and odds of anemia among older children aged 6 to 18 years The pooled analysis of 18 effect sizes demonstrated a significant association between food insecurity and anemia among older children (OR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.16, 1.83) (see Fig. 3 ). Due to substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 71.4%; p &lt; 0.001), we performed subgroup analyses, which showed that children aged 6 to 12 years exhibited a stronger association (OR = 2.25; 95% CI: 1.39, 3.62; I2 = 73.7%; p = 0.022; n = 3) compared to adolescents aged 13 to 18 years. The association was also more pronounced when both sexes were analyzed together (OR = 2.29; 95% CI: 1.74, 3.01; I 2 = 39.5%; p = 0.116; n = 11) (see Table 3 ). Sensitivity analyses and publication bias The sensitivity analysis assessment indicated that no single article markedly affected the risk assessments for the anemia among children and adolescents, as illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 1 . Publication bias evaluation suggested no significant bias for the risk of anemia among studies conducted on among 0 to 5 years olds children ( p = 0.093, Egger’s test; p = 0.778, Begg’s test) and 6 to 18 years olds children and adolescents ( p = 0.540, Egger’s test; p = 0.903, Begg’s test). As reported in Figs. 4 and 5 , the funnel plots were symmetrical for the association between food insecurity and the risk of anemia among 0 to 5 years olds children and 6 to 18 years olds children and adolescents, respectively. GRADE certainty of evidence Based on the GRADE framework, we have assessed the certainty of evidence by considering five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Given that all included studies are observational, the certainty of evidence begins at low by default. However, upgrades can be considered when factors such as a strong magnitude of association, dose-response gradients, or minimal confounding are present. For the association between food insecurity and anemia risk among older children (6 to 18 years), the certainty of evidence was classified as moderate, acknowledging potential confounding and residual bias despite consistent findings across studies. Similarly, for children aged 0 to 5 years, the certainty of evidence is rated as moderate, primarily due to concerns about heterogeneity in study results. Discussion This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to investigate the association between food insecurity and the odds of anemia among children and adolescents. Our findings contribute to the understanding of how socio-economic factors intersect with health outcomes in vulnerable populations. Our study revealed a significant association between food insecurity and an increased odds of anemia among both 0 to 5 years old children and 6 to 18 years old children and adolescents. The escalating issue of food insecurity globally heightens the susceptibility to various diseases, including behavioral issues [ 54 ], depression/anxiety [ 55 , 56 ], hyperactivity/inattention [ 57 ], diabetes mellitus [ 58 ], overweight/obesity [ 59 – 61 ], and complications related to undernutrition among children and adolescents [ 62 , 63 ]. Investigations into the connections between food insecurity and undernutrition have yielded varying outcomes [ 64 ]. While certain past research has identified a clear link between food insecurity and the likelihood of undernutrition-related issues such as low weight, stunted growth, and wasting during childhood and adolescence [ 65 , 66 ], other studies have failed to establish positive correlations, with one even indicating a negative association [ 67 – 69 ]. One of the reasons for the contradiction can be the difference in the method of measuring and classifying food insecurity in children. Diverse approaches and assumptions used to categorize food insecurity status can change how food-insecure households are classified, potentially elucidating the absence of a connection between extremely severe food insecurity and the risk of anemia in some studies [ 70 ]. Severe food insecurity in a household indicates that all members are experiencing a lack of food, with a very high probability that children are affected as well. However, an important factor influencing this relationship is the availability of subsidized food fortification programs, particularly for children’s foods [ 71 ]. In many settings, families experiencing financial constraints may opt for inexpensive fortified foods that are enriched with essential micronutrients, including iron. This dynamic could mitigate the expected impact of food insecurity on anemia prevalence, thereby weakening the observed association between food insecurity and anemia in certain studies. Acknowledging these factors is crucial for accurately interpreting the relationship between food insecurity and anemia risk in children and adolescents [ 72 , 73 ]. The results of many studies have shown that food insecurity, especially in the first years of life, has a significant effect on the occurrence of anemia in children, and there is no gender difference. For example, Engidaye et al. in a cross-sectional study in Ethiopia showed that as children age, the occurrence of anemia declined significantly, dropping from 40.5% among those aged 6–11 months to 11.3% among those aged 48–59 months. This suggests that younger children are the most susceptible to anemia. The probability of being anemic for children aged 6–11 and 12–23 months was approximately six times higher compared to children aged 48–59 months [ 74 ]. Contrary to previous claims, exclusive breastfeeding itself does not cause anemia in infants. Infants acquire iron stores from their mothers during pregnancy, which are typically sufficient to last for the first six months of life, after which iron-rich complementary foods should be introduced. However, maternal anemia during pregnancy can result in lower iron stores in newborns, thereby increasing the risk of anemia in early infancy [ 75 ]. Studies suggest that maternal iron deficiency is a key predictor of neonatal and infant iron status, emphasizing the importance of maternal nutrition and iron supplementation during pregnancy to prevent early childhood anemia [ 76 ]. The relationship between maternal anemia and child anemia is well-documented, with research showing that children born to anemic mothers have a higher likelihood of developing anemia themselves due to inadequate prenatal iron transfer and continued dietary deficiencies postpartum. This underscores the critical role of maternal health interventions in addressing early-life anemia risks [ 77 ]. In the present study, we found a significant association between food insecurity and increased odds of anemia in both children and adolescents. The pooled odds ratios indicated a consistent trend, where food insecurity was linked to higher odds of anemia across different age groups. While our findings suggest a dose-response relationship, we acknowledge that the study design and analytical approach do not allow us to claim a strictly linear association. Future research utilizing continuous exposure variables and regression models specifically testing linearity would be valuable in further elucidating this relationship. Maternal anemia stands out as another factor statistically correlated with childhood anemia. According to findings from various studies, children whose mothers were anemic had roughly four times the likelihood of developing anemia compared to children with non-anemic mothers [ 62 , 74 , 78 ]. This correlation has also been documented in research conducted in Cameroon [ 79 ], Northern Ethiopia [ 80 ], and Eastern Cuba [ 81 ]. One potential explanation for this link could be the shared living environment of mothers and children, which exposes them to similar physical, socioeconomic, and dietary circumstances. Additionally, maternal anemia might be connected to adverse birth outcomes like low birth weight and premature birth [ 74 ]. Therefore, the child’s hemoglobin levels heavily rely on the concentration of hemoglobin in the mother’s blood. Furthermore, maternal anemia could result in reduced fetal iron stores, and the iron content in breast milk might not suffice for the child’s daily iron needs. Consequently, because of these repercussions of maternal anemia, there’s a higher likelihood of childhood anemia in children born to anemic mothers. Hence, it’s crucial to screen pregnant women for anemia and provide consistent nutrient supplementation during their check-ups [ 79 , 82 ]. In the present study, the results showed that children aged 0 to 5 with food insecurity were 1.29 times more at risk of anemia. In line with our findings, Engidaye et al. observed that children from households experiencing food insecurity faced a 2.34 times higher risk of developing anemia [ 74 ], and this discovery is corroborated by analogous research conducted in the Tigray province and rural areas of Cameroon [ 83 ]. The increased prevalence of anemia among children residing in food-insecure households could stem from insufficient dietary intake, limited consumption of diverse food groups with low iron content, and adaptive strategies during periods of food scarcity. In times of food scarcity, households commonly employed strategies such as reducing meal portion sizes, decreasing meal frequency per day, prioritizing food for working family members, and opting for less preferred or cheaper food items to prevent depletion of reserved food supplies [ 20 , 84 ]. The present study had several strengths including comprehensive search strategy, clear eligibility criteria and subgroup analysis for main confounders. Despite the strengths of our systematic review and meta-analysis, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we used the NOS to assess study quality and the GRADE approach to evaluate the certainty of the evidence, there remains a risk of bias within individual studies, particularly due to the observational nature of the included studies. Some studies lacked sufficient adjustment for confounding factors, which could have influenced the estimated associations. Second, High levels of heterogeneity were observed across studies, which may affect the generalizability of the findings and suggest the presence of variability in study populations, methodologies, or settings. The methodologies for assessing food insecurity and anemia varied among the included studies, with some utilizing validated tools while others did not. This heterogeneity underscores the importance of standardizing measurement tools to improve comparability and reliability of results. Third, a potential limitation of our study is the absence of hemoglobin concentration as a supplementary outcome. While this could provide additional insight into the relationship between food insecurity and anemia risk, heterogeneity in hemoglobin reporting and classification criteria prevented a standardized analysis. Future research using individual participant data or standardized mean differences could help address this limitation. Fourth limitation of this study is that we focused on studies using specific validated tools for food insecurity assessment (e.g., HFSSM, HFIAS, EBIA). Other widely used measures, such as the Household Hunger Scale (HHS), were not included because they assess severe hunger rather than the full spectrum of food insecurity. Future research should examine how different food insecurity measures influence estimates of anemia risk. Finally, while our search strategy was comprehensive and included multiple databases, we recognize that studies published in non-indexed journals or those available in languages other than English may have been missed. Despite these limitations, the inclusion of these tools was deemed justified due to their applicability to vulnerable populations and their general alignment with broader constructs of food insecurity. Also, most of the studies did not specify the level of food insecurity, and because that we couldn’t include most of the studies in this subgroup. Moreover, the majority of included studies were cross-sectional, limiting the ability to establish causality between food insecurity and anemia risk. Longitudinal studies would provide stronger evidence of causal relationships over time. Finally, some studies did not adjust for potential confounding variables, which may have affected the accuracy of effect estimates. Conclusion In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provide compelling evidence of a significant association between food insecurity and an increased risk of anemia among both children aged 0 to 5 years and adolescents aged 6 to 18 years. Our findings underscore the critical importance of addressing food insecurity as a public health priority to mitigate the risk of anemia, particularly among vulnerable populations. By implementing evidence-based interventions and policies aimed at reducing food insecurity, policymakers, healthcare professionals, and stakeholders can work together to improve health equity and promote the well-being of children and adolescents worldwide. Future research should focus on harmonizing definitions and methodologies to ensure more robust associations.
