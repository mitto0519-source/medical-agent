Abstract In closed psychiatric settings, antipsychotic-induced lipid metabolism abnormalities pose significant clinical challenges. Constrained by limited patient mobility, fluctuating treatment adherence, and monotonous diets, traditional management struggles to adequately balance drug side effects with patient health needs. This study presents an integrated framework involving surveillance, intervention, and interdisciplinary collaboration. It includes a comprehensive “admission-to-intervention” monitoring system, low-glycemic diet combined with portion control, symptom-specific exercise programs, and strategies to improve treatment adherence. We establish quality metrics, including 100% baseline lipid testing for new admissions and ≥ 95% timely intervention rate for abnormalities. The patient-centered model reduces cardiovascular risks, improves patient outcomes, and offers a scalable approach for metabolic health management in resource-limited environments. Introduction In closed psychiatric management environments, lipid metabolism abnormalities caused by antipsychotic medications represent a critical clinical challenge [ 1 ]. Limited patient mobility, fluctuating treatment adherence, and monotonous dietary patterns underpin the inadequacy of traditional management approaches in addressing the conflict between drug side effects and patient health needs. To reconcile metabolic risk prevention with psychiatric care, a holistic framework integrating surveillance, intervention, and interdisciplinary collaboration is imperative [ 2 ]. This is particularly relevant given the differential effects of typical (e.g., haloperidol) and atypical (e.g., clozapine, olanzapine) antipsychotics on lipid profiles, with atypical agents often associated with higher risks of dyslipidemia [ 3 ]. The proposed framework addresses these nuances by prioritizing personalized surveillance based on medication type. Surveillance reinvention: from admission to proactive monitoring In closed wards, where patients have limited healthcare autonomy, an “admission-to-intervention” surveillance model is essential. Baseline lipid profiles—including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides [ 4 ], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)—are obtained upon admission, with electronic health records flagging high-risk individuals (e.g., body mass index [BMI] ≥ 24 kg/m² or users of Clozapine, Olanzapine and Quetiapine) [ 5 ]. During the initial treatment phase, lipid monitoring is integrated into routine blood tests like complete blood count and metabolic panels to avoid additional invasive procedures, thereby minimizing patient resistance to extra testing. This approach ensures metabolic surveillance is seamlessly embedded in standard care, allowing early detection of lipid abnormalities to reduce treatment-related adverse effects. In stable phases, a “6-month centralized screening + real-time alert” system triggers interdisciplinary intervention (psychiatrists, endocrinologists, dietitians) within 48 h of detecting LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L. Surveillance gaps in test-refusing patients are mitigated through bioelectrical impedance analysis of body fat percentage and waist-hip ratio, integrated with predictive modeling. Contextualized dietary and exercise interventions Dietary management in closed settings is standardized through collaboration between psychiatry and nutrition teams, implementing a low-glycemic index (LGI) diet featuring whole grains, deep-sea fish, and high-fiber vegetables. Portion control (≤ 150 g staple food/meal) and a “split-meal” strategy address binge eating. Exercise protocols are designed for fragmented participation: morning seated stepping to activate metabolism, afternoon Baduanjin for circulation [ 6 ], and evening virtual reality (VR) cycling for engagement. Symptom-tailored modifications include group ball games for negative symptom patients (combining calorie expenditure with social rehabilitation) and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) for manic patients to reduce TG levels while redirecting excess energy. Breaking the metabolism-treatment nonadherence cycle Antipsychotic-induced weight gain often fosters a cognitive bias of “medication = health harm.” During pre-treatment interdisciplinary shared decision-making using visual aids (e.g., animated videos), hepatologists simultaneously participate in metabolic risk assessment, systematically interpreting risk manageability by integrating liver function indices (e.g., ALT, AST) with lipid profile data [ 7 ]. In-hospital mindfulness training redirects focus from body image to functional benefits, while a “metabolism management reward system” reinforces adherence. For confirmed lipid abnormalities, timely initiation of metformin or statins, paired with visual trend feedback, rebuilds treatment confidence [ 8 ]. A tripartite contract among providers, patients, and families prohibits high-sugar/high-fat visiting foods to strengthen home support. Interdisciplinary collaboration as a catalyst for efficiency Cross-disciplinary teamwork is central to risk stratification and personalized care [ 9 ]. At admission, psychiatry, hepatology, endocrinology, and nutrition jointly assess medication selection, insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and dietary baselines. Weekly interdisciplinary rounds optimize treatment plans (e.g., switching from olanzapine to aripiprazole while evaluating CYP450 interactions). Pre-discharge planning includes home monitoring kits (e.g., portable lipid testing devices, digital scales, body tape measures, and symptom diaries), low-calorie meal guides, and community clinic follow-ups. Special populations require tailored approaches: gamified apps (“Metabolism Monopoly”) for adolescents, whey protein + vitamin D supplementation with resistance training for older adults, and preferential use of quetiapine with lipoprotein(a) monitoring for pregnant/lactating patients. Quality assurance and future directions Quality metrics include 100% baseline lipid testing for new admissions, ≥ 95% timely intervention for abnormalities, ≤2 kg weight gain during hospitalization, and a 20% LDL-C improvement. Quarterly multidisciplinary reviews using Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycles address gaps (e.g., non-invasive monitoring training). Future innovations may incorporate artificial intelligence (AI)-driven dietary image recognition and wearable devices for real-time energy expenditure tracking, advancing toward data-driven precision management. Expanding perspectives: implications for lipidomics and precision medicine While this framework focuses on traditional lipid markers (e.g., TC, LDL-C), it is important to recognize that these parameters may serve as proxies for broader lipidomic alterations—such as changes in sphingolipids, phospholipids, and oxylipins—that influence cellular membrane function, inflammatory signaling, and metabolic homeostasis [ 10 , 11 ]. For instance, antipsychotic-induced elevations in triglycerides may coincide with dysregulation of proinflammatory lipid mediators like arachidonic acid metabolites, linking metabolic and immune pathways [ 12 ]. Emerging technologies, such as mass spectrometry-based lipidomics, could further dissect these relationships, enabling the identification of precision biomarkers (e.g., specific lipid species predictive of cardiovascular risk) to tailor interventions in psychiatric populations. By acknowledging this broader lipidomic context, future iterations of our model may evolve toward data-driven, personalized strategies that align with the precision medicine agenda. Integrating lipidomics into routine clinical practice requires addressing technical barriers and establishing standardized interpretation protocols. However, as highlighted in recent research, the inclusion of lipidomic insights could transform metabolic management in psychiatry [ 13 ], shifting from reactive symptom control to proactive, precision-driven strategies. This aligns with the global push toward “metabolic psychiatry,” where a holistic understanding of lipid-metabolic pathways becomes central to patient care. Conclusion Managing lipid metabolism abnormalities in closed psychiatric settings necessitates a patient-centered care model that integrates metabolic surveillance, contextual interventions, and psychological support into daily practice. This interdisciplinary, lifecycle-oriented approach not only reduces cardiovascular risks but also improves patient outcomes through structured routines and social engagement. As a scalable paradigm, it offers a blueprint for integrating metabolic health into the core of neuropsychiatric care, ensuring holistic patient well-being in resource-constrained environments.
