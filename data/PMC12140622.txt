The publisher's version of this article is available at J Calif Dent Assoc Abstract Background: Access to dental care can be influenced by various factors, including socioeconomic status, race, ethnicity, household language, education level, insurance, and emergency situations. This study aims to assess dental care access of children of San Francisco families during the COVID-19 pandemic and identify barriers to dental care access. Methods: This cross-sectional study utilized data from the 2022 RAPID-SF Surveys conducted by the Stanford Early Childhood Center and the San Francisco Department of Public Health. The surveys, distributed across San Francisco, consisted of three baseline and three follow-up surveys. Associations between children’s past-year dental visits during the pandemic and demographic factors such as race, ethnicity, language, insurance, financial status, and education were evaluated. Results: A total of 680 families from San Francisco responded to baseline surveys (B1: 403, B2: 242, B3: 35). Follow-up surveys were excluded from analysis due to considerable loss to follow-up. About 34% reported difficulty accessing dental care for their children during COVID-19. Lower education, low income, and non-employer-based insurance were significantly associated with fewer dental visits ( p &lt; 0.05). Conclusions: The RAPID-SF survey identified significant oral health disparities in San Francisco during COVID-19, driven by income, education, and insurance type. Collaborative efforts by dentists, policymakers, and public health organizations are crucial to addressing these inequities. The RAPID-SF survey is a model for identifying disparities and guiding interventions to improve oral health outcomes during public health emergencies. Practical Implications: Strategies should be implemented to ensure San Francisco families have equitable access to dental services during public health emergencies. Introduction Maintaining optimal oral health is crucial for overall well-being and quality of life. 1 , 2 Regular dental visits are essential for the early detection of oral diseases and the prevention of more serious conditions in both adults and children, promoting long-term oral health. 3 However, dental care utilization is often multifactorial, leading many individuals to experience challenges when accessing dental care. 4 – 12 According to Andersen’s Health Service Use model ( Figure 1 ), health service use is influenced by predisposing factors (e.g., age, gender, and social structure), enabling factors (e.g., personal/family resources and community resources), and need factors (e.g., perceived and evaluated need for health services). 13 This model helps explain how socioeconomic status, education level, insurance coverage, and racial or ethnic background can hinder dental care access. 13 Evidence shows that lower incomes, lower levels of educational attainment, rural residency, inadequate insurance coverage, or belonging to racial minority groups can impact the likelihood of receiving regular dental visits. 12 , 14 – 17 Children from families facing these challenges can experience reduced access to dental services, negatively affecting their overall health, including their ability to eat properly, missing school or lack of concentration, and maintaining self-esteem. 14 – 17 Furthermore, emergency situations like the COVID-19 pandemic significantly impact the healthcare system and ultimately access to care. 18 During the pandemic, many patients faced significant delays in receiving treatment. A study involving a nationwide internet survey of adults in the United States (US) in the spring of 2020 found that nearly half (46.7%) of respondents delayed dental care due to the pandemic, with the majority (~75%) postponing routine checkups. 19 Among those who delayed care, ~12% postponed treatments for issues that bothered them, and 10.5% delayed planned treatments. 19 Similarly, a study of Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) serving low-income and uninsured populations aged 18–64 years showed that dental visits dropped more sharply than medical visits during the shelter-in-place periods of 2020, with an 81% decrease in April 2020 compared to the same month in 2019. 10 While medical visits rebounded to and surpassed pre-pandemic levels by June 2020, dental visits lagged in recovery, only surpassing pre-pandemic levels by November 2020. 10 There was a marked increase in demand for oral surgeries and teledentistry, whereas preventive services saw a significant decline. 10 Psychological stress-related dental conditions increased among all insurance types. 10 These findings align with broader trends during the pandemic, where delayed care led to an uptick in more urgent and invasive procedures. 10 In March 2022, the Stanford Center on Early Childhood conducted the RAPID-SF survey, entitled “Maternal and Adolescent Community Outreach During the COVID-19 Pandemic.” 20 This extensive multiphase survey explored the healthcare and financial challenges faced by families in San Francisco during the pandemic. 20 It gathered detailed information from families across San Francisco on various factors, including employment status before and during the pandemic, frequency of dental visits, access to food resources, education level, insurance status, and living expenses. 20 The goals of this initiative were the following: elevate community voices in decision-making, enhance data transparency, encourage interagency data sharing, reduce the burden of information collection from families by the city, and provide national data for benchmarking purposes. 20 Using the RAPID SF survey, this study aimed to assess dental care access among San Francisco families during the COVID-19 pandemic. It focused on identifying barriers to dental care during the pandemic and evaluated the overall impact of the pandemic on dental care accessibility. The findings will help the San Francisco Department of Public Health, California Department of Public Health, dental practitioners, and local health organizations develop strategies to improve access to dental services in future public health crises. Methods and Materials Study Design and Approval This cross-sectional study was approved by the UCSF Institutional Review Board as exempt. The data for this research was sourced from the RAPID-SF survey, conducted in collaboration with the Stanford Early Childhood Center and the San Francisco Department of Public Health. 20 Study Population and Inclusion Criteria The study population comprised parents and caregivers residing in San Francisco with at least one child between the ages of 0–17. Inclusion criteria consisted of residency in San Francisco, having at least one child in the specified age range, and consent to participate in the survey. There were no specific exclusion criteria. 20 Survey Administration The RAPID-SF survey was administered online using the Qualtrics platform and was available in multiple languages, including English, Spanish, Chinese, Filipino, Russian, and Vietnamese. Participants received a $5 gift card for each survey completed. The survey was self-administered and took a median of 15 minutes to complete. 20 Survey Instrument Development The RAPID-SF survey was developed using RAPID’s survey instrument (priori project), Item Bank, and infrastructure for rapid-cycle information gathering. 20 The survey included questions across the domains of demographics, income and employment, material hardship, child well-being, child care, child disability status, adult well-being, health care, family structure, school and extracurriculars, parenting stress and social isolation, financial strain, neighborhood resources and community connections, housing hardship, food security, racism and discrimination, safety, children and youth with special health needs, and physical activity and nutrition. The survey instrument was validated by the RAPID-SF team to ensure its relevance and comprehensiveness. We reviewed all survey questions to extract those pertinent to access to dental care and related study variables. Table 1 presents the survey questions related to this study. Data Collection Data was collected between April 27, 2022, and December 12, 2022. The survey was conducted in two phases: Phase 1: Three initial recruitment surveys conducted during April-May 2022 (B1), July-September 2022 (B2), and December 2022 (B3). Phase 2: Three follow-up surveys conducted in August 2022 (F1), October 2022 (F2), and December 2022 (F3). Recruitment and Retention of RAPID-SF Survey Participants The Department of Children, Youth and Their Families coordinated with other city agencies and San Francisco community-based organizations to implement recruitment strategies, including sending flyers, mailing postcards, e-mail blasts, and in-person tabling. Collaborating organizations included the Department of Public Health, Children’s Council, the San Francisco Public Library, Support for Families, and the Maternal, Child and Adolescent Health Section. Key Study Variables Independent variables of this study included race/ethnicity, language spoken at home, type of insurance, education level, and the outcome measure was past-year dental visits (PYDV). We created a new variable, “low income,” to serve as a proxy for financial hardship, defined by eligibility for Medicare/Medicaid, participation in free/reduced lunch programs, and receipt of food stamps. Statistical Analysis Statistical analysis was performed using Stata Software (version 17, StataCorp, Texas, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize and characterize the data, providing an overview of the key variables and their distributions. Logistic regression was employed to evaluate the association between the study variables and the outcome measure, PYDV. A significance level of 0.05 was set for all statistical tests. Results The study results are presented in three sections: prevalence of PYDV, association between PYDV and study variables, and challenges to dental care access during COVID-19 pandemic. Prevalence of PYDV Table 2 provides a descriptive analysis of the study variables collected from the baseline surveys (B1–3). It includes the prevalence of PYDV, categorized by race/ethnicity, language spoken at home, type of insurance, education level, and low-income status. Prevalence is presented as raw counts and percentages (n(%)) to indicate the absolute number of individuals who reported a PYDV and the relative proportion within each subgroup. A total of 680 families from San Francisco responded to baseline surveys (B1: 403, B2: 242, B3: 35). Follow-up survey data was excluded from analysis due to a considerable drop in respondent number. PYDV rates were generally higher among groups with employer-based insurance, higher education levels, and not classified as low-income. Figure 2 displays the number of respondents from the baseline surveys, those who had dental visits in the past year, and PYDV among those categorized under “low income” status. In the first two baseline groups, the proportion of low-income families accessing dental care was lower than that of the overall respondent population. However, in the third baseline group, low-income families exhibited a higher rate of dental care utilization. This deviation may be attributed to the survey distribution and smaller sample size. Associations Between PYDV and Study Variables Table 3 presents the logistic regression analysis examining the associations between PYDV and the study variables. Odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are provided. The reference categories for each study variable are indicated in the table. Race/Ethnicity Many families were either Asian or White. Asian families consistently had high PYDV rates, but no significant association was found ( p &gt; 0.05). Language Spoken at Home An overwhelming number of households identified English as their primary language. Spanish-speaking households consistently exhibited lower PYDV rates, while Cantonese and English-speaking households consistently exhibited higher PYDV rates. Despite these trends, language spoken at home did not show a significant association with PYDV ( p &gt; 0.05). Insurance Type Families with employer-provided insurance had higher odds of PYDV compared to those with Medicare/Medicaid, HealthySF, private insurance, or multiple insurance types ( p &lt; 0.05). Education Level Lower educational attainment was associated with reduced odds of dental visits, with the lowest odds observed among individuals with less than an associate’s degree ( p &lt; 0.05). Low-Income Status Low-income families had lower odds of PYDV compared to higher-income families ( p &lt; 0.05). Challenges in Dental Care Access During COVID-19 Pandemic Figure 3 illustrates how the COVID-19 pandemic served as a barrier to dental care access for San Francisco families. Of the 680 respondents from baseline surveys, 558 (82%) indicated that the COVID-19 pandemic impacted their access to dental care. The primary reasons included safety concerns, unavailability of appointments locally, and cancellations caused by the pandemic. According to the data, 34% of families reported challenges in accessing dental care during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, this number represents a slight overestimate due to 0.7% of families being able to access dental care during the pandemic through telehealth services and ER visits. The survey categorizes this group as encountering challenges, but they successfully obtained care through alternative means. Discussion Past year dental visits is an essential oral health indicator influenced by multiple factors including public health emergencies. The findings from the RAPID SF survey show that despite more healthcare resources in a highly urbanized environment like San Francisco, families faced significant challenges accessing dental care for their children during the COVID-19 pandemic. Families with lower educational attainment, low-income status, and those covered by Medicare/Medicaid, HealthySF, private insurance, or multiple insurance types were less likely to have a dental visit for their children in the past year. Our findings closely align with two pivotal studies on dental care disparities during the COVID-19 pandemic. Burgette et al. revealed that families experiencing income or job loss during the pandemic were significantly less likely to seek dental care for their children. 10 , 21 Similarly, Choi et al. highlighted a decline in the utilization of preventive dental services and an increase in psychological stress-related dental conditions among uninsured individuals, Medicaid enrollees, and private insurance recipients. 10 According to the self-reported California Health Interview Survey (CHIS) in 2020–2021, the PYDV for children in San Francisco declined from 83% to 73%, and for teens, PYDV declined from 93% to 86% in San Francisco reflecting the challenges posed by COVID-19. 22 Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that although the number of participants in CHIS is significantly larger than the RAPID-SF survey, both surveys reveal the same trend during the pandemic. To provide a clearer and more comprehensive discussion of our findings on dental care disparities and utilization during the COVID-19 pandemic, we have structured our analysis into subtopics guided by Andersen’s Health Service Model. Predisposing Factors Predisposing factors are characteristics that influence an individual’s likelihood of using health services. 13 These include demographic attributes like age or gender, social factors like education, and health beliefs like attitudes toward healthcare providers. 13 Our study found no association between dental care utilization and race/ethnicity. This might be due to the relatively small sample size in the RAPID-SF survey and diversity of the respondents in our study. Other studies have demonstrated low utilization of dental services among racial minorities. 23 , 24 Fisher-Owens et al. showed that significant racial/ethnic disparities exist in children’s oral health in the US, with Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black children more likely to have poor oral health, lack preventive care, and experience delayed or unmet dental needs compared to non-Hispanic White children. 24 Additionally, we found no association between the language spoken at home and PYDV. This contrasts with other studies that show non-English speakers are less likely to receive preventive or routine dental care. 24 , 25 Similar to race/ethnicity, this discrepancy may also be due to a relatively small sample size and its limited distribution to families in San Francisco. 25 , 26 Furthermore, in our study, families with less than an associate’s degree as their highest educational achievement were less likely to visit a dentist than individuals with a greater education level. This finding aligns with other studies as education, a key component of socioeconomic status, strongly influences oral health outcomes, such as dental caries prevalence. 27 – 29 Enabling Factors Enabling factors refer to logistical aspects that facilitate or impede healthcare access, including personal and family resources like income, health insurance coverage, and availability of healthcare services in the community such as work-force, clinics, and hospitals. 13 Our findings showed that low-income families were less likely to visit a dentist than their higher-income counterparts. According to the “2023 San Francisco Biennial Food Security and Equity Report”, San Francisco faces significant income and poverty disparities, with 10.3% of residents living below the Federal Poverty Level (FPL), highlighting the severe challenges low-income households face in one of the nation’s most expensive cities. 30 Insurance type emerged as a critical enabling factor in this study. Families with Medicare/Medicaid or Healthy SF were less likely to have had a dental visit than those with other employer-provided insurance. Interestingly, our findings also indicate that families with private insurance through an insurance company were less likely to visit a dentist than those with employer-provided insurance. We could not find a study exploring the differences between individual vs employer-provided private insurance, but we assume that coverage differences drive this discrepancy. Another aspect of dental utilization challenges could be related to the number of dentists who accept public insurance and their diversity. According to the San Francisco Dental Society, there are over 1,100 dentists practice in San Francisco, 31 yet only a limited number of dentists accept Medi-Cal Dental/Denti-Cal, 32 which is the most common form of public insurance in California. A survey distributed among ~30,000 dentists in California conducted by the ADA Health Policy Institute showed that of 1,400 respondents, only 31% accepted new adult Medi-Cal patients and 41% accepted new child Medi-Cal patients. 33 Several key strategies identified to raise Denti-Cal acceptance include increasing reimbursement rates to 55–75% of commercial fees, ensuring timely payments within one month, providing administrative assistance, reducing missed appointments, emphasizing the ethical obligation to treat Medi-Cal patients, targeting dentists in high Medi-Cal enrollment areas, simplifying administrative processes, and implementing regular surveys and feedback mechanisms to address dentist concerns. 33 These measures can make Medi-Cal participation more attractive, improving access to dental care for low-income populations. Need Factors Need factors refer to an individual’s personal perception of their need for healthcare and the professional evaluation of that need by healthcare providers. 13 The RAPID-SF survey included one question pertaining to challenges when attempting to visit a dentist. Our results showed that ~ 33% of respondents experienced difficulties in visiting a dentist for their children due to a lack of appointment, safety concerns during COVID-19 pandemic, and external pressures that led them to deprioritize dental visits. Despite the California Department of Public Health providing infection control guidelines for dentists, 34 many people remained apprehensive. These concerns likely stemmed from the perceived risk of exposure to the virus in dental settings, leading to hesitancy in seeking dental care for their children. This highlights the need for continued public education on the effectiveness of safety protocols and potentially more robust measures to reassure patients during such health crises. Preventive dental services, such as routine cleanings and fluoride treatments, are a significant aspect of dental care and rely on perceived and evaluated needs. Families with lower oral health literacy may undervalue preventive care, leading to delayed or missed appointments and ultimately lower utilization of dental services. 35 Resources like the Oral Health Literacy Toolkit, developed by the California Department of Public Health and Health Research for Action, could address this gap by providing families with a better understanding of preventive care in maintaining oral health. 36 By fostering greater awareness, dentists can enhance perceived needs and encourage families to prioritize preventive visits, even during public health emergencies. Teledentistry emerged as a critical solution to limited dental care access nationwide. 35 In California, dentists followed guidelines enacted by the California Department of Public Health for safe dental practices to resume services after the lockdown. 37 Dental practitioners were advised to adopt several strategies to ensure continued access to care, including expanding the use of teledentistry and mobile dental clinics. 35 , 38 However, according to our results, less than 1% of families in San Francisco utilized teledentistry. This low utilization might be due to the nature of dental conditions, which often require in-office treatment. 35 , 38 Nevertheless, teledentistry can provide remote consultations and triage, reducing the need for in-person visits and minimizing exposure risks during pandemics if the condition can be managed without compromising the patient’s health. 35 , 38 Role of Dentists and Public Health Organizations in Emergency Situations Developing and implementing emergency response plans that include protocols for maintaining dental services during public health crises is crucial. This involves ensuring the availability of personal protective equipment (PPE) and adapting practice operations to comply with safety guidelines. During the COVID-19 pandemic, dental practices throughout California implemented a number of adjustments to daily protocols such as performing routine staff testing and prioritizing less invasive, non-aerosolizing procedures. 37 , 39 Public health organizations should also emulate the RAPID-SF survey in gathering data during a public health crisis. Efficient and widespread data collection is vital to implementing timely interventions and learning from previous crises. Despite survey design and data collection limitations, the Stanford Center on Early Childhood and the San Francisco Department of Public Health acquired valuable information that can be applied to future emergencies. We recognize that surveys like CHIS gather statewide data and federal agencies gather nationwide data, but public health surveys in major urban areas could offer deeper insights to inform community-specific public health strategies. The findings of this study also indicate the need for systemic changes to address oral health disparities in emergency situations. As outlined in the California Oral Health Plan, targeted interventions such as enhancing the accessibility and comprehensiveness of public insurance programs and offering financial assistance to low-income families are essential. 40 Additionally, dentists can collaborate with policymakers and public health organizations to implement community-based initiatives, promote preventive care, and provide financial assistance to alleviate the cost burden for underserved populations. 41 Limitations It is important to recognize the limitations inherent to our dataset, particularly the small sample size and the varying participant numbers across baseline surveys. The first baseline survey included more participants compared to subsequent surveys, leading to uneven data distribution. Additionally, our analysis was limited to baseline data, as substantial attrition prevented the inclusion of follow-up data, further reducing the breadth of our findings. Moreover, several categories associated with a few variables had very low respondent numbers, limiting our capacity to assess causal relationships and generalizability. Conclusions The RAPID-SF survey identified significant oral health disparities in San Francisco during COVID-19, driven by income, education, and insurance type. Collaborative efforts by dentists, policymakers, and public health organizations are crucial to addressing these inequities. The RAPID-SF survey is a model for identifying disparities and guiding interventions to improve oral health outcomes during public health emergencies.
