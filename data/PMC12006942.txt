ABSTRACT Introduction : Sleep disorders in the insomnia spectrum, as well as nightmares, are among the most sensitive and persistent symptoms in children with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). There is currently no reference treatment or specific pharmacological treatment recommendation on the management of sleep disturbances in children and adolescents suffering from PTSD, despite the fact that they have a significant effect on daytime functioning and overall mental health of children as well as on family’s health and quality of life. In this respect, paediatric prolonged-release melatonin (PedPRM) has shown significant beneficial effects on insomnia disorders in children with autism spectrum disorders and positive effects on anxiety and depressive symptomatology. Our study will be the first randomized controlled trial to examine the efficacy of PedPRM melatonin on sleep disorders in children and adolescents with PTSD, as well as on PTSD symptoms, associated daytime functioning and overall mental health in these children and their caregivers. Methods/design : The MelatoSOM-Kids-PTSD study (French national hospital-based clinical research programme) will be a multi-centre prospective double-blind placebo-controlled parallel group clinical trial investigating the efficacy of paediatric prolonged-release melatonin to alleviate sleep disturbances in children and adolescents with PTSD (120 participants recruited over a 24-month period). The experimental group will be treated with active prolonged-release melatonin over 13 weeks (PedPRM). The control group will receive a placebo. The primary endpoint will be the difference in sleep diary derived total sleep time after 13 weeks of treatment in the PedPRM group versus placebo group. Secondary endpoints will be the differences in objective sleep quality parameters and daytime functioning before and after treatment, in children with PTSD and their caregivers. Discussion : This paper describes the MelatoSOM-Kids-PTSD protocol, which will evaluate the effectiveness of melatonin, a treatment that has already demonstrated an excellent benefit-risk ratio in the paediatric population over 4 years. KEYWORDS: Sleep disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, children, extended-release melatonin, treatment HIGHLIGHTS Unmet public health need with no specific pharmacological treatment recommended in the management of sleep disorders associated with childhood PTSD, despite these problems being prevalent and strongly associated with post-traumatic symptoms, deterioration in mental health, daytime functioning and quality of life in these children. Pioneering use of paediatric extended-release melatonin (PedPRM): The MelatSomPSD study is the first randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of PedPRM in children and adolescents with PTSD. Comprehensive outcome measures assessing not only the impact of PedPRM on sleep disorders but also its effects on PTSD symptoms, daytime functioning and overall mental health in children and their caregivers, ensuring an examination of both direct and indirect outcomes. Nuestro estudio será el primer ensayo clínico controlado aleatorizado que evalúe la eficacia de la PedPRM en los trastornos del sueño de niños y adolescentes con TEPT, así como en los síntomas propios del TEPT, el funcionamiento diurno asociado y la salud mental general de estos menores y sus cuidadores. Introduction 1.1. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in children and adolescents Exposure to potentially traumatic events is highly common in children and adolescents. According to the DSM-5, a traumatic event is defined as direct, indirect or repeated exposure to serious threats to physical integrity, serious injury or major psychological violence (American Psychiatric Association, 2013 ). In younger children, these exposures can potentially lead to the development of PTSD symptoms or have other developmental impacts, depending on a number of trauma-related, individual and environmental factors. As shown in a large community-based study on 1420 children in 11 counties in western North Carolina, 68% had experienced at least one traumatic event, whereas 37% had been exposed to several traumatic events in their lives (Copeland et al., 2007 ). After a traumatic event, about 30% of subjects develop PTSD symptoms (Kessler et al., 1995 ), half of whom will suffer from a chronic and highly impairing PTSD trajectory (Davidson &amp; Fairbank, 1993 ). Paediatric studies on PTSD symptoms show a prevalence of PTSD of 15.9%, even without including subthreshold symptomatology (Alisic et al., 2014 ), and an overall average rate of complex PTSD of 3.4% (Truskauskaite et al., 2023 ). At 18 years the prevalence rate is 4%, however, in specifically exposed clinical populations, the rate of PTSD rises as high as 71% (Gindt et al., 2022 ). These studies also indicate a chronic course in a significant proportion of children and adolescents with PTSD, as observed in adults. As such, in a prospective study of 808 Australian schoolchildren, post-traumatic stress symptoms had not decreased 26 months after a fire in the bush (McFarlane, 1987 ). In the aftermath of Hurricane Andrew, 70% of a group of 30 survivors ages 6–11 years still exhibited moderate to severe PTSD symptoms after 21 months (Shaw et al., 1996 ) and even the very young (under 6 years of age) showed a chronic course over a two-year follow-up period (Scheeringa &amp; Haslett, 2010 ). Once present, the post-traumatic symptomatology broadly affects the overall cognitive, affective and physical development of the child, with a significant long-term impact on mental and physical health persisting into adulthood, significantly reducing the quality of life of the patients, but also their families (Alisic et al., 2008 ; Cohen et al., 2010 ; Tyler, 2002 ). The younger the subject at the time of the initial trauma, the more pervasive is its developmental impact (Cicchetti &amp; Cohen, 1995 ). Early interventions for PTSD in children and adolescents with evidence-based therapies are thus of prime importance, not only to alleviate the burden for the patient short-term but also to avoid medium- and long-term negative outcomes, as well as to avert the massively negative impact on the overall quality of life for the patient and their caregivers (Cohen et al., 2010 ). 1.2. Sleep disturbances in children and adolescents with PTSD Both studies using parental reports as well as those using objective measurements such as actigraphy and polysomnography have demonstrated in paediatric PTSD a high prevalence of nightmares as well as symptoms in the insomnia spectrum, including sleep onset disturbances (longer time to fall asleep), fragmented sleep with multiple nocturnal awakenings, as well as early morning awakenings (Kovachy et al., 2013 ; Rolling et al., 2023 ). These sleep disturbances were among the most sensitive and persistent symptoms of PTSD in children and strong negative predictors of disease course and outcome if not addressed early-on. Taking the example of children exposed to war trauma and/or violence, over 51% of a group of 403 children aged 9–15 years living in refugee camps were experiencing sleep disturbances, 31% of whom ‘most of the time’ (Thabet et al., 2004 ). After witnessing a sniper attack, 77.1% of children reported sleep disturbances one month after the trauma (Pynoos et al., 1987 ). In this type of attack, 57.9% of the children still reported sleep disturbances at a 14-month follow-up (Nader et al., 1990 ). Retrospective studies examining sleep in 78 young adults (mean age 20 years) who were sexually abused as children (aged between 6 and 16 at the time of the trauma) reported, in comparison to 68 controls, significantly more sleep disturbances, including insomnia and nightmares, and more instances of pervasive PTSD (Noll et al., 2006 ). These studies and others (Llabre &amp; Hadi, 2009 ) demonstrate that not only do these sleep disturbances tend to become chronic, they may predict the development and persistence of PTSD and depressive symptoms in children and adolescents (Fan et al., 2017 ; Noll et al., 2006 ; Thordardottir et al., 2016 ). In an important longitudinal investigation, Brown et al. assessed sleep disturbance and fear of sleeping in a sample of 202 children (aged 8–15 years) 24 months after they experienced trauma from Hurricane Katrina (Brown et al., 2011 ). Severity of sleep disturbance was associated with the severity of PTSD symptoms. Therapeutically, it has been shown that psychotraumatized adolescents with sleep disorders respond less well to the gold standard of PTSD therapy, trauma-focused cognitive–behavioural therapy (Brownlow et al., 2016 ). The most specific sleep complaints are the appearance or increase occurrence of nightmares. Overall, prevalence rates for nightmares after a traumatic event range from 50% to 80% (Carrion et al., 2002 ; Pynoos et al., 1987 ; Thabet et al., 2009 ), and up to 100% in cases of major trauma (Mghaieth et al., 2007 ; Rolling et al., 2023 ). As a consequence, the DSM-5 has included the presence of nightmares as a diagnostic criterion for PTSD (American Psychiatric Association, 2013 ; Kaufman et al., 1997 ; Scheeringa &amp; Haslett, 2010 ). The dreams of children who have lived through a traumatic event can be distinguished from those of other children by their contents, including high levels of negative emotions and the presence of hostility (Punamäki et al., 2005 ), and in that they lead more systematically to nocturnal awakenings. Preliminary studies have shown that in children with PTSD higher sleep fragmentation (increased number of awakenings after sleep onset, increased number of sleep stage changes) with a significant change in sleep microarchitecture (increased micro-arousal index) correlates with the clinical severity of nightmares (Rolling et al., 2023 ). Last, a greater rate of sleep terrors, another type of parasomnia associated with anxiety, was also reported in PTSD (Sateia, 2014 ). Taken together, the literature on children and adolescents with PTSD suggests that (a) sleep disturbances, particularly nightmares, are frequent symptoms of PTSD; (b) sleep disturbances often occur following a trauma and may confer increased vulnerability for the development of PTSD and its chronic course; (c) sleep disturbances predict global PTSD symptom severity; (d) sleep disturbances are often a residual symptom following treatment of PTSD. Preliminary data suggest that (e) treatment of sleep disorders may yield significant improvement in PTSD symptoms, which prompts us to devise effective therapeutic approaches to sleep disorders in paediatric PTSD. 1.3. An unmet need for treatment of sleep disturbances in paediatric PTSD As shown in the adult literature, PTSD-focused treatments may lead to partial improvement in co-occurring sleep disorders, but they do not enable complete remission (Galovski et al., 2009 ; Zayfert &amp; DeViva, 2004 ), whereas the use of specific treatments targeting sleep disturbances have led to significant improvements in both sleep and global PTSD symptoms (Kovachy et al., 2013 ). These findings highlight the intrinsic severity of sleep problems in PTSD, as well as our gap of knowledge in identifying sleep treatments. They also challenge the notion that sleep disorders in PTSD are merely secondary symptoms best treated by treatment of the overarching disorder. Indeed, within the adult population, sleep slow-wave sleep (SWS) and REM sleep exhibit significant anomalies in PTSD (for review see Zhang et al., 2019 ). SWS is often reduced, fragmented, and impaired in its role of memory consolidation and emotional restoration. REM sleep is usually associated with specific mechanisms helping to regulate emotions, attenuating the intensity of reactions to intrusive memories (Zhang et al., 2019 ). In PTSD, REM sleep is frequently diminished or fragmented, marked by recurring nightmares and emotional dysregulation. These disruptions exacerbate PTSD symptoms and chronic fatigue, further hindering recovery. However, in children, data on these sleep anomalies remain scarce at this stage. Improving the quality and continuity of sleep plays a key role in PTSD, allowing traumatic memories to be reprocessed, facilitating their reorganization, reducing their emotional charge and enabling proper recovery. Moreover, restful sleep contributes to stress regulation by reducing hypervigilance and anxiety, two major symptoms of PTSD. Thus, targeting sleep disorders is essential to successfully treating PTSD (Kovachy et al., 2013 ). Treating sleep dysregulation after a trauma early in life could therefore have significant implications for reducing risk of PTSD and associated outcomes, both in childhood and across the lifespan. To date, only few studies have addressed specific treatments for sleep in children with PTSD, many of which are case studies or studies with very small sample sizes, reporting on the use of prazosin (Hudson et al., 2021 ), clonidine or guanfacine, and have mostly looked at the effect on nightmares. Although it is essential to develop new treatment strategies to address this public health issue, there are currently no clear national or international treatment guidelines on the management of sleep disturbances in children and adolescents with PTSD due to a lack of interventional studies in this field. Combining expertise in psychotrauma (Rolling et al., 2023 ), with longstanding expertise in paediatric sleep medicine (Schroder et al., 2019 ; Schroder et al., 2021 ), our project’s main aim is to study the efficacy of novel paediatric prolonged-release melatonine (PedPRM) on sleep disturbances and associated daytime functioning in children and adolescents with PTSD and their caregivers – in the first placebo-controlled double-blind randomized controlled trial of its kind (see Figure 1 ). 1.4. Rationale for using paediatric prolonged-release melatonin for sleep in PTSD Although the efficacy of paediatric prolonged-release melatonin (PedPRM) on sleep disorders in children and adolescents with PTSD has never been tested before, there are many arguments in favour of testing it as a candidate molecule in this indication. Indeed, a long-acting molecule would treat both sleep onset disorders and nightmare disorders of PTSD, whereas immediate-release melatonin would primarily treat sleep onset latency difficulties, due to its short half-life, rapid onset of action limited to the early hours of the night and phase-shifting effects (Emens &amp; Burgess, 2015 ; Gringras et al., 2012 ). In this respect, PedPRM has shown highly beneficial effects on insomnia spectrum sleep disorders (ASD) in children aged 2–17.5 years ( n = 125) with autism spectrum disorders (Gringras et al., 2017 ) and consequent positive effects on children’s daytime behaviour, as well as on caregivers’ sleep and daytime functioning (Gringras et al., 2017 ; Maras et al., 2018 ; Schroder et al., 2019 ; Schroder et al., 2021 ) is an age-adapted formulation of melatonin developed for sleep disorders in children and suitable for use from an early age on (&gt;2 years). As a result, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) approved PedPRM in September 2018 for paediatric insomnia, in children aged 2–18 years with ASD or Smith–Magenis syndrome ( European Medicines Agency ). In addition to its significant effects and total sleep time on school days, sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency and related daytime behaviours (Gringras et al., 2017 ; Schroder et al., 2019 ), the sustained-release formulation also offers a significant advantage in maintaining stable melatonin levels throughout the night (Arendt et al., 1985 ; Zisapel, 2010 ), including the last third of the night which is REM sleep enriched. This allows targeting not only sleep onset difficulties but also nighttime awakenings and nightmares, which are frequently associated with PTSD. The solid oral dosage form of PedPRM minitablets (3 mm in diameter) is swallowed whole and is designed to release melatonin gradually, mimicking the physiological secretion profile of melatonin to produce sustained plasma levels of melatonin for 8–10 h. This mechanism of action has the potential to act on sleep fragmentation present in children with PTSD (Rolling et al., 2023 ). Although a long-acting sleep-promoting molecule is required, there should be no significant hangover effects in the morning, and as obtained in the previous paediatric study (Schroder et al., 2019 ) we expect not only an improvement in sleep disorders associated with PTSD, but also in the symptoms of PTSD itself, and in daytime functioning and overall mental health (in particular on anxiety levels (Gross et al., 2009 )). Indeed, in adults, studies suggest that melatonin can improve sleep quality and the stabilization of circadian rhythms in various psychiatric disorders (Geoffroy et al., 2019 ). These effects, linked to better regulation of circadian rhythms and emotions, could also extend to children suffering from PTSD. For clarity and comparison, we will use the same study design and dosage optimization in this 13-week double-blind randomized controlled trial as in previous study protocols using PedPRM. 1.5. Objectives and hypothesis The main objective of this trial is to demonstrate, in children and adolescents aged 2–17.5 years with PTSD, the efficacy of PedPRM to reduce sleep disturbances, as measured by a greater increase in total sleep time (TST) on school days in the treatment group compared to the placebo group. We prioritize sleep time on school days to minimize age-related within-subject variability and avoid the compensatory effect of catching up on sleep at weekends. Our secondary objectives are to determine the efficacy of PedPRM to improve (i) other quantitative and qualitative measures of sleep and circadian sleep–wake rhythmicity in children and adolescents (sleep latency (SL), wake after sleep onset (WASO), longest continuous sleep period, sleep efficiency, nightmares, stability of sleep–wake rhythms, overall sleep quality), as well as (ii) daytime function and behaviour (daytime sleepiness, severity of PTSD, externalizing and internalizing daytime behaviour, anxiety, depression, emotional skills), (iii) parents’ sleep parameters, and (iv) parents’ daytime function and behaviour. Over a 13 weeks treatment period, we expect children and adolescents in the PedPRM group to improve not only their sleep (i.e. increased total sleep time on school days, decreased time to fall asleep, decrease in nightmare frequency and nightmare distress), but also daytime outcomes (i.e. PTSD severity, externalizing and internalizing behaviours, anxiety) compared to children in the placebo arm. We expect also to see a concomitant beneficial impact on sleep and daytime function in their caregivers. 2. Methods section 2.1. Study participants The study participants are shown in Table 1 . 2.2. Study design and procedures The study is a multi-centre double-blind placebo-controlled parallel group clinical trial (phase IIIb study) in which the safety and efficacy of this extended-release paediatric melatonin formula have already been demonstrated ( European Medicines Agency ). Group allocation will be conducted with a 1:1 randomization (see Figure 1 ). The experimental group will be treated with active prolonged-release melatonin (2 minitablets of 1 mg) over 13 weeks (PedPRM minitablet paediatric-appropriate) (PedPRM). The control group will receive a placebo treatment over 13 weeks, through equivalent placebo minitablets (2 similar minitablets) that are identical in appearance and formulation to the PedPRM minitablets. After 3 weeks of PedPRM treatment or placebo and a continuous sleep diary assessment, sleep diary-derived sleep variables will be evaluated to determine if dose modification is required. The primary endpoint will be the difference in sleep diary derived total sleep time on school days after 13 weeks of treatment with paediatric prolonged-release melatonin versus placebo. 2.4. Objective measurement of rest-activity cycles (sleep–wake rhythms) The actimeter is a wristwatch-like device containing a piezoelectric accelerometer that measures the intensity, quantity, and duration of physical movements in all directions (Motionwatch®, Cambridge Neurotechnology, UK; software (MotionWare 1.2.47)), thus assessing the rest-activity rhythms under ecological conditions. The actigraph is worn day and night over a 15-day period alongside a subjective assessment using a sleep diary. Activity is recorded in 1-min epochs, with the use of an algorithm to extrapolate the sleep–wake rhythm, providing an objective estimate of various sleep and circadian rhythms parameters, including total sleep time (TST), sleep onset latency (SOL), wake after sleep onset (WASO) and sleep efficiency. Non-parametric analyses allow for the evaluation of circadian rhythm measurements that can be interpreted as indicators of possible shifts (phase advances or delays) of sleep–wake rhythms, namely the onset of the 5 h of minimum activity over 24 h (L5 onset), the onset of the 10 most active continuous hours over 24 h (M10 onset), the relative amplitude (RA), intraday variability (IV), and interday stability (IS). An RA close to 0 indicates a lack of differentiation between day/night activity and rest, while an RA close to 1 indicates good day/night circadian amplitude. IV reflects the variability of rhythms recorded during a single day, and IS reflects the reproducibility of the rhythm from one day to the next over time. An IS close to 1 indicates a sleep–wake rhythm highly reproducible from one day to the next, whereas an IS close to 0 indicates an instable sleep–wake rhythm. Actigraphy has been validated as a measure of sleep quality for all ages included in this protocol (Smith et al., 2018 ). Moreover, Motionwatch is equipped with a luxmeter, which records not only activity but also ambient light exposure to control for the compliance to sleep hygiene recommendations. 2.6. Data collection process All study data will be anonymized and collected on paper, then entered into an electronic case report form by the investigator or a person designated by the investigator. Trial data will be managed using the CleanWEB® software solution marketed by TELEMEDICINE Technologies S.A.S. This company ensures data confidentiality, security and integrity in accordance with a security plan predefined by the sponsor and complying with international recommendations (ICH-GCP part 11). On completion of the final analyses, the database will be downloaded and stored securely at the University Hospitals of Strasbourg. Sleep data will be collected using an actigraph (Motionwatch®, Cambridge Neurotechnology, UK), then extrapolated and downloaded using motionware software (Sleep Analysis 5 software, Cambridge Neurotechnology, UK). The External Oversight Committee will decide on all safety-related issues: i.e. serious adverse events, the safety report and adverse events. At the end of its review, it will draw up and send written recommendations to the sponsor. These recommendations may include the continuation, modification or termination of the clinical trial. 2.7. Statistical analysis Descriptive analyses will be conducted for the full sample and separately by treatment group, reporting the frequency and percentage for qualitative variables; and mean, standard deviation for quantitative ones. The description will include sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, school enrolment/employment status and comorbidities), sleep and circadian rhythms measures (total sleep time on school days, sleep latency, wake after sleep onset, chronotype), and symptoms associated with PTSD (severity of PTSD, externalizing and internalizing daytime behaviour, anxiety, depression and Emotional skills) at V0 (see Figure 1 ). The analysis pertaining to the primary objective will be a linear regression adjusted on age, sex and recruitment centre, between the randomization group (treatment vs. placebo) and the difference between total sleep time on school days at V0 (before intervention) and V2 (after intervention) (see Figure 1 ). Sample size calculation was based on the study’s primary objective, which is the change in total sleep time (TST) on school days between V0 (pre-intervention) and V2 (post-intervention). We assumed a minimal clinically significant effect of 30 min increase in TST, with an estimated standard deviation of 45 min, based on previous data in similar populations. Statistical power of 80% ( β = 0.2) and alpha risk of 5% ( α = 0.05) were used, requiring a total of 102 participants + 15% de drop out to detect a significant difference (Gringras et al., 2017 ). Because U-shaped associations between TST cognition and mental health have been reported in adults, complementary analyses using non-linear statistical model will be implemented. Linear regressions will be conducted for each secondary objective, with the randomization group as the independent variable. The successive dependent variables for the regression will be the sleep, circadian rhythms, daytime function and behaviour, parents’ sleep parameters and parents’ daytime function and behaviour: and psychometric measures difference between V1 and V2. The inclusion of multiple secondary objectives allows for a comprehensive exploration of treatment effects across interconnected domains, shedding light on how sleep improvements may influence psychological, behavioural, and circadian functioning in both children and their parents. Results for secondary objectives, of which we will more specifically assess patient perception of sleep as assessed by the PSQI and nightmare-related distress, will be interpreted as exploratory and unadjusted for multiplicity to guide future hypothesis generation. To ensure comparability between psychometric measures which can differ across age, scores will be normalized per test, using within population z -scores. The analyses will be conducted according to the intention-to-treat approach. Per-protocol analyses will also be conducted as part of a sensitivity analysis. Hence, the analysis will be repeated including only subjects who have an acceptable compliance to PedPRM (between 70% and 100%). For all analyses, the significance will be set at a p -value inferior to .05 in bilateral tests. 3. Conclusion Sleep disorders associated with PTSD are significant risk and maintenance factors for PTSD, with a major impact on the development and quality of life of psychotraumatized children and adolescents, and their families. The diagnosis and treatment of these disorders is a major public health issue worldwide. Management of sleep disorders associated with PTSD involves promoting sleep hygiene, and treating sleep disorders associated with PTSD (with age-appropriate treatment tools) and other PTSD symptoms. In the current context, evaluating the efficacy of paediatric prolonged-release melatonin would open up new therapeutic prospects, adding to the existing modest therapeutic arsenal, with the advantage of being safe and easily diffusible worldwide, particularly in countries with little access to psychotherapy targeting trauma-related sleep disorders, or to other PTSD psychotherapies.
