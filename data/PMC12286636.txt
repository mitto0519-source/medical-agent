Abstract Recent calls to prioritize mental health discussions in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathemathics (STEM) have gained momentum, driven by alarming reports on the prevalence of mental health issues within STEM disciplines. Research has extensively studied anxiety and depression in both STEM graduate and undergraduate students, but there has been little focus on the overall mental health of life science undergraduates. We examined how undergraduate life science students perceive support for mental health in their classes, how their major influences their mental health, and how students manage these challenges. To investigate these ideas, we conducted semistructured interviews with 20 life science undergraduate students at a commuter, primarily undergraduate institution with a diverse student body. Our findings indicate that most students prioritized academics at the expense of their wellness and perceived a lack of support for their mental health from STEM instructors. Despite these challenges, some students used various strategies to maintain their mental health and many remained committed to their degrees, viewing the pursuit as worthwhile despite its adverse effects to their mental health. These findings underscore the critical need for more comprehensive and sustained mental health support in undergraduate life science education, ensuring students are not only academically successful but also emotionally resilient and well supported. status released display-pdf yes is-olf no is-manuscript no is-preprint no is-journal-matter no is-scanned no is-retracted no Received 2024 Sep 12; Revised 2025 Feb 28; Accepted 2025 Mar 10. INTRODUCTION “I hope that there is a shift in professors’ mentalities when it comes to their students and their mental health… And I really hope that [mental health] gets taken more seriously and into better account in the coming years… because I think too many students are way too stressed, especially STEM students. We are way too stressed. We are not sleeping well. We are anxious wrecks, and we are suffering very terribly from imposter syndrome. And I think to understand that and have that compassion is really crucial.” - Melissa, undergraduate biology student Mental health and wellness, defined as an individual's emotional, psychological, and social wellness ( SAMHSA, 2023 ), has become an increasingly prominent topic in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) fields ( Limas et al., 2022 ; Pester et al., 2023 ), driven by concerning reports of its rising prevalence in the broader academic community ( Assembly, 2014 ; Evans et al., 2018 ; Editorial, 2020 ). A nationwide study of primarily undergraduate students reported that over 60% of college students met the conditions for having at least one mental health condition—a rate that has doubled within the last decade ( Duffy et al., 2019 ; Lipson et al., 2022 ). Mental health issues affect individuals at all levels in STEM fields, with significant concerns documented among graduate students, particularly those from systemically excluded groups (e.g., women, LGBTQIA+, and people of color) ( Jensen and Cross, 2021 ; Schad et al., 2022 ). The prevalence of mental health conditions also increased among graduate and undergraduate STEM students during the COVID-19 pandemic ( Chirikov et al., 2020 ; Sifri et al., 2022 ). In a study of over 2000 undergraduates taking online classes during the pandemic, STEM students were more likely to report severe depression compared with non-STEM majors ( Busch et al., 2022 ). These statistics are especially concerning because mental health conditions can negatively influence undergraduate students' academic performance and retention in degree programs ( Eisenberg et al., 2009 ; Duffy et al., 2020 ). Interventions aimed at promoting mental health and wellness in STEM, such as workshops ( Cole et al., 2024 ; Williams et al., 2024 ), counterspaces ( Wilkins-Yel et al., 2023 ), and mindfulness curriculum ( Crone et al., 2023 ), and strategies for managing mental health, like self-care ( Ménard et al., 2021 ) and peer support ( Jensen et al., 2023 ), have shown promise to support mental health for students in STEM disciplines. However, qualitative research examining the broader effects of majoring in STEM on the mental health and wellness of undergraduate students has predominately focused on engineering majors ( Jensen et al., 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ). Despite growing attention to mental health in STEM, qualitative research specifically examining the impact of majoring in the life sciences on students’ mental health and wellness is lacking—particularly at primarily undergraduate institutions. Existing studies often center on research-intensive universities or specific mental health conditions, leaving a critical gap in understanding how these factors intersect in diverse educational contexts. This study seeks to fill this gap by exploring the experiences of life sciences majors at a primarily undergraduate institution, offering new insights into their unique challenges and needs. Understanding how majoring in the life sciences influences students’ mental health is critical for developing strategies to improve matriculation and retention, especially for students from systematically excluded identities in this field. Undergraduate students frequently perceive the culture within STEM fields as highly competitive and exclusionary, which can exacerbate stress and negatively impact mental wellness ( Seymour and Hewitt, 1997 ; Seymour and Hunter, 2019 ). Numerous studies have shown that STEM students describe the classroom culture as chilly and competitive, with many students believing courses are designed to “weed out” students—all of these are major reasons students leave STEM disciplines (e.g., Strenta et al., 1993 ; Seymour, 1995 ; Seymour and Hewitt, 1997 ; Suresh, 2006 ; Shapiro and Sax, 2011 ; Geisinger and Raman, 2013 ; Sanabria and Penner, 2017 ; Seymour and Hunter, 2019 ; González-Pérez et al., 2022 ). Teaching strategies like curved-grading ( Schinske and Tanner, 2014 ), unapproachable instructors ( Strenta et al., 1993 ; Seymour, 1995 ), and a heavy work load ( Seymour, 1995 ; Jensen et al., 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ) can foster feelings of isolation and competition amongst students, which can impact their mental health by increasing stress and anxiety. Introductory courses are also often designed to “weed out” students and can negatively impact students’ mental health and influence them to leave the major ( Seymour and Hewitt, 1997 ; Seymour and Hunter, 2019 ). This “weed out” culture in introductory STEM courses disproportionately impacts students from systematically excluded groups, who are less likely to pass these courses even with similar academic preparation and intent to major in STEM, ultimately reducing the diversity of students in STEM degree programs ( Hatfield et al., 2022 ). Student anxiety in introductory biology courses can also decrease students’ persistence in their major and lower academic performance, which has disproportionate impacts on women ( England et al., 2017 ; England et al., 2019 ). Even more concerning, students from marginalized groups, such as women, LGBTQIA+ students, and students of color, have to navigate harassment, discrimination, and microaggressions in STEM settings ( McGee, 2016 ; McGee et al., 2017 ; McGee, 2021 ; Fitzgerald-Russell and Kowalske, 2023 ; Reidy and Wood, 2023 )—all of which undoubtedly affect mental health and wellness. Research environments represent another type of learning environment that can negatively impact undergraduate students’ mental health. For example, Cooper et al. (2020) found that in life science research settings, factors such as failure, mistakes, and unsupportive mentors can exacerbate depression among undergraduate researchers. These challenges, compounded by the high-pressure environment characterized by STEM disciplines, highlight the urgent need to investigate undergraduate life science students’ mental health more comprehensively. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated mental health challenges among undergraduate students ( Son et al., 2020 ; Fruehwirth et al., 2021 ; Lee et al., 2021 ; Zarowski et al., 2024 ), particularly in online science courses ( Mohammed et al., 2021 ; Busch et al., 2022 ; Mohammed et al., 2022 ). Factors such as technology issues, social isolation, online exam proctoring, and difficulties in course structure contributed to increased anxiety and depression in online science courses ( Mohammed et al., 2021 ; Busch et al., 2022 ). Vulnerable student populations, including women, LGBTQIA+ students, and those facing financial instability, were disproportionately affected ( Mohammed et al., 2021 ; Busch et al., 2022 ). The amplified mental health challenges among science students during the pandemic highlight the importance of STEM instructors addressing modifiable course factors, such as exam proctoring and structural issues, to help reduce stressors and improve student wellness. Indeed, instructors can play a pivotal role in supporting students’ mental health and wellness by leveraging their frequent contact time with students in the classroom ( Di Placito-De Rango, 2018 ; Hsu and Goldsmith, 2021 ). Instructors are uniquely positioned to identify students who may be struggling and to share information about campus mental health resources ( Di Placito-De Rango, 2018 ). Many undergraduate students remain unaware of the mental health resources available to them on campus, emphasizing a critical gap in the dissemination of essential support services ( Eisenberg et al., 2007 ; Dobmeier et al., 2013 ; Kalkbrenner et al., 2022 ). Although many universities disseminate information about available mental health services, it is often buried within broad announcements with other important topics, such as registration and housing, reducing their visibility and impact. Instructors can help bridge this communication gap by dedicating a few minutes during a class to remind students about these resources—a simple yet effective way to support their wellness without requiring expertise in mental health ( Di Placito-De Rango, 2018 ; Hsu and Goldsmith, 2021 ). While we recognize that STEM instructors are not (nor should be) experts in mental health, they have a unique opportunity to identify students who are experiencing mental health struggles, offer support, and connect students to mental health resources ( Di Placito-De Rango, 2018 ). This need for proactive support is particularly critical in STEM disciplines, where the “weed out” culture and competitive course environments can worsen students’ mental health and wellness ( Seymour and Hunter, 2019 ; Jensen et al., 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ). By fostering a supportive atmosphere and normalizing conversations about mental health, STEM instructors can help counter these pressures and promote student wellness in a field where students may feel particularly vulnerable. Despite a rise in the use of mental health services among college students ( Lipson et al., 2018 ; Reidy and Wood, 2023 ), STEM students, particularly men, are less likely to access these resources ( Kalkbrenner et al., 2022 ; Kalkbrenner and Miceli, 2022 ). The stigma associated with mental health and a heavy workload can hinder students from seeking care ( Jensen et al., 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ). STEM instructors’ small but intentional efforts to normalize mental health conversations can play a crucial role in alleviating these barriers. For example, instructors who outwardly care about mental health can alleviate depression in students enrolled in online science courses ( Busch et al., 2022 ). Depression in undergraduate researchers can also be partially alleviated when faculty mentors share their experiences of failure because it humanizes them and makes them more relatable ( Cooper et al., 2020 ). Despite the potential benefits, recent findings reveal that few STEM instructors disclose their own mental health struggles, even though such transparency may normalize mental health challenges and foster supportive learning environments ( Busch et al., 2024 ). In light of these challenges, understanding how life science students perceive and experience mental health support in their academic environments becomes increasingly crucial. While much of the existing research has provided valuable insights, this study expands the focus beyond specific mental health conditions to explore how majoring in the life sciences is perceived to impact undergraduate students' overall mental health and wellness. Conducted at a diverse, commuter, primarily undergraduate institution, it also brings a fresh perspective by highlighting the experiences of students in an often-underrepresented context. We explored the following research questions: How do undergraduate life science students perceive support for mental health in their STEM classes? How do undergraduate life science students describe how majoring in STEM influences their mental health? How do undergraduate life science students manage their mental health and perceive therapy? We aim to build on existing literature by providing new insights that help educators better support students’ mental health and wellness and create more inclusive, destigmatized environments that prioritize mental health. MATERIALS AND METHODS Institutional Context This study was conducted at a mid-sized, university on the west coast designated as a Hispanic-Serving Institution (HSI), Asian American Native American Pacific Islander Serving Institution (AANAPISI), and a primarily undergraduate institution. Most of the student population are local to the region and commute to campus (85% of students). At the institutional level, undergraduate students at the time of the study were 61% female, 57% Persons Excluded due to Ethnicity or Race (PEERs, students who identify as Black or African American, Hispanic or Latinx, or Native American) ( Asai, 2020 ), 73% students of color (i.e., Black or African American, Asian, Hispanic or Latinx, Native American, Pacific Islander, or any of the these designations), 58% first-generation college students, and 40% transfer students. Participant Recruitment To adhere to institutional guidelines and minimize repeated outreach to the same students across concurrent studies, potential participants were identified by the university's office of Institutional Planning and Analysis. Initially, 510 undergraduate students majoring in the life sciences (i.e., biology, biotechnology) who were enrolled full-time in the Spring 2022 semester were identified—this does not represent all life science majors at the institution as some students were enrolled part-time or not enrolled that semester and thus excluded from the dataset. In June 2022, 255 of these students were randomly selected and invited via email to participate in interviews. These students represented a range of class years (from freshmen to seniors) and were pursuing various life science majors (i.e., biology, biotechnology). The recruitment email explicitly stated that the study aimed to investigate STEM students’ perspectives and experiences with mental health and wellness, as well as how STEM instructors talk about mental health and wellness in class. Twenty students consented to the study and completed interviews. Students received a $25 gift card for their time participating in the interviews. This study was determined exempt by the Institutional Review Board at California State University San Marcos (#1898646). Participants provided written consent to participate in the study. Interviews We used a semistructured interview approach to allow flexibility in the conversation and allow the interviewer to follow-up on any responses given by the interviewee that may lead to important information that related to our research questions ( Longhurst, 2003 ). A.N. and M.M.R. codeveloped the interview protocol, which was guided by our research questions aimed at understanding undergraduate life science students’ perceptions and experiences related to mental health and wellness. While the interview participants consisted of life science students, the interview protocol explicitly asked students to reflect on their experiences with STEM instructors across disciplines, including chemistry, physics, and biology, as life science majors at the institution under study take courses across these STEM disciplines. The initial interview protocol questions explored how students perceive support for mental health in their STEM classes, how they describe the influence of majoring in STEM on their mental health, and the strategies they use to manage their mental health (i.e., checking-in on their mental health, coping, and self-care). No formal definition of mental health was provided to participants during the interviews to allow them the freedom to discuss their mental health in their own terms, capturing a broad spectrum of experiences and avoiding bias toward specific interpretations such as clinical diagnoses. To validate the interview protocol, author (A.N.) conducted practice interviews with eight undergraduate students. The interview protocol was revised after these interviews to ensure questions were not misinterpreted by the participant until no further revisions were necessary. During the interview, students were invited to share as much as they were comfortable with and could skip any questions they did not want to discuss. After the interview, participants were asked if there was anything else they wished to elaborate on or discuss, then they were given a flyer with a list of mental health resources on campus and in the broader community. Interviews ranged from 19 to 91 min (average of 38 min) and all interviews were conducted by the same researcher (A.N). The final interview protocol can be found in the Supplemental Materials. Given that it was summer, and most students commute to the university for classes, students had the option to participate in the interview in person or on Zoom. All interviews were audio recorded, transcribed using the Otter.ai platform ( https://otter.ai ), and double checked by A.N. While checking the transcript, all identifying information (e.g., specific classes, names of individuals, and undergraduate research programs) were removed to protect participant confidentiality. After transcripts were deidentified, each interviewee was assigned a pseudonym not related to their identity. To characterize the student population that participated in interviews, participants were invited to fill out a demographics form after the interview. The demographic form collected data on participants’ major, year in college, transfer status, college generation status, socioeconomic background, race and ethnicity, sexual orientation, disability status, and gender (see Supplemental Materials). Students filled the question about their year in college based on the year they just completed since interviews were done over summer. Interview Analysis Our aim was to gain insight into how majoring in the life sciences impacted undergraduate students’ mental health and the extent to which they felt their mental health was supported by STEM instructors. Thus, we used inductive coding to analyze the interview transcripts because this approach allows themes to emerge organically from the data, providing a deeper understanding of students’ experiences and perceptions without being constrained by preconceived categories or hypotheses. All interviews were coded by two members of the research team (A.N. and M.M.R.) who consulted with author R.F.A. when there were discrepancies in coding. In the first phase of coding, each researcher (A.N., R.F.A., and M.M.R.) individually read and took analytical notes on the same two randomly selected transcripts within MAXQDA ( Birks and Mills, 2015 ). During this phase, researchers took detailed notes and memos to document key ideas shared by participants that directly addressed our research questions. Following this individual coding stage, the researchers convened to compare notes derived from the two interview transcripts and discussed initial emergent ideas they deemed significant from each interview ( Saldaña, 2021 ). This collaborative effort resulted in the creation of an initial codebook that was comprised of common, salient coconstructed codes (i.e., ideas) developed by the researchers that captured the main ideas of these two interviews. Similar codes were organized into themes that reflected the ideas shared by participants. Each researcher then used the initial codebook to independently code an additional four transcripts. For each transcript, A.N. and M.M.R. held meetings to review interview transcripts, discuss potential new themes or codes to be incorporated into the codebook, fine tune the descriptions of emergent themes, and collaboratively establish precise, operational definitions for each code's identification and application. The objective was to validate the existence of identified themes and uncover any additional emergent themes that might appear in the transcripts. A.N. and M.M.R. continued to revise the codebook until both were satisfied that it accurately represented all emergent themes from the initial six interview transcripts, which resulted in modifications to the initial codebook. At this phase, the researchers finalized the initial codebook, ensuring it accurately captured the emergent themes from the first six transcripts. In the second phase of coding, A.N. and M.M.R. then individually coded six randomly selected interview transcripts using the initial codebook, reaching a total of 12 coded interviews (60% of all transcripts). We used Cohen's Kappa in MAXQDA to calculate an interrater reliability of 0.85 for these six interviews ( Brennan and Prediger, 1981 ). Subthemes with substantial discrepancies (below 70% similarity) were jointly reviewed by A.N. and M.M.R. Revisions to the codebook were made to provide clarity for excerpts of text where researchers had discrepancies, which resulted in the final codebook. After finalizing the codebook, the researchers revisited the original 12 transcripts to ensure that any revisions made to the codebook were consistently applied, ensuring that all transcripts were analyzed using the final codebook. Data saturation for the dataset was reached after coding these 12 interviews, as no new codes or themes emerged in the remaining transcripts. One researcher (A.N.) coded the remaining eight interviews (40% of all transcripts). All identified themes are reported in the results section. The final codebook is in the Supplemental Materials. We do not present the exact counts of how many participants identified each theme because it may not adequately represent the broader context or significance of those themes in a larger, more diverse group of individuals. Author Positionality The author who conducted the interviews (A.N) has a background in STEM and was actively engaged in campus-based research activities at the time of study. Some of the participants were affiliated with the same research programs as A.N, fostering a sense of familiarity that contributed to a more conducive interview environment. A.N disclosed her own challenges as an undergraduate student in STEM courses and the associated impacts on her mental health to create a comfortable space for participants to share personal anecdotes. Additionally, A.N and M.M.R formulated the codebook based on their first-hand experiences in STEM classrooms and individual experiences navigating mental health challenges as people who hold marginalized identities in STEM. Students’ Perceptions of Support for their Mental Health and Wellness in the Life Sciences STEM Classes Often Lack Adequate and Frequent Mental Health Support and Policies. Overall, most students shared that their STEM instructors rarely mentioned the campus mental health services available to them and even fewer talked about mental health openly in the classroom. Numerous students voiced that their STEM instructors briefly mentioned mental health services on campus solely on the first day of classes, often using the syllabus as the primary method to communicate these resources. Many students noted that while the syllabus may be electronically available, few refer back to it after the first week of classes. Melissa: “From what I can remember, it's usually just in the beginning, I don't really remember any of them mentioning [mental health services] after that. Because I think they just assume we talked about it, and it's on the syllabus. So, you can look at it whenever you know. I don't know what happens to my syllabus after the first day.” Meghan: “I did not remember them mentioning [mental health services] at all, to be honest.” Many students described how their STEM instructors’ policies and expectations disregarded their mental health, and that the prioritization of high academic standards often overshadowed the wellness of students. Neil: “Maybe [STEM instructors bring up mental health] in the syllabus, but there's never been any leniency for late work or anything if you have a mental health breakdown or anything.” Jeanette: “Honestly, they don't really [talk about mental health], I've had maybe two or three professors out of my entire time here that have focused on [mental health], and been like if you are struggling, like reach out and have taken it very seriously. The rest of my professors though, haven't really brought it up at all. And not [that] they haven't really focused on it and it's more if you're not meeting the deadline, then maybe this isn't for you.” While campus mental health services are available to all students, the effectiveness of their communication by STEM instructors varies greatly, often limited to brief mentions on the first day of class. Students expressed a need for continuous awareness and easier accessibility to these resources throughout their academic journey, highlighting that mere mentions in the syllabus or the first day of class are insufficient. Further, some instructors had classroom policies that were unsupportive of students’ mental health (e.g., rigid attendance policies, dismissing requests for extensions without considering individual circumstances). Life Science Students’ Mental Health is Shaped by Instructor Interactions, with Peer Support as a Resource When Instructors are Unsupportive. Students indicated that their perceptions of STEM instructors’ support for mental health were shaped by interpersonal interactions. Instructor behaviors that were perceived by students as supportive of their mental health included granting assignment extensions, facilitating study groups, prioritizing mental health discussions in the classroom, acknowledging global events, and humanizing themselves through personal conversations. Students also appreciated it when instructors informed them about the university's mental health services and discussed mental health throughout the semester. Specifically, some students shared examples of how their STEM instructors were supportive of mental health in either one-on-one interactions or directly to the class. Ricky: “I've actually confided in [instructors] about a few occasions, and they've been really, really good on checking in with me and checking in [with] how I'm doing everything and working with me to try to lessen the burden and stress.” Sonya: “The professors that kind of emphasize in the beginning of the semester, and throughout the semester, as I noticed that students are stressing out about deadlines and stuff, those professors are like, ‘Hey keep reiterating that, talk to me. Don't worry about hard deadlines, stuff like that.’ Those types of professors, it does make it easier for me to try to approach them.” While some students shared positive experiences with their instructors being supportive and understanding of their mental health, many more students offered experiences of instructors who were not supportive of their mental wellness. Students described unsupportive instructors as engaging in actions such as criticizing students for receiving poor grades, lacking accommodations, flexibility, or understanding, being discouraging or condescending, seeming detached from their role, leaving students feeling pressured to learn concepts independently, and “weeding out” students. Several students described experiences where their STEM instructors were not accessible or seemed detached from their roles, which caused significant stress for students who sought their support but felt dismissed or unsupported. Maureen: “Some professors tend to be closed and it's just a job to them. So, once they're out of the classroom, they don't respond to emails, they don't talk to students, things like that. Those are the ones that are really stressful.” Jeanette: “My chemistry instructor my freshman year, my first semester made me feel so stupid for asking a simple question. Because he's like, ‘You should already know this.’ And I'm like, ‘I just got here, I shouldn't know anything.’” Maureen's experience of professors being unapproachable and treating teaching as “just a job” was echoed by many students who felt unsupported when seeking help. Students described instructors who would leave the classroom without engaging further, fail to respond to emails, and avoid interactions with students altogether, creating significant stress. Similarly, Jeanette shared how her chemistry professor made her feel “stupid” for asking a question, telling her, “You should already know this.” These types of interactions were common and left students feeling dismissed and hesitant to seek support, undermining their confidence and exacerbating the challenges of navigating rigorous STEM coursework. Experiencing a lack of support from instructors often caused stress and anxiety and drove some students to seek support from their peers. Multiple students conveyed that the stressfulness of STEM classes fostered a strong sense of community among peers that provided encouragement and motivation. Nina: “I think a lot of my STEM friends or people that I take the same class with, we tend to check on each other a lot, because we know how stressful it is. And you know, how hard of a balance it is with school and with personal life. So, I think we check on each other a lot.” Francis: “We [are] all kind of try supporting each other, not just academically, but hey, like, if you need to talk or if you need to go out, let's go out, let's do this, or just tell me what's going on, you know, but we do help each other a lot. And I think being STEM majors, especially because it's so difficult, we know ourselves that it is difficult, so we try reaching out as often as we can.” Students’ perceptions of their STEM instructors’ support for mental health were profoundly influenced by interpersonal interactions, where supportive behaviors such as providing assignment extensions and openly discussing mental health positively shaped students’ academic experiences. Conversely, students who encountered instructors who were critical or dismissive often felt alienated and stressed, relying instead on peer support to navigate being a life science major. Impacts of Majoring in the Life Sciences on Student Mental Health and Wellness Life Science Students’ Mental Health is Impacted by Academic Pressure, Extracurricular Challenges, and Competitive Course Culture. Students shared that there were multiple experiences specific to majoring in the life sciences that impacted their mental health positively and negatively. Participant responses from this theme included their experiences with academic pressure in STEM courses, speaking about the pressure to seek out extracurricular activities to be competitive for postgraduate opportunities, and describing the culture of STEM as toxic and competitive. Several students reflected that the pressure to perform well in their STEM courses, which were characterized by dense content delivery, long lectures, and high workloads dominated by a few high-stakes exams, negatively impacted their mental health and often led to burnout by the end of the term. Meghan: “[Studying and preparing for STEM classes] was just definitely very, very stressful. It was just so much to learn, like I learned cannot believe it's just two hours of just constant chapters. And it's just so much information going through your head that it kind of felt like a blur. Like when I would leave class, I literally forget everything that I just learned. So, it's just a lot of information, to be honest, it's just kind of stressful to remember everything [and] get ready for the exam, the exams, oh my god, the exams being like 75% of my grade. That was honestly ridiculous for me, like, oh my God, it was three exams and it'd be 75% of my grade. So, it was just so much pressure. If you didn't do well on the exam, you're not gonna do well in the class, point blank, period. There was just no way to improve your grades if you just did not do well on your first exam. So, it's definitely stressful.” Maureen: “I feel like so excited about starting a new semester but once I'm at the end of the semester, I'm just like, ‘Oh my gosh, I can't do this anymore.’ … At the end of the semester, I end up getting really burned out and then I either don't perform as well as I would or I just study so hard that after everything is done, it just kind of blacks out, I don't remember anything that happens.” In addition to feeling burnout from the workload required by the STEM courses students were enrolled in, some students described how the workload made it challenging to pursue and/or balance extracurriculars—especially for students with familial responsibilities or those who worked to fund their education. Nina: “There's just so many classes I have to take, especially as a pre med. There's just so many things I had to balance with extracurriculars, you know, volunteering, and school and personal life, because I have two younger sisters. So I have to take care of [them] pretty frequently. So if my parents are both working, it's just been, I don't know, it's just been a lot. Just a lot of stress in general with school, but, you know, [I'm] trying to get through it.” Yet some students discussed that engaging in extracurriculars gave them a chance to reconnect with their purpose and that they found joy in activities related to their major and career goals. Rhonda: “I would say [the STEM outreach event had a positive impact on my mental health] just because I'm able to do a lot of things outside of school and everything. So it just makes me happy and not have to always focus on school.” Ricky: “[Research] gives me more interest in the field. Just because instead of just going to class, doing homework, and then you know, reading and then taking notes on stuff. It's definitely re-sparked my interest and, keeps me definitely energetic and excited about my field. So that helps a lot.” Many students described how the competitive atmosphere and “weeder nature” of the STEM courses they were enrolled in caused them stress due to the pressure to perform well academically. Mable: “It's stressful, it definitely is hard to keep myself going. Because I feel like the expectation [with] STEM majors [is] a lot of the times you need to excel and be, you know, that specific, you need to be getting A's [in] your classes in order to get anywhere and you need to be doing all of this kind of research and all of this kind of stuff in order to make it in the STEM field… And I'm not someone who super excels at everything, and… I do well in my classes, I understand the material for the most part, and stuff, but it's stressful. It's very, very time consuming. If you're not one of those people who can just hear it twice and know it for the rest of your life, you know. So it takes a lot of work, for sure. It's very, very time consuming and stressful.” Some first-generation college students shared that they felt intimidated by the competitive nature of their STEM courses, which may limit their capacity to participate in class. Meghan: “I just felt so shy to say anything in front of other people who I felt like were better than me. Yeah, it's just very competitive. That's just the one thing [that] definitely affected my mental health, just to speak in class or to raise my hand or be too shy to even answer a question.” Students emphasized how the academic demands of STEM courses negatively impacted their mental health and that prioritizing extracurriculars was challenging but rewarding for students who were able to make that time. Lastly, students discussed how the competitive nature of STEM courses fostered stress and anxiety. Balancing Caregiving and Familial Responsibilities Negatively Impacts Life Science Students’ Mental Health and Academic Success. Beyond sharing how college-specific experiences affected their mental health, students described how caregiving, familial responsibilities, and jobs influenced their ability to navigate the demands of being a life science major. Their stories shed light on the complex interplay between academic pressures and personal challenges, highlighting the emotional and mental toll that balancing these responsibilities can take and how it hinders their ability to fully commit to being a life science major. Janet: “Having the idea of not even be able to help out my parents with the rent and stuff like that. And kind of doubting college, doubting everything and being like, when is the time that I'll see something that will actually help me and be stable and have income. Cuz I mean, money is money, you need it in order to live in and have a roof, food, stuff like that.” Camille: “Coming into the third semester, which would be the second year, I actually went through something very difficult like a family situation. I had a parent that got deported. So it was definitely hard. I failed. That was my first time ever failing the class. It was just so hard for me to catch up because I had to move out of my home the second day. I was trying to go to school every day, trying to help my mom with my sister, trying to work as well because my dad was the main provider. So, trying to have money to help and support my family. It was definitely very hard because of that. And because I don't, at least for me, I know everyone has a different opinion about immigration and stuff like that so I didn't really feel like I could talk to anyone about it or talk to my professors about my situation. I don't feel comfortable sharing that type of stuff. In the moment I wouldn't feel comfortable. It was very hard to do everything [with what] was going on. It was hard mentally too because I was trying to catch up trying to make sure I stayed on top of my homework and my classes. And it was just hard to be in class too because I would be in class, but at the same time in my head I would be thinking of like oh what's going to happen next? Like I'm also a DACA so it's like all these thoughts came into my head and came to my mind. It was just a very hard semester with that going on. And then the next semester was COVID. So it was a hard situation with another hard situation.” Students described how these challenges, such as caregiving, family emergencies, and needing to work, led to doubts about whether they should continue college and their life science degree, difficulties staying on top of homework and coursework, and struggles catching up after falling behind. Camille's reflection that it was “hard to be in class” captures the weight of balancing these responsibilities (e.g., caring for younger siblings, providing financially for the household) while managing the demands of a life science degree. Challenges such as financial stress and major family crises not only strained students’ mental wellness, but it also directly impacted their ability to succeed in their STEM courses, with some facing severe academic setbacks like failing a course. While these experiences are not exclusive to life science students, the context of their academic journey in STEM added layers of complexity to how students navigated these challenges. Academic Pressures Limit Students’ Mental Health Awareness and Self-Care, Forcing Students to Rely on Coping Mechanisms. As students have discussed, the pressure they feel to achieve at a high academic level in STEM can affect their mental health. During the interviews, students were asked to describe how aware they were of changes to their mental health, how they cope with these changes, and what self-care strategies they use to mitigate negative impacts to their mental health. Many students shared that the rigor of STEM and the pressure to do well academically limited their time to check-in on their mental health. Nina: “I don't think I'm really that aware of my mental health. I'm just kind of bad. I just kind of let it pass. I'll just be like, ‘Oh, it'll probably go away and I'm like [in] a day or two I'll be fine.’ Just because I'm so busy. I don't really have time to evaluate my mental health each day. Because I'm busy all the time. And then once I get home you know, usually I just study and then go to sleep so I don't really have that time to check in but probably should.” In addition to having a limited time to check-in on their mental health, some students also reflected on their ability to use coping mechanisms to respond to changes to their mental health. In our study, we have defined coping mechanisms as behaviors that arise in response to negative impacts to an individual's mental health (adapted from Folkman and Moskowitz, 2004 ). Students reported using a variety of coping mechanisms including relying on support from friends and family, removing themselves from situations that had negative impacts to their mental health, using therapy, and taking time away from school and academic responsibilities. While some students reported using coping mechanisms, many students shared that they were unprepared to come into college and were lacking coping mechanisms to help deal with the stressors that come with being a life science student. Glen: “So the first semester at college, I was [at] my worst. This [was] the lowest point of my life, not just with college, but also my personal life. And that was also when I took the intro to chemistry class that I got a C in, and I hated it. And then after that, I took [intro] to chemistry two and I did good, [and] after that took O chem and I got an A and I was like, ‘Alright, I'm solid now.’ But looking back… I feel like I wasn't even comfortable talking about my emotions back when I first came to university. But I think at a certain point, I realized like, ‘Okay, I have to start talking to people about this stuff.’ … I think [I can handle emotions more effectively now] because I was able to develop coping mechanisms that helped. It was the university/personal life that caused the stressors that made me have to develop them in the first place. But I think it was a good thing, because I was able to come out on the brighter side.” In contrast to coping mechanisms, we defined self-care as ongoing behaviors that individuals engaged in to minimize negative impacts on their mental health (adapted from Godfrey et al., 2011 ). Self-care strategies shared by students included exercise, journaling, spending time with loved ones, and disconnecting from technology. Many students expressed they struggled to prioritize self-care over their academic responsibilities, revealing the challenging time trade-off between self-care and the demands of school. Mable: “[School] definitely impacts [self-care] and how time consuming everything can be. Because I feel like I don't have time for personal stuff. Or I feel like if I go out for a couple hours with my friends, that's a couple hours I should have spent studying. And then I could have done better on that test. Yeah, it can be a guilt trip for sure.” Students indicated that the intense academic pressures often conflicted with maintaining their mental health and wellness, leading to limited awareness and management of their mental health. Many students reported a lack of time to engage in self-reflection or self-care due to the demanding nature of their studies. To cope, students used a variety of mechanisms such as support networks, therapy, and time away from academic stressors. Despite the challenges, some students managed to find a balance by prioritizing self-care activities and setting boundaries around their academic responsibilities, which significantly helped them maintain mental wellness amidst their rigorous academic schedules. Life Science Students Recognize Therapy's Benefits but Stigma and Time Constraints Prevent Use. In the interview, we also asked students about their general perceptions of therapy. While some students were open to the idea of going to therapy, many shared that prioritizing it or carving out time in their schedules was not something they did or that the stigma of therapy prevented them from using this resource. Sonya: “You know, I feel like I would [go to therapy] if I had the time. So that's the hardest thing, trying to balance school and work and then try and go spend time with friends and so on. Yeah, it really is difficult to find that time” However, once students used mental health resources, they began to understand its crucial role in their overall wellness and academic success. Many realized that seeking support not only improved their mental health, but also transformed their initial hesitancy about therapy into a sense of excitement about it—effectively breaking down the stigma. Melissa: “The only way I [could] really process and share those feelings and whatnot was through counseling and I feel like it did help for a bit.” Jeanette: “I think the biggest thing that helped me with it was therapy. And learning what it is, and how it comes on. And also finding triggers of how [my mental health] switches has definitely helped me [because] up until that point, I had no idea. And then once I started going, it helped me figure it out more. I was hesitant at first. Now, I love going, it's a great part of my week. But at the beginning, it was like, the stigma around it. But since I've been in it, it's been good.” Some students’ perspectives on therapy were shaped by personal experiences, while others were influenced by their cultural backgrounds and familial attitudes toward mental health care. Many students, especially those raised in as students described “traditional households,” perceived a stigma surrounding mental health therapy because expressing feelings and seeking external support are often discouraged. Maureen: “So I have grown up in a very conservative and traditional Vietnamese household. And a lot of, I don't know if it's most Asian cultures, but for sure, Southeast Asians, they tend to really hate mental [health] therapy and things like that. Like, you can't talk about your feelings… You're not allowed to share anything outside of the household. You can't talk to anybody about this stuff. So, I grew up very strictly, not being able to talk about everything. And I think that's probably where the bottling up comes from. But I'm really trying to not deal with that, because that makes everything way more stressful.” While many life science students recognized the potential benefits of therapy for managing their mental health, barriers such as busy schedules and cultural and/or familial stigmas often prevented them from seeking help. Those who did engage with mental health resources, however, generally found significant value in the support and insights gained from therapy. Life Science Degrees are Worth the Struggle Although this was not directly asked in our research questions, one of the emergent themes echoed by students was the belief that life science degrees are “worth the struggle.” Despite students reporting how majoring in the life sciences has impacted their mental health and wellness, many students shared they believed the degrees were rewarding. Students valued being in the life sciences due to factors such as the applicability of the field and the enjoyment of learning science concepts. Students also described how they gained a sense of accomplishment when they observed tangible growth in their learning, which strengthens the idea that hard work pays off. Jamie: “Well, particularly because I have one more year, I'm starting to see the light at the end of the tunnel. I've worked really hard to get to this point. So I'm starting to feel like, okay, all the hard work is adding up to something. I'm going to graduate and I'm going to be able to move on and do something with all of my schooling. Just thinking now that I'm getting close to the end, there's a reason I'm doing everything. And so it's much easier to just find the extra time and get the extra studying and all that. I think it definitely took to this point I had, I've had some very stressful years. But getting to this point, I'm like, you know what, I'm gonna get this done. I'm gonna move on. It's gonna be really great.” Diana: “I think [majoring in biology has] been very stressful, but I think it was worth it, because I'm doing something I really love to do.” This sentiment reflects a common experience shared by students in our study: students are driven to pursue a life science degree because of their passion, even when faced with significant impacts to their mental health. Majoring in the life sciences is difficult and stressful, but Diana sees the worth of experiencing it all because it's “something [she] really loves to do.” DISCUSSION To investigate students’ perception of support for mental health and wellness in STEM courses, how majoring in the life science impacts their mental health and wellness, and how they manage their mental health, we conducted semistructured interviews with 20 undergraduate students in the life sciences. Our study contributes novel insights by examining mental health in life science undergraduates within the unique context of a primarily undergraduate, commuter institution. For example, the intersection of academic pressure with external responsibilities, such as caregiving and employment, was particularly pronounced among our participants, many of whom expressed limited access to support systems. These findings emphasize the need to tailor mental health interventions to the unique challenges faced by students at primarily undergraduate institutions, where students often navigate academic demands alongside significant personal responsibilities. By focusing on these underrepresented contexts, our study expands the existing literature and offers critical insights into how majoring in the life sciences affects students’ mental health and wellness at a primarily undergraduate institution with a diverse student body. Below, we discuss our findings with an emphasis on recommendations for instructors to strengthen mental health and wellness support for undergraduates in STEM courses. Encourage Open Communication about Mental Health Throughout the Semester Our findings indicate that many students were largely unfamiliar with campus mental health services, as STEM instructors seldom communicated these resources beyond a cursory mention the first day of class or in the syllabus. Similarly, Vega and Meaders (2023) found that in a survey of &gt;3000 undergraduate students enrolled in life science courses at a large research-intensive university, few reported that their instructors communicated about health and wellness services beyond the first day of class or syllabus. Given that instructor communication about these services is positively associated with student sense of belonging and may motivate students to use resources ( Vega and Meaders, 2023 ), the limited discussion about these services may widen existing equity gaps in STEM classrooms. For example, college students from more privileged backgrounds are more likely to be aware of and use campus resources that support their success ( Armstrong and Hamilton, 2013 ). In the present study, students valued instructors who engaged in open discussions about mental health in the classroom, as it reinforced the perception of their instructors as supportive and approachable. Similarly, research in the life sciences demonstrates that instructors who openly address mental health can alleviate depression in students ( Busch et al., 2022 ) and that faculty mentors who share their own experiences of failure foster relatability and support for undergraduate researchers ( Cooper et al., 2020 ). While Wright et al. (2023) found that engineering students want their instructors to address mental health more openly and show genuine concern for student mental wellness, our novel findings extend this insight by showing that students in the life sciences also share these desires. This broader scope highlights that students in various STEM fields—not just engineering—value mental health discussions and see them as a critical component of supportive teaching. Conversely, when instructors were condescending about grades or student behaviors, students felt that their mental health was disregarded, leading to a diminished sense of care and support. This perception aligns with recent research that found students perceive condescending instructor language as promoting a negative or unsupportive learning environment ( Ovid et al., 2021 ; Harper and Thiry, 2024 ). We believe instructors can leverage noncontent language (i.e., “Instructor Talk”) ( Seidel et al., 2015 ; Harrison et al., 2019 ) to intentionally communicate about mental health services and foster a classroom environment that supports student mental health and wellness. This intentional approach may enhance students’ perceptions of their instructors and hopefully mitigate the negative impacts of unsupportive behaviors ( Harper and Thiry, 2024 ). Instructor behaviors that demonstrate support for students’ mental health may also increase instructor immediacy—the verbal and nonverbal communication that reduces the perceived social distance between students and instructors ( Witt et al., 2004 ). By actively showing concern and empathy, instructors can create a more approachable atmosphere, making students feel more comfortable seeking help with both academic and personal struggles. Further, students perceive open and caring STEM instructors as supportive, which may encourage their persistence in STEM majors ( Harper et al., 2019 ). We believe such intentional language and behaviors not only promotes a positive classroom climate, but also helps mitigate the mental health challenges students face in the life sciences. Instructors can play a crucial role in supporting students’ mental health by fostering an approachable environment and proactively addressing the challenges life science students face, including the competitive nature of the field. As our findings, along with other recent research ( Denton et al., 2020 ; Woodhead et al., 2021 ; Jensen et al., 2023 ), suggest, when students perceive a lack of support for their mental health from instructors, they often turn to their peers for emotional support. While peer support can be valuable, it is often a substitute for, rather than a complement to, the support students need from instructors. For example, peer support may not fully address the mental health challenges STEM students face due to the competitive and demanding nature of field ( Seymour and Hunter, 2019 ). In such environments, STEM instructors play a crucial role in fostering a supportive and approachable atmosphere, as the lack of approachability from instructors may discourage students from seeking support with their coursework or mental health challenges. We suggest that STEM instructors take proactive steps to address this by integrating discussions about mental health resources throughout the semester, rather than limiting them to the syllabus or first class meeting. These discussions can be brief, lasting just 1–5 min, with students in the present study emphasizing the value of repeated reminders about the available mental health resources. Additionally, instructors could provide reminders before stressful periods, such as exams or project deadlines, via the course management system, and when feasible, invite guest speakers from campus mental health and wellness services to make brief classroom announcements (e.g., 5–10 min) to further normalize these conversations. Mitigate Academic Pressure and Support Students Holistically The intense workload of life science majors, coupled with the competitive or “weeder” culture of these fields and the added demands of extracurriculars and familial pressures, can have a detrimental impact on students’ mental health. Our findings underscore how the academic pressure and competitive environment life science students must navigate negatively affects their mental health. While our findings align with previous research ( Seymour and Hewitt, 1997 ; Seymour and Hunter, 2019 ; Asghar et al., 2023 ; Jensen et al., 2023 ), they are novel because most studies focus on research-intensive universities, whereas this study was conducted at a primarily undergraduate, commuter institution with a diverse student population (73% students of color, 58% first-generation, and 61% female). Thus, we offer new insights into how mental health challenges emerge and affect the wellness of specific student populations within our institution's unique context. The impacts of academic pressure are particularly pronounced among students of color (e.g., McGee and Stovall, 2015 ; McGee, 2016 ; McGee et al., 2017 ; McGee, 2020, 2021 ), who make up 70% of our study participants. The relentless pressure not only exacerbates stress and anxiety but also contributes to feelings of isolation and marginalization, especially among students of color who already face systemic barriers and biases within academic environments ( McGee and Stovall, 2015 ; McGee, 2016 , McGee et al., 2017 ; Harper and Thiry, 2024 ). Instructors can alleviate academic pressure by implementing more flexible grading policies and restructuring assessments so that grades are not predominantly determined by exams, as these can lead to stress and burnout in students. For example, implementing a 48-h universal grace period for assignments using a course management system can help students manage last-minute difficulties; this approach, used by the senior author (M.M.R.) in her large-enrollment biology courses, requires minimal extra work and has been effective with few students requesting additional exceptions. Additionally, programming the course management system to automatically drop the lowest 1–2 assignment or quiz grades can provide a safety net for students who may face mental health challenges during the semester. Restructuring assessments by incorporating weekly quizzes that are automatically graded through the course management system can further reduce the weight of exams, helping to distribute grades more evenly and lower stress for students. This approach may also help address binary gender equity gaps in introductory biology courses, which are often exacerbated by high-stakes exams ( Ballen et al., 2017 ; Cotner and Ballen, 2017 ). Further, scheduling automated announcements during stressful periods, such as exams or project deadlines, reminds students of available mental health resources and reinforces that their instructor is available to support them. Providing deadline flexibility, fostering collaborative, inclusive classroom environments, and integrating these scalable strategies can support students’ mental health, especially those balancing external responsibilities or navigating systemic barriers to their success in the life sciences. In addition to academic pressure, students felt compelled to engage in extracurricular activities to enhance their competitiveness for postgraduate STEM career opportunities, even when their time was limited—a finding consistent with Jensen et al. (2023) . Those who did participate in activities, like research, found them fulfilling and beneficial to their mental health. Furthermore, many students reported that familial crises and responsibilities, such as caregiving and financial obligations, further exacerbated their mental health and wellness. These additional burdens strained students’ ability to focus on and prioritize their life science degree, sometimes leading to significant setbacks in their academic journey. Our findings underscore the crucial role instructors can play in supporting students by offering understanding and flexibility to minimize impacts on their academic progress. In fact, when instructors acknowledge students’ personal challenges and express empathy, it can provide a much-needed support system that enables students to navigate difficult circumstances ( Harper et al., 2019 ). Despite the numerous ways majoring in the life sciences impacted their mental health, students demonstrated remarkable resilience and used a variety of strategies to manage their mental health and persist in their academic journeys. While passion for their field was a key driver of persistence and could help students reframe their struggles as worthwhile, this same passion sometimes led to overwork and self-neglect ( Cech, 2021 ). These findings emphasize the critical need for academic environments that promote both persistence and well-being, ensuring students are not only driven to succeed but also supported in maintaining their overall wellness. Promote a Balanced Approach to Academic and Mental Health Priorities Students in the life sciences often found it challenging to allocate time to their mental health, frequently suppressing their emotional needs in favor of academic demands. This tendency mirrors a broader trend identified in studies focused on engineering students, where students often neglected their mental health and wellness in favor of academic priorities in a “keep your head down” mentality ( Jensen et al., 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ). However, our findings expand this pattern to life science majors, revealing that students from various STEM disciplines similarly prioritize academics over mental health, often suppressing emotional struggles to dedicate their limited time to studying. Instructors can better support students’ mental health by acknowledging the individual challenges students face and fostering a classroom environment that sees students as whole individuals rather than just academic performers. By prioritizing empathy and open communication, instructors can create a more supportive and understanding atmosphere that encourages students to balance their academic responsibilities with their wellness. For students who understood the importance of maintaining their mental health through therapy, many identified time constraints and the stigma surrounding therapy as significant barriers to accessing these resources. These barriers similarly deter engineering students from seeking therapeutic support ( Jensen et al., 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ). Students in our study shared that the stigma associated with therapy, whether stemming from personal beliefs or cultural backgrounds, hinders them from pursuing mental health support, a finding consistent with other research ( Cheng et al., 2013 ; Jensen et al., 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ). These findings underscore the critical need for more accessible mental health resources and culturally sensitive interventions in STEM education, ensuring that students are supported in both their academic and mental wellness. Limitations and Future Directions While our study offers valuable insights into undergraduate life science students’ perceptions of mental health support, several limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, our sample of 20 students from a single institution may limit the generalizability of the results to other educational contexts with different campus cultures and resources. Moreover, conducting the study over the summer likely restricted participation to those who were enrolled in summer classes or engaged in on-campus research. To mitigate this limitation and capture a more diverse group of participants, we offered the flexibility of completing interviews either in person or via Zoom. While several of the participants were affiliated with the same on-campus summer research program as the first author, our analysis identified similar themes across all participants, regardless of their affiliation with the research program. Notably, few participants discussed how their mental health was affected by their involvement in research; instead, they focused on their experiences in STEM courses and majors. Second, no formal definition of mental health was provided to participants during the interviews. This decision allowed for a broader exploration of students’ perceptions and experiences but may have introduced variability in how participants interpreted the term. Despite this potential variability, many students consistently discussed their mental health in terms of stress, anxiety, burnout, and emotional wellness, suggesting a shared saliency in these themes across participants. Third, we did not systematically examine the influence of demographic factors like gender, race, socioeconomic status, or academic standing, which shape students’ experiences and access to support. Future research should address these variables to provide a more nuanced understanding of how life science undergraduates manage their mental health across identity. Additionally, future research could incorporate quantitative or mixed-methods approaches to offer a broader understanding of the mental health challenges faced by students in the life sciences. Longitudinal studies are also needed to explore how these perceptions and the impact of STEM coursework on mental health change over time and as students move through their degree program from lower division to upper division STEM courses. Finally, our results do not include perspectives from students who left their life science major for various reasons, including impacts to their mental health and wellness. Thus, our results may be biased toward students who have persisted in their degree despite navigating challenges to their mental health. Future studies should evaluate the role that mental health plays in students’ decision to leave their STEM major. Despite these limitations, our study adds to the growing body of literature on mental health in STEM education and reinforces the critical need for ongoing support for student wellness in these demanding fields. CONCLUSION The exclusionary nature of STEM environments is well documented (e.g., Strenta et al., 1993 ; Seymour, 1995 ; Seymour and Hewitt, 1997 ; Suresh, 2006 ; Shapiro and Sax, 2011 ; Geisinger and Raman, 2013 ; Sanabria and Penner, 2017 ; Seymour and Hunter, 2019 ; González-Pérez et al. , 2022 ), yet there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding how both classroom dynamics and external factors impact the mental health of life science students, particularly those from systemically excluded backgrounds. Our study builds on the existing body of knowledge by providing a broader perspective on the mental health challenges faced by undergraduate students in the life sciences, expanding on previous qualitative research that has focused on engineering students ( Jensen et al. , 2023 ; Wright et al., 2023 ). Furthermore, our unique institutional context as a primarily undergraduate, Hispanic-serving, commuter institution with a diverse student body provides fresh insights into how these challenges manifest in underrepresented educational settings. Overall, our findings emphasize the critical need to focus on mental health in STEM education to foster more inclusive and supportive environments in undergraduate STEM classrooms. While some science instructors are aware of how factors such as a competitive environment, academic stress, and juggling responsibilities can affect students’ mental health, many report feeling ill-equipped to provide necessary support ( Sanders et al. , 2024a ; Sanders et al. , 2024b ). This disconnect illustrates the importance of equipping STEM instructors with the tools and knowledge to better support their students’ mental health. Our findings highlight the urgent need for STEM instructors to incorporate discussions about mental health into their classrooms and adopt supportive attitudes, addressing the current lack of attention to students’ mental health and overall wellness. As Melissa, one of our participants, articulated, there is a crucial need for greater compassion and understanding from instructors, who must take the mental health of their students seriously to effectively support those grappling with stress, anxiety, and imposter syndrome. We may be losing students in the life sciences who are poised to make significant discoveries because of the lack of focus on students’ mental health and overall wellness. Moving forward, it is essential that instructors and institutions recognize that student persistence in the life sciences is not solely determined by academic performance, but is also deeply connected to the support systems that help students navigate the challenges inherent in these demanding fields.
