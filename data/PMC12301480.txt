To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Abstract Physical fitness (PF) is an important marker of health and well-being. This study aimed to quantify associations of social environment and physical activity (PA) related factors with different aspects of PF among adolescents. The cross-sectional study included 4925 (51% girls) schoolchildren aged 11–18 years. Participants were tested for PF using the Eurofit battery (covering 8 aspects of PF), with their social environment (parents’ education, family’s and friends’ support for PA), personal motivation for PA, actual PA level, and sports participation status were self-reported. Age- and sex- adjusted body mass index (BMI) cut-points were used to classify weight status as overweight or normal weight. Associations were quantified using hierarchical linear regression analysis. Up to 45% of the variability in PF was explained by BMI and environmental factors. While age, sex, BMI and parents’ education associated with PF most strongly, personal motivation for PA (R 2 = 0.007 on average per aspect of PF), friends’ support for PA (R 2 = 0.009) and especially sports participation (R 2 = 0.014) were also significant correlates of PF. However, familial support for PA and current PA level were typically non-significant correlates. In conclusion, parents’ education, sports participation, personal motivation and friends’ support for PA should be promoted to foster PF of adolescents. Introduction It is widely recognised that low levels of physical activity (PA) associate with low physical fitness (PF) and predispose humans to chronic diseases and compromise well-being. Appropriate levels of PF on its own significantly reduce the risk of developing many modern diseases in later life 1 – 3 . Quite alarming is the fact that cardiovascular pathology can start in childhood 4 . A recent prospective longitudinal study showed that low levels of PF, and particularly low cardiorespiratory fitness, in adolescence was associated with an increased burden of cardiometabolic conditions in later life 5 . Evidence convincingly shows that, compared to non-athletes, high-performance athletes have an increased life-expectancy 6 – 9 and reduced chronic disease burden after sports career discontinuation 10 , 11 which strongly suggests that high levels of PA and PF early in life may be effective means of combating health issues Additionally, PF is also a significant marker of the cognitive performance 12 – 16 and mental health 2 , 17 , 18 thus high levels of PF stand as an important prerequisite for overall well-being. While the general decline in youth PF levels in recent decades 19 – 22 is quite concerning, some studies have suggested that some aspects of PF have not declined or have recently improved in several developed countries 23 , 24 . Overall, there remains a clear need for a better understanding of the factors associated with PF as an important step to reversing the negative trends in youth PF levels. An important factor for good health and PF is undoubtedly level of PA 25 – 29 . Importantly, while overall PA level is universally accepted as critical for good health and well-being, participation in intense organized sports seems to provide superior benefits when compared to leisure time PA 9 , 30 , 31 . In the current study we analysed the self-reported PA (meeting of failing to meet the WHO recommendations) and status of participation in organized sports. Social factors are among the various environmental factors known to be important for PA across various populations 32 . It is possible that the social environment influences PF levels and sports participation and is therefore important but an under-investigated health-related factor. Many environmental factors are significantly associated with PA levels among adults 32 and adolescents 33 including higher levels of leisure time PA which are associated with higher socioeconomical status 34 greater support from family and friends 35 – 38 and higher personal motivation 38 . However, we are not aware of any study that has analysed how the immediate social environment and personal attitudes towards PA are associated with PF levels. In this study we were interested in how various aspects of PF were associated with parents’ education, support for PA by parents and friends, as well as actual levels of PA and sports participation among schoolchildren. The main aim of this study, therefore, was to quantify the associations between social support for PA, motivation for PA, and self-reported PA levels with various aspects of objectively measured PF among adolescents. In addition, we quantified how PF was associated with non-modifiable biological factors like sex and age, as well as weight status and parents’ education. We hypothesized that (ranked here in the order of putative progressive importance) support for PA by family (1) and friends (2), personal attitude towards PA (3), reported PA level (4), and organised sports participation (5) would be significantly associated with PF. In other words, we expected that PA levels per se, as most directly affecting the condition, would be more strongly related to PF than the internal and external motivations for PA, because PA levels may directly affect PF whereas the motivations for PA may not. Even if adolescents spend a large amount of time with their family, we believe that increased social interaction and the influence of friends/peers during adolescence may be more important. Also, compared to overall PA levels, we anticipated that sports participation would be more strongly associated with PF because sport may better challenge the physical abilities assessed by a comprehensive PF test battery. Methods Participants and organisation Apparently healthy adolescents aged 11–18 years from Lithuanian secondary schools ( n = 4925) participated in PF and social environment data collection during their physical education classes. Regular secondary schools were selected from the national registry using nested random sampling. Across all ten counties of Lithuania, at least one school in the county centre and one in the periphery were selected to represent both urban and rural schoolchildren, respectively. In each school, all pupils in grades 5 to 12 were tested, provided they were not restricted from participation in physical education classes (e.g., due to illness or injury). Signed parental/guardian informed consent and participant assent were provided. PF data were collected in 2022 using the Eurofit test battery as described previously 19 . The Eurofit comprises the following fitness tests: flamingo balance (balance), plate tapping (upper-body speed/coordination), sit-and-reach (flexibility), standing broad jump (lower-body power), sit-ups (abdominal/core endurance), bent arm hang (upper-body strength/endurance), 10 × 5-m shuttle run (agility), and the 20-m shuttle run (cardiorespiratory endurance). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated after measuring the body mass and height of participants on the day of fitness testing, with international BMI cut points used to classify adolescents as overweight/obese or not 39 . In addition, questionnaires were provided and completed during a PE class scheduled 1-week later. Age (in whole years) and sex were self-reported. Before being recruited for the study, all schoolchildren and their parents/guardians read and signed the informed consent form. Permission to conduct the study and to apply the experimental protocols was obtained from the Lithuanian Sports University’s Committee of Bioethics (permission no. 27720) and the study complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Questionnaires for correlates of physical fitness Social support : Social support for PA from family and friends was assessed using a 13-item subscale of Sallis’ Support for Exercise Survey 40 . For both support by family and friends, identical questions were used. This scale included items that addressed encouragement to be physically active (e.g., “Gave me rewards for exercising”), involvement in PA (e.g., “Offered to exercise with me”), and facilitation (e.g., “Changed their schedule so we could exercise together”) during the last 3 months. Response options were: (1) never, (2) rarely, (3) a few times, (4) often, or (5) very often. Responses to two negative items (“Complained about the time I spend exercising” and “Criticized me or made fun of me for exercising“) were reversed. A higher total score corresponded with higher perceived social support. In the current study, the internal consistency for familial support was α = 0.87 and for friends’ support was α = 0.89, reflecting high reliability. Motivation for PA : Motivation for PA was assessed using a 9-item scale that captures school students’ attitudes toward exercising because of physical health motives (e.g., “I become physically stronger when I do sports”), cognitive health motives (e.g., “Exercise improves thinking”), understanding the importance of PA (e.g., “It is important to exercise”), and satisfaction with PA (e.g., “Exercising is fun”). Each answer had four options: (1) “No“, (2) “Rather No than Yes“, (3) “Rather Yes than No“, and (4) “Yes“. The internal consistency of this scale was Cronbach α = 0.92. The scale was constructed by the study authors and was based on the adjusted Pictorial Motivation Scale in Physical Activity 41 . Exploratory factor analysis with Promax rotation and principal Axis Factoring derived one factor (KMO = 0.927; d f = 36; p &lt; 0.001), explaining 57% of the variance. Confirmation factor analysis supported the one-factor model (CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.08 [0.08–0.09], SRMR = 0.03; χ 2 = 779, d f = 24; p &lt; 0.0001). The standardized estimates of item loads were from 0.69 to 0.81. Parents’ education level was provided by parents themselves when signing the consent form. Often one parent consented and answered on behalf of both parents/stepparents/guardians by providing information about whether their highest level of education (mother and father) was high school, vocational, college, or university. Education was then categorized as higher education (graduation from either college or university) or lower education. Physical activity was assessed by asking students “On average, how much time per day do you exercise or do sports in such a way that you sweat and breathe faster?“ Response options were: (1) almost none, (2) up to 30 min/day, (3) up to 1 hour/day, or (4) 1 hour or more per day. PA was then categorised as not meeting (answers 1 to 3) or meeting the PA recommendations (answer 4) of the World Health Organization ( https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/336656/9789240015128-eng.pdf?sequence=1 ). Participation in organized sports was assessed by asking students “Do you participate in any sports or other organized PA outside the school?” Answers referred to the frequency of participation per week from “None” to “Every day”. For this study, the item was dichotomized into “I participate” or “I do not participate” in organised sports. Grouping of correlates into four models We have included non-modifiable factors (i.e., independent of the will of the participants) into model 1. These were sex, age, and parents’ education. We also included BMI in model 1 as a known significant correlate of many aspects of PF. Parents’ and friends’ support for PA (model 2), personal (attitude towards PA, model 3) factors, and reported PA level and organised sports participation (model 4) factors were grouped according to their arbitrary potential for the effect on PF (in order from more distal to more proximate). Statistics Analyses were completed using SPSS statistical software (v28, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and Mplus (v8.0, Los Angeles, CA, USA). Descriptive characteristics were reported as frequencies (percentage) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. Differences in means were calculated by Student’s t -test for independent samples, while χ 2 test was employed to identify differences between sexes. The Cohen’s d value, a metric of the effect size, was calculated alongside the Student’s t and χ 2 values. The internal consistency of scales was measured using Cronbach’s alpha. Hierarchical linear regression analysis using four models was used to assess the associations between the outcomes (physical fitness variables) and the independent variables. The first model included four sociodemographic (age, sex, and parents’ education) and physical (BMI) factors. In the second model, social support from family and friends was added. The third model additionally included a more proximate factor of PF, a personal motivation for PA. The fourth model added the most PF proximate behavioural variables of reported PA level and organised sports participation. The effect of added variables in each model is presented by ΔR 2 and represents the additive explained variance on the outcome variables. For the sake of conciseness, β coefficients and their p- values of only the final model are presented. Results were considered significant at p &lt; 0.05. For a Confirmatory Factor Analysis, to confirm structure of the questionnaire, the following goodness of fit indices were employed: (1) the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval, a population-based fit index that is independent of the sample size. Values of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.08 indicate respectively excellent, good, and mediocre fit; (2) the Standardized Root Mean Squared Residual (SRMR)—a direct assessment of how well an a priori model reproduces the sample data. Values &lt; 0.05 were considered a very good fit, while values &lt; 0.08 were interpreted as a good fit; (3) the comparative fit index (CFI); and (4) Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) to compare the fit of a hypothesized model with that of a baseline model (i.e., the model with the worst fit), where values &gt; 0.90 indicate a good model fit, and values &gt; 0.95 indicate a very good model fit 42 . Results Descriptive outcomes Non-modifiable (participant-independent) factors (age, parents’ education and support for PA) for both sexes and the entire group are provided in the upper section of Table 1 , with modifiable (lifestyle) factors represented in the lower section of Table 1 . Only ~ 15% of adolescents reported meeting the recommended minimal level of at least 60 min of PA per day, even if a much larger proportion declared they had participated in organised sports activities at least once per week. Compared to girls, boys more often reported meeting the PA recommendation and participation in organised sport ( p &lt; 0.001). Of those participating in organised sport, 21.5% reported meeting the PA recommendation compared to only 3.5% who did not participate in organised sport. A total of ~ 11% of adolescents, more frequently boys than girls, were classified as overweight/obese. Motivation and support for PA by friends were higher among boys than girls, while there were no sex differences in familial support for PA. Correlates of physical fitness Most social and personal factors correlated significantly with several aspects of PF. Age and organised sports participation were two factors significantly associated with all aspects of PF; sex was associated with all aspects of PF except upper-body speed/coordination; and motivation for PA significantly correlated with all aspects of PF except balance (Table 2 ). BMI was significantly correlated with all aspects of PF except for flexibility and abdominal/core endurance. Support by friends was associated with superior PF, while familial support was not associated with any aspect of children’s PF except for a small negative association with Endurance. PA level was not associated with PF except for upper-body strength/endurance. Collectively, social and personal factors were most strongly associated with cardiorespiratory endurance and lower-body power, explaining as much as 39% and 45% of the variation, respectively (Table 2 ). Hierarchical linear regression analysis showed that model 1 factors (age, sex, BMI, and parents’ education) explained most of the variation in PF (bottom of Table 2 ), which differed among various aspects of PF. Some factors were more strongly associated with aspects of PF (e.g., age on lower-body power and cardiorespiratory endurance), others more modestly (e.g., parents’ education on most aspects of PF), and some non-significantly (e.g., sex on upper-body speed/coordination or BMI on flexibility and abdominal/core endurance) (upper part of Table 2 ). Model 2 factors (PA-related support) explained slightly more variability for some aspects of PF, whilst separate analyses of these two support factors showed it was friends’ support that was more strongly associated with PF than familial support. Motivation for PA ( model 3 ) consistently explained more variance across all aspects of PF. The two factors of model 4 diverged in their prediction power of PA level (dichotomized into meeting the recommendation for moderate-to-vigorous PA volume or failing to meet) having negligible effect by being associated only with hanging performance; meanwhile, organised sports participation had a significant positive effect across all eight aspects of PF. Discussion The results of this study revealed that in addition to well-established factors such as sex, age, and overweight status, social environment factors like parents’ education, friends’ support for PA, personal motivation for PA, and organised sports participation significantly explained the variation in the PF of Lithuanian adolescents. Factors associated with PF are complex, but it is clear that volume and type of regular PA are of primary importance to PF status 43 . In its own way, PA is associated with a myriad of factors 32 including the children’s household environment 44 and social skills (empathy) 45 . Importantly, among various benefits, regular PA is inversely associated with depressive symptoms in adolescents 46 . In addition, PA level is negatively associated with overweight/obesity status 32 , 47 explaining the negative association in our study between BMI and PF levels where body mass was either transported (i.e. running, jumping) or supported (i.e. hanging). Meeting current recommendations for PA levels did not significantly correspond to better performance on most PF tests except for bent arm hang performance, which may be because performance on this test is generally better in those with lower BMI (which may be an outcome of higher/increased PA levels). Notably, it was organised sports participation which was consistently and more strongly associated with all aspects of PF in our study and most strongly associated with cardiorespiratory fitness. However, we cannot infer causation because of our cross-sectional study design. This is supported by the established stronger association of muscular fitness with vigorous PA and organized sports participation than with overall PA level of children and adolescents 43 . Of relevance, even if we have not analysed unhealthy habits in this study, others have reported that organised sports participation is negatively associated with unfavorable behaviours among adolescents like smoking, using snus, and alcohol consumption 48 which might adversely affect PF levels. In our study, leisure time PA was an insignificant associate for vast majority of PF aspects when analyzed concurrently with another component of PA, namely participation in organized sports. However, inclusion sports participation as a separate factor of PA might have masked the effect of leisure time PA for PF in this context. Findings from other studies consistently indicate that level of overall PA positively associates with both physical and mental health. For instance, increasing levels of overall PA reduce risk of cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes and cancer, and are associated with numerous health benefits and delayed mortality 49 . The importance of overall PA could also be highlighted by the findings that prolonged daily sedentary behaviour (that is, high levels of physical inactivity) can negate the cardiovascular benefits of intense leisure time PA 50 . In a close relevance to context of this study, a 45-year observation demonstrated that cardiorespiratory fitness during adolescence is an important predictor of health outcomes in adulthood 51 . Social support has been proposed to be more important than the intrinsic desire for PA (exercise intention or ‘subjective norm’) of adults’ actual PA 52 . In addition, the importance of a close social environment in forming the attitude, norms, and habits towards PA have been observed by others 53 , 54 . In our adolescent population of 11- to 18-year-olds, both personal motivation and friends’ support for PA were associated with objectively measured PF. Interestingly, compared to girls, boys showed not only better performance on nearly all PF tests, but also had higher friends’ support and personal motivation for PA, and a higher percentage were active and participated in organised sport, despite a higher percentage being classified as overweight/obese. Consistent with previous European research 55 we found that upper-body speed/coordination was similar for boys and girls with boys outperforming girls on all other PF tests. Since lower PF during adolescence is associated with a higher risk of major non-transmittable (cardiometabolic) disease burden in later life 51 a practical consideration is warranted for promoting the development of PF in youth through PA, sports participation, and overall healthy lifestyle both to form health-promoting habits and to build functional capabilities. A close social environment positively affects PA level 35 – 38 and children’s PF is positively associated with the PF of their parents’ 56 . However, our findings on adolescents’ PF status highlight the importance of friends’ support but not familial support. As findings of the current study suggest, the mother’s education is relatively more important than the father’s education for the development of PF among adolescents. Although speculative, this may be because motherly care has been historically more important. It could be that PF levels among adolescents are more strongly influenced by peer support for PA than familial support for PA. The lack of an observed association of familial support for PA on PF deserves further investigation. Limitations and perspectives One of the limitations of the study was the self-reported levels of PA and the categorization into those meeting or not meeting the WHO recommended amount of PA per day. Self-report measures of PA may suffer from recall and social desirability bias, potentially resulting in inaccurate estimates of PA levels and associations. We did not objectively measure PA and the validity of our PA estimate may be questioned. While the adjusted Pictorial Motivation Scale has not been formally tested for validity and reliability, it was considered suitable for the current study by the study authors because it is internally consistent and because the scale was significantly associated with the phenomena of interest (e.g., the PF tests requiring maximal muscular effort). We have not analyzed the biological maturity of the participants, and since it is of importance for PF among adolescents, different associations may have been observed if biological age rather than calendar age had been investigated. Additionally, specifics of the engaged sports (i.e., more technical or physical, more endurance or power based) or the degree of involvement were not analysed. Moreover, a prolonged COVID-19 lockdown implemented for most of the organized formal PA about a year before the study might have reduced some aspects of PF 57 , 58 changed some social and personal factors, and thus putatively the relationships. The timescale of reconditioning after lockdown restrictions were lifted was unclear, and with our cross-sectional study design, it was not possible to examine such effects. The motivation of the schoolchildren to perform the PF tests maximally was not tightly controlled or even estimated due to the large-scale nature of the study. Our study design meant that causality could not be inferred because a temporal sequence could not be established. Finally, the results of this cross-sectional study are applicable to an apparently healthy general population of modern adolescents and may not be generalizable to the entire Lithuanian adolescent population. Future studies on the factors contributing to PF in young, apparently healthy populations could benefit from addressing the limitations mentioned above. This could be achieved by precisely stratifying participants into PA categories, particularly based on exercise intensity, and by expanding the sample to include a wider age demographic. Additionally, large-scale PA intervention studies, such as those like the Daily Mile initiative 59 could focus more on environmental support. This could include, for instance, educational and motivational input from renowned sporting champions. Such approaches may help reveal the potential of social interactions in combating PA and PF issues and improving health prognoses. The links between social environment, personal motivation for PA and actual PA levels with adolescents‘ PF remain poorly investigated. Finally, while the causal direction of the observed associations in the current study could not be established, there exists an opportunity to test the significant PF correlates for cause-effect relationships in the future studies. Conclusion Social environmental factors, independent of more stable factors such as sex, age and parental education, were significantly associated with all aspects of PF among adolescents. More specifically, motivation for PA, acting more through participation in organised sport, was significantly associated with all aspects of PF, with self-reported leisure-time PA levels not significantly related to PF. A novel finding was that familial support for PA was generally not significantly related to adolescents’ PF levels, while friends’ support for PA was significantly associated with half of the PF components. Therefore, beyond sports participation, it is recommended that parents’ education, personal motivation and friends’ support for PA be promoted to foster the PF of adolescents.
