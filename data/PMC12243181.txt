Background For use in population-level surveys, there is a need for brief measures of disordered eating (DE) that demonstrate validity among diverse adolescents. This study aimed to (1) assess the measurement invariance of a short DE scale among high school students in Canada, and (2) estimate differences in scale scores across student sociodemographic subgroups. Methods We used student-level survey data collected during the 2021/2022 school year in the COMPASS study. The sample included 24,639 students in grades 9–12 from 69 secondary schools in Alberta, British Columbia, and Ontario, Canada. Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis was applied to assess measurement invariance (i.e., configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance) of the 6-item DE scale, and one-way ANOVA and mixed linear regression were implemented to estimate scale score differences across population subgroups (by grade, gender, race and ethnicity, family affluence, and weight status). Results Results demonstrated full measurement invariance of the DE scale across student sociodemographic subgroups. Higher sum scale scores indicated greater engagement in DE thoughts or behaviours. Students in higher school grade (e.g., grade 12), transgender and gender diverse youth, and cisgender girls reported higher DE scores compared to their grade 9 and cisgender boy peers. Elevated DE scores were also observed among adolescents of Middle Eastern and Another or Multiethnic identity, students with less affluent households, and those with higher body weights, relative to their White, more affluent, and relatively lower weight peers, respectively. Conclusions Results indicate the DE scale measures the same construct across various subgroups of adolescents, providing confidence that differences in scale scores found by grade, gender, race and ethnicity, affluence, and weight reflect actual differences in DE rather than artifactual differences in scale interpretation. This scale warrants additional psychometric testing as a promising brief DE measure suitable for large population-level youth surveys. Our results provide important new evidence from a large contemporary sample of adolescents demonstrating elevated risk of DE among specific subgroups and emphasizing the need for targeted interventions. This study tested a new short measure of disordered eating (DE) for use in survey research among adolescents. The goal was to ensure the questionnaire works consistently across different groups of students, including boys, girls, transgender and gender diverse (TGD) youth, and students from different grades, racial and ethnic backgrounds, family income levels, and body sizes. Using data from over 24,000 students in grades 9 to 12 at 69 secondary schools in Canada, we found support for use of the DE measure across all groups. The measure appears to assess DE in the same way for all students, which means that any differences in DE scores between groups likely reflect real differences in DE thoughts and behaviours, and not different interpretations of the questions. Some groups of students were more likely to report DE than others. Groups at increased risk of reporting DE included older students, girls, TGD youth, and students from lower-income families, with higher body weights, and of Middle Eastern and Another/Multiethnic backgrounds. Overall, the study suggests that this brief DE measure could be useful for large youth health surveys. It also highlights the importance of providing extra support for the groups at greater risk of DE. A primary barrier is the lack of a brief DE scale that is suitable for incorporating into large population-level surveys (i.e., short and easy to complete) and that demonstrates robust psychometric properties in diverse adolescent populations. DE is described as “a persistent disturbance of body image that results in the maladaptive consumption of food” [ 3 ]. DE thoughts and behaviours are eating disorder (ED) symptoms that do not meet the threshold for a clinical diagnosis but are known risk factors for the development of an ED [ 4 ]. DE may include food preoccupation, weight or shape preoccupation and overvaluation, restrictive eating, compulsive or binge eating, self-induced vomiting, and misuse of laxatives, diuretics, diet pills, or steroids [ 3 ]. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis reported the overall global proportion of children and adolescents (7–18 years old) that engaged in DE was 22.4% (n = 63,181), with girls significantly more likely to report DE than boys (30% vs. 17%, p &lt;.001) 5 . A growing body of evidence highlights the heightened risk of DE behaviours among transgender and gender diverse (TGD) youth compared to their cisgender peers [ 6 – 8 ]. DE is often associated with other health risks, such as substance use [ 9 ] sleep disturbance [ 10 ] and inadequate nutrition [ 11 ] which are of particular concern during adolescence given the continued growth and development. Thus, DE may significantly impair adolescents’ physical health [ 12 – 14 ] and impact their mental health and cognitive functioning [ 15 , 16 ]. Further, given adolescents’ increased autonomy from parental figures in making day-to-day decisions, eating and other health and risk behaviours tend to become established during this period and track into adulthood [ 17 , 18 ]. In some individuals DE behaviours may progress to meet clinical diagnosis for an ED [ 17 , 18 ]. Without proper treatment, EDs can become chronic, severe, and relapsing conditions, with the second highest mortality rate of all mental disorders, surpassed only by opioid dependence [ 19 – 21 ]. Thus, better understanding DE among youth is crucial for planning and executing effective prevention and early interventions. Several factors have impeded implementation of DE measures in youth health surveillance. Brevity is particularly important for large population-level surveys, with multiple priorities competing for questionnaire space, and the need to keep the full questionnaire short to reduce burden on respondents and improve data quality (e.g., by reducing survey fatigue) [ 22 , 23 ]. There is no universally standardized measurement scale developed specifically for DE thoughts and behaviours in adolescents. Instead, researchers often rely on various measures that were originally developed for the assessment of clinical EDs (e.g., anorexia nervosa) [ 24 ] and related symptoms in adults, including the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) [ 24 ] Eating Attitudes Test (EAT) [ 25 , 26 ] SCOFF (Sick, Control, One, Fat, Food) questionnaire [ 26 , 27 ] and Weight Concerns Scale (WCS) [ 26 ]. While the SCOFF and WCS both only include five items [ 26 ] the EDE-Q (28 items) and EAT (40 items) are time-consuming to complete [ 25 , 28 ]. The psychometric properties of these tools have primarily been assessed in adult samples [ 24 ]. Scales validated in adult samples may not be suitable for adolescent populations given the unique developmental characteristics of adolescents, and physical and psychosocial differences between adolescence and adulthood. Of the handful of studies that have psychometrically assessed DE measures in youth samples [ 29 ] very few have been conducted within a Canadian context. Psychometric findings from other countries and cultures may not generalize to Canadian populations, given the socioeconomic and cultural differences in body ideals, eating behaviours and patterns, and related factors. Relatedly, existing studies in the field have historically been conducted primarily in homogenous samples, particularly among cisgender girls and women, and non-Hispanic White, thinner or lower weight, and mid-to-higher socioeconomic status (SES) populations, overlooking other sociocultural groups [ 30 – 32 ]. DE may be displayed differently in other populations and contexts, hence, leading to inappropriate conclusions that DE is only a concern of thin, White, middle-to-upper class, and cisgender girls and women, and consequently, the development of inappropriate and ineffective interventions [ 30 – 32 ]. Therefore, it is important to have a DE measure that demonstrates validity across various sociodemographic groups. Given concerns about DE measurement validity in understudied populations and calls for DE research in diverse samples [ 30 – 32 ] testing measurement invariance is of particular relevance, and yet is a less often considered psychometric characteristic. Measurement invariance refers to whether a given scale measures the actual latent construct it was intended to measure (e.g., DE) in a population, and whether it measures the latent construct consistently across population subgroups [ 33 ]. Establishing measurement invariance is a prerequisite for meaningful group-level comparisons [ 33 ]. Measurement invariance indicates that differences demonstrated by the given scale represent true differences among subgroups, rather than artifactual differences in the scale interpretation [ 34 ]. The current study aims to (1) assess measurement invariance of a brief DE scale among sociodemographic subgroups (i.e., by grade, gender, race and ethnicity, relative family affluence, and weight status) in a large sample of secondary school students in Canada, and (2) estimate differences in DE scale scores between these subgroups. If the measurement invariance of this DE scale can be established, this measure may provide a promising and feasible option to introduce into large surveys to address gaps in population-level youth research, and in turn, inform more effective interventions. Methods Study design and sample This cross-sectional study used student-level survey data collected during the 2021/22 school year as part of Year 10 of the COMPASS study. COMPASS (2012–2027) is a large prospective cohort study of adolescents attending secondary schools across Canada [ 35 ]. Schools were purposefully selected based on permitted use of active-information, passive-consent parental permission protocols, which are shown to reduce demands on schools, increase response rates, and reduce self-selection bias among youth [ 36 ]. All adolescents attending participating schools and not withdrawn by their parent/guardian were eligible to participate and could decline participation at any time. Student-reported data were collected using the COMPASS student questionnaire (Cq), an online survey designed to collect data once annually on sociodemographic characteristics, various health behaviour domains (e.g., physical activity, substance use), and correlates (e.g., bullying, school connectedness) during class time. The questions that assessed DE thoughts and behaviours among adolescents were first added to Cq in the 2021/22 school year that was administered to participating schools in the provinces of Alberta, BC, and Ontario. In 2021/22, a total of 27,003 students from 69 participating high schools in these provinces responded to the survey, of which 2,364 opted not to complete any or some of DE items, leaving 24,639 adolescents with complete response to the six DE items. All procedures received ethical approval from the University of Waterloo (ORE#30118), Brock University (REB#23–087), University of Alberta (#RES0050375; Pro00129206), CIUSSS de la Capitale-Nationale–Université Laval (#MP-13-2017-1264), and participating school boards. A more detailed description of the COMPASS study protocols can be found in print [ 35 ] or online [ 37 ]. Measures The COMPASS researchers derived and adapted a new 6-item brief DE scale from other existing ED/DE scales (e.g., EAT [ 25 , 26 ] EDE-Q [ 24 ] SCOFF [ 26 , 27 ]) with the goal to cover key DE thoughts and behaviours among adolescent populations, such as weight and shape preoccupation and overvaluation, restrictive eating, binge eating, and various compensatory behaviours, including those that may be more characteristic in boys (i.e., steroid use and changing eating habits in ways not limited to restrictive eating that are more thin-ideal oriented), within a minimal number of short items. Students were asked to indicate how often they have experienced the following in the last 12 months: (1) been preoccupied with a desire to be thinner , (2) made yourself throw up (vomit) on purpose after eating , (3) used laxatives , diet pills , diuretics (water pills) , or steroids to control your weight or shape , (4) changed your eating habits in order to manage your weight or shape , (5) ate much more than most people would under the same circumstances and felt that you could not stop , and (6) had a definite fear of gaining weight or being overweight . Responses were scored on a 5-point scale: 0 = never, 1 = a few times, 2 = monthly, 3 = weekly, 4 = daily. Sum scores ranged from 0 to 24, with a higher score indicating higher engagement in DE thoughts and behaviours. The internal consistency of the six DE items in our sample was α = 0.79. To evaluate the clarity and comprehensibility of the DE scale for youth, an online meeting was conducted with seven youth from the COMPASS Youth Engagement Committee, diverse in various sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., gender and sexuality, age, cultural background, province). The youth representatives were informed of the discussion topic in advance and could leave the meeting at any time without repercussions. To support participants, information and resources on mental health and ED helplines (e.g., Kids Help Phone and National Eating Disorder Information Centre) were provided both before and immediately after the meeting. No youth representatives were asked to discuss their own or their peers’ experiences. Participants were asked their interpretation of the items, and whether they believed youth would understand the items and would feel comfortable answering honestly. Overall, participants interpretated the items as intended, agreed that the language was clear and understandable, and believed that youth would feel comfortable providing honest responses given study protocols that preserve anonymity during data collection. The Cq also asked students to self-report their sociodemographic characteristics including grade (9, 10, 11, 12), and perceived family financial comfort relative to their average peers at school (“More comfortable”, “As comfortable”, “Less comfortable”). Student-reported height and weight were used to calculate weight status using Body Mass Index (BMI) categories by age and sex based on the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines (“underweight”, “healthy weight”, “overweight”, and “obesity”) [ 38 ]. To avoid stigmatizing language and because categories were based solely on BMI, the weight categories will hereby be referred to as “lower weight”, “healthy weight”, “high weight”, and “higher weight”, respectively [ 39 , 40 ]. Students also reported their current gender identity (“Girl/Woman”, “Boy/Man”, “Non-binary person”, “Two-Spirit”, “I describe my gender differently”, and “I prefer not to say”) and sex assigned at birth (“Female”, “Male”, and “I prefer not to say”), which were used to create three categories: “cisgender girls”, “cisgender boys”, and “transgender and gender diverse (TGD) youth”. In respect of participants’ choice to not self classify, students that reported “I prefer not to say” for either gender ( n = 530) or sex ( n = 575) were removed. Students also indicated their race or ethnicity by selecting one or more response option in the Cq (“White”, “Black”, “East Asian”, “Latino”, “Middle Eastern”, “South Asian”, “Southeast Asian”, “Multiethnic”, “Another category”, “I do not know”, and “I prefer not to answer”). For the purposes of measurement invariance assessment, participants were classified as: racialized (collapsing Black, East Asian, South Asian, Southeast Asian, Latino, Middle Eastern, Multiethnic, and Another category) vs. non-racialized (White) students. Statistical analysis Measurement invariance When assessing the measurement invariance, we conducted preliminary data analysis and found that some cell sizes were too small to proceed with further analysis, particularly within the “weekly” to “daily” categories (e.g., only 17 TGD participants responded that they used laxatives, diet pills, diuretics, or steroids to control weight or shape on a weekly basis). Therefore, with consideration of both frequency of responses and ED clinical thresholds, we collapsed the original five response options to three categories: “Never” (reports of “Never”), “Occasionally” (reports of “A few times”), and “Regularly” (reports of “Monthly”, “Weekly” or “Daily”). Measurement invariance testing was conducted using Mplus version 8.1 [ 41 ] with categorical estimation (i.e., weighted least squares means and variance adjusted estimator). Measurement invariance of the DE scale was assessed using a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) within a four-step procedure, in which stringent equality constraints were increasingly placed on model parameters between groups (e.g., grade 9 vs. 12, cisgender girl vs. cisgender boy). In the first step, no equality constraints were placed on the model and the configural invariance was tested to confirm whether the underlying factor structure (or latent construct) was consistent between groups [ 42 ]. This configural model is the origin for subsequent steps. Second, factor loadings for each DE item were restricted to be equal across groups in the metric model to test for the metric invariance. This metric invariance is a prerequisite for making valid group comparisons [ 43 ]. Third, equal factor loadings and item intercepts were imposed on the scalar model and the scalar invariance was investigated to verify whether mean item-level differences can be fully explained by mean factor-level differences [ 33 ]. In the final step, factor loadings, item intercepts, and item residuals were all restricted to be equal across groups in the strict model, and the strict invariance is a requisite for making defensible item-level comparisons [ 44 ]. This four-step approach to gradually adding constraints allows us to identify which specific parameters contributed to model misfit, and in turn, to differences in the interpretation of the latent construct [ 34 ]. Two criteria were used to establish measurement invariance of DE [ 45 , 46 ]. The first criterion was that at least two of fit indices need to meet established cut-off points to declare adequate model fit at each level of testing: comparative fit index (CFI) &gt; = 0.950; square root mean residual (SRMR) &lt; = 0.080; and root mean standard error of approximation (RMSEA) &lt; = 0.080 [ 33 ]. The second criterion specified that at least two of the changes in fit indices must not exceed established cut-off points at any given level of testing (i.e., from the model with fewer equality constraints on parameters to the more constrained model, such as metric vs. configural models, scalar vs. metric models, and strict vs. scalar models): ΔCFI&lt;= -0.010; ΔSRMR &gt; = 0.030; and ΔRMSEA &gt; = 0.015 [ 47 ]. Given the large sample size in this study, we did not rely on χ [ 2 ] goodness-of-fit and Δχ [ 2 ] values as indices of model fit [ 48 ]. DE score differences by sociodemographic characteristics DE scores were treated as a continuous variable when exploring differences across population subgroups. One-way ANOVAs were applied to test if there were significant differences in mean DE scale scores across student subgroups. If mean score differences were identified, post-hoc Bonferroni tests were implemented to check which specific group/groups differed from others. After establishing measurement invariance, mixed linear regression was used to detect the sum score differences across student subgroups, with reporting β-estimates alongside 95% confidence intervals. The procedures of “PROC ANOVA” and “PROC MIXED” adjusting for covariates and school-level clustering were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, 2016), respectively. Missing data analysis A total of 8.8% ( n = 2,364) of COMPASS Year 10 students were missing all or partial DE responses. Possible reasons for this include the sensitive nature of the DE items, the full autonomy in survey completion allowing students to skip any question, and time limit during in-class administration of our lengthy survey instrument. These factors may have contributed to intentional or unintentional non-response on DE items. Some item non-response was also observed across measures of grade ( n = 52, &lt; 1%), gender ( n = 1,046, 4.3%), race and ethnicity ( n = 1,353, 5.5%), family affluence ( n = 122, &lt; 1%), and weight status ( n = 10,019, 40.7%). When applying One-way ANOVA tests to detect mean DE score differences across student subgroups, participants with missing DE data and the respective sociodemographic characteristic were alternately removed from the analysis; for example, 2,416 (2,364 students had missing DE data and 52 students had missing grade value) participants were excluded to test mean DE score differences across Grade subgroups. The same approach was implemented for assessing measurement invariance. Moreover, when conducting mixed linear regression to investigate sum DE score differences across subgroups, participants with missing DE data and any sociodemographic characteristics were removed from the analysis, as to adjust for all other sociodemographic characteristics as covariates. Additional analyses comparing differences in DE measures by student characteristics between the analytic sample and the total sample (i.e., including participants missing DE responses) are summarized in Appendix Table S1 . Mean DE scores were similar between students with complete DE responses and those with missing responses, and statistical significance in DE mean score differences across subgroups remained stable before and after removing missing values. Results Sample characteristics Sample characteristics are reported in Table 1 . There were 24,639 adolescents in our analytic sample. Most students were identified as cisgender girls (48.3%) and cisgender boys (46.7%), while TGD students comprised 5% of the sample. Over half of students were non-racialized (57.1% White). Approximately 61% of adolescents considered their family to be as financially comfortable as the average student in their class. Nearly half of participants had a BMI considered to be “healthy weight” (44.3%). The mean DE score in the total sample was 5.1 (SD = 5.0, range: 0–24). DE means and standard deviations (SDs) by student sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1 . One-way ANOVA tests showed statistically significant variations in DE mean scores for all student sociodemographic subgroups at p &lt;.0001. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that participants in grade 9 had significantly lower DE mean scores than students in all higher grades. TGD youth had the highest DE mean scores, followed by cisgender girls, while cisgender boys had the lowest DE mean scores. Adolescents of East Asian identity showed significantly lower mean scores than all other racial and ethnic groups, with exception of South Asian identity. Students who perceived their families to be relatively less financially comfortable reported higher DE mean scores than their peers that reported their families to be more and as financially comfortable. Adolescents with a higher weight status had the highest mean DE scores across the four weight groups, followed by adolescents with a high weight status, while lower weight students had the lowest mean DE scores. Descriptive statistics by DE items before and after collapsing the response options are reported in Table 2 . Before collapsing, the highest proportion of participants responded “Never” to each of the six DE items, followed by “A few times” responses, while relatively low proportions of students responded to “Monthly”, “Weekly”, or “Daily” options (e.g., &lt; 2% of participants answered either “Monthly”, “Weekly”, or “Daily” to purging and pills/steroids use). After collapsing, 34.5%, 33.4%, and 29.7% of students reported “Regularly” (combined “Monthly”, “Weekly”, and “Daily” responses) experiencing weight preoccupation, changing eating habits, and fear of weight gain, respectively, and 4.7%, 3.0%, and 14.5% of students “Regularly” engaged in purging, pills/steroids use, and binge eating, respectively. The mean DE scale scores remained stable before and after collapsing response categories. Measurement invariance Table 3 displays the measurement invariance results of the one-factor model in the total sample and across student sociodemographic subgroups. The CFA model demonstrated an overall good fit for DE (CFI = 0.986; SRMR = 0.051; RMSEA = 0.085 [90% CI: 0.082–0.089]) in the total sample. In terms of subgroups (grade 12 vs. 9; cisgender boys vs. cisgender girls; TGD youth vs. cisgender girls; racialized vs. non-racialized; less financial comfortable vs. as financial comfortable; higher weight vs. healthy weight), the configural models for all student subgroups indicated excellent fit (grade: CFI = 0.985; SRMR = 0.054; RMSEA = 0.088 [90% CI: 0.083–0.093]; gender: cis-boys vs. cis-girls: CFI = 0.984; SRMR = 0.066; RMSEA = 0.081 [90% CI: 0.078–0.085]; TGD youth vs. cis-girls: CFI = 0.991; SRMR = 0.042; RMSEA = 0.073 [90% CI: 0.068–0.078]; race and ethnicity: CFI = 0.986; SRMR = 0.050; RMSEA = 0.084 [90% CI: 0.080–0.088]; financial comfort: CFI = 0.986; SRMR = 0.048; RMSEA = 0.083 [90% CI: 0.078–0.087]; weight status: CFI = 0.990; SRMR = 0.041; RMSEA = 0.068 [90% CI: 0.063–0.073]). Equality constraints gradually placed on the factor loadings, item intercepts, and item residuals did not result in a substantially worse fit for the metric, scalar, or strict models for any of subgroups, indicating full measurement invariance for all characteristics considered. Standardized, item-by-item factor loadings by student characteristics are shown in Table 4 . DE scale scores among student subgroups Results of the mixed linear regression model showing the adjusted likelihood of an average one-point increase in DE sum scores by student characteristics are represented in Table 5 . Compared to participants in grade 9, DE scores were increasingly higher in grades 10 (β = 0.23, p &lt;.05), 11 (β = 0.48, p &lt;.0001), and 12 (β = 0.52, p &lt;.0001). In comparison to cisgender boys, the likelihood of a one-point-increase in DE sum score was much higher for cisgender girls (β = 3.80, p &lt;.0001), and TGD youth (β = 3.76, p &lt;.0001). Students who perceived their family to be relatively less financially comfortable reported higher average sum scores (β = 1.52, p &lt;.0001) relative to their as comfortable peers. Varied DE sum scores were present across racial and ethnic groups; adolescents of Black (β = -0.53, p &lt;.05) or East Asian identity (β = -0.59, p &lt;.005) had lower scores, while those of Middle Eastern (β = 0.53, p &lt;.05) or Another category/Multiethnic identity (β = 0.29, p &lt;.05) had higher scores, compared to White adolescents. In comparison to healthy weight adolescents, DE scores were lower in participants with lower weights (β = -1.45, p &lt;.0001) but higher for those with high (β = 2.21, p &lt;.0001) and higher weights (β = 3.55, p &lt;.0001). Discussion We assessed the measurement invariance of a brief DE scale in a large cohort study of secondary school students in Canada and examined differences in DE across population subgroups. We found strict measurement invariance of the DE scale by student grade, gender, race and ethnicity, relative family affluence, and weight status. This finding indicates that statistical differences observed in DE scale scores may represent true differences in DE thoughts and behaviours between student subgroups, rather than artifactual differences in scale interpretation. This study also provides contemporary evidence of DE in a large sample of adolescents. Targeted interventions should consider subgroups found to be at higher risk of DE, including TGD youth, cisgender girls, adolescents of Middle Eastern identity, students from relatively less affluent households, and those with higher body weight. This brief scale provides a promising DE measure suitable for inclusion in surveys to help fill the gap in population-level data on DE among adolescents. With measurement invariance demonstrated in our sample, future research may consider exploring additional psychometric properties (e.g., test-retest reliability, convergent validity) to further strengthen the scale’s reliability and applicability across diverse adolescent populations. The brevity of this scale is an important feature to ensure feasibility for inclusion in surveillance survey research. Many existing measures are lengthy and time-consuming to complete, such as original EDE-Q and EAT, which consist of 28 and 40 items respectively. Further, the psychometric properties of existing ED tools have predominantly been tested among adult samples [ 24 , 25 ]. Among the handful of studies that have psychometrically assessed DE measures in adolescents [ 29 ] very few have been conducted within a Canadian context. Strict measurement invariance and a large sample size enabled us to provide important evidence in DE differences across adolescent sociodemographic characteristics (i.e., grade, gender, race and ethnicity, family affluence, and weight status). Of these characteristics, the most pronounced DE differences were observed by student gender, weight status, and family affluence. Our findings indicated that TGD youth had the highest engagement in DE, followed by cisgender girls, while cisgender boys displayed the lowest engagement. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that TGD youth are at higher risk for DE compared to their cisgender peers [ 6 – 8 ]. TGD youth may encounter unique body- and gender-related stressors. They may feel their biological body does not align with their perceived gender identity [ 49 , 50 ]. As a result, distress may arise due to the gender incongruence and DE behaviours may be used to minimize secondary sex characteristics (e.g., breasts or beards) [ 51 , 52 ]. For example, transfeminine adolescents (i.e., assigned male at birth but self-identified as girls) may engage in restrictive eating/using diet pills to meet beauty standards for girls/women, while transmasculine adolescents (i.e., assigned female at birth but self-identified as boys) may engage in muscle-building behaviours/steroid use to achieve a muscular body ideal [ 53 ]. For gender diverse youth (e.g., non-binary), the underlying factors and patterns of DE may differ. They may limit food intake or engage in excessive exercise, purging, or laxative use to achieve a leaner or less curvaceous body for a more androgynous appearance and reduce physical traits associated with binary gender identities [ 54 ]. Cisgender girls in our study were more likely to engage in DE than cisgender boys, consistent with the literature indicating that girls and female adolescents typically report higher levels of DE than boys and male adolescents [ 55 – 57 ]. These differences are likely driven by sociocultural gender roles and expression. Societal expectations often emphasize thinness for girls and muscularity and leanness for boys, consequently, leading to different DE behaviours among them. Girls are more likely to perform dietary restriction and/or purging to reduce weight or body size, while boys are more likely to focus on exercise, and use steroids and supplements to increase muscularity and size [ 55 – 57 ]. Historically, research has often overlooked DE in males or boys. While at lower risk relative to their peers, our study adds to the existing knowledge that DE concerns and behaviours do occur among adolescent cisgender boys, potentially more frequently than previously perceived [ 58 , 59 ]. Although our DE scale included some more masculine-oriented items, it might not fully capture the range of concerns and behaviours specific to adolescent boys or men. This limitation reflects a common challenge in DE scale development, where more typical masculinity-specific factors such as the drive for muscle gain and leanness, behaviours like over-exercising and supplement use, and sociocultural pressures such as muscularity ideals promoted by media and sports culture, often remain underrepresented [ 60 , 61 ]. This study identified that adolescents with higher body weight were more likely to engage in DE, while lower weight adolescents were less likely to exhibit DE, compared to their healthy weight counterparts. These findings are consistent with growing evidence that higher weight children and adolescents were at greater risk of DE thoughts and behaviours [ 62 – 64 ] in contrast to historically extant literature and clinical and societal perceptions that individuals at risk of DE/ED are always thin or low weight [ 65 – 67 ]. These misperceptions and clinical criteria may contribute to DE among higher weight adolescents being overlooked. It is well-documented that higher weight adolescents are at greater risk of being teased/bullied by peers and family members, relative to their normal weight counterparts [ 62 – 64 ]. Experiences of weight-based stigma and discrimination may contribute to DE thoughts and behaviours [ 68 , 69 ]. This study also adds to literature of higher DE risk in association with lower affluence [ 70 , 71 ]. Relatedly, recent literature has identified links between food insecurity– defined as limited or uncertain access to adequate food (in terms of quality and quantity) due to economic and social conditions [ 72 ]– and DE [ 70 , 71 , 73 ]. Food insecurity may mediate the association between family affluence and adolescent DE. Fluctuations in food availability have been hypothesized to result in appetite dysregulation, inconsistent eating patterns, and DE in adolescents [ 73 ]. Unique to the current study, we assessed students perceived relative affluence, rather than absolute affluence. Perceived affluence relative to an average classmate at school provides a salient reference group, and can capture adolescents’ subjective experiences and social comparisons, which may better reflect the psychosocial impact of economic status in adolescents [ 74 , 75 ]. Students who perceived their family to be relatively less affluent reported greater DE than their peers that perceived their family to be as affluent. As adolescents are particularly sensitive to social comparisons and social standing, DE may emerge to meet sociocultural appearance ideals in compensation for perceptions of lower affluence. DE differences by student grade and race and ethnicity are more minimal, compared to those found by gender, weight status, and affluence. We found a pattern of increasing engagement in DE with increasing school grade in our study, consistent with longitudinal evidence that DE behaviours rise from childhood to young adulthood [ 76 – 78 ]. Several factors likely contribute to this trend. Adolescence represents a critical period of physical development, identity exploration, and growing autonomy. During this time, social acceptance and peer relationships become more significant, often leading to increased social comparisons and a greater focus on appearance. Increased exposure to appearance-related messages (e.g., through social media) may further contribute to DE thoughts and behaviours [ 79 , 80 ]. Results indicated moderately lower engagement in DE among Black and East Asian students, while students of Middle Eastern and Another or Multiethnic identities exhibited moderately higher DE scores, compared to their White counterparts. These results provide valuable evidence, as the small sample sizes of most prior studies have limited ability to examine disaggregated data across racial or ethnic groups. As a result, researchers have often collapsed response options, which potentially masks important differences within groups. Our finding for lower DE among adolescents of Black identity aligns with existing literature [ 65 , 81 ]. Black and African American populations often encourage a relatively curvier body shape for women/girls, potentially serving as a protective factor against restrictive eating and purging behaviours for these adolescents [ 82 , 83 ]. Our finding of relatively higher engagement in DE among Middle Eastern adolescents is also consistent with literature [ 84 , 85 ]. Middle Eastern adolescents often face unique challenges from traditional beauty ideals, Western beauty standards, and acculturation stress, which can exacerbate body dissatisfaction and increase DE risk. Our finding of relatively lower engagement in DE among East Asian adolescents contradicts literature showing an unrealistically thin ideal to be widely reinforced across media outlets for East Asians [ 86 ] and Asian Americans [ 87 ]. The discrepancy between our study and prior research may be attributed to environmental and cultural contexts [ 86 , 87 ]. Adolescents in our study may reside in multicultural environments where diverse body ideals are promoted, mitigating the influence of thin ideals. Furthermore, social and familial factors, such as the role of collectivism, academic pressures, or different levels of acculturation, might influence DE behaviours differently across contexts. Specifically, East Asian culture emphasizes emotional restraint, family harmony, and academic achievement [ 88 , 89 ] which may play a role in shaping body image and eating behaviours, potentially leading to different expressions or reporting of DE. In addition, stigma surrounding mental health [ 88 ] may discourage open discussion or recognition of DE thoughts or behaviours in some East Asian communities, contributing to potential underreporting in DE assessment. These cultural considerations may help explain differences between our findings and that of the cited literature. Importantly, this study allowed us to examine DE among adolescents of East Asian, South Asian, and Southeast Asian identities, filling an important gap in the literature where these groups have often been collapsed into a single “Asian” category or studied individually. While the data used in the current study were collected during the 2021–2022 school year, a time when public health restrictions (e.g., school closures, limited access to resources and facilities) were gradually being lifted, the remaining effects of the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., social isolation, disruption to daily routines) might still have been influencing students’ mental health and engagement in DE thoughts and behaviours [ 4 ]. However, the observed patterns across sociodemographic subgroups are largely consistent with the findings from pre-pandemic research [ 6 , 55 , 62 , 65 , 71 , 78 ] suggesting that the differences identified in our study likely reflect persistent disparities rather than pandemic-specific effects. Strengths and limitations As discussed, the large and relatively diverse sample is a considerable strength of this study, that enabled us to examine measurement invariance and DE differences by various sociodemographic characteristics. Several limitations also need to be considered. First, COMPASS was not designed to be nationally or provincially representative. Also, self-reported data may introduce recall error or social desirability bias. Adolescents who did not respond to DE items may be at higher risk of performing DE thoughts and behaviours. However, the full school samples, with schools located across varied regions (e.g., rural to large urban), favourable response rates, and the use of passive consent protocols, help to support generalizability. In addition, not using student names to preserve their anonymity may improve student comfort in reporting. Second, despite incorporating some masculine-oriented items, this scale may not fully address DE concerns and behaviours specific to adolescent boys or men, such as the drive for muscle gain, over-exercising, and sociocultural pressures from media and sports culture [ 60 , 61 ]. This highlights a common issue in DE scale development, where male-specific factors are often underrepresented. The absence of direct validation against full-length ED measures is the third limitation of this study. Future research should include direct comparisons between the 6-item scale and existing ED/DE instruments to further evaluate convergent validity. Fourth, it is important to acknowledge the potential underreporting of DE among lower weight adolescents in our sample. Prior research has found that adolescents and young adults with bulimic eating disorders may have underestimated their DE thoughts or behaviours when using self-reported instruments, due to denial, minimization, or lack of symptom recognition [ 90 , 91 ]. Future studies should consider incorporating multi-informant or clinical interview approaches to improve the assessment of DE across the weight spectrum. Conclusion This study supports full measurement invariance of a 6-item DE scale across various sociodemographic characteristics among adolescents in Canada. The observed scale score differences by student grade, gender, race and ethnicity, family affluence, and weight status may therefore represent true differences in DE thoughts and behaviours. With strict measurement invariance demonstrated in our large and diverse sample, this DE scale shows promise for the inclusion in population-level survey research. Future studies are encouraged to explore additional psychometric properties to enhance the scale’s reliability and applicability for adolescent health surveillance research.
