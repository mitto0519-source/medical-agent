For commercial re-use, please contact journals.permissions@oup.com Abstract Study Objectives School start times impose constraints on sleep–wake timing that may influence academic achievement. We used large university archived datasets to test the hypothesis that larger differences in timing of students’ diurnal learning behavior on school days relative to non-school days would be associated with lower grades. Methods Diurnal learning-directed behavior was examined in 33 645 university students by analyzing their learning management system (LMS) login rhythm. We tested the associations between the phase-difference in students’ behavioral rhythm on school days versus non-school days with grade point average, LMS-login phase on non-school days (LMS-login chronotype), and school start time. We also tested the chronotype-dependent effects of school start times on diurnal behavior to determine whether students obtained better course grades when their first class of the day was in synch with their LMS-login chronotype. Results Students whose LMS-login rhythm was more than 2 hours earlier on school days had significantly lower grades than their peers. The change in LMS-login phase was larger in students with a later LMS-login chronotype and for earlier school start times. Minimal changes in LMS-login phase and higher course grades were observed when students’ first class of the day was aligned with their LMS-login chronotype. Conclusions Our findings demonstrate that school start times have a profound impact on students’ diurnal learning behavior with implications for grades. Universities can potentially improve learning by starting school later to minimize differences in diurnal learning behavior between school days and non-school days. Statement of Significance Students with a larger temporal discrepancy (i.e. phase difference) in sleep and learning behavior on school days relative to free days may have a learning disadvantage. To explore this possibility, we measured the diurnal rhythm of digital learning activity in 33 645 university students. Students whose diurnal rhythm was earlier on school days relative to non-school days had a lower grade point average. Larger phase differences in behavior were observed in students with a later chronotype and for earlier school start times. Course grades decreased as students’ first class of the day became more misaligned with their diurnal learning behavior on non-school days. Universities can potentially reduce circadian/ behavioral misalignment and improve students’ learning by scheduling fewer early morning classes. Introduction The circadian timing of sleep–wake behavior has important implications for performance and health. Normally, the circadian system ensures that the sleep–wake rhythm is timed appropriately with the solar day [ 1 ]. However, social schedules have a strong influence on sleep–wake choices and can shift behavior out of synch with the circadian clock [ 2 , 3 ]. In adolescents and university students, the timing of sleep–wake behavior often differs between school days and non-school days. Students are more likely to sleep at their preferred times on non-school days (i.e. according to their chronotype) in phase with their circadian clock [ 4 , 5 ]. In contrast, early class start times on school days impose constraints on sleep timing that can cause sleep to shift earlier and shorten due to waking up earlier [ 6 ]. The shift in sleep–wake behavior alters students’ timing of light–dark exposure and may lead to resetting of their circadian clock [ 7–10 ]. If the shift in sleep behavior is too large; however, the circadian clock cannot keep pace due to its slower kinetics. Early school start times are therefore thought to contribute to greater circadian misalignment [ 6 , 11 , 12 ], especially in students with a later chronotype [ 12 ]. This is a major concern because some studies have found that the discrepancy in sleep timing on school days versus free days is associated with lower academic achievement, behavioral problems, depression symptoms, and poorer physical health [ 13–16 ]. Recent studies have shown that diurnal behavior can be measured in university students by analyzing their interactions with the learning management system (LMS) used to administer and deliver course content [ 6 , 17 , 18 ]. Students frequently log in to the LMS to access course materials, upload assignments, participate in online discussions, and take quizzes. LMS-logins accumulate in large numbers over time and can be used to construct individualized diurnal profiles of students’ learning-directed behavior [ 18 ]. Using circular statistics, these aggregated data can be used to estimate a student’s median LMS-login phase, which serves as a phase marker of the timing of their learning rhythm with respect to local clock time [ 18 ]. Consistent with results for sleep behavior, students’ LMS-login phase is usually earlier on school days compared with non-school days [ 6 , 17 , 18 ]. Therefore, the phase change in the LMS-login rhythm on school days may be a relative indicator of circadian misalignment of diurnal learning behavior. A key advantage of this approach is that LMS-login phase can be derived passively in nearly the entire student population. As proposed in prior work [ 17 , 18 ], this may enable universities to assess how their educational practices and policies (e.g. school start time and class scheduling) contribute to circadian/ behavioral desynchrony and learning outcomes, while also making it possible to identify at-risk students who may benefit from targeted interventions. It is important to test whether the discrepancy in LMS-login phase between school days and non-school days is associated with academic achievement, as universities could use this information to guide decision-making and educational interventions. Only one study has examined this relationship and found that grade point average decreased for larger phase differences in LMS-login behavior (referred to as “social jet lag” in the article) [ 18 ]. However, this result was based on correlational analyses using averaged performance data across different groups of students. The strength of the association may be substantially weaker when accounting for individual variability in academic performance. In addition, the effect size for LMS-login phase differences on academic achievement has not been measured, nor has the threshold level of misalignment been determined which gives rise to a significant decrement in grade point average. Prior analyses have also not adjusted for demographic variables (e.g. sex, age, and academic year of matriculation) that may influence students’ academic performance or their diurnal learning behavior. Moreover, there has yet to be a detailed analysis of how the phase of students’ LMS-login rhythm is modulated by their chronotype and school start time. Addressing these knowledge gaps is important for understanding the drivers of behavioral desynchrony in university students and the potential impact on academic achievement. The first objective of our study was to test the association between the change in LMS-login phase on school days versus non-school days with grade point average. We hypothesized that university students with a larger phase-change in diurnal learning behavior on school days would have a lower grade point average, adjusting for covariates. We also sought to determine the effect size and threshold level of change in LMS-login phase that was associated with significantly worse academic performance. The second objective of our study was to characterize the chronotype-dependent influence of school start time on the direction and magnitude of change in students’ LMS-login phase. We predicted that students whose school start time was more closely aligned with their LMS-login phase on non-school days (i.e. aligned with their LMS-login chronotype) would show minimal change in their diurnal learning behavior on school days. We further predicted that students would obtain higher grades when their first class of the day was in synch with their LMS-login chronotype. Methods Ethics statement Permission to analyze university archived student data was obtained from the Institute for Applied Learning Sciences and Educational Technology (ALSET), National University of Singapore (NUS). ALSET stores and links de-identified data for educational analytics research, including students’ demographic information (age, sex, ethnicity, country of citizenship, academic year of matriculation, school/faculty of enrollment, and type of residence), course enrollment and grades, and interactions with the LMS. Students consented to making their data available for research when they enrolled at NUS and signed the Student Data Protection Policy. Analyses of university archived data were exempt from review by the NUS Institutional Review Board because they were performed retrospectively on data that were de-identified to the researchers. The research was approved by the NUS Learning Analytics Committee on Ethics (LACE) which oversees educational research. Determination of phase of diurnal learning behavior using LMS-login data Students’ logins on the LMS were analyzed over 5 semesters (from 2016/17 semester 2 to 2018/19 semester 2). The starting dataset comprised 18.9 million logins from 40 511 students. The dataset included all undergraduate students who enrolled in at least one course held on campus. Students from the Faculty of Medicine and Faculty of Dentistry were excluded because these schools have a different type of curriculum, grading system, and LMS than the other schools at NUS. Login data were sorted by students’ course timetables into non-school days (days with no classes) and school days (days with classes). Data were extracted for 33 645 students who had at least 24 logins on non-school days and at least 24 logins on school days. This criterion ensured that there were sufficient data for estimating the phase of each student’s LMS-login rhythm. Each student’s time-stamped logins were analyzed separately on non-school days and school days using circular statistics to determine the median LMS-login phase [ 18 ]. The LMS-login phase was expressed in local time where the research was performed (Singapore, where the Universal Time Coordinated is +8 hours and daylight saving time is not observed). The “phase-change” or “phase-difference” was calculated as the difference in LMS-login phase on school days relative to non-school days expressed in hours. Positive values indicated an earlier phase on school days, and negative values indicated a later phase on school days. To avoid confusion, the terms phase shift, phase advance, and phase delay were not used to describe our results because they are typically used to describe circadian phase responses. In instances where the direction of the phase-change was ambiguous due to the circular properties of time (e.g. a phase-change of +13 hours or −11 hours is equivalent with respect to clock time), we chose the value that was within ±12 hours. Circular statistics were calculated with the “circular” package (version 0.4–93) [ 19 ] using R statistical software (version 3.6.3) [ 20 ]. Academic performance Students’ course grades were extracted for the 5 semesters that LMS logins were analyzed. At NUS, students are given a letter grade that is converted to a number for calculating the grade point (A+ and A = 5.0, A− = 4.5, B+ = 4.0, B = 3.5, B− = 3.0, C+ = 2.5, C = 2.0, D+ = 1.5, D = 1.0, and F = 0.0). Students earn course credits based on the estimated workload hours per week, which includes contact hours for lectures, tutorials, and laboratories, as well as hours spent on preparatory work, projects, and assignments. The grade point average represents the cumulative performance weighted by the credits earned in each course. Hence, a B+ (i.e. grade point of 4.0) earned for a 4-credit course would count more toward a student’s grade point average, as compared with a B+ earned in a 2-credit course. To calculate grade point average, the grade point for each course was first multiplied by the number of credits for that course. The sum of all values across courses was then divided by the sum of all course credits. For example, if a student obtained a B+ for a course worth 4 credits, a C in a course worth 4 credits, and an A- in a course worth 2 credits, the grade point average would be calculated as 3.30 [(4.0 × 4 credits + 2.0 × 4 credits + 4.5 × 2 credits)/(10-course credits)]. The grade point average was determined for each student over the period that they used the LMS (ranging from 1 to 5 semesters per student). Statistical analyses of LMS-login phase-differences and academic performance Students were binned by 1-hour increments into 24 groups by the difference in their LMS-login phase on school days relative to non-school days (i.e. spanning ±12 hours). A sample size calculation indicated that at least 500 students per group were needed to detect a 0.10 difference in grade point average between groups (SD = 0.56; power = 0.80, alpha = 0.05). We chose a difference value of 0.10 as the benchmark change in grade point average because our previous work indicated that this corresponded to a small but meaningful effect size for academic achievement (Cohen’s d ≅ 0.20) [ 6 ]. We restricted our analyses to the 10 groups with at least 500 students, which included a total of 32 309 students (Difference in LMS-login phase: −2 to −1 hours, n = 781; −1 hour to 0, n = 1910; 0 to 1 hours, n = 4191; 1 to 2 hours, n = 6773; 2 to 3 hours, n = 6898; 3 to 4 hours, n = 5047; 4 to 5 hours, n = 3236; 5 to 6 hours, n = 1862; 6 to 7 hours, n = 1052; 7 to 8 hours, n = 559). Demographic characteristics were compared among phase-difference groups using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. The association between the difference in LMS-login phase on school days versus non-school days (independent variable, 10 categories) and grade point average (dependent variable, continuous measure) was examined using a linear mixed-effects model. The change in LMS-login phase was entered as a fixed effect factor (reference category = 0 to 1 hours) with school/faculty of enrollment included as a random effect factor (Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences, Faculty of Engineering, Faculty of Science, Business School, School of Design and Environment, School of Computing, Faculty of Law, Yale-NUS College, Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine Nursing Program, School of Continuing and Lifelong Education, Yong Siew Toh Conservatory of Music, Multi-disciplinary Degree Programs, or Others). Covariates included sex (female, male), age in years, ethnicity (Chinese, Indian, Malay, and Others), country of citizenship (Singaporean, Singapore Permanent Resident, and Foreigner), academic year of matriculation (2011/12 or earlier, 2012/13, 2013/14, 2014/15, 2015/16, 2016/17, 2017/18, and 2018/19), and type of residence (on campus, off campus, and mix of on and off campus). The model did not include LMS-login chronotype (login phase on non-school days) as a covariate because this variable was strongly correlated with the LMS-login phase difference (see results; Pearson’s r = 0.65). Students’ class year was not entered into the model because it was redundant with their year of matriculation. The linear mixed-effects model was implemented using the “lme4” (version 1.1–29) and “lmerTest” (version 3.1–3) packages with R Statistical Software [ 21 , 22 ]. Satterthwaite’s method was used to perform t -tests for fixed effects. Upon finding evidence of a statistically significant association between the difference in LMS-login phase on school days versus non-school days and grade point average ( p &lt; .05), multiple comparisons between LMS-login phase-difference groups were performed using Tukey’s test using the “emmeans” package (version 1.6.1). Effect sizes were calculated with the “dabest” package (version 0.3.0) using Python 3.7.8 and R statistical software [ 23 ], with the reference group defined as LMS-login phase-difference values ranging from 0 to 1 hour. Associations of LMS-login chronotype and school start time with changes in diurnal learning behavior on school days The strength of the relationship between the LMS-login phase difference versus LMS-login chronotype was tested using Pearson’s correlation analysis ( n = 33 645; threshold of significance, p &lt; .05). As described above, the change in LMS-login phase was calculated as the difference in LMS-login phase on school days relative to non-school days, and LMS-login chronotype was defined as the LMS-login phase on non-school days. Given that phase data are circular, the data were visually inspected before performing Pearson’s correlation analysis. Some phase-difference data were displaced by 24 hours to approximate a linear relationship in the corresponding scatter plot (e.g. a phase-difference of −10 hours is equivalent to a phase-difference of +14 hours with respect to clock time). We added 24 hours to the phase-difference value if the LMS-login phase on non-school days was later than 20:00 and the phase-difference exceeded −6 hours (i.e. ranging from −6 to −12 hours), and we subtracted 24 hours from the phase-difference value if the phase on non-school days was earlier than 08:00 and the phase-difference was greater than +6 hours (i.e. ranging from +6 to +12 hours). The relationship between LMS-login phase-difference values and school start time was evaluated by sorting students’ logins by their first class of the day (i.e. by their school start time). For each start time, LMS-logins were extracted over a 24-hour period starting from 19:00 on the prior day. A given student could have multiple school start times across the week as determined by their course timetable. In each semester (AY2016/17 semester 2, AY2017/18 semester 1, AY2017/18 semester 2, AY2018/19 semester 1, AY2018/19 semester 2), the median LMS-login phase was determined for non-school days and for each school start time (08:00, 09:00, 10:00, 11:00, 12:00, 13:00, 14:00, 15:00, 16:00, 17:00, and 18:00) by pooling login data across students (No. of logins per semester sorted by school start time: range on non-school days = 768 215 to 1 180 852; range on school days = 4126 to 708 454). The association between the change in LMS-login phase and school start time was tested using a linear-mixed-effects model. School start time was included as a fixed effect factor (reference category = No classes) with semester included as a random effect factor. The analysis did not adjust for student-level characteristics (e.g. age, sex, ethnicity, or year of matriculation) or school/course-level characteristics because LMS-login phase was determined on a per-semester basis for each school start time using data that were pooled across students. Multiple comparisons between school start times were performed using Tukey’s test. Chronotype-dependent effects of school start time on LMS-login behavior and course grades The change in LMS-login phase on school days (dependent variable) was measured in response to when students’ first class of the day took place relative to their LMS-login phase on non-school days (independent variable), i.e. the phase of the school start time was expressed relative to students’ LMS-login chronotype (see below for more details and an example). The data were plotted in a similar style as a circadian phase response curve, except that all variables were expressed relative to LMS-login phase (i.e. a behavioral variable) rather than circadian phase (not measured in our study). The utility of plotting the data this way is to show the chronotype-dependent effects of school start time on students’ diurnal learning rhythm. The premise is that the earliness or lateness of a school start time depends not only on the local time that the class starts, but also on students’ chronotype. For example, a class held at 10:00 might be considered early for students with a later chronotype, and late for students with an earlier chronotype. Hence, the x -axis represents the conditions that can give rise to a change in diurnal behavior on school days (i.e. the degree of mismatch between the school start time and chronotype), and the y -axis represents the change in diurnal behavior that actually occurs (i.e. the phase-change in diurnal behavior that may result in behavioral/circadian misalignment). There was insufficient data to estimate the LMS-login phase on a per-student basis for each of their school start times. We, therefore, pooled data for students with a similar LMS-login phase who shared the same school start time. First, we sorted students into 24 hourly groups by their LMS-login phase on non-school days (i.e. by their LMS-login chronotype). Login data for each student were then sorted by their first class of the day (08:00, 09:00, 10:00, 11:00, 12:00, 13:00, 14:00, 15:00, 16:00, 17:00, and 18:00). Hence, there were 264 possible combinations of LMS-login phase and school start time (24 phase bins × 11 school start times). There were 43 combinations with insufficient data (&lt;24 logins on either school days or non-school days). For each of the remaining 221 combinations of LMS-login phase and school start time, students’ logins were pooled and circular statistics were used to determine the median login phase. The number of students ranged from 1 to 6304 for each combination of LMS-login phase and school start time. In the same set of students for each combination, logins were pooled on non-school days to determine the median LMS-login phase. Circular statistics were performed with the “circular” package (version 0.4–93) using R statistical software [ 19 , 20 ]. The LMS-login phase difference for a given combination of LMS-login chronotype and school start time was determined by subtracting the LMS-login phase on school days from the LMS-login phase on non-school days. The “initial phase” of each school start time was determined relative to the LMS-login phase on non-school days. Hence, the initial phase can be viewed as a measure of how closely aligned the school start time is with students’ LMS-login chronotype. The initial phase was expressed in degrees (the 24-hour cycle was converted to 360 degrees; 1 hour = 15 degrees) with the LMS-login phase on non-school days defined as 0 degrees. For example, if students’ first class of the day occurred at 10:00 and their LMS-login phase was 16:00 on non-school days, the initial phase of the school start time would be 270 degrees (i.e. 90 degrees earlier than their login phase). By comparison, if students’ first class of the day occurred at 18:00 and their login phase was 16:00 on non-school days, the initial phase of the school start time would be 30 degrees. The chronotype-dependent effects of school start time on diurnal learning behavior were also visualized by plotting the data in the style of a phase transition curve [ 24 ]. The “final phase” of the school start time (i.e. expressed relative to the LMS-login phase on school days) was plotted against the initial phase of the school start time (i.e. expressed relative to the LMS-login phase on non-school days). As described above, the initial phase was expressed in degrees (the 24-hour cycle was converted to 360 degrees; 1 hour = 15 degrees) with the LMS-login phase on non-school days defined as 0 degrees. By comparison, the final phase was expressed in degrees with the LMS-login phase on school days defined as 0 degrees. The final phase is equivalent to adding the change in phase (expressed in degrees) to the initial phase. For example, if the initial phase of the school start time was 270 degrees, and the LMS-login phase was 3 hours earlier on school days, the final phase would be calculated as 315 degrees (270 degrees + 45 degrees). By comparison, if the initial phase of the school start time was 30 degrees, and the LMS-login phase was 1 hour later on school days, the final phase would be calculated as 15 degrees (30 degrees + −15 degrees). As described in prior work, data were fitted using an iterative smoothing procedure to minimize the perpendicular distance from the data to the curve [ 25 ]. Results were plotted using SigmaPlot 14.5 software (Systat Software, Inc.). Chronotype-dependent associations of school start times with grades were assessed by extracting course grades that corresponded to students’ first class of the day (i.e. using the same data described in the previous sections). A given course was included in the analysis if it was the student’s first class of the day on at least 1 day of the school week. Next, we calculated the phase of students’ first class of the day relative to their LMS-login phase on non-school days (i.e. relative to their LMS-login chronotype). As described above, the initial phase of the school start time was expressed in degrees (0 degrees = LMS-login phase on non-school days). Students’ course grades were then sorted in 15-degree bins (i.e. in hourly increments). Based on our sample size calculation for detecting differences in grade point average (see above), we limited our comparisons to bins with grades from at least 500 students (phase-bin, sample size: 0–15°, n = 4484; 15–30°, n = 2912; 30–45°, n = 1,551; 45–60°, n = 686; 150–165°, n = 1060; 165–180°, n = 2529; 180–195°, n = 4873; 195–210°, n = 8412; 210–225°, n = 13 243; 225–240°, n = 18 130; 240–255°, n = 20 502; 255–270°, n = 20 455; 270–285°, n = 18 623; 285–300°, n = 16 501; 300–315°, n = 14 117; 315–330°, n = 11 777; 330–345°, n = 9186; 345–360°, n = 6751). We pooled the data from 60–150° (i.e. spanning 6 hours) into a single bin ( n = 696) because the sample sizes were small in this range and we wanted to use all available data. For a given school start time phase-bin ( n &gt; 500 students per bin), we calculated the mean course grade and 95% CIs. Simple effect sizes (i.e. the difference in mean course grades) were calculated with the “dabest” package (version 0.3.0) using Python 3.7.8 and R statistical software [ 23 ], with the reference group defined as a school start time phase-bin of 345–360°. A two-sided permutation t -test was performed for comparisons with the reference group. The P value for a given comparison represents the likelihood of observing a “statistically significant” effect if the null hypothesis of zero-difference is true (threshold for statistical significance, p &lt; .05). Results Change in diurnal learning behavior on school days versus non-school days is associated with academic achievement The diurnal rhythm of LMS-logins on school days and non-school days was analyzed in 33 645 university students. In each student, the temporal distribution of LMS-logins was used to determine his/her LMS-login phase ( Figure 1A ). The change in LMS-login phase was determined for school days relative to non-school days. Students were binned hourly into groups according to the direction and magnitude of change in their LMS-login phase. The distribution of phase-difference values showed that most students (89.9%, n = 30 252) had an earlier login rhythm on school days ( Figure 1B ). Students whose LMS-login phase difference was larger in the positive direction on school days (i.e. change to an earlier phase) were younger with a more recent date of matriculation, and they were more likely to be female and reside on campus ( Supplementary Table S1 ). Students whose LMS-login phase difference was in the negative direction on school days (i.e. change to a later phase) were older, more likely to live off campus, and they were more likely to be enrolled in the School of Continuing and Lifelong Education which caters to working adults. Students with a larger change in LMS-login phase on school days relative to non-school days had a lower grade point average ( Figure 1C ) adjusting for sex, age, ethnicity, country of citizenship, year of matriculation, and location of residence (on or off campus) ( Supplementary Table S2 ). Multiple comparison tests showed that grade point average was significantly lower for all groups whose LMS-login phase was more than 2 hours earlier on school days, as compared with students with minimal change in their LMS-login phase (reference group = 0 to 1 hours; Tukey’s test, p &lt; .001 for all pairwise comparisons). Effect sizes ranged from small to medium and increased for larger LMS-login phase-differences (Cohen’s d range = −0.08 to −0.38 for phase-difference values &gt;2 hours) ( Figure 1D ; Supplementary Table S3 ). Chronotype-dependent associations of school start time with diurnal learning behavior and course grades The change in students’ LMS-login phase on school days was strongly correlated with their LMS-login chronotype (Pearson’s r = 0.65, p &lt; .001) ( Figure 2A ). Students with a later LMS-login phase on non-school days showed a larger phase change in the positive direction (to an earlier phase) on school days, whereas students with an earlier LMS-login chronotype were more likely to show a phase change in the negative direction (to a later phase) on school days. The change in LMS-login phase on school days was also associated with students’ school start time ( Figure 2B ). Students’ login data were sorted by their first class of the day in each semester, and then they were then pooled across students for each school start time (ranging from 08:00 to 18:00) to generate population-level estimates of LMS-login phase. All school start times were associated with an earlier login phase compared with non-school days (Tukey’s test, p &lt; .001 for all pairwise comparisons), with larger changes in LMS-login phase for earlier school start times ( Figure 2B ; Supplementary Table S4 ). The change in LMS-login phase was greatest when students’ first class started at 08:00 (3.42 hours, 95% CI = 3.22 to 3.62 hours), and was significantly larger compared with all start times that began at 10:00 or later (Tukey’s test, p &lt; .001 for all pairwise comparisons). Next, we assessed the chronotype-dependent effects of school start time on diurnal learning behavior by expressing school start times relative to students’ LMS-login phase on non-school days ( Figure 3A–B ). The direction and magnitude of change in LMS-login phase were dependent on the initial phase when school started relative to students’ LMS-login chronotype. When students’ first class started earlier than their LMS-login phase on non-school days, there was a phase change in the positive direction to an earlier phase. Conversely, when students’ first class started later than their LMS-login phase on non-school days, there was a phase change in the negative direction to a later phase. The magnitude of the phase difference increased for school start times that were more out of synch with students’ LMS-login chronotype. Differences in LMS-login phase of up to ±12 hours were observed when school started in antiphase with students’ LMS-login chronotype. The chronotype-dependent effects of school start time on students’ diurnal learning behavior were also visualized by plotting the data in the style of a phase transition curve, in which the phase of students’ first class of the day was expressed relative to their LMS-login rhythm on non-school days (the initial phase) and school days (the final phase) ( Figure 3C ). The range of values for the final phase was much narrower than the range of values for the initial phase, indicating that the timing of students’ diurnal behavioral rhythm changed strongly in the direction of their first class of the day (i.e. toward the daytime when classes were held irrespective of the initial phase). Next, we tested whether students’ course grades varied according to whether their first class of the day was aligned with their LMS-login chronotype ( Figure 3D ). Course grades were highest when students’ first class of the day occurred within a few hours of their LMS-login phase on non-school days and decreased as school start times deviated further from their LMS-login chronotype. Hence, grades were higher when there was little or no change in the phase of their diurnal learning rhythm on school days versus non-school days, and lower when phase differences were large ( Figure 3A–B ). Relative to students whose first class of the day took place within an hour of their LMS-login chronotype (initial phase of school start time = 345–0°), course grades were lower when the initial phase of the school start time was more than 4 hours earlier or more than 2 hours later (Difference in course grades, p &lt; .05 for all comparisons ranging from 15° to 300°, except from 30 to 45° where p = .141) ( Figure 3D ). Discussion The present study used passively collected LMS-logins to characterize university-wide relationships of students’ diurnal behavior with academic achievement and school start times. Most students showed an earlier rhythm of learning directed behavior on school days, and the change in phase relative to non-school days was inversely associated with grade point average. We, therefore, reproduced the findings from the only other study to investigate diurnal LMS-login behavior and academic performance [ 18 ], while also extending the work by measuring the associated effect sizes, determining the threshold change in LMS-login phase associated with a lower grade point average (i.e. &gt;2 hours), and adjusting for covariates in the analysis. We further showed that the change in LMS-login phase on school days was greater in students with a later login rhythm on non-school days (i.e. later LMS-login chronotype) and was amplified for earlier school start times. Minimal changes in LMS-login phase and higher course grades were observed when students’ first class of the day was aligned with their LMS-login chronotype, which usually corresponded to start times in the late morning or afternoon. In contrast, large changes in LMS-login phase were observed when classes occurred out of phase with students’ LMS-login chronotype and, in such instances, course grades were lower. Our results suggest that the timing of students’ classes relative to their preferred diurnal learning rhythm may have implications for their academic achievement. Our findings extend prior work demonstrating that students’ digital traces can be used to measure differences in diurnal behavior that occur on work or school days versus free days [ 6 , 18 ]. Recently, we showed that students’ LMS-login rhythm was associated with their sleep–wake timing assessed by actigraphy [ 6 ], indicating that diurnal rhythms of learning-directed behavior and sleep are temporally related. The effects of school start time on LMS-login phase may therefore provide a window into changes in sleep behavior, even though these measures capture different types of information. Related studies indicate that effects of social schedules on diurnal behavior can be inferred from social media or smartphone interactions [ 26–28 ], but these studies did not investigate associations with school or work performance. An important advantage of using LMS-logins is that these data can be linked directly to other types of student data including grades. As shown here and in previous work [ 18 ], university students with a larger phase-change in their LMS-login rhythm on school days had a lower grade point average. The effect sizes that we observed for changes in diurnal behavior and grade point average were comparable to many educational interventions for improving school performance (a Cohen’s d of 0.4 corresponds to a medium effect size for educational outcomes) [ 29 ]. Substantially lower academic achievement (|Cohen’s d | &gt;0.25) was observed when students’ LMS-login phase on school days was at least 5 hours earlier compared with non-school days, corresponding to about 10% of our student population. Our findings for LMS-login behavior and grade point average are consistent with prior studies that investigated changes in self-reported mid-sleep timing on school days versus free days. In university students, larger changes in mid-sleep timing were associated with lower grade point average and lower course grades, adjusting for covariates [ 15 , 30 , 31 ]. Comparable findings have been reported in children and adolescents, in whom a greater discrepancy in mid-sleep timing on school days and weekends was associated with lower academic achievement [ 14 , 32–35 ]. Hence, interventions that reduce the temporal discrepancy in diurnal behavior between school and non-school days could have a meaningful impact on students’ learning if these variables are causally related. Consistent with previous studies of self-reported sleep behavior, we found that the change in timing of students’ LMS-login rhythm on school days was related to their LMS-login chronotype. Prior work showed that the change in mid-sleep timing on work days versus free days (a surrogate marker of “social jet lag”) showed an approximately linear positive correlation with the sleep debt-adjusted midpoint of sleep on free days (a commonly used definition for chronotype) [ 2 ]. These findings suggest that late-type individuals must adjust their behavior by a larger amount to their work schedule. We interpret our findings in a similar way, whereby students with a later LMS-login chronotype must adjust their diurnal learning behavior by a larger amount to their school schedule, especially for earlier school start times. We also found that the change in LMS-login phase was greatest for early morning classes, which is consistent with our previous results for the midpoint of actigraphy-determined sleep in university students [ 6 ]. Our results for students’ diurnal learning behavior, therefore, resemble previous findings for sleep, whereby later chronotype and earlier school start times may give rise to large changes in behavior and circadian misalignment. Our study provides evidence that effects of school schedules on students’ diurnal learning behavior and grades are chronotype-dependent. Here, we took the view that the earliness or lateness of school start times is determined not only by the clock time, but also by when the class starts with respect to a student’s chronotype. The direction and magnitude of phase change in diurnal learning behavior were strongly influenced by whether the start time was aligned with students’ chronotype. Minimal changes in diurnal learning behavior were observed when students’ first class of the day occurred in synch with their LMS-login chronotype. By comparison, large changes in LMS-login phase were observed when students’ first class of the day deviated further from their LMS-login chronotype. Our findings extend prior work in high school students demonstrating interactive effects of school start time and chronotype on sleep behavior, whereby chronotype-dependent changes in sleep timing on school days were greater when school started in the morning rather than the afternoon [ 12 , 36 , 37 ]. We showed that students’ course grades were related to whether their school start time was aligned with their LMS-login chronotype. Students’ grades for their first class of the day were highest when school started within a few hours of their LMS-login chronotype, which suggests that performance is better when students do not have to adjust their learning rhythm to their school schedule. By comparison, course grades were lower when classes were more misaligned with students’ LMS-login chronotype. Our findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that late-type students may have an academic disadvantage for classes and examinations held in the morning [ 12 , 15 , 34 , 38 , 39 ]. Together, these results suggest that students who need to adjust their diurnal behavior by a greater amount to their school schedule may perform more poorly due to circadian misalignment and associated changes in sleep (e.g. shorter sleep that is poorer in quality). The shapes of the curves that we observed for chronotype-dependent effects of school start time on diurnal learning behavior ( Figure 3A–B ) resembled findings for circadian light responses in humans for a 3-cycle stimulus of bright light [ 24 ]. However, the mechanisms underlying changes in LMS-login phase (a behavioral measure) and circadian phase (e.g. melatonin or body temperature phase) are different between studies. The earlier laboratory study measured physiological circadian rhythms before and after the light stimulus using highly controlled experimental procedures. In our study, it is likely that the students’ diurnal learning rhythm had an endogenous circadian component but was also influenced by environmental factors and personal choices. We assume that changes in phase in LMS-login behavior on school days were predominantly driven by the imposed social schedule, as opposed to “Type 0” phase-amplitude resetting of the circadian clock [ 24 , 25 ]. Previous studies have shown small intra-individual changes in the phase of the melatonin rhythm between weekdays and weekends (i.e., typically less than an hour) [ 8 , 40 ]. Therefore, the large changes in LMS-login phase that we observed on school days versus non-school days cannot be explained by resetting of the circadian clock. The findings in our study are conceptually related to “social jet lag,” which is a term used to describe circadian misalignment of behavior with social schedules [ 2 , 3 ]. The term originated from the observation that sleep timing often differs on work days compared with free days. Social let lag was conceptualized as a chronic state in which the influence of the circadian clock on sleep behavior is more apparent on free days whereas the social schedule dominates on work or school days. In practice, circadian misalignment caused by work or school schedules is rarely measured directly because there is no scalable method for reliably determining circadian phase in large samples of free-living individuals. Instead, most studies operationally define social jet lag as the difference in mid-sleep timing on work or school days versus free days [ 3 ], based on the assumption that this can be used as a surrogate for the phase-angle difference between the circadian clock and the imposed social schedule [ 3 ]. While there is a precedent for using the term social jet lag for describing differences in the phase of LMS-login behavior and Twitter activity on school days/ weekdays versus free days [ 18 , 27 ], we did not use this term to describe our findings because it is unclear whether our data satisfy the conceptual definition of social jet lag. We did not measure circadian phase and hence did not establish whether the change in LMS-login phase on school days versus non-school days was associated with a change in phase angle. While the LMS-login phase on non-school days may be a relative indicator of students’ preferred diurnal rhythm, it is unlikely that this measure can provide a reliable estimate of circadian phase. Additional studies are needed to establish the degree to which changes in LMS-login phase reflect circadian misalignment of behavior, including measures of objectively verified sleep and circadian phase. There are several limitations of using LMS-login data to infer changes in sleep–wake behavior and associated effects on academic performance. Students’ diurnal pattern of LMS usage may be influenced by environmental factors (e.g. job, family responsibilities, social commitments) and personal preferences unrelated to their sleep pattern. Students may also log in more frequently near the start of classes to check for course announcements or to download course materials. Hence, the LMS-login rhythm is likely more sensitive to school start time compared with the sleep rhythm. Students' LMS-login pattern may also be influenced by psychological learning characteristics including motivation, self-regulation, and conscientiousness, which are important determinants of academic success [ 30 ]. Relatedly, we did not investigate the mechanisms linking students’ diurnal LMS-login behavior with academic achievement, which may include circadian desynchrony, later chronotype, shorter sleep, sleep disturbances, and poorer mental health [ 13 , 16 , 41 , 42 ]. Notably, prior studies have shown that a larger discrepancy in sleep timing on work days/school days versus free days is associated with negative metabolic outcomes (e.g. higher body mass index, obesity, and type-2 diabetes), poorer health lifestyle behaviors (e.g. unhealthy dietary choices, lower physical activity, and substance abuse), and mental health problems (e.g. behavioral problems, mood disorders, depression, and bipolar disorder) [ 13 , 16 , 33 , 43 ]. Future studies should therefore test whether diurnal LMS-login behavior is related to students’ health outcomes, as this may impact their well-being and learning potential. In conclusion, we have shown that school start times have a profound impact on university students’ diurnal learning-directed behavior with implications for academic achievement. The optimal time for starting school may be in the late morning if the goal is to minimize behavioral/ circadian misalignment of students’ sleep and learning behavior with their class schedule [ 44 ]. Universities could delay the start of the school day and/or offer more classes at times that are better aligned with students’ learning rhythm [ 39 ]. Providing on-demand recorded video lectures would also allow students to access course materials at their preferred learning times. Additionally, universities could use LMS data (or other digital traces) to identify students with large weekday–weekend discrepancies in diurnal behavior and late-night activity who would benefit from individualized counseling on their sleep and learning behavior. In these students, behavioral interventions could facilitate earlier sleep and learning patterns to minimize phase differences in their behavioral rhythm on school days and non-school days. Looking forward, we envisage that LMS data can be used by universities to assess the impact of their class scheduling practices on students’ diurnal learning behavior, and for assessing interventions to improve sleep and well-being. This is important because sleep problems and poor learning strategies are major barriers to students’ success and future career options.
