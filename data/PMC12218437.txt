ABSTRACT Objective There is currently a lack of intervention trials focusing on parent management strategies for Internet gaming disorder (IGD) and smartphone addiction. The aim of the current study (ACTRN12622001355763) was to examine the efficacy of the Tech Diet Parent Program (TDPP), an online parent management video program, in reducing IGD and smartphone addiction symptoms and associated developmental impacts. Method A total of N = 689 parent participants (with school-aged children) completed baseline measures and 153 parents provided 6-week follow-up data after completing the TDPP. Results Fifty-nine percent had a child at baseline with high levels of IGD and/or smartphone addiction symptoms. At 6-week follow-up 60% of children with high levels of IGD symptoms and 32% of high levels of smartphone addiction were significantly lower post-program completion. Increased levels of program dosage and compliance were found to be important factors in reductions to IGD and smartphone addiction symptoms, particularly for the high symptom sample. Higher dosage and compliance was also correlated with a significant reduction in the negative developmental impacts of screen use on the children, notably in the sub-domains of physical/social development and education. Conclusions These findings have implications for utilising online parent management strategies for the prevention and intervention of both IGD and smartphone addiction, either as a stand-alone intervention or in conjunction with established individual treatments like CBT. Key Points What is already known about this topic: (1) Technology use (smartphone, computers, tablets) is increasingly common among younger children. (2) Internet gaming disorder and smartphone addiction in children and teenagers are of increasing concern for parents, schools, and communities. (3) Cognitive behavioural therapy and pharmacological interventions for IGD are the most common treatment modes. What this topic adds: (1) This study found encouraging evidence for using parent education and behavioural management strategies (TDPP) for successful intervention of both IGD and smartphone addiction. (2) This study found TDPP was successful in reducing developmental impacts associated with IGD and smartphone addiction, most notably in the areas of physical/social health and education. (3) This lays the groundwork for clinicians seeing children and adolescents with IGD, smartphone addiction and other screen-related concerns to consider parent-based interventions as a viable option, particularly in the case of the child refusing to access services. KEYWORDS Internet gaming disorder IGD smartphone addiction developmental impacts parent intervention Similarly, for the group of people who had a score above the clinical cut-off for either or both the SABAS and IGD-10, there was a significant negative correlation between the dosage/compliance score and the time 2 – time 1 change score for IGDT-10-P scores ( r (83) = −.27, p = .012). Thus, for this group greater program dosage and compliance was linked to a greater reduction in IGDT-10-P symptomology. However, for participants who did not meet the high level criteria for IGDT-10-P/SABAS-P, no significant correlation between dosage/compliance scores and IGDT-10-P score reduction emerged, r (66) = −.090, p = .467, as illustrated in Figure 2 . Dosage and developmental impacts There was a significant negative correlation between dosage/compliance scores and total developmental impact scores, r(151) = −.26, p = .001, suggesting that greater program engagement was linked with reduced developmental impacts. When subscale correlations were computed, two subscales had significant negative correlations (Social/Physical Activity, r(151) = −.27, p = &lt;.001; Education, r(151) = −.20, p = .016) and two failed to reach significance (Emotional, r(151) = −.033, p = .685; Behavioural, r(151) = −.100, p = .340), suggesting that greater program engagement was particularly linked to reduced impacts on schooling and social and physical development. Multiple linear regression was used to test if specific parent management strategies (5 strategies) significantly predicted reduced SDIQ-P scores (i.e., impacts on Emotional; Social &amp; Physical, Behavioural, and Educational Development). Overall model fit for SDIQ-P scores was significant, F (5, 147) = 2.315, p = .047, R 2 = .073. Strategy 1, Managing the home WIFI with set limits was a significant predictor of reduced developmental impacts, and Strategy 5 Implementing consequences (screen time off) for breaking set rules approached significance as a predictor (see Table 6 ). Discussion Promising findings emerged for this TDPP intervention for families where one or more children have high levels of IGD and/or smartphone addiction symptoms. In the community sample used, 18.4% met criteria for clinical IGD (using the same cut-off as Hülquist et al. ( 2022 ), 48.9% met criteria for smartphone addiction and 59.9% of participants’ children met criteria for either IGD and/or smartphone addiction at baseline, resulting in a majority of the sample meeting the clinical threshold at baseline. Ramadan Ghareb and Abd Alsemia Elewa ( 2021 ) in clinic trial reported 73.6% of parents scored their child at clinical levels (32+) of the Gaming Addiction Screening Test (GAST) at baseline, followed by a reduction to 37.9% at three-month follow-up. Pornnoppadol et al. ( 2020 ) reported similar in clinic delivery results with parents in their “parent only treatment group” reporting a drop from 70% to less than 20% at six-month follow-up. When analysing the efficacy of TDPP ( N = 153) in reducing IGD symptoms compared to other parent interventions this study started with a smaller sample of 19.6% ( N = 30) with IGDT-10-5+ at baseline, and 7.8% ( N = 12) at six-week follow-up. This represents an encouraging 60% success rate in reducing symptoms to more healthy levels. For children with medium-high levels of smartphone addiction (SABAS) at baseline, there was a smaller but significant 32% success rate. To the authors’ knowledge this is the first study that reported intervention success rates for online parent intervention strategies of IGD and smartphone addiction and provides strong preliminary evidence for the TDPP’s efficacy (even after only 6 weeks of intervention) in reducing both smartphone addiction and particularly IGD symptoms. In comparison to recent studies utilising CBT treatment, programs report significant reductions in IGD symptoms when compared to wait list control groups; however, they do not report analysis of how many reach non-clinical symptom profile post treatment (André et al., 2023 ; Ji &amp; Wong, 2023 ; Lindenberg et al., 2022 ). In line with past studies (e.g (Lopez-Fernandez et al., 2019 ; Su et al., 2020 ), males were over-represented with IGD, making up around three quarters of the sample. Interestingly, age data revealed that the peak ages at which parents sought help ranged between 9 and 14 years old. This suggests problematic screen use, often thought of as mostly an issue for adolescents (Stevens et al., 2020 ) is clearly also a problem for tweens in upper primary school. This echoes the finding of (Marshall, under review-a ) which also found substantial screen use problems in tweens. Retention and drop-out Of the original sample at baseline ( N = 689), only 153 completed the follow-up questionnaires at 6 weeks, representing a 77.8% drop-out rate. This is slightly better than the previous online parent intervention study by (Marshall under review-a ) which found an 84% drop-out rate, but significantly short of the 18.6% drop-out reported in Hülquist et al. ( 2022 ). There are several possible explanations for this finding. First, Hülquist et al. ( 2022 ) ran their program at an in-patient clinic at a hospital, and it may be that hospital-based programs gain greater parental engagement. It is also possible that the face-to-face treatment delivery used by Hülquist et al. is superior in engaging parents and establishing rapport to reduce drop-outs. As we established earlier, the only comparable parent intervention using an online method of delivery was Raj et al. ( 2023 ) who reported a drop-out rate of only 2.2% for parents of pre-school children measuring screen time. The TDPP drop-out rates may indicate online delivery methods are not effective when applied to older children and adolescents as opposed to pre-school children and smartphone addiction and IGD as opposed to screen time. It is also plausible that some parents used the program as a preventative tool given that 40% of the sample at baseline did not meet concerning or high symptom levels, and therefore did not complete follow-up measures. Finally, it is possible that a substantial percentage of this study’s drop-outs were parents who completed the modules online, effected change in their family, but had little motivation to conclude the follow-up questionnaires as there was no financial incentive on offer and/or no therapeutic relationship with the research clinicians that encouraged them to fulfil their promise to complete the final survey. Dosage and outcomes The hypothesis that increases in the dosage and compliance score would be positively correlated with IGD and smartphone addiction symptoms was supported. A higher dosage and compliance score was significantly correlated with a decrease in both IGD and smartphone addiction symptomology, an outcome consistent with findings from other parent management intervention studies (Hülquist et al., 2022 ), which reported reduced IGD scores. Interestingly, this was true for the children who reported high-level symptoms at baseline, but not those with normal levels. This highlights the value of the intervention and is contrary to the conclusion of Brandhorst, Lahres, et al. ( 2022 ) that such interventions may be more suited as a preventative measure than a reactive set of strategies in the presence of high symptoms. The hypothesis that a higher dosage and compliance score would be positively related to a decrease in developmental impacts (as measured by the SDIQ-P) was supported. This suggests that greater engagement with the TDPP program itself is associated with significant improvements in developmental impacts commonly associated with screen related disorders, most notably those related to education (e.g., interrupted schooling and reduced academic achievement) and the social/physical activity domain (e.g., greater sleep and activity, improved relationships). Thus, TDPP seems to not only help reduce IGD and smartphone addiction symptomology, it also seems beneficial in terms of reduced developmental problems related to screen use. Implications Whilst this study was not a controlled trial and had low retention rates, it provides preliminary evidence for the efficacy of TDPP. TDPP is conveniently accessible online, which is a significant factor for busy or working parents. The program is easy to access and affordable to roll out at scale in a E-Health model. Furthermore, TDPP offers hope for clinicians working with, and parents of, children or adolescents who do not acknowledge impacts of screens and gaming use and refuse to access services. Limitations The following limitations need to be considered: Firstly, the research team did not ask parents to disclose their child’s chronological age, just their school year. While the data around their school year (or grade) does provide some age-related demographic information, it is not as accurate as the age itself. Second, parents completing the SABAS-P were instructed to select “strongly disagree” if their child did not own a phone. In retrospect there should have been an option for “not applicable” to separate non phone users from lowly or non-impacted phone users. Third, the drop-out rates noted are quite high, possibly biasing which type of families remained in the sample. Fourth, this study would benefit from a longer follow-up period of 3–6 months to ensure the improvements in symptoms and developmental impacts are maintained in the medium to long term. Finally, and perhaps most important, this intervention trial did not include a control group. Now that several studies have shown parent management interventions to be effective, controlled and randomised controlled trials are imperative to provide a more robust body of evidence moving forward. Future directions Future research is needed to clarify whether the high drop-out rates could potentially be corrected with financial incentives or more human interaction via case management, phone call check-ins, or text message prompts, all of which were not within the resources available to the research team. Further, future research would also benefit utilising a more robust design including: a randomised control group, a longer follow-up period to measure long term effects of the intervention, and the feasibility of combining TDPP with other treatment modes. Conclusions Parent management interventions are gaining traction in the treatment of IGD and smartphone addiction in children and adolescents. The present study provides evidence that parent intervention can be a highly effective mode in treating IGD and smartphone addiction, despite the absence of individual therapy with the index child or adolescent. Moreover, the parent management intervention was effective in reducing associated developmental impacts often associated with excessive screen use, specifically in the areas of physical health, social development and education.
