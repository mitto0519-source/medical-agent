Abstract Late-onset depression (LOD) refers to depression that newly appears in elderly individuals without prior depression episodes. Predicting future depression is crucial for mitigating the risk of major depression in prospective patients. This study aims to develop machine learning models to predict future depression. Using public data from the nationwide panel survey ‘Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging,’ we employed latent growth modeling and growth mixture modeling to identify four latent classes of depression trajectories in the elderly Korean population. Based on the results of binary logistic regression, we selected 12 variables capable of distinguishing the LOD population from the reference population and tested 12 machine learning (ML) algorithms. While most ML algorithms showed acceptable predictive capability, Random Forest Classifier and Gradient Boosting Classifier demonstrated superior performance. Consequently, we successfully established new ML-based LOD prediction programs. These programs could be further developed into self-checking online tools, expected to serve as decision support systems for primary medical care and health screening services. Introduction Depression is a prevalent mood disorder among the elderly population 1 . For individuals aged 60 and above, the overall prevalence of clinically diagnosed depressive disorders is estimated to be around 5.7% 2 . The frequency of depression increases progressively with age, peaking among those over 85 years old 3 . Depression in older adults is clinically recognized as Late-Life Depression (LLD) or Geriatric Depression. As major depression is diagnosed according to the stringent criteria set by DSM-5, many individuals with depressive symptoms are not captured in epidemiological data for depressive disorders. Consequently, the prevalence of depressive symptoms is estimated to be much higher than that of diagnosed depression. Indeed, a study of 1,304 community-dwelling adults aged 60 and above reported that while the prevalence of symptomatic depression is around 4%, the prevalence of reported depressive symptoms is as high as 27% 4 . LLD is characterized by an atypical cluster of depressive symptoms that often fail to meet the DSM-5 criteria for diagnosing depressive disorders 2 . Nevertheless, these symptoms have significant clinical importance as they are closely associated with poor health outcomes and a lower quality of life. Since LLD is frequently overlooked, many patients miss the opportunity for appropriate treatment. LLD is not a self-limited condition that resolves spontaneously without medical or counseling interventions 5 . Based on the age at which the first episode of depression occurs, LLD is divided into two categories: Early-onset depression (EOD) and late-onset depression (LOD). EOD refers to depression diagnosed during young or middle age, which persists or recurs in later life 6 . In contrast, LOD refers to depression that first appears in elderly individuals experiencing no prior episode of significant depressive disorders 1 , 7 – 9 . Although there is no consensus on the specific age that distinguishes between EOD and LOD, ages 60 or 55 are generally used as the cutoff for classification 7 . EOD and LOD each account for approximately half of LLD cases 10 . When LLD patients receive appropriate pharmacological and/or psychological treatment, 65–75% respond effectively to the interventions 11 . Some psychological improvements may be observed about two weeks after the initiation of therapy, and typically their full effects may be realized after several months 12 . Active intervention against depression can significantly enhance the quality of life, functional capacity, medical health status, and lifespan. The earlier the intervention begins, the better the outcomes for LLD patients. Practically, ongoing depression is easily recognized by patients and their families, so becomes overt subject for clinical treatment. However, in case of LOD that is not currently manifested, the disease tends to be overlooked because its progression is too slow to be recognized by patients and families. Moreover, it is not easy to identify LOD due to its atypical depressive symptoms 9 , 13 . Hence, predictive programs are required for the early diagnosis and intervention of LOD. In this study, we explored health and social risk factors for LOD using data from the nationwide panel survey ‘Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging’ 14 . Additionally, we developed new machine learning algorithms to predict LOD based on the identified risk factors. Methods Study population The Korean longitudinal study of aging (KLoSA) is a comprehensive nationwide panel survey targeting the Korean population aged 45 years and older. This survey was initiated by the Korea Labor Institute in 2006, and has been conducted biennially since its inception 14 . The KLoSA survey adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Data collection methodologies followed all relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The KLoSA database is anonymized, and strict confidentiality guidelines are applied to protect participant information. The initial wave in 2006 included data on demographics, family composition, health information, medical history, and socio-economic status, collected from 10,254 individuals. These data have been continuously updated through subsequent waves. To offset any attrition due to participant death or non-response, new members have been enrolled to maintain the panel’s size. In the present study, we utilized data from seven waves of KLoSA (2006 to 2018). Given that the COVID-19 pandemic and stringent quarantine regulations are well known to have significantly impacted mental health 15 , 16 , data from the eighth and ninth waves were excluded to avoid any confounding effects on depressive mood. Out of the original 10,254 participants, 6,136 individuals were successfully tracked across the 12-year period, while the remaining participants either passed away or were lost to follow-up. To minimize potential confounding effects of psychiatric medication on depressive mood, we excluded 818 individuals with a documented history of psychiatric drug use. Consequently, our analysis included data from 5,318 participants (Fig. 1 ). Of the participants, 165 subjects in the class 1 were found to be already depressed at the first season of survey. Since the data from the subjects undergoing depression might confound those from LOD subjects, they were excluded in this study. Finally, the data from 5153 subjects were analyzed in the following step. The ages of male and female participants are 76.9 ± 8.81 (the mean ± S.D., N = 2221) and 77.0 ± 9.46 ( N = 2932). Assessment of depressive mood Depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item version of the Center for epidemiologic studies depression scale (CES-D10) 17 . This inventory consists of the following questions: “I was bothered by things that usually don’t bother me,” “I had trouble keeping my mind on what I was doing,” “I felt depressed,” “I felt like everything I did was an effort,” “I did relatively well” (reverse scoring), “I felt fearful,” “My sleep was restless,” “I lived without much complaint” (reverse scoring), “I felt lonely,” and “I could not get going.” Each of the 10 items is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, yielding a score range from 10 to 40. The total CES-D10 score reflects the severity of depression. The scale demonstrated a strong internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.867 in the first wave. Population classification according to mood changes In the initial step, we conducted latent growth modeling (LGM) using the KLoSA dataset to delineate individuals’ trajectories of depressive symptoms. Three LGMs were tested - the ‘No Growth Model,’ the ‘Linear Growth Model,’ and the ‘Quadratic Growth Model.’ Ultimately, the ‘Quadratic Growth Model’ was selected for further analysis. Next, we implemented growth mixture modeling (GMM) to explore the heterogeneity in depressive symptom trajectories 18 . The categorized trajectory groups were utilized as the outcome variable in the subsequent phase of the analysis. Model evaluation was performed by considering metrics such as akaike information criteria (AIC), bayesian information criteria (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC), Entropy, bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and lo-mendell-rubin LRT (LMR LRT). A decrease in AIC, BIC, and SABIC values signifies an improvement in model fit, while an increase in entropy indicates enhanced trajectory classification. LMR LRT and BLRT were employed to compare the results of the k-1 class model with the k class model. A significant p-value (&lt; 0.05) for each parameter indicates that the k class model is superior to the k-1 class model 19 , 20 . Upon considering all fit statistics, we determined that there were four latent classes depicting individual changes in depressive symptoms. Descriptive statistics were analyzed using IBM SPSS 27.0, while LGM and GMM analyses were conducted using the Mplus program 21 . Screening of LOD-related variables From the 1st to the 7th waves of the KLoSA data collection, a broad range of variables was gathered to provide a comprehensive profile of the participants. These variables included nominal and numerical variables. Nominal variables were the participants’ age, sex, marital status, final level of education, and household income quartile. Additionally, information was collected on residence type, presence of housemates, type of religion, drinking and smoking habits, use of dentures, and experience of pain. Data on chronic diseases, visual or auditory discomforts, and participation in various social meetings were also recorded. Numerical variables were also collected, including body weight and height. The number of housemates, siblings, and children for each participant was documented. Participants provided self-rated health assessments and detailed their economic status, including household income, assets, and debts, as well as any allowances received. Furthermore, physical activities, such as weekly exercise time and grip strength, were measured. Information on social activities, such as meeting with friends, engaging in outdoor activities, sightseeing, pursuing hobbies, self-development, or volunteering, was also gathered. This extensive and detailed collection of variables enables a comprehensive examination of the multiple factors that may influence the participants’ depressive mood and overall health. To incorporate all datasets from the seven waves, the mode values for nominal variables and the mean values for numerical variables were calculated, and subjected to binary logistic regression analyses to identify risk factors for LOD. predictable variables. The variable data obtained from only the first wave, however, were used for ML-based prediction of LOD. Machine learning algorithms The study conducted twelve machine learning (ML) algorithms, which are briefly summarized in (Supplementary Table 1 ). We performed the classification modeling to predict the binary class of LOD using features returned by feature selection methods. Machine learning pipelines were constructed utilizing Python (version 3.10.12) and PyCaret (version 3.3.2). Categorical features were transformed using one-hot encoding, converting them into a binary matrix suitable for input into machine learning models. The dataset in the first wave was divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets, with stratified sampling employed to ensure proportional representation of each class. To mitigate class imbalance in the training dataset, the dataset was preprocessed using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), and then subjected to stratified ten-fold cross-validation. A simple imputation method was adopted for addressing the missing data. The models were tuned for the same number of iterations, with hyperparameters optimized to maximize the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) score. Model performance was assessed by examining the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score were also evaluated using a confusion matrix. For model interpretability, feature importance was calculated for tree-based models, while feature coefficients were determined for linear models. To further explain the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions, we used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values 22 . The machine learning pipeline was implemented in Google Colab and the code is available at “ https://github.com/snuhl-crain/ML_for_LOD ”. Results System framework Depressive symptoms were evaluated using CES-D10 scores. Four latent classes of depression trajectories were identified through latent growth modeling (LGM) and growth mixture modeling (GMM) analyses. The class representing preexisting depression (Class 3) was excluded from this study to focus on predicting new-onset depression. A set of features was directly extracted from the remaining three classes, which were used to train and test twelve machine learning (ML) models. Each ML model was trained with data from 3607 individuals, and its performance in predicting LOD was tested using new data from 1,546 individuals. The framework of the present study is illustrated in (Fig. 1 ). Classification of population The study investigated the trajectories of changes in depression mood over a 12-year period among 5318 individuals based on their CES-D10 scores. The analysis compared the fit of three trajectory models: No Growth Model, Linear Growth Model, and Quadratic Growth Model (Table 1 ). Results indicated that the No Growth Model was deemed inadequate due to suboptimal fit indices, with Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and tucker-lewis index (TLI) values below 0.9 and a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) above 0.1 23 . In contrast, the Linear Growth Model demonstrated satisfactory fit indices, suggesting a good model fit. However, the Quadratic Growth Model was found to outperform the other models in terms of fit analyses, exhibiting the most favorable performance based on the evaluation metrics utilized. Therefore, the Quadratic Growth Model was selected for further classification of depression trajectories. To optimize the number of latent classes, the analysis considered various criteria such as AIC, BIC, SABIC, Entropy, LMR LRT, and BLRT for models ranging from 1 to 7 classes (Table 2 ) 24 . The reduction in AIC, BIC, SABIC, and BLRT values with an increasing number of classes, alongside satisfactory Entropy values (&gt; 0.7), suggested that models with more classes were more favorable. However, the substantial decrease in LMR LRT between the 4-class and 5-class models indicated that a 4-class classification was most appropriate for analyzing depression trajectories. Subsequently, the study grouped the 5,318 individuals in the KLoSA dataset into 4 latent classes based on their depression trajectories. By statistically analyzing the growth factors (b 00 , b 10 and b 20 ) in the Quadratic Growth Model (Supplementary Table 2), we plotted prediction curves of depression trajectories for the 4 identified classes (Fig. 2 ). The findings revealed four distinct latent classes reflecting the chronological patterns of depression development in the elderly Korean population. Class 1 comprised 953 (17.9%) individuals exhibiting ‘new-onset increasing depressive mood,’ potentially indicative of Late-Onset Depression (LOD). Class 2 included 2,677 (50.3%) individuals with ‘no depressive mood,’ while Class 3 consisted of 165 (3.1%) individuals displaying ‘early high depressive mood’ persisting over the 12-year period. Lastly, Class 4 encompassed 1,523 (28.6%) individuals demonstrating a ‘low decreasing depressive mood’ pattern. Binary logistic regression analysis of predictor variables As aforementioned, this study focused on utilizing machine learning-based programs to predict LOD represented by class 1, but excluded pre-existing depression represented by class 3 from the investigation. A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to compare class 1 with the non-depressive reference group comprising classes 2 and 4. The regression was fitted between the status of class 1 and 57 independent variables. Based on the regression coefficients, 12 independent variables were selected to serve as input data for the machine learning programs to predict LOD. These input data encompassed 7 dummy variables and 5 numeric variables. The findings from the regression analyses, including the regression coefficients and variable selection, are outlined and summarized in (Supplementary Table 3 ). The descriptive statistics of the final variables for late-onset depressive and non-depressive participants over a 12-year period are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. Statistical analyses were conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables and unpaired two-tailed t-tests for continuous variables to compare the two groups. Notably, the proportion of females and the mean age were significantly higher in the late-onset depressive group compared to the non-depressive group. As anticipated, all examined variables demonstrated substantial differences between the two groups. These findings suggest that the 12 variables may serve as effective predictors for distinguishing between elderly individuals with late-onset depression and non-depressed ones. ML analyses for predicting LOD The study conducted twelve machine learning (ML) algorithms to predict the inclusion of target subjects in class 1. All models tested here showed acceptable predictive capabilities. Notably, RF and GBC models demonstrated excellent prediction performances with high levels of accuracy, precision, F1 score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Area Under the Curve (AUC). Despite the notable performances of the top two models, with minimal differences in predictive ability among them, no single model stood out as clearly superior. Evaluation of ROC curves (Fig. 3 ) revealed that all ML models achieved AUC scores exceeding 0.68, indicating their potential as effective tools for depression prediction. RF and GBC models exhibited superior performances based on their AUC scores (&gt; 0.73). The confusion matrices of the top models are presented in (Fig. 4 ). Based on the matrices, the performance indices are calculated, as shown in (Table 3 ). These models achieved impressive metrics, such as accuracy &gt; 0.79, precision &gt; 0.44, recall &gt; 0.46, F1 &gt; 0.45, and MCC &gt; 0.32. Feature importance analysis identified key predictors that were distinctly influential across each model, as shown in (Table 4 ). In both RF and GBC, important predictors included pain, low income, friend meetings, social meetings, and gender. Notably, the patterns of feature importance in most ML models, except for LIGHTGBM and ADA, were somewhat similar to those observed in RF and GBC. In contrast, LIGHTGBM exhibited significant differences in feature importance compared to RF and GBC. It highlighted age, the number of living siblings, auditory discomfort, friend meetings, and residence in a big city as critical features. ADA also displayed a unique pattern of feature importance, emphasizing the significance of friend meetings, social meetings, residence in a big city, and gender. Overall, factors such as living alone, denture usage, age, and the number of living siblings were relatively less important across most machine learning models analyzed here. These findings offer insights into the key predictors driving LOD predictions and highlight the variability in variable importance across different ML algorithms. Discussion The risk factors associated with diagnosed depression in middle-aged and elderly individuals have been extensively explored in previous studies, which highlight family history, sleep disorders, chronic diseases, and stress as main contributors to major depression. In clinical practice, the focus is typically on diagnosing and treating depression rather than solely on identifying individual risk factors. Treatment strategy for diagnosed depression is usually the most compelling issue regardless of specific risk factors present in a given case. However, the challenge lies in identifying and intervening LOD before symptoms manifest. The ability to predict and potentially prevent depression is a valuable approach for early intervention of LOD. With this objective in mind, the study aimed to develop machine learning (ML) models capable of predicting depression before symptoms manifest. Our predictive models have the potential to forecast the development of LOD, offering a proactive approach to mental health management and prevention strategies. This study identified distinct patterns in the progression of depressive symptoms, as assessed by CES-D10, among elderly individuals in Korea. Through the application of LGM and GMM, we clustered depression trajectories into four distinct groups. Notably, the proportion of individuals in class 1 (designated as LOD) was 17.9%, while class 3 (representing preexisting depression) accounted for 3.1%, with classes 2 and 4 combined to form the reference group at 78.9%. Class 3 was excluded from further analyses since preexisting depression was not the primary focus of the study. Subsequent investigations using logistic regression analyses identified class 1-associated risk factors, which include 12 variables such as physical pain, residency in a non-major city, living alone, low income, use of dentures, lack of social and friend meetings, absence of siblings, visual or auditory discomfort, female, and increasing age. Furthermore, the study conducted ML models to predict LOD using these variables. While most models exhibited acceptable discriminative capabilities, RF and GBC stood out with exceptional performances across various parameters compared to other models. The classification of depressive symptom trajectories in this study aligns with previous longitudinal research findings, as supported by Musliner et al. 21 , endorsing the optimal nature of the 4-class classification. Costello et al. 25 analyzed the data collected from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health ( N = 11,559), and explored trajectories of self-reported depressed mood from age 12 to 25. Their study showed a lower proportion (2.4%) of late escalating depressed mood, which was interpreted as indicative of future depression, compared to the higher proportion (17.9%) of future depression observed in the current study. This notable contrast in the prevalence of future depression suggests that elderly adults exhibit a heightened vulnerability to new-onset depression compared to younger individuals. Likewise, Lin et al. 26 examined the data from the China health and retirement longitudinal study (CHARLS), focusing on 2,650 Chinese elderly individuals aged over 60. Their analyses using LGM and GMM also supported the 4-class grouping as the most suitable approach for delineating depression patterns in the older population. The four classes identified in their study closely match the classes observed in this research: new-onset increasing symptoms (corresponding to class 1), no symptoms (class 2), persistently high symptoms (class 3), and slowly decreasing symptoms (class 4). Furthermore, a notable similarity was found in the proportions of the new-onset increasing symptoms population (16.8%) in Lin et al.‘s study and the class 1 population (17.9%) in the current study. The comparisons between these two studies from different countries suggest that the distribution of depression populations may not vary significantly across different regions, highlighting a consistent pattern in depressive trajectories among elderly populations. The application of ML algorithms in predicting depression onset was recently explored across different populations. In a study by Su et al. 27 , demographics, health-related factors, and chronic diseases were analyzed as potential risk factors using a longitudinal dataset from 1,538 elderly adults in the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Study. The researchers tested six ML algorithms to forecast depression onset within the following two years. They found that ‘LR with Lasso Regularization (LRLR)’ and ‘Deep Neural Network (DNN)’ exhibited superior prediction performances compared to other models. The reported AUC and accuracy values were 0.629 and 0.659 for LRLR; 0.644 and 0.571 for DNN. In this study, we achieved AUC and accuracy values of 0.74 and 0.78 for the Random Forest (RF) model, and 0.73 and 0.79 for the Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC). It is important to note that these performance metrics cannot be directly compared across studies due to differences in datasets and methodologies. Nonetheless, a preliminary evaluation suggests that the performance of our models appears to be slightly superior. Su et al. 27 identified 15 significant predictors of depression onset, including age, gender, residence, marital status, scores on Activities of Daily Living and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living, self-rated health scores, and various chronic diseases. Among these, only three variables - age, gender, and residence - overlap with the predictors in our ML models, while the remaining variables differ substantially. In addition, Lin et al. 26 also investigated the application of ML algorithms to predict new onset depression in home-based older adults. Their study analyzed CES-D10 scores along with 23 predictor variables, encompassing demographics, health-related factors, and cognitive abilities, utilizing data from 2650 individuals aged over 60. The researchers implemented three ML models: Random forest (RF), Gradient boosting Decision Trees (GBDT), and Support Vector Machines (SVM), to forecast the emergence of depressive symptoms. All models demonstrated strong predictive performances, with no significant differences among them. The AUC and accuracy values were 0.763 and 0.774 for RF, 0.770 and 0.806 for GBDT, and 0.741 and 0.809 for SVM, which are comparable to our findings in terms of AUC and accuracy. Lin et al. 26 employed 25 variables for their ML-based prediction of new onset depression, five of which - age, gender, urban/rural residence, household income level, and social activities - are also included in our analysis. Notably, these variables were more predictive of persistent high depression, exhibiting higher AUC and accuracy values, than they were for new onset depression. Although their ML models effectively predict late-life depression, they may not be equally adept at specifically predicting late-onset depression. In terms of predictors, our results indicate that several factors, including low income, pain, friend meeting, and social meeting consistently emerged as significant predictors across most models. Low income has been well-documented as a persistent predictor of depression. Notably, a study conducted by Arvind et al. 28 surveyed 9,666 households across 12 states in India to explore the prevalence of depressive disorders and associated socioeconomic risk factors. The odds ratio for depression among individuals in the first income quintile, compared to those in the fifth quintile, was found to be 1.93 (95% CI 1.54–2.46; P &lt; .001). In the current study, the odds ratio for late-onset geriatric depression in the first income quintile was markedly higher at 2.47 (95% CI 2.12–2.87). This discrepancy suggests that low income has a more profound impact on depression in older adults compared to the general adult population, as indicated by the findings from the Indian survey. In addition, pain is a well-known predictor for geriatric depression. For instance, Denkinger et al. 29 conducted a longitudinal cohort study involving 1130 older adults, revealing that the number of painful body areas, as well as pain frequency and severity, were significantly correlated with late-life depression. In our analysis, we identified both ‘friend meeting and social meeting’ as important predictors for depression. These factors can be classified under the broader category of social interaction (or network). A substantial body of evidence indicates that lower levels of social interaction are significantly associated with an increased risk of developing depression among older adults 30 – 32 . Interestingly, ‘living in a big city’ was found to be inversely related with late-onset depression. “Larger cities provide a buffer life against depression”. This was a shocking statement from a PNAS article by Stier et al. 33 . They developed a statistical model that co-analyzes socioeconomic network structure and individual risk of depression, and found that this model predicted lower depression rates in larger cities. Recently, Chen et al. 34 also addressed a growing debate about whether urban living has a beneficial effect against depression, and concluded that those who lived in urban areas were inversely related with late-onset depression, as shown in Supplementary Table 3. Yet, why urban residency lowers the chance of depression has not been uncovered so far. Because there are numerous differences in terms of natural, social, economic, educational, and other situations between urban and rural regions, it is not easy to clearly mention about some factors to affect depression. This issue remains to be investigated in the future. In this study, we defined depressive symptoms based solely on self-reported diagnoses using the CES-D-10 scale, which represents a limitation of our research. Self-reported diagnoses may not be as reliable as diagnoses based on DSM-5 criteria performed by trained psychologists or psychiatrists 35 . However, the CES-D-10 has been widely adopted in epidemiological studies due to its outcomes consistently aligning with clinical diagnoses. It is important to note that the CES-D-10 alone does not adequately capture the full spectrum of depression severity. Therefore, we utilized CES-D-10 scores to represent the degrees of depressive symptoms rather than a clinical diagnosis of depression. Additionally, another limitation of our study is the lack of an online self-assessment system for depression. Although the depression-predicting machine learning models were thoroughly validated using the KLoSA datasets, their practical application for elderly individuals is hindered without an accessible depression assessment system. Establishing such a system will be a crucial next step in advancing our research. To have a better performance in predicting geriatric depression, the use of deep learning models might be a compelling option. Deep learning models allow for the construction of multi-layered and complex models tailored to specific problems. They are advantageous for learning high-dimensional, large-scale, and non-linear data due to their flexibility and diverse architectures. However, they pose challenges in interpretability and demand more computational resources and training time than machine learning models. In the present study, we could not establish deep learning models because of limited facilities and techniques. Future research will explore complex AI models, including deep learning and ensemble learning. The focus of our study lies in predicting future depression rather than evaluating current depression. The prediction of future depression is necessary to identify and intervene with prospective patients who do not exhibit overt symptoms at present. We successfully classified individuals into a future depression group and a reference group that remained depression-free for 12 years. Furthermore, our study involved the development of depression-predictive ML models that exhibited optimal performances in forecasting depression onset. The potential application of these ML algorithms as self-checking programs accessible through computers or smartphones holds promise for enabling early detection of geriatric depression. Such an innovative invention could help prevent the progression of depression to a more severe level by facilitating timely intervention and support for individuals at risk of developing the condition. Conclusion We here propose the ML algorithms with 12 demographic and sociomedical variables to predict LOD in elderly individuals. This study has the potential to offer new insights into effectively distinguishing individuals who may experience depressive symptoms in the future from those likely to maintain stable moods at a community level. The implementation of ML models as a decision support system holds promise for enhancing primary medical care and health screening services by enabling the early identification of individuals at risk of developing depression. While the predictive abilities of the ML algorithms are not yet considered highly satisfactory for deployment without concerns of misdiagnosis, there is considerable potential for further enhancements of the predictive models. Future efforts should focus on refining the existing predictive models and identifying additional variables that can enhance the predictive capabilities of the ML algorithms. By continuing to refine and expand upon the current study, advancements can be made towards developing more accurate and reliable predictive models that can aid in the early detection and prevention of LOD in elderly population. From a clinical point of view, the self-checking system with these algorithms will help older adults and their family members assess the likelihood of future depression, thereby managing it proactively. Furthermore, they may assist mental health professionals, including clinical social workers and clinical psychologists, in counseling elderly clients who are concerned about the onset of depression.
