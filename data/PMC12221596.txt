Dysregulation of gene expression can lead to abnormal brain function, with alternative splicing playing a crucial role in proper brain development. Emerging evidence suggests that dysregulated RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) contribute to aberrant splicing patterns, disrupting neuronal processes and increasing susceptibility to neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Understanding how misregulated RBPs alter splicing mechanisms is crucial for elucidating their role in ASD pathogenesis. Additionally, this knowledge is essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at correcting splicing-related abnormalities. This review highlights recent advancements in our understanding of the interplay between RBPs and alternative splicing in ASD and explores promising RNA-targeting therapeutic approaches. Genetic investigations have identified a broad array of risk genes, many of which converge on key biological processes such as synaptic function, chromatin remodeling, and early neural development ( Satterstrom et al., 2020 ). While ASD is widely recognized as a complex interplay between genetic predispositions and environmental influences, the precise pathogenic mechanisms underlying the disorder have yet to be elucidated. RNA splicing is a fundamental regulatory step in eukaryotic gene expression, with alternative splicing playing a pivotal role in generating diverse mature mRNAs from a single pre-mRNA. This process enables the production of different protein isoforms with distinct functions, which is essential for gene regulation during development and tissue morphogenesis. The regulation of splicing is a highly intricate process, governed by cis-acting sequences within pre-mRNAs and trans-acting splicing factors, including RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). RBPs play a crucial role in ensuring the precise execution of splicing events, particularly in neuronal development. Recent studies have underscored the critical role of alternative splicing and RBPs in neurodevelopment, particularly their involvement in ASD. Dysregulated RBPs have been increasingly linked to aberrant splicing patterns associated with ASD, contributing to altered neuronal function and neurodevelopmental abnormalities. In this review, we explore the roles of RBPs in the regulation of alternative splicing, emphasizing how their dysregulation leads to splicing defects implicated in ASD. Additionally, we briefly introduce emerging research on RNA-targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at correcting these splicing abnormalities. Regulators and Mechanisms of Pre-mRNA Splicing RNA splicing is an important step in eukaryotic gene expression, where exons are joined and introns are removed to generate mature mRNA ( Black, 2003 ). Key elements involved in splicing include the 5’ splice site, 3’ splice site, branch point, and polypyrimidine tract within introns ( Fig. 1A ). The splice sites define the boundaries between exons and introns, while the branch point plays a crucial role in forming the lariat structure during intron removal by the spliceosome ( Konarska et al., 1985 ). The catalytic activity of spliceosome is driven by uridine-rich small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (U snRNPs—U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6), which consist of small nuclear RNAs and associated proteins ( Matera and Wang, 2014 , Will and Luhrmann, 2011 ). These small nuclear RNAs are critical for recognizing and binding to specific RNA sequences involved in splicing. In addition to the spliceosome, RBPs serve as key splicing regulators ( Fig. 1A ) ( Tao et al., 2024 ). Most human genes contain multiple exons regulated by alternative splicing, allowing a limited number of genes to generate extensive transcriptomic diversity. Alternative splicing produces various splicing patterns, categorized as exon skipping (ES), intron retention (IR), alternative 5’ or 3’ splice site (A5SS or A3SS), and mutually exclusive exon ( Fig. 1B ) ( Weyn-Vanhentenryck et al., 2018 , Xu et al., 2002 ). These alternative splicing events can lead to frameshifts, leading to transcripts with premature termination codons (PTCs). These PTC-containing transcripts are typically recognized and degraded by the nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) pathway, thereby reducing overall protein expression levels. RBPs facilitate spliceosome assembly and guide it to interact with specific RNA elements within pre-mRNAs, ensuring accurate recognition of splice sites and proper exon-intron processing ( Kumari et al., 2022 ). Among RBPs, serine-/arginine-rich (SR) proteins are well-characterized regulators that bind to exonic splicing enhancers and intronic splicing enhancers to promote exon inclusion ( Jeong, 2017 ) ( Fig. 2A ). In contrast, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) bind to exonic splicing silencers and intronic splicing silencers, generally inhibiting exon inclusion ( Wang and Burge, 2008 , Zhu et al., 2001 ). However, recent studies suggest that the roles of SR proteins and hnRNPs may not be as rigidly defined, with growing evidence pointing to more intricate and context-dependent regulatory mechanisms in splicing ( Howard and Sanford, 2015 , Liu et al., 2020 ). RBP Network in the Regulation of Alternative Splicing The regulation of alternative splicing is governed by a complex network of RBP-RBP interactions, wherein diverse RBPs bind to specific elements within pre-mRNA and engage in intricate interactions. In some instances, distinct RBPs cooperate to promote alternative splicing, whereas in other cases, they may compete for binding to identical RNA sequences, thereby repressing splicing. The context-dependent activities of these RBPs determine whether an exon is included or skipped, and their combinatorial actions fine-tune transcript diversity and stability ( Fig. 2B ). High-throughput studies have identified numerous RBP-binding sites and their associated functions, with many datasets accessible through user-friendly web browsers ( Cook et al., 2011 , Paz et al., 2022 ). Additionally, advances in deep learning have further refined the prediction of RNA-binding specificities ( Niu et al., 2021 , Pan et al., 2020 , Yan et al., 2024 ), thereby enhancing our understanding of RBP-RNA interactions. Collectively, these studies substantially contributed to a comprehensive understanding of post-transcriptional gene expression regulation ( Street et al., 2024 , Van Nostrand et al., 2021 ). Furthermore, ongoing large-scale analyses of RBP-RNA interactomes contribute to mapping the intricate landscape of RNA processing networks ( Fradera-Sola et al., 2023 , Shchepachev et al., 2019 ). Beyond RBP-RNA interaction networks, RBP expression networks are also established through post-transcriptional auto- and cross-regulation mechanisms ( Fig. 2C ). Certain RBPs bind to their own pre-mRNAs to modulate protein levels, forming autoregulatory feedback loops. Additionally, RBPs can bind to the pre-mRNAs or mRNAs of other RBPs to regulate their expression, creating dynamic regulatory clusters that vary based on cellular context ( Muller-McNicoll et al., 2019 ). These clusters can exhibit local cooperation or competition, indicating that RBPs may act synergistically or antagonistically to control shared targets ( Dassi, 2017 ). Furthermore, hierarchical RBP chains connect these clusters, potentially coordinating gene expression across distinct functional modules within the post-transcriptional network ( Quattrone and Dassi, 2019 ). Moreover, some RBPs form ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes through interactions with RNA and recruit other RBPs to RNP ( Fig. 2D ). These RBP-RBP-RNA interactions are commonly involved in regulating various aspects of RNA metabolism, including alternative splicing. RBPs and Alternative Splicing in Neurodevelopment Neuronal development is a highly regulated process characterized by precise temporal and spatial control, leading to significant changes in both neuron morphology and function ( Weyn-Vanhentenryck et al., 2018 ). RBPs regulate protein expression in developmentally specific manners, ensuring the proper transition from neuronal progenitors to mature neurons. The abundance and activity of RBPs are highly dynamic, undergoing substantial modulation to regulate alternative splicing patterns for proper developmental processes. Many RBPs play key roles in splicing regulation, particularly during complex processes such as neuronal development, as illustrated in Figure 3 ( Fisher and Feng, 2022 , Tao et al., 2024 ). Changes in RBP abundance, along with alterations in chromatin state and transcriptional regulation, collectively shape the precise splicing events required for proper cellular function and neurodevelopment ( Porter et al., 2018 ). Several neuronally expressed RBPs, including PTBP, RBFOX, and fragile X mental retardation protein (FMRP), are central to neurogenesis, axon guidance, synapse formation, and synaptic plasticity as they are essential for cytoskeletal remodeling, axon guidance, and synaptic assembly during neurodevelopment ( Fig. 3 ). Alternative splicing plays a critical role during cell differentiation, organ development, and tissue homeostasis ( Baralle and Giudice, 2017 , Zheng and Black, 2013 ). Notably, alternative splicing is especially prevalent in the human brain compared with other tissues, with its influence becoming particularly significant during neuronal development ( Kjer-Hansen and Weatheritt, 2023 , Pan et al., 2008 ). In this context, alternative splicing impacts key processes, including neuronal differentiation, synaptic formation, and cortical maturation. Events such as cassette exon inclusion and IR are common in the cerebral cortex throughout life, underscoring the importance of alternative splicing in generating the diverse protein isoforms essential for neuronal differentiation and maturation ( Mazin et al., 2013 , Satterstrom et al., 2020 , Zhang et al., 2014 ). A well-known example of alternative splicing in neuronal development is DLG4 gene (PSD-95), a scaffold protein essential for synapse maturation during late neuronal differentiation ( Samojedny et al., 2022 ). The generation of PSD-95α or PSD-95β is regulated by the splicing of exon 2 in combination with either the α exon or the 3 β exons. Inclusion of the β exons results in the incorporation of an L27 domain and lacks palmitoylation sites, which reduces membrane localization but enhances glutamate receptor-mediated excitatory synaptic transmission ( Han and Kim, 2008 ). Another alternative spliced isoform of DLG4 involves skipping of exon 18 in the embryonic brain, leading to the production of an unstable transcript containing a PTC ( Zheng et al., 2012 ). Another example is SHTN1 (Shootin 1), which generates 2 isoforms through the inclusion or skipping of exons 15 and 16. These isoforms regulate axonal growth and specification during early axonogenesis: The long isoform (with exon 15/16 inclusion) promotes axonal growth, while the short isoform (with exon 15/16 skipping) facilitates axon specification ( Zhang et al., 2019 ). Beyond these examples, many presynaptic and postsynaptic proteins are regulated by alternative splicing, altering their functional and structural properties essential for neurodevelopmental processes ( Li et al., 2007 , Norris and Calarco, 2012 , Su et al., 2018 ). Indeed, alternative splicing directly influences synaptic function and stability, shaping neuronal differentiation, synaptic plasticity, and the overall development of the nervous system ( Bulovaite et al., 2022 , Furlanis and Scheiffele, 2018 , Zheng, 2020 ). Thus, disruptions in splicing regulation can have profound consequences on neurodevelopment and synaptic formation. Aberrant splicing has been implicated in various neurodevelopmental disorders, often involving neural-specific microexons and RBPs regulating brain-specific splicing programs ( Engal et al., 2024 , Liang et al., 2021 , Nikom and Zheng, 2023 ). Splicing Dysregulation in ASD ASD is a heritable neurodevelopmental disorder influenced by genetic variations, including copy number variations and single-nucleotide variants ( Choi and An, 2021 , Jiang et al., 2022 , Rein and Yan, 2020 , Vicari et al., 2019 , Zhang et al., 2024 ). These genetic variants contribute to ASD pathogenesis by disrupting synaptic balance, particularly by increasing the number of excitatory synapses. Despite extensive studies on copy number variations and other genetic variants, the genetic diagnostic yield for ASD remains lower than expected. This discrepancy can be attributed to limited coverage of genetic tests, challenges in interpreting noncoding variations, and complex inheritance patterns involving multiple genes ( An et al., 2018 , Engal et al., 2024 , Kim et al., 2024a , Kim et al., 2024b , Kim et al., 2024c , Kim et al., 2024d , Satterstrom et al., 2020 ). In recent years, the role of alternative splicing in neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD, has gained increasing recognition ( Engal et al., 2024 ). Studying alternative splicing isoforms across human postmortem brain tissues from fetal to adult stages is essential for understanding the pathogenesis of neurodevelopmental disorders. Many ASD-associated genes undergo significant splicing transitions during brain development, particularly during mid-fetal neurogenesis, when critical regulatory networks for neuronal specification and connectivity are established ( Kim et al., 2024a , Kim et al., 2024b , Kim et al., 2024c , Kim et al., 2024d ). Disruptions in this finely regulated splicing landscape could contribute to ASD by altering the expression of key neurodevelopmental isoforms, as observed in transcription factor genes in ASD ( Fazel Darbandi et al., 2024 ). These findings highlight the need to investigate both stage-specific and brain-region-specific splicing isoforms, as their dysregulation may hold key insights for therapeutic intervention. This section focuses on how altered splicing of genes critical for fetal brain development, neuron formation, and synaptic plasticity may serve as a molecular mechanism driving ASD pathogenesis ( Quesnel-Vallieres et al., 2019 ). Several genes implicated in ASD have been shown to exhibit abnormal splicing patterns, including NLGN3 , NLGN4X , NRXN1 , SHANK3 , and CADPS2 —all of which play critical roles in synaptic function. Region-specific splicing of the NRXN1 gene has been observed in the prefrontal cortex, potentially influencing synapse specification in higher-order cognitive circuits ( Fuccillo et al., 2015 ). These mutations in the splice sites of these genes can result in frameshifts, leading to the activation of the NMD pathway or the production of dysfunctional proteins that disrupt synaptic signaling. Another class of splicing events that contribute to ASD involves the regulation of highly conserved neural microexons ( Ustianenko et al., 2017 ). These microexons, typically ranging from 3 to 30 nucleotides in length, are essential for generating protein isoform diversity and mediating protein-protein interactions during neurogenesis before birth ( Irimia et al., 2014 , Mazin et al., 2021 , Ustianenko et al., 2017 ). Microexons are often identified using targeted PCR followed by sequencing or bioinformatics tools, such as ATMap and OLego, after performing high-resolution RNA sequencing ( Parada et al., 2021 , Wu et al., 2013 ). Efforts to analyze global transcriptome changes in ASD have been ongoing, with a particular focus on identifying familial risk factors associated with the disorder ( Chau et al., 2021 , Kim et al., 2024a , Kim et al., 2024b , Kim et al., 2024c , Kim et al., 2024d ). One such initiative is the Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI), which aims to advance understanding of the genetic and biological mechanisms underlying ASD ( Abrahams et al., 2013 ). SFARI has been instrumental in global ASD research, providing comprehensive resources such as data on de novo genetic mutations and associated phenotypic information. These resources have significantly contributed to progress in ASD genetics and splicing regulation. Dysregulated RBPs in ASD As reviewed above, the coordinated action of RBPs ensures the regulated splicing, which is essential for maintaining normal function and development ( Fisher and Feng, 2022 , Parra and Johnston, 2022 ). Imbalances in RBP expression can significantly disrupt neuronal development, leading to neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD, as well as neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's ( Parra and Johnston, 2022 , Schieweck et al., 2021 ). Furthermore, these RBPs not only regulate each other’s expression and splicing targets but also participate in broader regulatory networks that fine-tune mRNA processing, stability, and localization ( Fig. 2 , Fig. 3 ). Disruption of this interplay may lead to aberrant neuronal circuit formation and functional deficits characteristic of ASD. Recent studies have identified protein-truncating variants in genes involved in splicing regulation, which are associated with developmental delays and cognitive impairments. For instance, protein-truncating variants in ARHGEF9 and IQSEC2 have been linked to intellectual disability (ID), with IQSEC2 specifically associated with cognitive deficits ( Satterstrom et al., 2020 ). These findings underscore the critical role of RBPs in maintaining proper neuronal function and highlight their involvement in ASD and other neurodevelopmental disorders. In this section, we summarize key RBPs essential for neurodevelopment and explore their roles in ASD pathogenesis in Table 1 . SR Proteins and SR-Like Proteins SR proteins and SR protein-like factors play a crucial role in neuronal development, and disruptions in these proteins are increasingly linked to ASD. For example, the loss of B52, the Drosophila ortholog of human SRSF6, results in increased axon branching by reducing the splicing of choline acetyltransferase ( Liu and Bossing, 2016 ). This regulatory role appears to be conserved across species, as changes in SRSF5 expression and elevated acetylcholine levels have been observed in individuals with bipolar disorder, further suggesting a connection between splicing regulation and neurodevelopmental disorders. In the context of ASD, splicing factors like SRRM4, an SR-related protein, regulate the inclusion of neural-specific microexons (∼50 nucleotides) in genes critical for neuronal growth and synaptic function. SRRM4 binds to the UGC motif between the polypyrimidine tract and 3’ splice sites, ensuring proper splicing of neural microexons. In ASD, reduced SRRM4 levels are associated with abnormal microexon inclusion, leading to neuronal dysfunction. For instance, in SRRM4 -depleted Neuro-2a cells (a mouse neuroblast cell line), the inclusion of exon L in the protrudin gene is reduced, impairing neurite growth during neurogenesis ( Ohnishi et al. 2017 ). Additionally, SRRM4-mediated inclusion of microexon 4 in the neural-specific CPEB4 gene further underscores the role of SRRM4 in neuronal function. CPEB4 regulates polyadenylation and protein expression of ASD-associated genes ( Gonatopoulos-Pournatzis et al., 2020 , Olla et al., 2023 , Parras et al., 2018 ). While no direct evidence links SRRM4 to CPEB4 splicing, the microexon in CPEB4 prevents protein aggregation by interacting with histidine clusters, allowing reversible gene regulation in response to neuronal stimulation ( Garcia-Cabau et al., 2025 ). Understanding the role of microexons, such as those in CPEB4 , and identifying their splicing regulators is essential for unraveling the molecular mechanisms underlying ASD. hnRNP hnRNPs are critical regulators of alternative splicing and are increasingly recognized for their role in the pathogenesis of ASD. Proteins like hnRNP A1, hnRNP U, and hnRNP H regulate splicing by binding to specific RNA motifs, thereby influencing exon inclusion or skipping ( Huelga et al., 2012 , Levengood and Tolbert, 2019 ). Dysregulation of hnRNP’s expression or function can lead to widespread splicing alterations in key transcripts essential for neuronal development and synaptic function. In the context of ASD, aberrant splicing of genes involved in synaptic plasticity, axonal guidance, and neuronal signaling has been linked to hnRNP dysfunction. hnRNP A1 binds to UAGGGA/U-rich motifs and represses exon splicing of genes involved in neurotransmitter signaling and dendritic spine formation, and disruptions in this regulation may contribute to ASD phenotypes ( Clarke et al., 2021 , Clarke et al., 2025 , Salapa et al., 2024 ). Similarly, HNRNPU plays a role in maintaining 3D chromatin architecture, which affects alternative splicing by altering the spatial organization of regulatory elements and the accessibility of splicing factors. Mutations in HNRNPU are linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, including ID and epilepsy ( Fan et al., 2018 , Tammer et al., 2022 ), potentially impacting splicing and contributing to ASD-related phenotypes ( Bramswig et al., 2017 ). Although hnRNP proteins are known to regulate neurodevelopment and alternative splicing, direct links between specific hnRNP proteins (eg, hnRNP H) and key ASD-associated genes such as SHANK3 remain to be explored. Future research is needed to better understand the precise roles of hnRNPs in ASD pathogenesis and assess their potential as therapeutic targets. PTBP Polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1) and its paralog PTBP2 (neuronal PTB, nPTB) are key regulators of alternative splicing, particularly in neurons, where they regulate each other through cross-regulation ( Boutz et al., 2007 , Spellman et al., 2007 ). These proteins typically bind to 15 to 25 pyrimidine-rich bases (cytosine or uracil). PTBP1 is highly expressed in neural progenitor cells (NPCs) but is downregulated during differentiation, allowing PTBP2 to take over splicing control in immature neurons. For example, in NPCs, PTBP1 binds exon 10 and its flanking intron of the Flna transcript, promoting ES, which contributes to brain-specific malformations ( Zhang et al., 2016 ). Notably, this process is independent of PTBP2, illustrating the distinct roles these 2 closely related RBPs can play in splicing regulation. Additionally, PTBP1 and PTBP2 bind to pyrimidine-rich tracts in intron 17 of DLG4 (PSD-95) transcripts, where they cooperatively suppress the inclusion of exon 18 in early neurons, each NPC, and immature neurons ( Zheng et al., 2012 ). PTBP1 expression is regulated by miR-124, which induces exon 10 skipping in PTBP2 pre-mRNA, generating a PTC and triggering degradation via the NMD pathway ( Makeyev et al., 2007 ). This regulatory mechanism ensures that faulty mRNAs are degraded, maintaining proper gene expression and preventing defects in neuronal differentiation ( Liu et al., 2023 , Zhang et al., 2019 ). Interestingly, comparative genomic analyses have identified rare biallelic point mutations in sequence-stretched genomic regions associated with human-specific social and behavioral traits ( Doan et al., 2016 , Wigle et al., 2023 ). Indels between PTBP2 and DPYD are implicated in abnormal neuronal development and ASD-related phenotypes ( Doan et al., 2016 ). These findings suggest that regulatory mutations affecting PTBP2 expression may contribute to the etiology of ASD by disrupting neuron-specific splicing programs during brain development. CUGBP Elav-Like Family The CUGBP Elav-like family (CELF) of RBPs plays a crucial role in alternative splicing regulation during neuronal development. CELF2 is enriched throughout the forebrain, and CELF 3 to 6 are specifically expressed within neurons and neuroblast cells, while CELF1 exhibits broad expression across most cell types ( Peng et al., 2024 ). Six CELF family proteins regulate alternative splicing of genes involved in nervous system function in animal models and human disease ( Dasgupta and Ladd, 2012 , Ladd, 2013 ). Among them, CELF4 is particularly associated with neurological symptoms, and its dysfunction has been linked to ASD. Haploinsufficiency of CELF4 has been identified in patients with 18q12.2 deletions, presenting symptoms such as low IQ, developmental delay, and behavioral disturbances related to ASD ( Halgren et al., 2012 ). Moreover, the first clinical case of maternally inherited CELF4 haploinsufficiency further reinforces its direct association with ASD ( Barone et al., 2017 ). In C. elegans , Unc-75 and Exc-7, 2 CELF homologs, exhibit distinct functions in excitatory cholinergic neurons ( Norris et al., 2014 ). Both proteins are coexpressed in excitatory neurons, where they promote the inclusion of exon 16 in Unc-16 , a JNK-signaling scaffold protein. In contrast, in inhibitory interneurons, only Exc-7 is expressed, leading to exon 16 skipping. In mice, heterozygous knockout of Celf2 led to autism-like behaviors, including social impairments, anxiety, and structural brain changes, such as reduced cortical thickness and dendritic spine density ( Norris et al., 2014 ). RNA sequencing and RNA immunoprecipitation-sequencing (RIP-seq) analyses of the Celf2 knockout cerebral cortex revealed differential expression of genes related to dendritic and synaptic processes, suggesting Celf2 plays a role in neuronal development and may contribute to ASD pathogenesis ( Duan et al., 2025 ). Even though the studies directly investigating CELF-mediated target alterations remain limited, accumulating evidence supports a significant link between CELF proteins and ASD. Thus, further research is needed to clarify their functional roles in neurodevelopment. RBFOX RBFOX proteins act as antagonists to PTBP1 during the transition from neuronal NPCs to neurons, regulating neuron-specific exons. For example, RBFOX proteins facilitate neuronal differentiation by mediating the switch of NIN (ninein) from a centrosomal isoform in NPCs to a noncentrosomal isoform in neurons. The RBFOX family (RBFOX1, RBFOX2, and RBFOX3) comprises RBPs primarily expressed in neurons, the heart, and muscle ( Kim et al., 2009 , Kuroyanagi, 2009 , Nakahata and Kawamoto, 2005 ). RBFOX1 and RBFOX2 are essential for early neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, whereas RBFOX3 is more involved in mature neuronal function ( Jacko et al., 2018 ). These proteins often compensate for one another, and the deletion of 2 or more RBFOX proteins results in stronger impacts on splicing regulation during neuronal development ( Lal et al., 2013 ). Studies have demonstrated that RBFOX expression is downregulated in autistic brains, and its alternative splicing targets overlap with ASD-associated genes, including Shank3 , Cacna1c , and Tsc2 ( Weyn-Vanhentenryck et al., 2014 ). Additionally, RBFOX1 preferentially binds downstream intronic sequences, promoting ES in synaptogenesis-related genes like CAMK2G , NRCAM , and GRIN1 , which have been found to be dysregulated in ASD samples ( An et al., 2014 , Voineagu et al., 2011 ). RBFOX1, a key regulator of neural development and alternative splicing, is strongly implicated in ASD. Disruptions in RBFOX1 or its regulatory pathways may alter the network of target genes linked to autism ( Hamada et al., 2016 , Weyn-Vanhentenryck et al., 2014 ). These findings underscore the role of RBFOX1 in neurodevelopment, particularly in regulating synaptic function and plasticity, and its potential involvement in ASD pathogenesis. Supporting this, studies have reported downregulation of RBFOX family members in autistic brains and have identified 48 direct RBFOX target genes from the SFARI database, all of which are involved in synaptic transmission, ion channel regulation, and cytoskeleton organization ( Weyn-Vanhentenryck et al., 2014 ). These findings highlight the critical role of RBFOX proteins in neuronal development and a potential molecular mechanism underlying ASD. One major target of RBFOX1 in ASD is CPEB4 , a regulator of mRNA polyadenylation. RBFOX1 influences the splicing of CPEB4 's exon 3 and neuron-specific microexon 4, both of which affect the length of the poly(A)-tail length and are implicated in ASD risk genes. Mouse models overexpressing CPEB4 isoforms that exclude microexon 4 exhibit reduced poly(A)-tail length, further emphasizing the importance of microexon splicing in the regulation of ASD-related genes ( Parras et al., 2018 ). Thus, RBFOX1’s role in the splicing of CPEB4 and other key neuronal genes underscores its significance in neurodevelopment and its disruption as a potential ASD-related mechanism. Neuro-oncological ventral antigen Neuro-oncological ventral antigen (NOVA) family, consisting of NOVA1 and NOVA, encodes neuron-specific RBPs that regulate alternative splicing and 3'-end processing. The KH domain of NOVA1 preferentially binds to pyrimidine-rich sequences within introns flanking neuron-specific exons, influencing exon inclusion or skipping depending on the binding site location ( Jensen et al., 2000 , Dredge et al., 2005 , Teplova et al., 2011 ). In Nova1 -null mice, defective motor neuron branching in the spinal cord and brainstem has been linked to aberrant splicing of pre-mRNA transcripts encoding the inhibitory glycine receptor α2 subunit (GlyRα2) E3A and GABAA γ2L ( Jensen et al., 2000 , Dredge and Darnell, 2003 , Dredge et al., 2005 ). These defects are absent in the forebrain, where NOVA1 is not expressed. In contrast, Nova2 -knockout mice exhibit deficits in inhibitory synaptic plasticity, highlighting the role of NOVA proteins in controlling neuronal excitation ( Huang et al., 2005 ). NOVA proteins also target synaptic proteins like Gephyrin and Girk2, which are essential for excitatory and inhibitory synaptic plasticity ( Huang et al., 2005 , Ule et al., 2005 , Luscher and Slesinger, 2010 , Tyagarajan and Fritschy, 2014 ). A high-throughput sequencing cross-linking immunoprecipitation (HITS-CLIP) study mapped over 16,000 NOVA2-binding sites in the mouse genome, identifying its role in synaptic proteins and axon guidance through genes such as Dcc , Robo2 , Slit2 , and Epha5 ( Ule et al., 2003 , Licatalosi et al., 2008 ). Notably, NOVA proteins are also implicated in paraneoplastic opsoclonus-myoclonus ataxia syndrome, where NOVA has been identified as a target antigen ( Allen et al., 2010 ). Mutations in NOVA2 have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD and ID. De novo frameshift and truncating mutations affecting KH RNA-binding domains of NOVA2 lead to defects in RNA binding and splicing regulation, impairing neurite outgrowth and axonal pathfinding ( Mattioli et al., 2020 , Scala et al., 2022 ). Recent studies identified 265 NOVA2-dependent retained introns, demonstrating that NOVA2 suppresses IR and regulates PTBP2 association, suggesting that NOVA2 acts as a scaffold for PTBP2 in alternative splicing regulation ( Saito et al., 2019 ). IR plays a key role in RNA processing, allowing for rapid transcriptome changes in response to developmental stimuli ( Braunschweig et al., 2014 , Mauger et al., 2016 , Wong et al., 2013 , Wong and Schmitz, 2022 , Yap et al., 2012 ). These findings highlight NOVA2's essential function in neurodevelopment and its involvement in ASD and cognitive impairments, underscoring the need for further research into the regulation of IR as a potential therapeutic target. Other KH Domain-Containing RBPs KH domain-containing RBPs play essential roles in RNA metabolism and neuronal function. Among these, SAM68 and FMRP are key regulators of alternative splicing and synaptic plasticity, both of which are implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD. SAM68 and SLM Family Proteins SAM68 regulates alternative splicing in neuronal and non-neuronal cells, particularly by regulating exon inclusion in NRXN1 (Neurexin1) pre-mRNA, which affects presynaptic and postsynaptic interactions ( Tabuchi and Sudhof, 2002 , Sudhof, 2008 ). This regulation is calcium-/calmodulin-dependent and modulates synaptic adhesion through interactions with GluD2/Cbln1 ligands. However, Sam68 knockout does not impact Nrxn2 splicing ( Resnick et al., 2008 , Iijima et al., 2011 ). Closely related SLM proteins (SLM1 and SLM2) also regulate synaptic adhesion molecules. SLM2 influences the splicing of Nrxn1 isoforms and targets molecules such as Cadm1 , Tenm2 , and Adgrl3 ( Ehrmann et al., 2013 , Traunmuller et al., 2023 ), while SLM1 plays a critical role in Nrxn splicing and cerebellar morphogenesis ( Iijima et al., 2014 ). FMRP and Fragile X Syndrome Another critical KH domain protein, FMRP, is associated with fragile X syndrome (FXS), a disorder linked to splicing dysregulation ( Salcedo-Arellano et al., 2020 , Bleuze et al., 2021 ). In Fmr1 -deficient mice, widespread mis-splicing is observed in both brain and peripheral tissues. FMRP regulates the translation and autosplicing of Mbnl1 , which affects MBNL1 protein localization and alters downstream RNA splicing, a pattern also found in human FXS brains ( Jung et al., 2023 ). Additionally, alternative splicing of FMR1 produces multiple isoforms, including a recently identified microexon (exon 9a) found in FXS-like patients. This leads to a truncated FMRP protein, potentially disrupting RNA-binding activity and neuronal function ( Fu et al., 2020 ). Together, SAM68, SLM2, and FMRP play crucial roles in alternative splicing, synaptic function, and neurodevelopment. Dysregulation of these proteins and their targets, such as neurexins and FMR1, is strongly associated with neurodevelopmental disorders including ASD, suggesting that understanding their splicing mechanisms may offer new therapeutic insights. ELAVL ELAV-like RNA-binding proteins (ELAVLs) are essential for neuronal excitability, synaptic transmission, and cognitive function. Among them, ELAVL2 (HuB) binds adenine-/uridine-rich elements and plays a pivotal role in early neurogenesis and synaptic development in the central nervous system. ELAVL2 interacts with RBFOX1, regulating fetal frontal cortical development and splicing of key genes, including CADM1 (Cell Adhesion Molecule 1, involved in neurite outgrowth and synapse formation), LMNB1 (Lamin B1, essential for nuclear integrity in neurons), and RTN4 (Reticulon 4, regulates axonal regeneration and neurite outgrowth) ( Berto et al., 2016 ). Mutations in CADM1 and RTN4 are frequently found in ASD patients, and LMNB1 dysregulation has been suggested as a potential biomarker for ASD ( Zhiling et al., 2008 , Glatt et al., 2012 , Li et al., 2012 , Wang et al., 2021 ). While CADM1 directly contributes to synapse formation and neural circuit stabilization, RTN4 modulates axon growth and regeneration, suggesting that interventions targeting either pathway could restore circuit integrity in ASD. Given its direct role synapse formation and axonal remodeling, CADM1 and RTN4 represent a promising therapeutic target for ASD-related splicing regulation. RBP Network in Neurodevelopment and ASD The interplay of multiple RBPs contributes to distinct patterns of alternative splicing in neurodevelopmental disorders ( Jha, 2023 , Mullari et al., 2023 , Huang et al., 2025 ). Several regulatory motifs are enriched near alternatively spliced exons in a brain-specific manner ( Cereda et al., 2014 ). For instance, RBFOX binding upstream of microexons enhances their inclusion, particularly for exons shorter than 51 nt, while PTBP1 generally acts as a repressor of microexon inclusion ( Li et al., 2015 ). Combinatorial actions between RBPs have been reported for NOVA and RBFOX for many target genes: synergistic effect on Gabrg2 and Mtap7d2 , additive effect on Numb , Syne2 , and Pbrm1 , and antagonistic actions on Arhef12 and Alcam ( Zhang et al., 2010 ). As a neuron-specific RBP, SRRM4 forms a uniquely organized RBP complex interacting with U2 snRNP subunits, binds to the 3’ splice site of neural exons, activating spliceosome assembly and counteracting PTBP-mediated repression ( Raj et al., 2014 ). In support of these interactions, brain-enriched alternative splicing has been associated with upstream motifs bound by neuron-specific RBPs such as NOVA and ELAV3, underscoring their role in establishing brain-specific splicing profiles ( Cereda et al., 2014 ). Many ASD-associated RBPs, including FMRP, RBFOX1/2/3, PTBP1/2, NOVA1/2, and CELF4, not only regulate neuronal transcripts but also modulate the splicing or stability of other RBPs, forming the intrinsic regulatory network. Recently, BrainRBPedia, a comprehensive database comprising 1,072 RBPs with annotations of functional, tissue-specific, and disease-related features, was developed to facilitate the study of RBPs in neurodevelopmental disorders. This integrated resource enables systematic prioritization and analysis of ASD- and ID-associated RBPs. Disruption in RBP networks can propagate throughout layers of post-transcriptional regulation, potentially amplifying molecular disturbances that contribute to ASD phenotypes. Therapeutic Strategies for ASD Individuals with ASD often encounter challenges in social interactions and experience delays in language and communication development ( Foxx, 2008 ). Currently, ASD is not considered curable, and therapeutic interventions primarily focus on nonpharmacological methods aimed at enhancing social communication and mitigating problematic behaviors through behavioral therapies, including social skills training and sensory integration therapy. These approaches are frequently combined with pharmacological treatments, such as antidepressants and anxiolytics, to alleviate social interaction difficulties and reduce aggressive behaviors. While conventional treatments are largely symptom-focused, gene therapies represent a more targeted strategy, aiming to address the genetic basis of ASD ( Weuring et al., 2021 ). Specific ASD subtypes may benefit from biological treatments, particularly as underlying mechanisms become better understood ( Hong and Iakoucheva, 2023 ). Two emerging therapeutic strategies are garnering significant attention. The first involves DNA-targeting treatments, such as gene editing technologies like CRISPR, to correct genetic abnormalities associated with ASD ( Carroll, 2023 ). The second strategy focuses on mRNA-targeting treatments, utilizing technologies like antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) and splice-switching oligonucleotides to modulate gene expression and alter splicing patterns ( Kim and Krainer, 2023 ). For example, a CPEB4 -targeting splice-switching oligonucleotide was developed by blocking the binding of hnRNPC/PTBP1 in CPEB4 mRNA, resulting in reduced microexon inclusion ( Martinez-Pizarro et al., 2024 ). Furthermore, targeting a downstream 3’ splice site of exon 5 led to a dose-dependent increase of microexon 4 inclusion and the protein levels of FOXP1 and AUTS2—both high-confidence ASD risk genes and direct CPEB4’s targets. Since the protein levels of FOXP1 and AUTS2 are reduced in ASD, restoration of their expression could potentially alleviate ASD-related deficits. Recent advancements in the CRISPR-Cas system, particularly with the integration of Class II type VI CRISPR-Cas13, have enhanced RNA-targeting strategies ( Wang et al., 2019 ). For instance, A-to-I RNA editors targeting methyl CpG-binding protein 2 ( MECP2 ) demonstrated high on-target editing efficiency by precisely correcting mutations in the brainstem and cerebellum of mouse models. It effectively restores endogenous MeCP2 protein levels and ameliorates severe respiratory abnormalities associated with Rett syndrome ( Nagarajan et al., 2006 , Wen et al., 2017 , Sinnamon et al., 2022 ). Another example is the high-fidelity CRISPR-Cas13 system, hfCas13x.1, designed to target the UBE3A-ATS antisense transcript, which typically silences the paternal Ube3a allele in neuron-derived cells ( Li, et al. 2023 ). When delivered under a neuron-specific promoter, this system successfully reactivated Ube3a expression in the cortex and hippocampus of Angelman syndrome-related models, a disorder often linked with ASD ( Greer et al., 2010 , Li et al., 2023 , Roy et al., 2023 ). It restores Ube3a expression and alleviates disease-related symptoms such as motor deficits and obesity for up to 4 months. Finally, small-molecule drugs that target RNA or ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) are being explored to modulate RBP function, such as FMRP, with the goal of mitigating ASD-related symptoms ( Hunt et al., 2021 ). CONCLUSION AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES Alternative splicing plays a dynamic role in neurodevelopment, contributing to transcriptomic complexity and influencing processes such as neuronal differentiation, synaptic formation, and cortical maturation ( Wen et al., 2023 ). Investigating alternative splicing isoforms across human postmortem brain tissues—from fetal to adult stages—is essential for understanding the pathogenesis of neurodevelopmental disorders such as ASD. Further research should extend beyond de novo germline mutations in ASD patients to explore novel genetic factors. Increasing evidence suggests that brain somatic mutations play a significant role in ASD, emphasizing the need to identify somatic mutations that influence splicing variants ( Bae et al., 2022 ). These postzygotic mutations may impact splicing regulators or cis-regulatory elements in ASD-associated genes, contributing to neurodevelopmental abnormalities ( An and Kim, 2024 ). A systematic approach integrating single-cell and long-read sequencing technologies will be essential for uncovering the role of brain somatic mutations in ASD pathogenesis. Additionally, short tandem repeats are prevalent in the human genome and have been implicated in ASD ( Trost et al., 2020 , Kim et al., 2024a , Kim et al., 2024b , Kim et al., 2024c , Kim et al., 2024d ). Given their potential to influence alternative splicing ( Davenport and Swanson, 2024 ), identification of short tandem repeats affecting splicing dynamics is a key next step. Advances in long-read sequencing now allow precise characterization of repeat-mediated splicing disruptions, providing new opportunities to investigate their regulatory impact on ASD-related genes. Rapid evolution of RNA sequencing technologies has significantly advanced RNA research, expanding our understanding of RNA biology and its therapeutic potential. Additionally, advancements in analytical tools and user-friendly platforms have made RNA sequencing more accessible, even to nonexperts in bioinformatics ( Lee et al., 2024 ). This progress highlights the importance of conducting comprehensive studies on RBPs and their targets, as understanding these interactions is essential for developing precise and effective therapies. The emergence of large language models also holds promise for furthering our understanding of splicing regulation in ASD. Given the distinct sequence patterns underlying splicing complexes, models trained on large-scale transcriptomic and genomic datasets could improve the identification of sequence motifs that influence splicing outcomes. These models may also help elucidate the effects of genetic variation on ASD’s phenotypic heterogeneity, including functional subtypes, symptom severity, and sex differences in brain development. Many ASD-associated genes undergo significant splicing transitions during brain development, particularly during mid-fetal neurogenesis, when critical regulatory networks for neuronal specification and connectivity are established ( Kim et al., 2024a , Kim et al., 2024b , Kim et al., 2024c , Kim et al., 2024d ). Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of brain-region-specific and stage-specific splicing isoforms is also necessary for therapeutic development, particularly for designing ASO-based interventions. Since ASOs modulate RNA expression by targeting specific splice sites, their effectiveness hinges on accurately identifying the predominant isoforms expressed during both developing and mature brain. Mistargeting could lead to unintended off-target effects or failure to restore physiological splicing patterns. Thus, high-resolution transcriptomic datasets—spanning fetal to adult brain stages and including long-read sequencing data—are essential resources for selecting optimal ASO targets. Establishing a publicly available repository of brain isoform data would greatly facilitate precision medicine approaches in ASD, providing a valuable resource for designing splicing-modifying therapeutics tailored to neurodevelopmental contexts. With continued research in this area, the field holds promise for driving innovation and paving the way for transformative treatments for ASD and other neurodevelopmental disorders.
