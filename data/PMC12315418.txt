To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . Background Migraine is closely related to sleep disorders. Vitamin D is known to regulate sleep, cell proliferation and differentiation. The aim of our study was to measure the predictive value of vitamin D for sleep disorders in paediatric migraine. We hypothesized that vitamin D would be apredictor of sleep disorders in paediatric migraine. Methods We conducted an analysis of serum vitamin D levels in 150 paediatric migraine. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used as a measure of sleep quality. Based on the PSQI scores, the subjects were divided into normal sleep group and sleep disorder group. Correlation analysis and regression analysis were employed to explore the potential etiology of sleep disturbances in paediatric migraine. Results There was significant difference in the prevalence of sleep disorders between paediatric migraine and the control group (Odds ratio [OR] = 4.91,95% confidence interval [CI] = 3.25–7.40, P = 0.001). Additionally, the regression analysis demonstrated that vitamin D could serve as a predictive factor for sleep disorders in paediatric migraine (OR = 0.008, 95%CI: 0.023–0.225, P = 0.004). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis revealed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.957 (95% CI 0.925–0.988), with a cutoff value of 15.84 ng/ml, a sensitivity of 87.5%, and a specificity of 93.7% for serum vitamin D. Conclusion Our study confirms the association between sleep disorder and vitamin D levels in paediatric migraine. The use of vitamin D supplementation as a treatment strategy for sleep disturbances in paediatric migraine needs further investigation. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of moderate to severe headache, often accompanied by nausea, photophobia, phonophobia, and sometimes aura, which characterized by recurrent headaches and can be classified into two subtypes based on symptoms, including migraine with aura (MA) and migraine without aura (MO) [ 3 – 7 ]. The selection of treatment for migraines continues to be unsatisfactory due to reported lack of efficacy and significant side effects [ 8 ]. Therefore, the identification of migraine risk factors and their prevention remains of significant clinical values. Sleep disorders are also highly prevalent among children and adolescents and are particularly common in those diagnosed with migraine [ 9 , 10 ]. Previous study had found that up to 60% of pediatric migraineurs experience clinically significant sleep disturbances [ 11 , 12 ]. Another study demonstrated that poor sleep quality in children is associated with increased headache frequency and greater migraine-related disability [ 13 ]. These findings highlight the importance of assessing sleep health in pediatric migraine populations to inform early intervention strategies [ 14 , 15 ]. Previous studies provide evidence supporting lower sleep quality in individuals with migraine compared to those without migraine [ 16 , 17 ]. Self-reported poor sleep quality has been associated with increased migraine frequency or progression to chronic migraine [ 18 , 19 ].The potential role of vitamin D in the pathogenic mechanism of migraine has attracted increasing attention [ 8 ]. Moreover, there is a strong association between vitamin D deficiency and sleep disorders [ 20 , 21 ]. However, the underlying mechanism of the comorbidity between paediatric migraine and sleep disorder with respect to vitamin D deficiency remains to be further investigated. Recent research suggests that vitamin D may influence sleep regulation through multiple neurobiological pathways [ 22 , 23 ]. Vitamin D receptors are distributed in several brain regions involved in sleep–wake regulation, including the hypothalamus [ 24 ]. It is known to modulate melatonin synthesis, reduce neuroinflammation, and affect neurotransmitters such as dopamine and serotonin, all of which play important roles in both migraine and sleep physiology [ 25 , 26 ]. These mechanisms support the biological plausibility of a link between vitamin D deficiency, sleep disturbances, and migraine, particularly in pediatric populations. Therefore, this study examines the levels of vitamin D in paediatric migraine and sleep disorder as well as in patients with migraine alone, aiming to explore the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and the occurrence of sleep disorder in paediatric migraine patients. The findings of this study can provide biological support for the comorbidity mechanism of paediatric migraine and sleep disorder, thus offering new insights for clinical diagnosis and treatment. Materials and methods Research subjects This retrospective case-control study included 150 pediatric migraine patients who were identified through review of medical records from June 2018 to June 2023. Subspecialty Department at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital, from June 2018 to June 2023. In addition, during the same period, a healthy control group of 150 individuals without a history of migraine but with headache-like symptoms, matched for age and gender, was recruited from the Health Examination Center of our hospital. The research protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and their families, and all collected research data were stored in accordance with ethical guidelines for medical research. Inclusion and exclusion criteria The inclusion criteria for patients are as follows: the migraine group should include patients aged 6 to 17 years who meet the diagnostic criteria for migraine as defined by the International Headache Society (ICHD-III); Both migraine with aura (MA) and migraine without aura (MO) subtypes were included in the study [ 27 ]. 2) First-time diagnosis and no prior medication use related to migraine or sleep disorders. 4) Ability to complete the questionnaires and assessments with parental assistance as needed. 5) Informed consent obtained from legal guardians and assent from the child when appropriate. Exclusion criteria 1) History of neurological disorders (e.g., stroke, central nervous system infections, demyelinating diseases, intracranial tumors). 2) Presence of other diagnosed sleep disorders (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome). 3) Comorbid systemic diseases known to affect vitamin D metabolism (e.g., metabolic syndrome, endocrine disorders, liver or kidney disease). 4) Recent use of medications that influence vitamin D levels or sleep (e.g., vitamin D supplements, antidepressants, sedatives). 5) Psychiatric disorders or family history of severe mental illness.6) Developmental delay or intellectual disability affecting assessment compliance. 7) Refusal to provide informed consent. Clinical assessment The Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6), which has been validated in pediatric populations, comprises six items: pain, role functioning, social functioning, vitality, emotional distress, and cognitive functioning. Patients respond using a five-point Likert scale ranging from “Always” to “Never,” with higher scores indicating greater migraine impact on daily life [ 28 ]. Moreover, the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA14) and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD24), both of which have been applied in pediatric clinical settings with acceptable reliability and validity, were used to assess emotional symptoms [ 29 ]. Sleep conditions and grouping The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), consisting of 23 items, has been widely used over the past month to measure sleep quality [ 30 ]. The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. Based on the PSQI score, subjects were classified into two groups: the sleep disorder group (PSQI &gt; 5) and the normal sleep group (PSQI ≤ 5). Laboratory assessment Although fasting is not strictly necessary for vitamin D measurement, participants were instructed to fast for 12 h prior to blood collection in accordance with the hospital’s standard biochemical testing protocol. This approach ensured consistent sample quality and minimized confounding metabolic variations that could influence other concurrent blood markers. According to the standardized operating procedure for biochemical indicator testing of serum vitamin D and other parameters established in our laboratory, all subjects were sampled for venous blood on the second morning after admission. The blood samples were collected in heparinized tubes and immediately centrifuged and sent for testing. The serum levels of vitamin D were determined using the ABBOTT Architect i2000SR automated immune detection system and the chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay method. Vitamin D deficiency was defined as serum vitamin D concentration of less than 20 ng/mL, while a concentration of equal to or greater than 20 ng/mL was considered normal. Statistical analysis The analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics version 22.0 software. Abnormal data were represented using quartile intervals (M, P25, P75), while normal data were represented using mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical data were expressed as quantities (%). Spearman or Pearson analysis was performed to investigate the clinical characteristics and vitamin D levels in patients with RLS. Three regression models were constructed with the presence of sleep disorders as the dependent variable (yes = 1, no = 0). Model 1 represented the unadjusted model, while Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, and BMI. Model 3 included adjustments for age, gender, BMI, years of education, duration of illness, and sunlight exposure. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted using these three model indicators as independent variables. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive value of serum vitamin D levels for sleep disorders in migraine patients. A significance level of P &lt; 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Results Demographics and clinical characteristics A retrospective analysis was conducted on 233 paediatric migraine patients using inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in the inclusion of 150 migraine patients (Fig. 1 ). The participants were divided into two groups based on their PSQI scores: a sleep disorder group ( n = 103) and a normal group ( n = 47). Baseline characteristics of all participants are summarized in Table 1 . Compared to the normal group, the sleep disorder group had lower serum vitamin D levels and higher HIT-6 scores ( P &lt; 0.001). Based on the standard threshold of &lt; 20 ng/ml, vitamin D deficiency was present in 91.3% (94/103) of patients in the sleep disorder group and 61.7% (29/47) of patients in the normal sleep group. Additionally, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender, BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, disease duration, education level, and sunlight exposure ( p &gt; 0.05) (Table 1 ). Association between migraine and sleep disorders risk among patients with migraine According to the PSQI scores, 103 paediatric migraine were diagnosed with sleep disorders, while 21 patients in the control group were diagnosed with sleep disorders. There was significant difference in the prevalence of sleep disorders between patients with migraine and the control group (OR = 4.91,95%CI = 3.25–7.40, P = 0.001) (Table 2 ). Correlation analysis The correlation analysis showed a negative correlation between serum vitamin D levels and PSQI, HAMA14, HAMD24, and HIT-6 scores ( p &lt; 0.05). A correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between serum vitamin D levels and HIT-6 scores ( r = − 0.257, p = 0.012), indicating that patients with lower vitamin D levels reported a greater migraine burden. Partial correlation analysis, after controlling for age, gender, BMI, disease duration, education level, and sunlight exposure, still showed a positive correlation between serum vitamin D levels and PSQI, HAMA14, HAMD24, and HIT-6 scores ( p &lt; 0.05). There were no significant correlations between vitamin D levels and gender, age, BMI, disease duration, education level, and sunlight exposure in both patient groups ( p &gt; 0.05). Table 3 for the correlation analysis results. Regression analysis Table 4 presents the three regression models. In Model 1, serum vitamin D was found to be an independent risk predictor for poor sleep quality in paediatric migraine patients ( p &lt; 0.001; odds ratio = 0.131; 95% confidence interval: 0.058–0.498). In Model 2, after further adjusting for age, gender, and BMI, serum vitamin D remained an independent risk predictor for poor sleep quality in paediatric migraine patients ( p &lt; 0.001; odds ratio = 0.036; 95% confidence interval: 0.049–0.379). In Model 3, after adjusting for age, gender, BMI, disease duration, education level, and sunlight exposure, serum vitamin D continued to be an independent risk predictor for poor sleep quality in paediatric migraine patients ( p = 0.004; odds ratio = 0.008; 95% confidence interval: 0.023–0.225). ROC analysis The predictive value of serum vitamin D for poor sleep quality in paediatric migraine patients was determined by ROC analysis, and the results are shown in Fig. 2 . The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.957 (95% CI 0.925 ~ 0.988). The critical value of serum vitamin D was 15.84 ng/ml, with a sensitivity of 87.5% and a specificity of 93.7%. Discussion The observed association between vitamin D deficiency and sleep disturbances in pediatric migraine adds to growing evidence linking metabolic and neuroinflammatory pathways with migraine pathophysiology. These findings support a biologically plausible connection, which may involve circadian regulation, neurotransmitter modulation, and neuroimmune interactions. In the context of pediatric populations, the role of vitamin D may be particularly important given its involvement in both neurodevelopment and immune regulation. The connections between sleep quality, vitamin D and migraine Migraine and sleep disorders are common and often burdensome chronic conditions, with high prevalence rates in the general population [ 31 , 32 ]. They have a significant socioeconomic impact and cost burden [ 33 , 34 ]. We found that the prevalence of sleep disorders in patients with migraine was approximately 70%, consistent with previous reports [ 12 , 19 , 35 ]. Previous research has confirmed the presence of sleep disturbances in the majority of migraine patients, primarily manifested as difficulty falling asleep [ 36 ]. Additionally, existing studies have reported an association between vitamin D deficiency and poor sleep quality in patients with migraine [ 37 – 39 ] and our findings also suggest a significant negative correlation between serum vitamin D levels and PSQI scores. Furthermore, a substantial body of clinical and epidemiological research indicates that sleep disorders are risk factors for depression in migraine patients [ 19 , 40 ] which aligns with our study results revealing significantly higher HAMA14 and HAMD24 values in migraine patients with poor sleep quality. Vitamin D has been reported to exert specific functions in the central nervous system by affecting cell proliferation, acting as a neuroprotective and potent antioxidant agent, and modulating neurotransmitters [ 41 – 43 ]. Previously observational evidence has demonstrated that the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency is significantly higher in migraine individuals and vitamin D deficiency was independently associated with migraine [ 44 – 47 ]. Moreover, prior meta-analysis incorporating six randomized controlled trials revealed that supplementation of vitamin D significantly reduces the frequency and duration of monthly headache attacks in comparison to the control group of migraine patients [ 48 ]. The probable mechanism of vitamin D regulation of sleep among migraine patients The complex relationships between sleep disorders and migraine point to a common shared pathophysiology [ 33 ]. There is a growing body of evidence suggesting that vitamin D can regulate the circadian rhythm of sleep [ 49 , 50 ]. Recent biochemical and functional imaging studies have found that central nervous system structures and neurotransmitters are involved in the pathophysiology of migraine, highlighting the importance of regulating normal sleep architecture [ 51 , 52 ]. Furthermore, according to most existing research, migraine attacks follow diurnal variations, with peak occurrence in the early morning or late at night [ 53 ]. Moreover, existing evidence indicates that hypothalamic and brainstem dysfunction are common pathological mechanisms underlying migraine and sleep disorders [ 54 , 55 ]. These structures are involved in sleep-wake physiology as well as pain transmission and modulation, and their functional impairment may explain the observed bidirectional relationship between migraine and sleep disorder [ 56 ]. Our hypothesis posits that vitamin D may be involved in this interaction process, thus modulating the sleep-wake cycle. Several studies have indicated that physiological levels of vitamin D can inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-10 [ 57 ]. Moreover, Neurogenic inflammation plays a role in migraine characterized by altered levels of cytokines [ 58 – 60 ]. Numerous studies have investigated the link between neuroinflammation and sleep disturbances [ 61 ]. The anti-inflammatory effects of vitamin D may provide another explanation for how vitamin D deficiency can lead to sleep disorders. Additionally, vitamin D is a major sleep-regulating hormone that can regulate the production of melatonin. Some evidence suggests that melatonin is beneficial for patients with sleep disturbances associated with various neurological disorders [ 62 , 63 ]. However, more research is needed to fully understand the complex relationship between vitamin D and sleep disorders. Limitations and recommendations There are several limitations to our study that need to be acknowledged. Firstly, our study design is unable to establish a causal relationship between vitamin D deficiency and paediatric migraine, warranting further prospective research. Secondly, our assessment of sleep disorders in the study was limited to a single approach, and future studies should encompass a wider range of sleep disorders. Lastly, future longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the causal relationship between vitamin D deficiency and sleep disturbances in pediatric migraine. Moreover, studies incorporating objective sleep assessments and intervention trials using vitamin D supplementation may offer more definitive evidence for clinical application. Fourth, the study relied on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a subjective self-report measure of sleep quality. Although PSQI is widely validated and used in pediatric populations, it may not capture objective sleep parameters such as sleep architecture, latency, and arousals. Future studies incorporating objective sleep measurements like polysomnography or actigraphy are warranted to validate and extend our findings. We did not assess specific types of sleep disturbances (e.g., difficulty falling asleep, early awakening, excessive daytime sleepiness), as the PSQI provides a global score rather than diagnostic categories. Future research should aim to identify which particular domains of sleep are most strongly affected in pediatric migraineurs with vitamin D deficiency, in order to guide targeted interventions. Conclusion In conclusion, this study has provided initial evidence of the association between vitamin D and poor sleep quality in paediatric migraine patients, which could serve as a useful biomarker for developing treatment strategies for diagnosing and preventing migraine with coexisting sleep disorders. Future work should aim to confirm the efficacy of vitamin D supplementation as a treatment strategy for sleep disorders in paediatric migraine patients in larger cohorts.
