Abstract Abstract Objectives Japanese people are known to get the least amount of sleep in the world, and in particular, the increase in sleep deprived young women is a serious problem. Sleep deprivation is considered to be associated with depression, but the factors involved in this problem are unclear. This study aimed to examine the association between sociodemographic factors, lifestyle choices, sleep-related characteristics and depressive symptoms in young Japanese women. Design Web-based cross-sectional study. Participants We distributed an online survey aiming to recruit young Japanese women ages 18 to 29 from 8 November 2022 to 2 February 2023. Primary and secondary outcome measures Sociodemographic, lifestyle, health, and sleep characteristics were compared among participants according to the presence or absence of depressive symptoms, as assessed by the Patient Health Questionnaire 2 (Cut-off value: 3 points). Results Overall, 540 participants aged between 19 and 29 years were enrolled in the study. The overall prevalence of depressive symptoms was 15.6%. The group with depressive symptoms had a higher rate of current smoking status at 38.5% (unadjusted OR 3.716, 95% CI 1.624 to 8.502). Multiple logistic analyses revealed that depressive symptoms were associated with increased sleep onset latency (β=0.282; p=0.048; OR, 1.325; 95% CI, 1.003 to 1.752) and daytime dysfunction (β=0.550; p&lt;0.001; OR, 1.733; 95% CI, 1.281 to 2.343) after adjusting for the effects of smoking habits. Conclusions Depressive symptoms were found to be associated with sleep disorders in young Japanese women, and paying attention to sleep onset latency and daytime dysfunction which are one of the strongest related factors to depressive symptoms could be a clue to improving sleep disorders and depressive symptoms. Strengths and limitations of this study This study utilised a self-administered online questionnaire survey targeting young women to investigate the association between sleep disorders and depressive symptoms. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine this association in a Japanese population and one of the first to explore the impact of sleep quality on depressive symptoms. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire 2 screening tool, which provides an initial indication of potential depressive symptoms but does not replace clinical diagnosis. As the study involved a retrospective online survey about past habits, potential recall bias may have influenced the findings. Introduction Insomnia is prevalent in Japan, as indicated by the findings of the 2021 survey report on ‘Time Use Across the World’ by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. The data reveal that Japan has the lowest average daily sleep duration at 442 min (7.36 hour), with South Korea following closely at 471 min (7.85 hour) and Sweden at 483 min (8.03 hour). 1 Additionally, the average sleep duration of Japanese women (435 min) was found to be shorter than that of men (448 min). A previous cross-sectional study of 54 269 adults reported that shorter sleep duration was associated with greater susceptibility to coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, and diabetes, each of which have a lifestyle-related component of risk. 2 Furthermore, insomnia can potentially exacerbate mental health issues. 3 Sleep and depression are closely related, with disorders in both areas being more prevalent in women than in men. 4 5 Individuals who habitually get an adequate amount of sleep (7–8 hours) have a reduced risk of depression. 6 Therefore, the risk of depression can increase with sleep disturbances. A meta-analysis conducted in 2011 on longitudinal epidemiological studies revealed that individuals with insomnia who were not experiencing depression had double the risk of developing depression than those without sleep disturbances. 3 However, previous studies on sleep quality have yielded inconsistent results. Thus, examining sleep quality associated with depressive symptoms may bring us closer to elucidating the bidirectional association. Furthermore, it has been noted that young Japanese women have characteristics that make them prone to negative emotions. 7 Recent studies have found that depression was associated with difficulty in maintaining sleep (aOR=2.76, p&lt;0.001), early morning awakening (aOR=1.72, p=0.009) and difficulty falling asleep (p=0.015), 8 including issues such as difficulty in maintaining sleep and early morning awakenings. A previous cross-sectional study found that previous smokers and occasional smokers had a higher risk of depression compared with non-smokers, with daily smokers having the highest risk. 9 Data from the Taiwan Biobank indicated that smoking is significantly associated with depression, particularly in younger women. 10 Nevertheless, whether these findings apply to the Japanese population is unclear. Hence, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between depressive symptoms and sociodemographic factors, lifestyle choices, and sleep-related characteristics among young Japanese women. We hypothesised that depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances are associated and lead to a decrease in sleep quality in a cohort of young Japanese women. Methods Study design and participants A cross-sectional study using snowball sampling was conducted from 8 November 2022 to 2 February 2023. The study targeted young Japanese women aged 18–29 years, residing in various regions of Japan. An online survey was administered using Google Forms (Alphabet, Mountain View, CA, USA). Invitation messages containing links to online surveys were sent out through acquaintances of Japanese researchers involved in this study. The recipients were encouraged to share the survey with their peers at universities or organisations. The recipients were fully explained the age and gender of the participants and the purpose of the study before the link was shared with them. Before commencing the online survey questions, participants were provided with a summary of the survey questions, study objectives, inclusion criteria in the survey, estimated time required and guidelines. The following instructions were provided: participants could respond anonymously, each participant should submit only one response and participants could opt out of the study at any point during the survey process. Participants indicated their agreement to participate by checking a box on the questionnaire. Only those who provided consent proceeded to answer the questions after reviewing the survey summary and notes. Informed consent for the survey was obtained through the agreement text and checkboxes on the first screen of Google Forms. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) age, 18–29 years, (b) Japanese woman residing in Japan and (c) agreement to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) current or history of psychiatric and sleep disorders and (b) current use of tranquillisers and sleep-inducing medications. A total of 900 questionnaires were administered. Figure 1 illustrates the participant flowchart, from recruitment to the final number of respondents analysed. This study adhered to the recommendations of the checklist for reporting results of internet e-surveys (CHERRIES), 11 conformed to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Instruments and data collection The questionnaire collected sociodemographic data, including age (years), height (cm), body weight (kg), body mass index (BMI) calculated from height and weight (kg/m 2 ), studentship status (yes/no), living arrangement (alone/with others) and involvement in sports community (yes/no). It included a screening test for psychosocial disturbances related to depressive symptoms over the past 2 weeks. The Patient Health Questionnaire 2 (PHQ-2) was used to evaluate the frequency of depressed mood and anhedonia. 12 Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with a maximum total score of 6. A score of ≥3 was defined as positive for depressive symptoms. PHQ-2 is suitable for simplicity and rapid screening, and it can be administered in a shorter time compared with PHQ-9, making it useful in large-scale surveys or environments with time constraints. 12 The Health Practice Index (HPI) was used to evaluate lifestyle habits. 13 The HPI questionnaire comprised seven health behaviour indicators, including exercise habits (yes/no), breakfast consumption (eating/not eating), snacking between meals (eating/not eating), sleeping for more than 7 hours (yes/no), smoking status (yes/no), alcohol consumption level (low/high) and BMI classification (slim/normal/overweight/obese). The HPI global score was determined by assigning 1 point for each desirable habit marked as ‘yes’ and 0 points for ‘no’. Furthermore, the participants were asked about their caffeine consumption frequency (≥3 times/week, yes/no), habits of studying until just before going to bed and watching TV until just before bedtime (≥3 times/week, yes/no), sedentary time on weekdays and weekends (min/day), and screen time for leisure and study/work (min/day) as health-related characteristics. 14 15 The Generalised Anxiety Disorder 2-item Scale (GAD-2) was used as a brief screening tool for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). The GAD-2 included the following questions: ‘In the past 2 weeks, have you been nervous or anxious almost every day?’ and ‘In the past 2 weeks, have you worried or thought about extraneous things almost every day?’. 12 Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day), with higher scores indicating the presence of GAD. For the psychological assessment of participants’ health and well-being, the WHO Five Well-Being Index-Japanese (WHO-5-J) was also employed. 16 The index comprises five questions, and the WHO-5-J scores were calculated as the sum of the scores for each item, with scores ranging from 0 to 25; higher scores indicate greater levels of happiness and well-being. The Japanese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI-J) was used to examine sleep-related lifestyle habits. The PSQI-J was employed to assess sleep quality over the past month before the study and has been validated in many previous studies. 17 18 The PSQI can generate seven component scores (C1: subjective sleep quality; overall subjective evaluation of sleep; C2: sleep onset latency; evaluation of ease of falling asleep; C3: sleep duration; evaluation of total sleep time; C4: habitual sleep efficiency; evaluation of the ratio of actual sleep time to bedtime; C5: sleep disturbance; evaluation of frequency of mid-awakenings; C6: use of sleep medication; evaluation of frequency of medication use for sleep; C7: daytime dysfunction; evaluation of depressed mood and sleepiness associated with sleep problems) from a total of 18 items, along with the PSQI Global Score, which indicates the degree of sleep disturbance. In addition to bedtime, rise time, sleep duration (h) and time taken to fall asleep after going to bed (min), the time of returning home was also obtained. To identify the presence of sleep disorders, the cut-off score for the PSQI-J was set at 5.5 points. 18 Statistical analysis The respondents were categorised into two groups based on a PHQ-2 cut-off value of 3 points: those without depressive symptoms and those with depressive symptoms. Sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, health-related characteristics and sleep-related habits were compared between the groups. Data collected from the online survey were analysed using IBM SPSS V.28.0 for Mac (IBM Japan Co., Tokyo, Japan). Before the analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the data. In the case of non-normative analysis, data were presented in the form ‘median (IQR: Q1–Q3)’. The unpaired t-test, Mann-Whitney U test and χ 2 test were used to compare sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, health-related characteristics and sleep-related habits between the groups with and without depressive symptoms. The Bonferroni correction was applied in the χ 2 test to account for multiple comparisons. The method for calculating 95% CIs for the OR of the χ 2 test was based on the method of Szumilas. 19 Based on the PSQI-J cut-off score of 5.5 points, similar comparisons were conducted by dividing participants into groups with and without insomnia (see online supplemental tables 1 and 2 ). A logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors influencing depressive symptoms, with a PHQ-2 score of ≥3 coded as 0 and ≤2 as 1. The presence or absence of depressive symptoms served as the dependent variable, while the PSQI-J subitems were considered independent variables. OR and CIs were calculated for the dependent variables. The variance inflation coefficient was computed to evaluate the possibility of multicollinearity among independent variables in the multivariate regression analysis. Statistical significance was set at p&lt;0.05. Sample sizes were calculated with a t-test (effect size d=0.5 (medium), alpha error=0.05, power=0.80, allocation ratio N2/N1=1) using G* power 3.1. 20 The effect size was set based on the assumption that the difference in PSQI-J scores between the group with depressive symptoms and the group without depressive symptoms is moderate. According to the test results, the study required a minimum of 128 participants. Patient and public involvement statement No patients participated in the study’s design, recruitment or implementation. While the results were shared directly with each study participant, they were not communicated to other participants. Results The survey was distributed to 900 participants, and 614 responses (response rate: 68.2%) were received. Among the respondents, 74 individuals were excluded for the following reasons: seven were outside the survey age range, five had mental illness, 51 used medications for menstruation or hormone regulation and 11 provided invalid or incorrect responses. Therefore, the final analysis included 540 respondents. Out of these, 456 were categorised into the group without depressive symptoms, while 84 were placed in the group with depressive symptoms based on the PHQ-2 cut-off value points. The prevalence of depressive symptoms in the study sample was 15.6%. Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents with and without depressive symptoms The mean ages of the groups without and with depressive symptoms were 21.9 (SD = 2.2) and 22.2 years (SD=2.2), respectively. The mean BMIs were 20.8 kg/m 2 (SD=2.4) and 20.5 kg/m 2 (SD=2.5), respectively. No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding studentship status, living arrangements and participation in a sports community ( table 1 ). The percentages in the ‘Total’ column were calculated using the total number of participants (n=540) as the denominator. The percentages for the presence or absence of insomnia were calculated using the value in the ‘Total’ column for each group as the denominator. These rules apply to all tables. Lifestyle habits and health-related characteristics of the groups with and without depressive symptoms The HPI global scores were 10.0 (IQR: 9.0–11.0) in both groups, with no significant differences observed. Regarding smoking habits, non-smoking status (no) was more prevalent in the group without depressive symptoms (85.6%), whereas current smoking status (yes) was significantly higher in the group with depressive symptoms (38.5%). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding caffeine consumption, study time before bedtime, watching before bedtime, sedentary time, and screen time. However, a statistically significant difference was observed between the groups with and without depressive symptoms regarding the GAD-2 and WHO-5-J scores (p&lt;0.001) ( table 2 ). Respondents’ sleep-related habits with and without depressive symptoms No significant differences were found in return home time, bedtime, exercise time and sleep duration between the two groups. However, the time taken to fall asleep from going to bed was significantly longer in the group with depressive symptoms, at 30.0 (IQR: 15.0–60.0) min, than in the group without depressive symptoms, at 15.0 (IQR: 10.0–30.0) min (p&lt;0.001). The global PSQI-J score of the group with depressive symptoms was 7.0 (IQR: 5.0–9.0), significantly greater than 5.0 (IQR: 3.0–7.0) of the group without depressive symptoms. Significant differences were also observed in the PSQI-J subitems for sleep quality, sleep onset latency, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication and daytime dysfunction (p&lt;0.001, p&lt;0.001, p=0.049, p=0.045, p=0.020, and p=0.020, respectively) between the two groups ( table 3 ). Relationship between the presence of depressive symptoms and PSQI-J subitems Multiple logistic analyses were performed to assess the factors influencing sleep quality in relation to depressive symptoms using the PHQ-2 score as the independent variable. This analysis revealed statistically significant associations between sleep onset latency in the PSQI-J sub-items after adjusting for the effects of smoking habits (β=0.282; p=0.048; OR, 1.325; 95% CI, 1.003 to 1.752) and daytime dysfunction (β=0.550; p&lt;0.001; OR, 1.733; 95% CI, 1.281 to 2.343) ( table 4 ). Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents with and without insomnia The mean ages of the groups without and with insomnia were 22.0 (SD = 2.3) and 21.8 years (SD=2.2), respectively. The mean BMIs were 20.7 kg/m 2 (SD=2.3) and 20.8 kg/m 2 (SD=2.6), respectively. No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding studentship status, living arrangements and participation in a sports community ( online supplemental table 1 ). Lifestyle habits and health-related characteristics of the groups with and without insomnia The HPI global score was 10.0 (IQR: 9.0–11.0) in the non-insomnia group and 11.0 (9.0–11.0) in the insomnia group, with no difference between the groups. The percentage of participants who eat breakfast at least 5 days per week was 66.7%, of which 55.3% showed no insomnia, and 44.7% showed insomnia. On the other hand, 33.3% of participants did not eat breakfast, among whom 45.0% showed no insomnia, while 55.0% showed insomnia, demonstrating a significant difference (p=0.024). A statistically significant difference was observed between the groups with and without depressive symptoms regarding the GAD-2, WHO-5-J, and PHQ-2 scores (p&lt;0.001) ( online supplemental table 2 ). Discussion To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between sleep quality and depressive symptoms among young Japanese women. The percentage of sleep-deprived young women in Japan is increasing, 21 and the duration of sleep time is progressively decreasing. Under these circumstances, our findings suggest that young women with depressive symptoms are more likely to experience sleep onset latency and daytime dysfunction. Alternatively, depressive symptoms caused by certain factors may prolong the time to fall asleep and decrease productivity in studying and working during the day. Our study revealed a high prevalence of depressive symptoms (15.6%), consistent with the results of Ueda et al , who reported a 17.4% depression rate in a nationally representative sample of Japanese women in 2020. 22 This increasing prevalence of depression is a critical concern for Japan compared with other countries worldwide. In 2013, 7.9% of the overall population in Japan was reported to be affected by depression; however, this figure was doubled to 17.3% in 2020 following the COVID-19 outbreak. 22 23 Notably, 64.3% of the participants in our study were students. The prevalence of depression among university students worldwide is estimated to be 32%, 24 whereas in the community, it is approximately 13%. 25 Additionally, the period of school-year transition, particularly for students, can significantly impact mental health. 26 The depression rates in our cohort of young Japanese women aged 18–29 years at 2 years after COVID-19 can be added to previous findings. Regarding lifestyle characteristics, the smoking rate was remarkably higher at 38.5% in the group with depressive symptoms. A survey by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare indicated that 7.6% of Japanese women currently smoke habitually, a trend similar to that in the present study. 21 A 2017 systematic review highlighted a connection between smoking and the subsequent onset of depression. 27 The ‘self-medication’ hypothesis suggests that individuals may smoke to alleviate symptoms of depression. 28 Conversely, smoking has been reported to interfere with natural adaptive coping mechanisms and potentially induce depression in the opposite direction. 29 While many individuals stress that they cannot quit smoking, they need to be educated that cigarettes can be a source of stress. Quitting smoking may help reduce stress levels. Smoking poses a risk to mental health and cardiac health, as it has been reported to cause coronary heart disease and stroke. 30 No significant differences were observed in sleep duration between the groups with and without depressive symptoms. In 2020, a cross-sectional study in Greece showed that individuals without depression had a significantly longer sleep duration of 6:24 (SD 1:09) than those with depression, who had a sleep duration of 6:05 (SD 1:22). 8 Additionally, a 2019 longitudinal study concluded that shorter sleep duration was associated with an increased risk of depression among high school adolescents. 31 However, some studies have identified no association between sleep duration and the risk of depression. 32 , 34 One reason for these discrepancies could be the use of self-report questionnaires for sleep duration, which should be acknowledged as a limitation in large-scale questionnaire surveys. Consequently, the relationship between short sleep duration and the risk of depression warrants further investigation. The analysis of the PSQI-J subitems revealed that sleep quality, sleep onset latency, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medications and daytime dysfunction were all poorer in young Japanese women with depressive symptoms. Logistic regression analysis showed that sleep onset latency and daytime dysfunction were factors associated with depressive symptoms. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting an association between poor sleep quality and increased risk of depression using the PSQI. 35 36 However, the current literature shows an unclear association between specific aspects of sleep quality and the risk of depression. For instance, Vorvolakos et al 8 found an association between the risk of depression and difficulties in maintaining sleep and early morning awakening, but not with difficulty in initiating sleep. Jaussent et al 37 demonstrated that difficulties initiating and maintaining sleep, rather than early morning awakening, predicted the onset of future depressive symptoms. Yokoyama et al reported that the risk of depression was only related to difficulty in initiating sleep and not to difficulty in maintaining sleep or early morning awakening. 32 Additionally, Almeida et al 38 found that only difficulty in falling asleep was associated with depression, and maintaining sleep and early morning awakening did not predict the onset of depression later in life. A study by Morita 39 involving young Japanese adults aged 19–25 years found that a delayed sleep–wake schedule, daytime sleepiness and difficulty in initiating sleep were associated with depression, consistent with our results. The discrepancies in these findings may be attributed to differences in race, age and assessment indices used to evaluate sleep quality. Our results could characterise the association between sleep quality and the risk of depression in young Japanese women. In the ‘National Health and Nutrition Survey’ conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare in 2019, the highest percentage of Japanese men and women aged 20–29 identified ‘engaging in activities such as using mobile phones, checking emails, or playing games before bedtime’ as a hindrance to ensuring their sleep, at 43.2% and 42.7%, respectively. 21 The blue light emitted by cell phones and television has been linked to sleep disorders and may contribute to poor sleep quality, including sleep onset latency and daytime dysfunction. 40 While reducing blue light exposure at night is considered the most effective approach, it can be challenging to recommend this strategy to young individuals living in today’s information-driven society. A 2016 systematic review suggested that increasing physical activity during the day may improve night-time sleep. 41 Furthermore, a study in the USA involving 3081 adults revealed that individuals who engaged in daytime exercise were 35% less likely to experience excessive sleepiness and 45% less likely to have difficulty in concentrating when tired than those who did not engage in exercise. 42 Implementing strategies to address sleep onset latency and daytime dysfunction, such as reducing blue light exposure at night and increasing physical activity during the day, may reduce the risk of depression. This study showed that the presence or absence of depressive symptoms in young Japanese women was associated with a high smoking rate, but not with the presence or absence of insomnia. Nicotine is the main psychoactive substance in cigarettes, and it has the effect of regulating and enhancing emotions. It has been reported that this effect causes a temporary decrease in depressive symptoms, leading to an increased rate of smoking among people with depressive symptoms. 43 44 Furthermore, it has also been reported that smoking can trigger depressive symptoms, 45 and the relationship between depressive symptoms and smoking is a strong interaction in both directions, and these results are considered to be the same for Japanese people. On the other hand, while previous studies have reported that smoking habits increase the risk of developing insomnia and that insomnia can lead to the initiation of smoking behaviour, 46 this survey showed a tendency to deny the relationship between smoking and insomnia. In a previous study of approximately 10 000 Japanese people, the factors contributing to insomnia among Japanese people were shown to include a lack of exercise, skipping breakfast and a state of obesity, but smoking was not shown to be related to insomnia among Japanese people. 47 This study was conducted only among Japanese people, leading to the result that no relationship was found between smoking and insomnia. This study had some limitations. First, the depressive symptoms status was not clinically determined; instead, we relied on the previously validated 12 PHQ-2 screening tool. Second, potential recall bias might have occurred because of self-reported sleep characteristics. However, self-reported sleep assessments using the PSQI have been reported to be reliable even in non-clinical populations. 48 Third, there was a risk of sampling bias because the survey was shared with colleagues at the university and organisation using snowball sampling. Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study did not allow for a longitudinal examination of depressive symptoms occurrence and sleep habits. Future research should address these limitations and investigate the long-term impacts of sleep-related behaviours on depressive symptoms in a larger population of young Japanese women. Conclusion To the best of our knowledge, this is the first web-based cross-sectional study that specifically targeted young Japanese women. Our findings suggest that while the presence of depressive symptoms may not directly impact sleep duration, a decline in sleep quality, as assessed by the PSQI-J, does. Furthermore, long sleep onset latency and daytime dysfunction may indicate early stages of depression.
