Abstract Backgrounds Fear of falling (FoF) is characterized by persistent concerns about falling, and empirical investigations are needed to examine the influence of perceived FoF on the broader and more active dimensions of social functioning. This study aims to explore the association between FoF and social participation in older adults, as well as the effects of changes in status over time. Methods This longitudinal panel study analyzed data from eight waves (2006–2020) of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging, comprising 8,632 participants aged 60 years and older (3,735 males and 4,897 females). FoF and activity restriction were assessed, with social engagement defined as involvement in at least one formal social activity, and the frequency of participation was also measured. Additionally, these statuses were categorized to reflect changes from the previous wave. Generalized estimating equation models were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Results Over a mean follow-up period of 8.94 years, the average level of social engagement, accounting for variations across all waves, was 73.1% among males and 66.5% among females. Compared to the no FoF group, individuals who had FoF exhibited significantly lower likelihoods of engaging in formal social activity in males (OR: 0.77, CI: 0.67–0.87) and females (OR: 0.84, CI: 0.75–0.94), while those with both FoF and activity restriction exhibited even lower odds of engagement (males, OR: 0.53, CI: 0.44–0.63; females, OR: 0.72, CI: 0.63–0.83). Additionally, FoF is associated with a tendency to impede newly initiated or ongoing social engagements, with more pronounced effects observed in individuals who reported persistent FoF over time. Conclusions FoF is associated with reduced social engagement in older adults, and therefore, individuals with FoF may require special attention to prevent negative health outcomes resulting from social isolation caused by FoF. Introduction Falls are a major global public health concern, causing approximately 684,000 fatalities annually, and are the second leading cause of unintentional injury-related deaths [ 1 ]. Fear of falling (FoF), often used to refer to a generalized concern about falling, is an emotional response to a real or perceived threat to one’s balance and may occur without prior falls [ 2 ]. Some individuals with FoF avoid activities due to their fear, which can lead to adverse behavioral outcomes such as overly cautious movements and unnecessary activity restriction [ 2 ]. The prevalence of FoF among community-dwelling older adults varies widely (21–85%) [ 3 ]; in South Korea, where one-quarter of older adults experience falls, about 80% report FoF [ 4 ]. With the global aging trend including South Korea, FoF is expected to affect more older adults in the future. Depending on its intensity, FoF can be adaptive; however, excessive fear may lead individuals to become overly cautious in their environment, resulting in activity restriction [ 5 ]. Prolonged restriction may contribute to physical deconditioning, muscle atrophy, and postural instability, ultimately increasing the risk of subsequent falls [ 6 , 7 ]. FoF has also been associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including social isolation, reduced life satisfaction, and depressive symptoms, suggesting its potential negative impact on the social dimension [ 8 , 9 ]. Nevertheless, FoF does not necessarily lead to activity restriction [ 10 ], underscoring the need for empirical investigations into its potential effects on more complex activities [ 11 ]. While the adverse consequences of FoF are well documented, most research has focused on physical and psychological domains [ 12 , 13 ], with relatively little attention to social participation. Even when considered, social participation is often subsumed under broader measures such as activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), rather than being examined as a distinct construct [ 14 ]. Moreover, FoF is subject to temporal fluctuations [ 15 ], implying that analyses of its associations must account for this variability, thereby emphasizing the necessity for longitudinal research to complement previous cross-sectional findings [ 3 ]. Meanwhile, the World Health Organization (WHO) defines healthy ageing as the process of developing and maintaining the functional capability that enables well-being in age [ 16 ]. This functional capacity encompasses active social participation, which may be hindered by FoF and its related psychological factors, presenting barriers to healthy aging [ 17 , 18 ]. Despite complex interrelations, social engagement is strongly associated with improved health outcomes [ 19 ]. One defining trait of FoF is the potential for self-imposed restriction of activity despite sufficient physical ability, leading to negative health outcomes. This modifiability suggests the opportunity of combining social engagement with physical activity interventions [ 20 , 21 ], as social engagement can reduce barriers to an active lifestyle by enhancing social support and motivation [ 22 , 23 ]. Therefore, it is imperative to explore the practical relationship between FoF and social engagement in the context of healthy aging, considering geographic and temporal variations as well as key risk factors such as gender, age, physical impairments, depressive symptoms, and fall history [ 3 , 24 ]. This study aims to investigate the impact of FoF on the actual social engagement of older adults, accounting for temporal variability. 2. Methods 2.1. Data and participants This study used data from eight waves of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), conducted biennially from 2006 to 2020. KLoSA investigates various aspects of aging among Korean adults, with participants aged 45 and older selected through multistage stratified probability sampling based on geography and housing type. KLoSA covers various topics, including demographic trends, labor force participation, income and asset status, consumer spending, retirement planning, social welfare impacts, health outcomes, and intergenerational income transfers [ 25 ]. The inclusion and exclusion process for study participants is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1 . Since the effects of FoF in older adults and the implications of social activities due to employment may differ, the study focused on individuals aged 60 and above, which aligns with the typical retirement age in South Korea. Accordingly, of the initially available 11,174 individuals (63,215 observations), after excluding the value of the waves under the age of 60 (20,710 observations) and those with missing data for any of the variables used in this study (3,930 observations), a total of 8,632 individuals (38,576 observations) were included. Among them, 3,735 were male and 4,897 were female participants and their average age was 71.7 years (SD = 8.01). 2.2. Variables 2.2.1. Social engagement The outcome variable, social engagement, was assessed by a question allowing for multiple answers: “Are you currently engaged in any of the listed groups?” The listed formal social groups included: (1) religious organizations; (2) social meetings with friends or neighbors; (3) leisure, culture, or sports clubs; (4) family or school reunions; (5) voluntary groups; (6) political organizations; and (7) other organizations. Engagement in social activities was defined as involvement in any of the aforementioned formal social groups: yes or no. For subgroup, a score was assigned based on the frequency of formal social engagement per group category: 3 (high: “almost every day,” “2–3 times in a week”), 2 (moderate: “1 time a week.” “2 times a month”), or 1 (low: “1 time in a month,” “1–2/3–4/5–6 times in a year,” “barely active in a year,” “barely active”) [ 26 ]. Following this, the sum of the social engagement frequency score was classified into 5 groups, accounting for plural engagement, with higher scores indicating more active engagement: Over 4, 3, 2, 1, or 0 (no social engagement). Additionally, using the question “Do you have any close friends, family, or neighbors? How often do you get together with them?”, the frequency of informal social contact was scored equivalently, and the score of formal social engagements was added to classify groups: Over 4, 3, 2, 1, or 0 (no social engagement). 2.2.2. Fear of falling (FoF) FoF was defined using two questions to categorize a variable into three distinct categories: “Do you usually worry about falling?” with response options were “not worried at all”, “a little worried”, and “very worried”. “Are there activities that you would like to do or typically engage in, but avoid because of a fear of falling?” with response option were “yes”, or “no”. Individuals who responded, “a little worried” and “very worried” to the FoF question for the wave and were categorized as having FoF. In each wave, respondents who responded "a little worried" or "very worried" to the FoF question and answered "no" to the FoF with activity restriction question were categorized as only FoF, while those who answered "yes" were categorized as FoF with (activity) restriction. Individuals who did not fit into either of the two FoF categories were allocated to the no FoF group. 2.2.3. 2-year changes of social engagement and FoF To further examine changes in status categories over time and the associated probabilities, the responses from the preceding two years corresponding to the survey periods were re-categorized based on the values from the reference wave. Only individuals who responded at least twice were included, the transitions in FoF were classified into four groups: (1) none: two consecutive years of no FoF, (2) developed: transitioning from no FoF to FoF two years prior, (3) disappeared: transitioning from FoF to no FoF two years prior, and (4) persistent FoF: two consecutive years of FoF. In a similar manner, social engagement was also classified into four groups: (1) continuous disengagement: two consecutive years of disengagement, (2) newly engaged: transitioning from disengagement to engagement two years prior, (3) newly disengaged: transitioning from engagement to disengagement two years prior, and (4) continuous engagement: two consecutive years of engagement. 2.2.4. Covariates Sociodemographic and health-related factors were controlled as time-varying covariates, which were determined based on the model selection approach and other studies on FoF [ 9 , 27 ]: Age, household income quartile, education level, area of residence, type of residence, marital status, body mass index (BMI), limitations in ADL (dressing, washing, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed, going to the toilet, and controlling excrement and urine) and IADL (grooming, doing household chores, preparing a meal, doing the laundry, going out for a short distance, using transportation, shopping, managing financial matters, making/receiving phone calls, and taking medications), depressive symptoms using the Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression 10-item (CES-D 10) scale [ 28 ], fall experiences during the past two year, the number of chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, cancer or malignant tumors, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, psychiatric disease, arthritis, and rheumatism), physical activity exceeded 150 min per week [ 29 ], informal social contact (at least once in a month) [ 30 ], and year (eight waves). 2.3. Statistical analyses Given the potential sex differences influenced by FoF [ 31 , 32 ], and as indicated by p-values for interaction in our sample (0.3152 for "only FoF" and 0.0003 for "FoF with restriction," using the no FoF group as reference), all statistical analyses were conducted stratified by sex. For descriptive analyses, we investigated the distributions of the first wave (2006) study participants’ general characteristics according to the social engagement. Chi-square tests were performed to examine their associations. To analyze the longitudinal survey data, which may include correlations within subjects resulting from repeated measurements, we employed a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model with the logit link function to estimate the population-averaged effects of FoF on the binary outcome of social engagement, adjusting for all covariates. The covariance structure was selected as an independence correlation matrix based on the smallest Quasi-likelihood under the Independence model Criterion (QIC). We examined the association between FoF and social engagement, including the two-year changes in FoF. Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted with stratified covariates (independent variables), and p-values for interaction were provided to test for categorical differences. Two multinomial variables based on social engagement scores were used to explore the trend in the magnitude of associations across increasing categories. In cases where informal social contact was used in the score calculation, it was excluded from the adjustments in the model. In all models, the longitudinal weights of the panel data were applied, and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to express the effect sizes. A two-sided p &lt; .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4M7 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). 3. Results 3.1. Descriptive analyses Table 1 shows the general characteristics of the study population at the initial assessment in 2006. At the baseline, 2,328 of the 5,415 participants were males and 3,087 were females with 67.4% of males and 61.4% of females reporting formal social activity engagement. Additionally, the proportion of social engagement was 76.9% for male and 70.0% for female participants with informal social contact were present, compared to 28.6% for male and 23.8% for female participants without informal social contact. The results of the chi-square test between FoF and social engagement were significant in both males and females ( p &lt; .0001). 3.2. Association between FoF and social engagement Table 2 presents the results of the GEE analysis of FoF and social engagement. Compared to the no FoF group, older adults with only FoF exhibited significantly lower odds of engaging in formal social activities (males, OR: 0.77, CI: 0.67–0.87; females, OR: 0.84, CI: 0.75–0.94), while those with both FoF and activity restriction exhibited even lower odds of engagement (males, OR: 0.53, CI: 0.44–0.63; females, OR: 0.72, CI: 0.63–0.83). 3.3. Association between FoF and social engagement by change category Table 4 presents the results of analysis between FoF and four categories stratified by social engagement change. Compared to the no-FoF group, both the only FoF and FoF with restriction groups exhibited significantly lower odds of newly engaging in and continuing social engagement for both sexes. However, there were no significant differences in the odds of newly disengaging from social activity across the groups. Table 5 presents the results of analysis between stratified by FoF change and social engagement. Compared to the group with no-FoF that remained unchanged, those who persistently experienced FoF exhibited significantly lower odds of social engagement for both sexes. Similarly, groups with newly developed or disappeared FoF also showed reduced odds of engagement, although this finding was not statistically significant for males. 3.4. Additional analyses Table 3 presents the subgroup analysis stratified by independent variables. Individuals with a history of falls in the previous two years showed no significant differences in social engagement between the only FoF group and the FoF with restriction group compared to the no-FoF group. On the other hand, effect sizes were intensified in specific subgroups: among males with depressive symptoms, among individuals of both sexes with multiple chronic diseases, and among those lacking informal social contact. Fig. 1 displays the analysis stratified by social engagement frequency score and score combined with informal social contact. Compared to no-FoF population and 0 score, only FoF and FoF with restriction groups showed lower odds across all formal social engagement frequency scores in both sexes. However, when reconstructing social engagement frequency scores by combining informal social contacts, the only FoF group demonstrated a decreasing trend of odds as the score increased for both sexes: (males [1, OR: 0.94, CI: 0.75–0.92; 2, OR: 0.73, CI: 0.65–0.82; 3, OR: 0.71, CI: 0.62–0.81; over 4, OR: 0.71, CI: 0.61–0.83] and females [1, OR: 0.78, CI: 0.71–0.85; 2, OR: 0.72, CI: 0.65–0.80; 3, OR: 0.78, CI: 0.70–0.87; over 4, OR: 0.70, CI: 0.61–0.80]). 4. Discussion Individuals with FoF were less likely to engage in social activities compared to those without FoF, and this association was apparent in the group reporting activity restriction among both males and females aged 60 and over. FoF is associated with reduced initiation and maintenance of social engagement, particularly among individuals who report persistent fear over time. The association was also stronger with the rising frequency of social engagement, especially when considering informal social contacts such as family and friends. A previous study based on the U.S. population reported an association between FoF and reduced social participation, which is consistent with the findings of the present study [ 14 ]. However, in that study, no significant association was observed among individuals with FoF who did not report activity restriction. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in the prevalence of FoF across populations, the possibility that a two-year interval was insufficient to capture variability in social engagement, or the combined analysis of males and females. Other previous research has highlighted constraints and the avoidance of both physical and social activities as consequences of FoF, implying the decline in social participants [ 33 , 34 ]. The restriction of daily activities induced by FoF may stem from the repercussions of past falls or diminished physical capabilities [ 35 ]. Furthermore, a decline in mental health, exemplified by symptoms of depression, could also be associated with FoF, creating a barrier that obstructs active social engagement [ 35 ]. On the other hand, a longitudinal examination involving Mexican Americans revealed that the impact of social support on the new onset of FoF in older adults could vary depending on whether the source of support was friends or family [ 15 ]. However, when fully adjusting for various health-related variables, the results were not statistically significant, suggesting that the direction of this correlation may be induced by FoF [ 15 ]. Additionally, according to a cross-sectional study, those with FoF had lower levels of social support, but there was no difference between those who had both activity restriction and FoF [ 35 ]. This finding contrasts with the results of the current study, possibly due to differences in the participants' living environments or the temporal variability of FoF and social engagement over time. It follows that a time gap may be involved in the manifestation of this association. In this study, changes in the state of FoF were associated with lower levels of social engagement, although the effect size was less than for persistent FoF. Especially the impact of FoF was not observed in terms of withdrawing from existing social activities; however, differences were detected in behaviors related to maintaining ongoing activities or engaging in new social activities. It might be due to the re-establishing of social activities disrupted by FoF requiring considerable time and effort to form interpersonal relationships, which could lead to abandoning efforts to participate in social activities, particularly in old age [ 36 ]. The association for adult males intensified with greater frequency of social activity, especially when combined with contacts in close relationships. This may be because, in older age, social activities are influenced not only by formal groups but also by informal ones [ 37 ]. The differences between males and females may arise from the tendency of females to form closer social networks [ 38 , 39 ], or the possibility that females are more likely to be active as their participation in social activities increases, in contrast to males [ 40 ]. Additionally, males with depressive symptoms exhibited a stronger association, which might be attributed to their heightened sensitivity to the relationship between health and social engagement [ 41 ]. Compared to females, males who form smaller social networks can be more vulnerable to the prevalence and onset of depression [ 42 ]. Furthermore, several studies have reported an association between FoF and chronic diseases [ 43 ], which are also linked to lower levels of social engagement [ 44 ], thus providing a possible interpretation of the subgroup analysis results. This study has an advantage in that it employed nationally representative longitudinal data from South Korean older population while also taking into account the association of changes in FoF. Further investigation may be necessary to understand the progression of this association and to determine the exact directions, mediators, or confounding factors that influence it. Additionally, results from different contexts with varying prevalence of FoF or other FoF diagnostic tools may also be required. Although this study reveals important findings, it has several limitations. First, the KLoSA dataset relies on self-reported information [ 25 ], which may introduce recall bias, and the two-year interval between surveys may be too long to capture associations sensitively. Second, it could be difficult to generalize the findings to settings outside of South Korea due to the wide variations in the prevalence of FoF and the potential for social interaction effects to vary throughout communities. Third, despite utilizing longitudinal panel data for our investigation, establishing a conclusive causal link between FoF and social participation remains challenging. Fourth, in this study, a direct question regarding the presence of FoF was used, which may raise concerns similar to those related to the sensitivity of a single-item evaluation of FoF. While this methodological approach is practical—particularly due to its brevity and demonstrated test–retest reliability—it may nonetheless limit the ability to fully capture the multidimensional nature and impact of FoF on social engagement [ 43 ]. Results of an analysis using other measuring tools, such as the Survey of Activities and FoF in the Elderly or Falls Efficacy Scale, might differ. Finally, there may be unaddressed residual confounding factors associated with the variables. 5. Conclusions This study observed a negative association between FoF and social engagement, including both formal and informal social activities, even among older adults without activity restrictions. Additionally, when considering changes in participants' conditions over time, individuals with FoF were less likely to engage in new social activities or maintain existing ones. Therefore, this FoF population may require particular attention to prevent health deterioration caused by social isolation associated with FoF.
