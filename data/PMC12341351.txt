Abstract Purpose This study investigated spontaneous locomotor activity and metabolic phenotype in animal models of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and major depressive disorder (MDD), with a focus on motivation to engage in voluntary exercise. Methods Spontaneous locomotion, voluntary wheel running, and metabolic phenotypes were assessed in Shank3B -knockout mice (ASD model) and stress-susceptible mice exposed to chronic restraint stress (CRST SUS , MDD model) using indirect calorimetry and behavioral tests. Results Shank3B -knockout mice exhibited self-injurious repetitive behaviors resulting in skin lesions, while CRST SUS mice showed behavioral despair indicative of stress vulnerability, along with a marked reduction in spontaneous locomotor activity and decreased motivation for voluntary exercise. Metabolic dysregulation was evident, including alterations in oxygen consumption, carbon dioxide production, respiratory exchange ratio, and energy expenditure. Conclusions Behavioral and metabolic alterations in psychiatric disorders are closely linked, with reduced motivation for exercise emerging as a salient phenotypic signature. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions that restore intrinsic motivation and energy balance. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms and developing therapies to enhance physical activity engagement in psychiatric conditions. INTRODUCTION The coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic has profoundly reshaped global mental health landscapes, exposing significant vulnerabilities within existing support systems and highlighting the widespread psychological distress present in modern societies [ 1 ]. The pandemic underscored systemic limitations in addressing chronic neuropsychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), major depressive disorder (MDD), and anxiety disorders, all of which compromise individuals’ well-being and impose considerable socioeconomic burdens [ 2 ]. There is growing societal demand for a reassessment of national mental health policies and the development of effective countermeasures against the persistent neglect of mental health issues, which were often overshadowed by the successful control of disease transmission and the restoration of daily life. Mental illnesses can often be identified early and managed effectively with appropriate pharmacological interventions. However, the inherent heterogeneity of these conditions and the lack of a clear understanding of the mechanisms underlying behavioral disorders continue to pose substantial challenges to definitive treatment [ 3 , 4 ]. Depression and anxiety are rooted in monoamine deficiency and dysfunction within the brain. The primary therapeutic agents commonly used are selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, and monoamine oxidase inhibitors [ 5 , 6 ]. In ASD, besides genetic factors, reduced neuroplasticity and neurotransmitter imbalances contribute to specific behavioral symptoms. As a result, antidepressants, antipsychotics, and anticonvulsants have been proposed to address these symptoms [ 7 ]; nonetheless, such medications have significant limitations, including low remission rates, adverse side effects, and long-term safety concerns in the management of core behavioral disturbances and comorbid symptoms of mental illness [ 7 - 9 ]. From this perspective, current treatments do not fully address the multifaceted pathophysiology of mental illness. A comprehensive therapeutic approach should take into account hereditary factors and neurobiological abnormalities, such as metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, gut microbiome alterations, and changes in neuroplasticity [ 5 , 10 ]. Exercise is a nonpharmacological, multifaceted health maintenance intervention that provides an alternative to invasive treatments. Regular physical activity can alleviate symptoms of mental illness and may address underlying neurobiological abnormalities [ 3 , 10 , 11 ]. In particular, exercise promotes recovery and regeneration within the nervous system by modulating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and neurotransmitter balance (e.g., serotonin and dopamine), suppressing neuroinflammatory responses, reducing oxidative stress, improving mitochondrial function, and enhancing neuroplasticity through increased expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [ 3 , 10 - 12 ]. Extensive research in animal models of depression and ASD supports exercise as a safe and sustainable intervention that complements existing pharmacological treatments. Nevertheless, individuals with mental disorders frequently demonstrate lower levels of physical activity and voluntary exercise engagement [ 13 - 16 ], which appears to be closely associated with physical inactivity and metabolic dysfunction, ultimately contributing to the progression of pathophysiological deterioration. Increasing motivation for voluntary exercise may not only reduce the risk of developing such disorders but may also alleviate symptoms or even facilitate recovery in affected individuals. Voluntary wheel running (VWR) in an ASD model has been shown to restore synaptic integrity and improve both self-injurious repetitive behaviors and social behavioral deficits [ 17 , 18 ]. Furthermore, exercise effectively reduces depressive and anxiety-related behaviors by enhancing stress resilience [ 19 , 20 ]. However, previous studies have largely focused on the benefits of exercise, with limited analysis of spontaneous locomotor activity and voluntary exercise engagement. Additionally, the metabolic impact of variations in spontaneous locomotor activity levels remains insufficiently explored. To move beyond simple confirmation of exercise efficacy, we assessed metabolic changes alongside spontaneous locomotor activity and exercise volume in animal models of ASD and MDD. Our findings underscore the importance of exercise motivation as a potential therapeutic target and suggest that treatment strategies should integrate both behavioral engagement and underlying metabolic alterations. MATERIALS AND METHODS Animals Strain-matched Shank3B knockout (KO) mice ( Shank3B −/− ), which display excessive grooming and self-injurious repetitive behaviors, and C57BL/6J mice susceptible to stress, were used to investigate metabolic alterations along with spontaneous locomotion and exercise in ASD and MDD. In male Shank3B −/− transgenic mice (Strain #017688; Jackson Laboratory, USA) used for the ASD model, the PDZ domain (Axon 13–16) of the SH3/ankyrin domain 3 (Shank3) gene is replaced with a neomycin resistance (neo) cassette. These mice are crossed with wild-type (WT) mice for heterozygous breeding. Homozygous Shank3B −/− (Shank3-KO) mice (n =6) and WT littermates (n=6), aged 15–16 weeks and weighing 24–28 g, were used in experiments. The primer sequences used for genotyping were as follows: WT and mutant alleles, 5´-TGACATAATCGCTGGCAAAG-3´ and 5´-GCTATACGAAGTTATGTCGACTAGG-3´; common allele, 5´-GAGACTGATCAGCGCAGTTG-3´ Animals were housed in a pathogen-free environment with controlled conditions (12-hour:12-hour light-dark cycle, 20°C ±2°C, 50%–55% humidity), with ad libitum access to food (SAFE ® R04-10, Aston Pharma, England) and water. Chronic Restraint Stress Chronic restraint stress (CRST) was administered as previously described [ 21 ]. Briefly, 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice weighing 23–25 g were individually placed in 50-mL polyethylene tubes with adequate ventilation, inducing immobilization for 2 hours daily (from 10 AM to 12 PM) for 14 consecutive days. A total of 16 mice were used, with 8 in the CRST group and 8 in the sedentary (SED) control group. After each CRST session, mice were returned to their home cages and allowed free access to their standard diet and water. Identification of Individual Mice Susceptible and Resilient to Stress Mice were classified as susceptible or resilient to stress based on immobility times measured in the tail suspension test (TST) and forced swim test (FST) following CRST [ 11 , 21 ]. Individual behavioral test scores were converted to z-scores (z =[x–μ]/σ), reflecting the deviation of each score from the group mean in standard deviation units. Z-scores were analyzed using the K-means clustering method, an unsupervised machine learning algorithm that groups data points based on similarity. Mice grouped in the cluster with a centroid closest to the SED control dataset were classified as “resilient,” whereas those grouped in the cluster most distant from the SED dataset were identified as “susceptible” ( Fig. 1 ). Behavioral Test Behavioral assessments were performed as described previously. Test results were video recorded using an automated tracking system (SMART, Panlab, Spain) or manually scored. Before testing, mice were acclimated to the testing room. All apparatus components were cleaned with 70% ethanol and thoroughly dried between trials to eliminate olfactory cues before introducing the next subject. Self-grooming test Male homozygous Shank3B -KO and WT mice (15–16 weeks old) underwent the self-grooming test [ 22 ]. After a 20-minute habituation period, each mouse was placed in an acrylic cage (20 × 28 × 13 cm), and behavior was video recorded for 10 minutes under low-light conditions (20 lux). Grooming behavior was evaluated from 11 PM to 12 AM during the dark cycle. All grooming sequences —including face wiping, scratching/rubbing of the head, ears, and tail, and whole-body grooming using the paws and tongue —were manually recorded for both duration and frequency. Tail suspension test The TST was performed as previously described [ 11 , 21 ]. Mice were suspended by adhesive tape attached to the tip of the tail, 70 cm above the floor. During a 6-minute session, cumulative immobility time was measured over the last 5 minutes, after excluding the initial minute of excessive struggling. Immobility, indicative of behavioral despair—a core symptom of depression—was defined as the period when mice ceased struggling and remained completely motionless, except for minimal movements necessary for respiration. Forced swim test The FST was conducted as previously reported [ 11 , 21 ], without a pre-exposure session. Each mouse was placed in an inescapable transparent cylinder (15-cm diameter ×27-cm height) filled with tap water at 23°C–25°C for 6 minutes. The first minute was excluded to account for initial struggling, and cumulative immobility time (minimal movement to keep the head above water) was measured during the remaining 5 minutes. Escape behaviors, such as swimming (horizontal movement) and climbing (upward forepaw movement), were excluded from analysis. Measurement of Spontaneous Locomotor Activity and Basal Calorimetry Indirect calorimetry was performed to assess metabolic phenotype [ 23 ]. To minimize stress and confounding variables, mice were acclimated for 72 hours in an airtight metabolic cage connected to an indirect calorimetry system (Phenomaster, TSE Systems, Germany) prior to measurements. O₂ consumption, CO₂ production (VCO₂/VO₂), respiratory exchange ratio (RER), energy expenditure (EE), spontaneous locomotor activity (measured by laser beam breaks), and VWR were automatically recorded over 24 hours while mice had unrestricted access to food and water. The sample flow rate in the cage was 0.35 L/min, and a reference cage was used for air calibration. Statistical Analysis Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Prism Inc., USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 29.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between 2 groups were made using the 2-tailed Student t-test. For data that were not normally distributed, the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U-test was employed. K-means clustering was applied to classify behavioral characteristics in stressed mice into 2 clusters: stress-susceptible (CRST SUS ) and resilient (CRST RES ). Clusters were represented by their centroids, defined as the mean of all points within the cluster. Data are presented as mean ±standard error of the mean, and statistical significance was defined as P &lt; 0.05. RESULTS Shank3B -KO Mice Displaying Excessive and Repetitive Self-Injurious Autistic-Like Behavior Shank3B -KO mice exhibited significantly increased repetitive self-injurious grooming behaviors, leading to pronounced skin lesions. Specifically, these mice demonstrated a higher frequency and prolonged duration of grooming actions, including face wiping, scratching or rubbing of the head, ears, and tail, as well as whole-body grooming with the paws and tongue, compared with WT mice (all, P &lt;0.01) ( Fig. 2B-D ). These behavioral alterations are attributable to the deletion of the Shank3B gene ( Fig. 2A ) [ 22 , 24 ]. Identification of Mice Resilient or Susceptible to Stress Using Multifaceted Behavior Analysis C57BL/6J (naïve) mice subjected to CRST exhibited significantly increased immobility times —indicative of behavioral despair—in the TST (P &lt;0.01) and FST (P &lt; 0.001) compared to naïve control mice ( Fig. 1B and C ). Behavioral traits of individual mice, converted into standardized z-scores, were plotted in a 2-dimensional matrix incorporating both TST and FST scores ( Fig. 1D ). K-means clustering analysis identified 2 distinct clusters: stress-susceptible (CRST SUS ) and stress-resilient (CRST RES ) groups ( Fig. 1D ). The majority of mice exposed to CRST were classified as stress-susceptible (approximately 75% [6 of 8]), while a subset demonstrated stress resilience (approximately 25% [2 of 8]), as shown in Fig. 1D and E . Accompaniment of Psychiatric Conditions by Altered Spontaneous Locomotor Activity and Basal Metabolic Phenotypes To characterize the basal metabolic phenotypes of Shank3B -KO and CRST SUS mice, we measured spontaneous locomotor activity (general activity and VWR) using basal calorimetry within their breeding environments ( Figs. 3 and 4 ). Without disrupting circadian rhythms, Shank3B -KO mice displayed reduced locomotor activity and VWR over a 24-hour period compared to WT mice. Notably, during the active phase (dark cycle), Shank3B -KO mice exhibited a marked decrease in spontaneous locomotor activity (P &lt; 0.01) relative to WT mice, suggesting an overall lower level of physical activity ( Fig. 3A , right panel). While VWR activity in Shank3B -KO mice was also lower than in WT controls, this difference did not reach statistical significance ( Fig. 3B ). Furthermore, indirect calorimetry revealed no significant differences between groups in O₂ consumption, CO₂ production, RER, or EE during the active phase, despite lower locomotor activity and VWR in the Shank3B -KO mice ( Fig. 3C–F ). In contrast, during the inactive phase (light cycle), Shank3B -KO mice exhibited unexpected metabolic alterations, including significantly higher O₂ consumption (P &lt; 0.001), CO₂ production (P &lt; 0.001), and EE (P &lt; 0.001) ( Fig. 3C, D, and F ). A similar pattern was observed in CRST SUS mice, which tended to show reduced spontaneous locomotor activity during the active phase (dark cycle) compared to SED controls, although this difference was not statistically significant ( Fig. 4A , right panel). However, VWR activity was significantly reduced during the active phase (P&lt;0.001; Fig. 4B , right panel), indicating decreased voluntary exercise in stress-susceptible mice. Significant alterations in basal metabolic rate were also associated with these behavioral phenotypes. Specifically, CRST SUS mice exhibited significantly lower O₂ consumption (light cycle, P &lt;0.01; dark cycle, P&lt;0.01), CO₂ production (dark cycle, P&lt;0.01), and EE (light cycle, P &lt;0.05; dark cycle, P &lt;0.01) in both inactive and active phases compared to SED mice ( Fig. 4C, D, and F ). Additionally, a significant reduction in the RER was observed in CRST SUS mice (light cycle, P&lt;0.001; dark cycle, P&lt;0.001), indicating a stress-induced metabolic adaptation marked by a shift from carbohydrate oxidation toward increased fat oxidation. DISCUSSION This study demonstrated the relationship between motivation to engage in spontaneous locomotor activity and metabolic phenotype, providing insight into how exercise might be integrated into mental illness treatment. In both ASD and MDD models, reductions in spontaneous locomotor activity and voluntary exercise volume were observed alongside metabolic abnormalities. These findings suggest that physical inactivity and subsequent metabolic disturbances may contribute to disease progression. Mutations or deletions in the Shank3 gene are established contributors to neurodevelopmental disorders, particularly ASD and 22q13.3 deletion syndrome (Phelan-McDermid syndrome) [ 13 , 25 ]. Shank3 plays a crucial role in regulating neural functions linked to core ASD symptoms, such as social interaction, repetitive behaviors, and sensory processing [ 22 ]. In our study, Shank3B -KO mice exhibited reduced locomotor activity and avoidance of voluntary exercise compared to WTs, reflecting the deficits and exercise-avoidant behaviors reported in individuals with ASD. Previous research has reported a positive correlation between reduced locomotor activity in ASD, neurotransmitter imbalances, and behavioral deficits [ 13 ]. Therefore, the aberrant neural connectivity and impaired neuroplasticity seen in Shank3B -KO mice may contribute to diminished motivation for physical activity. Similarly, the significant reduction in locomotor activity during the active phase in Shank3B -KO mice suggests a potential link with neurobiological defects. CRST SUS mice also showed a significant reduction in VWR activity. These results are consistent with earlier findings that mice exposed to chronic stress display behavioral despair (as measured by TST and FST) and a concomitant reduction in physical activity [ 26 , 27 ]. This behavioral pattern is closely associated with exercise avoidance, apathy, and behavioral despair. Furthermore, stress-susceptible individuals may show decreased motivation for physical activity, representing a distinct pattern of reduced exercise motivation in the context of stress vulnerability [ 26 , 27 ]. Physical activity and voluntary exercise are closely tied to both mental health and basal metabolism, playing critical roles in the prevention and recovery from mental disorders [ 19 , 20 ]. In this study, Shank3B -KO and CRST SUS mice exhibited elevated metabolic rates during the inactive phase but reduced metabolic rates during the active phase relative to controls. Although it remains difficult to establish a definitive causal relationship between metabolic dysregulation and diminished physical activity or exercise engagement, our findings indicate a strong association between metabolic deficits and behavioral impairments in psychiatric conditions. This highlights the therapeutic potential of exercise-induced metabolic enhancement in mitigating mental disorders [ 28 ]. Metabolic abnormalities in ASD have been identified, with mitochondrial dysfunction and energy metabolism imbalance proposed as key pathological mechanisms [ 29 , 30 ]. These findings are consistent with reports of elevated metabolic rates and increased oxidative stress in individuals with ASD [ 31 ]. Chronic stress is also known to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and disrupt neuronal energy metabolism [ 32 ]. In this context, the elevated metabolic rates seen in Shank3B -KO and CRST SUS mice during the inactive phase, despite reduced locomotor activity and voluntary exercise, may reflect inefficiencies in energy metabolism. Collectively, our results emphasize that individuals with mental disorders are likely to exhibit reduced spontaneous locomotor activity and voluntary exercise engagement, which are closely linked to underlying neurobiological impairments, metabolic dysfunction, and maladaptive behavioral traits. As those with mental disorders often encounter difficulties in maintaining regular physical activity, tailored approaches —such as incorporating environmental reward systems (operant conditioning) and behavioral economic interventions —may enhance motivation for voluntary engagement [ 33 , 34 ]. Moreover, considering the neurobiological abnormalities present in depression and anxiety, integrating treatments that target both core and comorbid symptoms (e.g., adjusting exercise intensity) with physiological enhancers—such as BDNF-promoting agents or anti-inflammatory therapies — may provide greater therapeutic benefits [ 11 , 35 ]. This study has several limitations. First, the excessive and repetitive self-grooming behaviors frequently observed in Shank3B -KO mice may have influenced metabolic measurements [ 22 , 24 ]. The metabolic phenotyping system employed here is limited in its sensitivity to subtle movements, potentially introducing variability into the recorded data. Second, interindividual variations in spontaneous locomotor activity and VWR, inherent to both Shank3B -KO and CRST SUS mice, create challenges in experimental control. Future studies should employ more precise behavioral detection systems and include larger sample sizes to increase data reliability. This study is significant in that it comprehensively analyzed spontaneous locomotor activity and metabolic phenotypes in ASD and MDD models. Notably, the decline in exercise engagement is tightly linked to underlying biological and metabolic abnormalities, extending beyond mere behavioral deficits. Therefore, interventions for mental disorders should prioritize enhancing motivation, rather than simply advocating for increased exercise. Future research should further elucidate the mechanisms contributing to reduced exercise motivation by examining neurotransmitter alterations, inflammatory markers, and mitochondrial function. Additionally, human-based studies are needed to develop effective and sustainable intervention strategies to support exercise engagement.
