Objectives This study aims to provide a practical framework for Korean medicine (KM) researchers on effectively utilizing Korean National Health Insurance (NHI) data, focusing on database characteristics, access methods, and methodological considerations for generating high-quality evidence in KM research. Methods This study analyzed the NHI data system structure, analyzing its two major databases the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) and Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA). We examined available data types, access protocols, and methodological approaches, with particular emphasis on their application in KM research. The study synthesized current challenges and opportunities specific to KM research using these databases. Results Our analysis revealed three key findings 1) The distinct characteristics and complementary nature of NHIS and HIRA databases, with NHIS providing comprehensive longitudinal data suitable for cohort studies and HIRA offering detailed cross-sectional data optimal for utilization pattern analysis; 2) Multiple data access options including Sample Research DB and Customized Research DB from NHIS, and Patient Sample Data and Customized Research Data from HIRA, each with specific advantages for different research designs; 3) Critical methodological considerations for KM research, particularly addressing the limited coverage of KM services (0.8% of total covered items) and the high proportion of non-covered services in KM practices (24.2-49.2% across different institution types). Conclusion For effective utilization of NHI data in KM research, we recommend 1) Implementing hybrid research designs that combine health insurance data with institutional data to overcome limited KM coverage; 2) Establishing clear operational definitions and applying appropriate statistical methods for bias adjustment; 3) Working toward developing a specialized Korean Medicine Utilization Cohort Database. The Korean health insurance system, which was established in 1977, extended its coverage to Korean medicine (KM) services in 1987 and has continuously expanded its scope, albeit with limitations [ 2 , 3 ]. Therefore, health insurance data include information about KM service utilization for the entire population [ 3 ]. Although KM services account for a limited portion of the health insurance system, the academic community has shown significant interest in health insurance data for policy development, institutional improvement, and academic research given the comprehensive information on national healthcare utilization [ 3 ]. The Korean health insurance data are collected by a single insurer, the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), and the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA), which performs reviews of claims and quality assessment of healthcare services [ 4 ]. The data represent one of the primary sources of healthcare big data within the Korean healthcare system, including information on income and property, medical service utilization at healthcare institutions, and health examination data for the entire population [ 1 ]. Furthermore, with the revision and implementation of the “Personal Information Protection Act” in August 2020, data accessibility has been significantly improved. Prior to 2020, access to health insurance data was limited to public institutions performing their statutory duties and select researchers [ 5 ]. However, now all researchers can access health insurance data under institutional guidelines. In particular, to support policy and academic research using health insurance data, the NHIS operates the National Health Insurance Sharing Service (NHISS), and the HIRA operates the Healthcare Big Data System (HBiG), providing researchers with various forms of collected insurance-related data. Additionally, following the implementation of the government’s data opening policy, data linkage to other institutions has become possible owing to administrative procedures. Through the NHISS, which operates based on the National Health Information Database, the NHIS has supported 6,318 research projects from 2014 to November 2022 [ 6 ], and the HIRA has supported 1,088 research projects through the HBiG from 2015 to 2021 [ 7 ]. Research utilizing health insurance data is expected to continue increasing due to the Korean government’s data opening policy. The continuous increase in research using Korean health insurance data indicates the high academic value of the data, as demonstrated in various studies [ 8 ]. However, although researchers are often drawn to the abundance of health insurance data, they lack adequate comprehension of the data generation processes within the Korean health insurance system and its inherent characteristics, leading to the application of methodologies that fail to account for these fundamental aspects [ 9 ]. Therefore, this study aimed to provide KM practitioners and researchers with practical guidance on understanding and utilizing health insurance data effectively. This study makes several significant contributions to the field of KM research. First, it provides an essential methodological framework for conducting high-quality clinical research using health insurance data, which is particularly valuable for KM interventions where large-scale clinical evidence is needed. Second, it presents a detailed analysis of KM-specific features within health insurance databases, allowing researchers to better understand the scope and limitations of available data for treatment effectiveness studies. Third, it highlights critical methodological considerations specifically relevant to KM research, helping researchers design more robust studies. Finally, it provides actionable recommendations for improving research quality, which are particularly relevant for studies investigating the clinical effectiveness and utilization patterns of KM treatments. MATERIALS AND METHODS This study conducted a structured analysis of the Korean national health insurance system in three phases. First, we analyzed the structural characteristics of two major health insurance databases (NHIS and HIRA) by reviewing their data composition, collection processes, and coverage scope. Second, we examined the data access protocols and various research database options available through the NHISS and HBiG. Finally, we evaluated methodological considerations specific to KM research by analyzing current limitations, challenges, and potential solutions when utilizing these databases. We focused on identifying KM-specific features within the health insurance data structure, including coverage rates of KM services, patterns of non-covered services, and data accessibility issues. The analysis included assessment of both the Sample Research Database and Customized Research Database of the NHIS, as well as Patient Sample Data and Customized Research Data of the HIRA, which examined their strengths and limitations for KM research applications. CHARACTERISTICS AND UNDERSTANDING OF NATIONAL HEALTH INSURANCE DATA To utilize Korean health insurance data, it is important to understand the Korean health insurance system. Since 2000, the Korean health insurance system has been integrated into a single state-operated insurer, and most medical institutions are subject to the Mandatory Designation System (under Article 42 of the National Health Insurance Act) [ 10 ]. Healthcare providers are obligated to provide insurance-covered treatments, and the medical fee system primarily operates on a fee-for-service basis. Therefore, health insurance data previously held by multiple insurers before 2000 were integrated into the current NHIS. Furthermore, owing to the Mandatory Designation System, most medical institutions provide insurance-covered treatments, resulting in all medical utilization records (except for non-covered items and other insurance applications) being consolidated within the NHIS. Additionally, the NHIS maintains information on citizens’ income, property, workplace insurance status, and residence for insurance premium calculations. Although scholars may disagree about the efficiency of the Korean health insurance system (universal coverage, single insurer, mandatory designation, and fee-for-service system) from an institutional perspective, its features present unparalleled advantages from a data perspective [ 1 ]. The NHIS manages health insurance, long-term care insurance, and social insurance premium collection, maintaining approximately 3.9 trillion records of information on the entire population. This information includes qualification and premium collection data linked to 36 public institutions, patient medical utilization information, and health examination information comprising health behavior and measurement data. The HIRA also maintains medical treatment records for the entire population, excluding insurance premium data and health examination information [ 11 ]. Both the NHIS and HIRA can provide medical utilization records based on health insurance data. However, there are some differences in data composition and characteristics according to each institution’s establishment purpose. The NHIS, as an insurer, provides researchers with information on qualification and insurance premiums, medical utilization, health examination, and long-term care. Although the HIRA primarily provides data focused on medical treatment records, it additionally provides medical imaging data, supplementary pharmaceutical information, drug utilization review information, pharmaceutical distribution management information, appropriateness evaluation data for medical care benefits, and data on certain non-covered items based on user requirements [ 4 ]. TYPES AND CHARACTERISTICS OF HEALTH INSURANCE DATA As mentioned earlier, Korean health insurance data, derived from the national health insurance system, are collected and managed by both the NHIS and HIRA according to their respective roles and provided to researchers. The types, characteristics, and usage methods of research-oriented health insurance data provided by the NHIS and HIRA are described in this section. 1. Types and characteristics of health insurance data in the NHISS The NHIS has constructed and provided a separate database for analysis purposes, known as the National Health Information Database (729 tables, 599.7 billion records, 16.4 TB), which contains pseudonymized versions of all health insurance data accumulated during the operation of the national health insurance. The data include subscriber qualification and premium information, health examination records, medical treatment details submitted by healthcare providers, and medical institution information [ 12 ]. The National Health Information Database consists of qualification and premium, general medical statement, detailed medical statement, patient diagnosis, prescription details, healthcare institution, health examination, and long-term care-related tables ( Fig. 1 ). The qualification and premium tables include demographic information such as residence area and gender, along with health insurance premium information based on property and income levels. The tables containing medical utilization records (general medical statement, detailed medical statement, patient diagnosis, and prescription details tables) are connected through common keys in a one-to-many relationship (1:N). Specifically, general medical statement tables, organized according to the medical fee claim units of healthcare institutions, contain information such as treatment start date, medical department, healthcare institution, primary and secondary diagnoses, number of inpatient/outpatient days, and total medical fees after review. Detailed medical statement tables, connected in a one-to-many relationship, include detailed claim information for each medical service (consultation, prescription, examination, etc.), comprising unit price, daily dosage/frequency, total administration period/frequency, and amount. Patient diagnosis tables, also connected in a one-to-many relationship with general medical statement tables, contain all additional diagnoses not included in the general medical statement, including primary and secondary diagnoses. Prescription details tables contain information about external prescriptions and pharmacy utilization. Health examination tables consist of physical measurements, examination results, and questionnaire contents including lifestyle habits, medical history, and family history from primary and secondary health examinations. Healthcare institution tables include workforce and hospital room information for medical institution management. Long-term care insurance information, collected since the introduction of the long-term care insurance system in 2009, consists of the long-term care basic table (activities of daily living scores, certification survey scores, and grade determination for long-term care insurance beneficiaries), the long-term care benefit table (claim details for various types of long-term care benefits such as home visit care, bathing, and nursing), and information about institutions providing long-term care services. The National Health Information Database, constructed based on the operating system of the NHIS for research purposes, is presented in two main forms (Sample Research Database and Customized Research Database) according to the request of researchers. The Sample Research Database, which consists of data structured by theme, is created by extracting samples through stratified sampling or simple random sampling based on specific criteria. Currently, it is provided in three cohort forms (Sample Cohort, Health Examination Cohort, and Elderly Cohort), with their main characteristics summarized in Table 1 . Unlike the Sample Research Database, the Customized Research Database is processed and provides customized data according to the requirements of researchers. Data are available depending on the scope of health insurance data and are presented in a form that prevents the identification of individuals, corporations, and organizations. However, when research objectives cannot be achieved with health insurance data alone, data linkage to other institutions is possible through ‘Pseudonymization and Combination of Pseudonymized Information’ [ 13 ]. Although both Sample Research Database and Customized Research Database applications are open to government departments, public institutions, public research institutions, private companies, and individuals conducting academic research, policy research, or scientific research for public benefit, there are differences in usage methods. The Sample Research Database includes cause of death data from Statistics Korea, which are not included in the Customized Research Database; thus, if needed, separate data linkage applications must be made through the Microdata Integrated Service of Statistics Korea. Additionally, although both databases are accessed through authorized virtual rooms in the NHIS system, the Sample Research Database can be accessed through a Virtual Private Network connected to the remote research analysis system from personal computers. However, the Customized Research Database can only be accessed by visiting one of the 12 nationwide Big Data Analysis Centers operated by the NHIS. 2. Types and characteristics of health insurance data in the HBiG The HIRA has constructed separate databases for research purposes from data accumulated during the review of medical care benefit claims and evaluation of medical care appropriateness, including medical service information, pharmaceutical information, medical material information, medical resource information, healthcare quality evaluation information, and non-covered service information. Since 2015, these data have been provided to academic and industrial researchers through the HBiG [ 14 ]. The research-oriented health insurance data provided by the HIRA are similarly divided into two main forms (Patient Sample Data and Customized Research Data). Patient Sample Data, which consist of annual cross-sectional data on medical treatment records for a sample of national medical service users, are divided into Total Patient Dataset, Inpatient Dataset, Elderly Patient Dataset, and Pediatric Patient Dataset, as shown in Table 2 . Each dataset includes general statement details, medical treatment details, diagnosis details, and external prescription details recorded in medical care benefit claims. Customized Research Data consist of research data processed according to research purposes from medical care benefit claims data. The data can be constructed from January 2007 up to 8 months before the current point in time and are provided to researchers according to the conditions of diseases, medical procedures, and medications. The composition of the provided data is identical to that of the Patient Sample Data. Although data usage applications are similar to those for NHIS data, there are some differences in usage methods. For Customized Research Data linked to other data, cases where personal identification risk is deemed high, and cases where users belong to an industry, data can only be accessed by visiting one of the 14 nationwide Big Data Centers of the HIRA. Customized Research Data and Patient Sample Data not falling under these categories can be accessed through online analysis systems (Virtual PC, Virtual Desktop Infrastructure System) using virtual IDs allocated to each research project. EFFECTIVE METHODS FOR UTILIZING HEALTH INSURANCE DATA Korean health insurance data, comprising national medical utilization records, have demonstrated their value in various studies [ 8 ]. However, to derive accurate results aligned with research topics through more effective use of health insurance data, several points need to be considered. First, health insurance data only include medical utilization within the health insurance system and exclude non-covered services. Additionally, although both the NHIS and HIRA maintain and provide the same medical utilization records, there are some differences in the meaning of the data due to each institution’s role in the operation of the health insurance system. In the health insurance system, healthcare institutions claim payments through the HIRA for amounts excluding patient copayments after providing treatment. When the HIRA receives medical care benefit claims, it goes through various review stages and notifies the NHIS of the outcome, after which the NHIS pays the reviewed claims to healthcare institutions. Consequently, both organizations can provide researchers with reviewed medical treatment data. For the HIRA’s general medical statement data (200T), the total amount of reviewed medical care benefits equals the sum of each medical service in the detailed medical statement data (300T); however, for the NHIS’s general details (20T), the total amount of reviewed medical care benefits does not equal the sum of each medical service in the medical treatment records (30T) ( Table 3 ). This is because the NHIS’s medical treatment records (30T) do not reflect the claim adjustments of the HIRA for each medical service. However, as the reduction rate following a review by the HIRA was only around 1.0% in 2019 [ 15 ], the difference in statistical analysis results would be minimal for unrelated research topics. Nevertheless, caution is needed when calculating the amount for each medical service from the total medical expenses. Additionally, as health insurance data are claim-based, healthcare institutions can submit separate monthly claims for the same disease if a patient’s treatment period is long. Therefore, depending on the research topic, it may be necessary to apply the concept of “episode of care,” which measures a unit from disease onset to termination as one event. However, when restructuring claim-based health insurance data into episode-based data, careful consideration is needed in determining the start and end of treatment. Second, the primary diagnosis listed in health insurance claims data may differ from the actual disease as it could be a preliminary diagnosis based on patient complaints and symptoms before confirmation or the diagnosis code might have been modified (upcoded) by healthcare institutions to prevent claim reductions [ 16 ]. Third, clear operational definitions of analysis variables are necessary. If a specific disease is assumed to be an analysis variable, operational definitions need to be established by comprehensively considering various provided data items given that health insurance claims data might not represent accurate diagnoses. For chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, a potential approach would be to consider health examination data such as fasting blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood cholesterol levels or medication history information based on the detailed medical statement (such as main ingredient codes). Fourth, when using NHIS data for case-control studies within cohorts, the research design needs to reflect differences between exposed groups (cases) and unexposed groups (controls). Particularly in exposure-matched cohort studies, when cohort-type unexposed subjects are too large in number or practically inaccessible for analysis, matching with similar unexposed subjects is carried out at approximately 1-10 times the number of exposed subjects based on demographic factors, health examinations, and comorbidities [ 9 ]. Although this research methodology is frequently applied in retrospective cohort studies using NHIS data, when selecting groups in case-control studies within cohorts or exposure-matched cohort studies, confounding bias, information bias, and publication bias may occur, necessitating adjustment between groups. This is because failure to control for confounding factors can lead to an incomplete experimental design, potentially biasing causal inference [ 17 ]. However, according to Lim et al. [ 9 ], 81.4% of all reviewed papers using health insurance data did not design a research with bias adjustment; notably, the rates of non-application of bias adjustment were 31.7%, 84.1%, and 94.4% in exposure-matched cohort studies, case-control studies within cohorts, and case-control studies, respectively, for which bias adjustment is particularly important. The study emphasized the necessity and importance of bias adjustment and suggested methods such as propensity score matching and landmark analysis [ 9 ]. Fifth, when using health insurance claims data, interpretation should consider both statistical and clinical significance as there is a high possibility of obtaining statistically significant results due to the large sample size effect, even when not actually statistically significant. For instance, in a large-scale analysis with millions of patients, even a small difference of 0.1% in treatment outcomes might show statistical significance (p &lt; 0.05); however, such a difference may have negligible clinical impact in real-world practice. To address this issue, researchers should report and emphasize effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside p-values as these metrics are less sensitive to sample size effects and provide more meaningful information about the practical significance of findings. Additionally, setting predetermined thresholds for clinically meaningful differences (i.e., minimal clinically important differences) before data analysis can help maintain focus on practical significance. Specifically, when analyzing KM interventions, researchers should consider using standardized mean differences or risk ratios rather than relying solely on p-values as these measures can better reflect the practical importance of observed effects in large-scale health insurance data. Sixth, when planning research using sample data from the NHIS or HIRA for specific groups, it is necessary to verify the sample size in advance to derive meaningful results, which is particularly important in KM research. In the case of the Sample Cohort of the NHISS, the number of KM inpatient claims in 2019 was approximately 33,000 (around 11% of Western medicine inpatient claims), whereas that of KM outpatient claims was about 2.15 million (approximately 16% of Western medicine outpatient claims) [ 18 ]. Although these numbers may not seem small in terms of the data volume, the number of connected data points could decrease significantly when certain conditions (e.g., limiting research subjects to specific groups) are applied. Seventh, for KM research, a combination of health insurance data with data from other institutions might be more effective. According to the 2022 Health Insurance Patient Medical Expense Survey, although the overall non-covered service burden rate (ratio of non-covered medical expenses to total medical expenses in healthcare institutions) was 14.6%, the rates for KM hospitals and clinics were 49.2% and 24.2%, respectively [ 19 ]. This finding suggests that many aspects of KM services might be excluded from health insurance claims data. Additionally, NHIS data are suitable for longitudinal studies; however, HIRA-provided data are organized by year and cannot be linked between years, making them more suitable for cross-sectional studies. When using data from the Sample Cohort of the NHIS, recent increases in de-identification measures for sensitive conditions and related fee codes (2,645 special conditions and 570 legally notifiable infectious diseases) [ 18 ] necessitate a preliminary review for related research. In addition, due to a recent increase in data usage applications, a shortage of virtual accounts, and a lack of data provision personnel, a waiting time of 6-7 months (or 7-12 months or more for data linkage to other institutions) may be necessary, which should be considered when planning research using health insurance claims data [ 19 ]. As shown in Table 4 [ 20 - 24 ], recent studies utilizing health insurance data in KM research have presented various methodological approaches and research topics. These studies highlight both the potential and limitations of health insurance data in KM research, particularly in terms of understanding utilization patterns, treatment effectiveness, and healthcare costs. The methodological approaches used in these studies can serve as references for future research design. CONCLUSIONS AND SUGGESTIONS Considerably more national-level data are produced in the healthcare field compared to other sectors. Based on the national statistical data approved by Statistics Korea as of October 2024, 64 (4.8%) out of a total of 1,343 data types are in the healthcare domain [ 25 ], indirectly suggesting an abundance of national-level data sources for generating these statistical data. Furthermore, the amount of healthcare data is expected to increase rapidly due to various national policy initiatives related to healthcare information, such as the emergence of smart healthcare, continuous development of information and communication technology, and establishment of five major big data platform projects following the announcement of the Bio-health Industry Innovation Strategy by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in 2019. Furthermore, various public data opening policy initiatives by the Korean government have allowed research using large-scale data sources from the NHIS and HIRA. These data sources are actively used to understand actual healthcare situations in Korea as they are based on national medical utilization. These sources are advantageous in that detailed medical utilization records under the fee-for-service system can be verified, and detailed information about medications and treatments is included. Although there are some usage restrictions, research on rare diseases or events and data linkage to other institutions are possible with clear research objectives and appropriate administrative procedures. However, diseases subject to diagnosis-related group payment systems have the disadvantage of lacking specific treatment details [ 25 ], and research on non-covered items is impossible as only covered medical utilization is included. Additionally, as diagnosis codes might be inaccurate, efforts should prioritize the clear operational definitions of diseases. For KM research using health insurance data, it is important to recognize that information on KM utilization is very limited in health insurance data. As of 2023, out of 8,776 insurance-covered items, only 69 (0.8%) items are related to KM, including acupuncture, cupping, and moxibustion [ 8 ]. Therefore, research designs limited to specific diseases treated with KM need to identify data limitations in advance. Recently, attempts have been made to overcome these limitations by combining health insurance data with data from specific institutions. Notably, in the 2023 study conducted by the Jaseng Medical Foundation research team on “The Effect of Korean Medicine Treatment on Subsequent Surgery Rates in Patients with Lumbar Disc Herniation,” data from four KM hospitals were combined with 2010-2019 patient data from the HIRA [ 26 ]. Institutional guidelines for combining data are available; however, various complex administrative procedures are involved when combining health insurance claims data with data from specific institutions. Although strict administrative procedures for personal information protection are necessary, more streamlined procedures aligned with the main purpose of the established system are urgently needed, and academic efforts are also required. The KM community needs to work toward establishing a “Korean Medicine Utilization Cohort Database (provisional name),” similar to the NHIS’s existing “Elderly Cohort Database” and “Health Screening Cohort Database” for specific groups. Furthermore, large-scale cohort studies based on big data, departing from traditional clinical trial designs, may be necessary for KM research. Particularly, big data-based large-scale cohort study designs including usual care recipients as control groups would be more effective for evaluating the effects of specific treatments or procedures to acquire evidence in KM. Most importantly, researchers should maintain a clear understanding of both the Korean health insurance system and the characteristics of its claims data structure. Although advanced analytical techniques such as machine learning are increasingly being applied to health insurance claims data, the focus should remain on producing meaningful and accurate results rather than complex technical analyses. Future research should emphasize clear operational definitions, a proper research design, and detailed methodological descriptions to enhance both research quality and reproducibility.
