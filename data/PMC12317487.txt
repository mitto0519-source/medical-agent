Disordered eating or eating disorders (ED) and psychosis commonly co-occur and have a degree of overlap, however there are no current clinical guidelines or comprehensive clinical guidance for integrated treatment. This lived experience case offers insight into symptom cross-over, perception and beliefs that may occur in coexisting ED and psychosis (i.e., eating disorder voice, psychotic voice-hearing, delusional beliefs about food, eating disorder-driven overvalued beliefs about eating/the body, body image disturbance, depersonalization, derealization). The detailed examples within this case highlight helpful treatment approaches to support trust and safety in care and prevent patient deterioration in co-occurring diagnosis of longstanding anorexia nervosa, schizoaffective disorder and trauma/PTSD. Lived experience involvement in research and clinical guidance development is needed in order to further understanding of co-occurring conditions such as ED, psychosis and trauma, to improve shared decision-making, and treatment personalization. Psychosis or ED may co-occur or have overlapping symptoms in a variety of presentations (i.e., food refusal due to negative psychotic symptoms, transient psychosis to metabolic or cognitive disturbance) [ 5 – 8 ]. Likelihood of concurrence may be increased due to factors such as shared genetic vulnerability [ 9 – 15 ] and overlapping clinical symptomatology [ 16 – 20 ], age of onset [ 21 – 23 ], cognitive-emotional features [ 24 ], glutamatergic dysregulation [ 25 – 27 ], malnutrition and metabolic or electrolyte disturbances [ 7 , 8 , 28 ]. Multiple theories have been developed regarding the mechanisms for co-occurrence and symptom overlap including a spectrum of overvalued ideas in EDs to delusion [ 29 – 33 ], EDs as a defence against psychosis [ 20 , 34 ], and apathy and withdrawal as a defence against disordered eating (DE) [ 20 , 35 ]. Shared vulnerability factors have also been suggested as potential mechanisms for co-occurrence and symptom overlap, including trauma, dissociation, insecure attachment, ego boundary loss, disturbed self-organisation, paranoia, negative body image, social anxiety, and maladaptive beliefs [ 36 – 53 ]. It may be that there are multiple distinct ED-psychosis/psychosis-DE variations within the co-occurrence that require personalised treatment approaches (i.e., schizophrenia and AN co-occurrence; ED with voice-hearing; psychotic-spectrum disorder with DE). Psychosis and ED/DE co-occurrence may significantly compound health impacts, and may result in delayed diagnosis, diagnostic overshadowing, or misdiagnosis [ 42 , 54 – 56 ], highlighting the need for clinical guidance for their assessment and treatment. In clinical samples, among 3–10% of ED patients have been estimated to have schizophrenia [ 57 ], and an estimated 10–30% of individuals with AN experience delusions [ 29 , 32 , 33 , 58 ]. Among 10–15% of ED patients are estimated to experience transient psychotic episodes [ 7 ]. A recent systematic review of AN and schizophrenia co-occurrence indicated that compared to the general population, schizophrenia patients had a higher prevalence of AN [ 54 ]. There were mixed findings for the prevalence of schizophrenia in AN [ 54 ]. Patients with psychotic-spectrum disorders have also been reported to have 2 to 4 times higher rates of disordered eating compared to control participants, with rates of disordered eating behaviours of up to 10-41.5% [ 59 ]. Binge-eating may be particularly common, and experienced by 4.4–45% [ 59 , 60 ], potentially in association with medication treatment (i.e., medication-induced metabolic dysregulation) [ 60 – 64 ]. Past-year presence of an ED has been found to be significantly associated with co-occurring diagnosed psychotic disorder (odds ratio: 12.92), and probable psychotic disorder (odds ratio: 28.42) [ 16 ]. Psychosis has been suggested to be a specific risk for ED onset [ 65 ], with presence of any psychotic symptoms at age 13 associated with 1.5 increased risk for disordered eating behaviour and ED severity at age 18 [ 65 ]. Higher cumulative lifetime psychotic symptoms and symptoms over 12 months have also been associated with greater likelihood of a positive ED screen and lifetime ED diagnosis [ 66 ]. There is a need for clinical guidance for screening and treatment for patients with these presentations, due to the risk for co-occurrence, overlap [ 16 , 54 , 66 , 67 ] and potential for eating disturbance during psychosis or transient psychosis in ED (i.e., due to malnutrition, metabolic dysregulation, sleep disturbance) [ 7 , 42 , 59 , 60 ]. Need for integrated and personalised treatment There are current clinical guidelines for eating disorders [ 68 – 72 ], their intersections (i.e., 73 , 74 ) and psychotic-spectrum conditions [ 75 – 81 ]. While there are integrated treatment guidelines for co-occurring substance use and ED or psychosis (i.e., 82 , 83 – 85 ), no current clinical guidelines exist for the integrated treatment of co-occurring ED-psychosis/psychosis-DE (hereafter referred to as ED-psychosis). Despite the lack of guidelines for integrated treatment for co-occurring ED-psychosis, clinicians are encouraged to adopt a trans-diagnostic approach to assessment and treatment, and consider integrated treatment and collaboration among ED and psychosis treatment programs [ 67 , 86 – 89 ]. There is a lack of clinical guidance and clinical examples of trans-diagnostic approaches and integrated treatment advice to guide clinical approaches in these circumstances. There may be a particular difficulty in approaching personalized treatment for individualised with ED-psychosis co-occurrence without such clinical guidance or comprehensive guidelines for transdiagnostic and integrated treatment. There is a need to develop greater understanding of co-occurring disordered eating, ED, psychosis and psychotic-spectrum disorders. Further, clinical practice guidelines may ignore the preferences and wishes of people with lived experience in their treatment, and focus on clinical priorities [ 90 ], which may detrimentally impact patient care [ 91 , 92 ]. Therefore, involvement of people with lived experience is critical to the development of clinical guidance. In an attempt to bridge some of these gaps, the following case study is outlined to provide insight into ED-psychosis co-occurrence and potential treatment approaches that may support individuals with these presentations, particularly those with high levels of trauma/PTSD. Involving people with lived experience in research, clinical guidance, and guideline development is critical to deepen understanding of complex co-occurring needs, optimising shared decision-making processes, and personalizing treatment. Personal impact As someone with lived experience of ED-psychosis co-occurrence, the lack of clinical guidance for integrated, personalized treatment negatively impacted my care, as well as that of my family and treating teams. The under-recognition of this co-occurrence led to diagnostic overshadowing and years of delayed treatment for my AN, often misinterpreted as severe appetite loss from negative psychotic symptoms or delusions. This delay severely affected my health and life, leading to a gap in treatment, repeated hospitalizations and non-response to inpatient, outpatient, and medication treatments. In nearly all hospitalizations, my ED and psychosis treatment were not integrated. Often, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) felt forced to treat only the ‘most significant threat to life,’ neglecting the other due to lack of resources or guidance. For instance, they would try to determine whether food refusal or suicidality was driven by AN or psychosis, then prioritise accordingly. This approach often led to further deterioration, escalating suicidality, self-harm, and, in two cases, suicide attempts. When psychosis was solely targeted, nursing staff would observe my self-starvation for days before intervening, which led to deterioration, increasing distress, and hopelessness—affecting both myself and other patients, and resulting in mutual iatrogenic trauma. I lost trust in the medical system, self-harmed from distress, and later disengaged from care, leading to medical self-neglect. When ED treatment was solely targeted, untreated psychotic symptoms meant I was unable to engage in treatment or accept nutrition. This led to mistrust of staff, repeated High Dependency Unit (HDU) admissions, and 24/7 nursing care. I pulled out feeding tubes due to not accepting my need for them or due to paranoid delusions about them, resulting in tube feeding under restraint and use of multiple forms of restraint and seclusion. These events caused significant iatrogenic trauma for myself and likely staff. These experiences highlight the need for integrated treatment when ED and psychosis co-occur. I am sharing aspects of lived experience of co-occurring psychosis and ED in hopes that it may support clinical learning for improved patient care and outcomes. Lived experience examples and suggestions for treatment Below, I provide examples from my lived experience of co-occurring post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD), longstanding anorexia nervosa (AN), and schizoaffective disorder, and treatment in public system inpatient contexts. I outline my experiences of co-occurring psychotic and eating disorder symptoms, dissociation and somatization, and suggestions for what could have, or did, support my treatment engagement when these symptoms co-occurred. I will describe the challenges I experienced, particularly in relation to ED-psychosis co-occurrence (and also in relation to trauma), and provide recommendations for how treatment may be approached. Eating disorder voice and psychotic voices Co-occurring symptoms My experience of psychotic voices includes a distinct sensory-felt and dissociative component, which distinguishes them from the ED voice, even when the content overlaps. For instance, psychotic voices might command me not to eat due to a belief that my food is poisoned. These voices often come with a felt texture, colour or shade, an emotional ‘shadow-sense,’ intense dissociation, and a strong sense of fear, confusion, or discord. They usually feel external, though can seem internal or both. For me, psychotic voices often have an echo-like quality—muddled, overlapping, or as if multiple voices are murmuring, whispering, talking, or shouting at once. In contrast, the ED voice feels more consistent, persistent, and intense. It’s targeted and overwhelmingly critical. It brings a narrowed focus, similar to what I’ve experienced with self-harm; attention focusing inward—something not present with psychotic voices, which are more globally focused on the external environment. The ED voice is clearer, stronger, and distinctly singular in comparison. It less often has a felt text or colour, and this does not change (when present, it feels sharp and metallic, or when accompanied by strong dissociation, like a warm and numbing blanket). During inpatient treatment, I often heard voices with varying content. Psychotic voices and the ED voice are distinct but can occur in rapid succession. For example, while sitting in a patient lounge, I might hear psychotic voices murmuring that people are watching me or stealing my thoughts. This would come with dread and the feeling that my body was changing shape—my skull and skeleton expanding, my sense of physical boundaries dissolving. I’d then attempt to regain a sense of self by ‘body checking’—touching my shoulders, wrists, elbows, or waist. Then, the psychotic voices might shift into the ED voice, which I could identify by its singular sharper tone and different sensory qualities. It would begin criticizing me—calling me worthless, powerless, deserving of pain. Or it would begin issuing commands, likely to prevent further body disturbance and regain a sense of agency: to refuse food and water, starve to death, exercise to collapse, remove a feeding tube, or self-harm. I would then tell staff I believed others were stealing my thoughts or that my body was changing. I’d insist I couldn’t take in nutrition, believing it would worsen the collapse or disintegration of my body. As my distress escalated, I would remove my feeding tube, refuse food, and/or self-harm. Suggested treatment support When these experiences occurred, it would have helped to have nursing staff: Sit down with me, validate that my sense of body disturbance and boundary loss must be very distressing. Reflect to me that my body is whole and that it isn’t changing, and that my thoughts and body are my own, and are safe. Explain that exteroceptive confusion happens for people with both eating disorders and psychosis, and that it was likely that I was currently experiencing a sense of confusion about my body’s size, shape and where my body exists in space. Affirm that experiences like feeling as though my body’s boundaries are disappearing can happen for people with experience of trauma, psychosis and eating disorders—and that this can also lead to upsetting thoughts. Reassure that these feelings will go away. Suggest that together we use some sensory soothing strategies to reduce the sense of body disturbance. Example 2. Lived experience example of co-occurring ED and psychotic voice-hearing I experienced psychotic and ED voice-hearing together during inpatient treatment as a trauma response. During an involuntary admission for psychosis and a suicide attempt at age 21, I became distressed after seeing visitors. I became non-verbal, and communicated my distress by hitting an object against the floor, after which I sat down on the floor, dissociated, and was still. Staff interpreted this as ‘becoming violent’. I was pushed and held down to the floor by nursing staff, injected with antipsychotic sedatives, then taken to a HDU, where I was forcibly stripped, and dressed in an anti-suicide smock. Once immobilized by the restraint clothing, I was then taken into seclusion, and put on a mattress, lying on my side. In seclusion and the smock, I was unable to move and struggling to breathe easily. This triggered traumatic memories and embodied sensations of being strangled as a child. My body entered a ‘freeze-collapse’ trauma response, with full limb immobilization. I began hearing external voices saying I would die if I did not escape, and that if the drugs wore off, I had to fight or outwit my captors to survive. I became catatonic. After release from seclusion, I experienced motor control problems and could not verbally communicate. I stopped eating for ten days, hearing voices warning my food might be drugged—which would leave me vulnerable to further immobilization and too weak to protect myself. Staff monitored me and interpreted this as psychotic decompensation. On day ten, I was told that if I didn’t eat, I’d be restrained again and given an NG tube. I was frightened of being put in another restraint smock and put back in seclusion. I heard external voices warning me a nurse might fake inserting the tube before using it to choke me—echoing past trauma of strangulation and betrayal. My ED voice commanded me to eat enough to get discharged, return to restriction, and regain agency over my body. Psychotic voices told me to follow ED treatment to allow discharge and escape from my captors and danger. These voices can be conceptualised as a Detached Protector Mode [ 93 ], consistent with both psychotic and ED symptoms linked to trauma and changes in liminal space [ 43 , 49 , 94 ]. In effort to be discharged, I became the “perfect patient,” ‘performing recovery’— claiming I no longer heard voices, appearing to follow the meal plan while secretly purging, water-loading, hiding food, and concealing weights on my body during weigh-ins. I denied urges for non-homeostatic eating, self-harm, or suicide. But on discharge, I was still hearing voices, acutely suicidal, experiencing trauma intrusions, and was fearful of engaging with medical services. Suggested treatment support When these experiences occurred, it would have helped to have nursing staff: In the initial incident of distress, it would have been helpful to have staff recognise that I had been feeling unsafe, and may need a higher level of support. Staff could support this need by accompanying me to my room to talk, asking me if I would like a sensory soothing kit or activity for distraction, or what might help me to feel safe. If I was struggling to verbally communicate, staff could supply me with pen and paper. This could have been a compassionate response to my distress which avoided re-traumatization and iatrogenic harm. After my distress reduced, it would have been helpful for a staff member to ask me if feeling upset (i.e., experiencing potential trauma resurgence) had led to hearing any voices. And if so, to ask me what the voices were saying, or if the voices were worried about anything. Then I would be able to help staff become aware of voices commanding me not to eat, or my fears for my safety (paranoid voice-related content). When an incident of seclusion and restraint had occurred, it would have been helpful for staff members to acknowledge how frightening it had been for me, and to discuss its impact on me. It would have been helpful for them to talk to me about why they had taken these actions – such as they had believed they were doing it to keep me safe, even if I had felt like I was being put in danger. It would have been helpful if they asked if I was now experiencing a loss of trust in them, and if they asked how they could help me feel safe. It would have been helpful if they also asked if I was hearing any voices that were feeling afraid, that were worried, or instructing me to do anything. These actions could help to re-build trust and a sense of safety. Example 3. Delusional belief and medical deterioration During several psychotic episodes (ages 20, 21, and 22), I developed delusional beliefs that I was already dead or decomposing and therefore no longer needed food or water (i.e., 95 ). At times, I believed my body was made of non-human materials—such as plant matter, fabric, or light—and that only small sips of water were necessary for survival. I also heard voices telling me that I didn’t need to eat or drink, creating a circular belief in my ability to survive without nutrition. Because of my pre-existing longstanding anorexia nervosa and history of chronically low food intake and low body weight, these beliefs quickly led to rapid medical deterioration. Helpful treatment interaction During one hospitalisation, a brief interaction with a nurse reduced my distress: A nurse reflected to me that although my body did not feel real to me, this was a symptom of my psychosis. She reminded me that it was called ‘depersonalization’. She also reminded me that unsettling beliefs sometimes happened in psychosis too, and that I was experiencing both disconnection from my body, and unsettling beliefs. She said that it must be very strange and upsetting, but reassured me that the feelings were going to go away. Suggested treatment support When these experiences occurred, it would have helped to have nursing staff: Repeatedly explain to me that derealization, depersonalization, and psychoform and somatoform dissociation were what I was regularly experiencing. Help me to reduce the distress associated with these experiences, by supporting me with guided grounding techniques and sensory soothing to help me reconnect to my body (such as with deep breathing, 5 senses exercises, or creating a sensory soothing box to use). If I was resistant to the idea that my body was alive and needed water and nutrition, gently suggest to me that it wouldn’t ‘hurt’ me if I wasn’t alive, but if I was alive, it would help and would make me feel better; therefore, it was important to try. We needed to cover every possibility, as the most important thing is that I am kept safe - which meant taking care of my body. If the delusional belief is that I did not need food or water because I was a spiritual being or a deity, an argument or suggestion could be: many spirits or deities are left offerings by people who care about them (like food and drink). Or they may visit earth and walk among people in a human body, and eat and drink with them. While in a mortal form, they must eat or drink because their body is like a human’s. Therefore, even if I don’t feel human , I can still eat with food and drink given to me by people who care about me, and eat and drink with people as though I am a human. Example 4. Paranoid mistrust of staff during eating disorder hospitalisation After medical stabilisation for AN at age 20, I was transferred to a psychiatric ward for ongoing psychiatric and meal plan stabilization. Once I no longer required 24/7 nasojejunal (NJ) feeding, I began transitioning to bolus feeds. On the first day of eating, the crunchy, grainy texture of the food triggered a strong threat response and traumatic association. It reminded me of sand, stepping on broken glass, and past moments of being threatened with a weapon by someone I trusted. I felt as though there was glass in my mouth, which evoked intense feelings of betrayal, fear, and led to dissociation. This led to a paranoid mistrust of staff and a delusional belief that glass had been crushed into my food and tube feeds to harm me. I accused staff of hiding glass in my food and NJ feeds, and refused to eat. My refusal affected other patients at the dining table, so staff supported them to continue eating and brought my meal to my room. I refused food and tube feeds, began sabotaging and removing NJ tubes, and required multiple re-insertions and feeding under restraint. I accused staff of crushing up glass and forcing it down my tube when I was asleep, and mixing glass and medication with water and putting it through the tube when they ‘flushed’ the tube. The delusion felt confirmed when I asked a nurse why I hadn’t received my medication, and she said she’d “put it down the tube,” despite my prior refusal to receiving medications that way. It reinforced my concerns about my safety and bodily autonomy. I stopped sleeping, which worsened paranoia, psychotic symptoms, PTSD flashbacks, and suicidality. As my distress escalated, I told staff I didn’t care if they killed me. I began making ligatures, which were found by staff, and I was placed under 24/7 surveillance in the HDU. In other hospitalisations, I also experienced delusions that NG or NJ tubes were being used to control, read, or steal my thoughts—or that the tubes were parasitic and would kill me in my sleep. These beliefs seemed connected to traumatic experiences where my safety, voice, and boundaries weren’t respected, and where I was threatened or harmed. I often feared sleeping, believing I would be attacked or killed, and struggled to trust others to care about my wellbeing. Helpful treatment interaction During this period, a brief interaction with a nurse reduced my distress: A nurse validated that my distress had meaning and made sense in the context of my trauma history, and supporting me with a grounding technique. This helped to foster a sense of trust, reduced my dissociation, reduced my sense of threat, and helped me feel cared for. Suggested treatment support When these beliefs occurred, it would have helped to have MDT members work with me to integrate my eating disorder and psychosis treatment needs as part of my treatment plan, such as: Sit down with me, validate that my distress had meaning and made sense in the context of my life and trauma history, and to help me with grounding techniques to reduce dissociation. Ask me what helped me feel safe, and what makes me feel threatened, and to ask me about the sensory environment, to identify any stimulus that may contribute to my sense of threat and could be changed. Work with me to create a sensory soothing kit, potentially with the support of an Occupational Therapist, to continue to use sensory soothing and grounding during distress, particularly dissociation, derealization, and depersonalization. Work with me to write a ‘reminder list’ during my inpatient stay. This could be read and talked about with me every morning with my nurse, to support a sense of safety, reduce disorientation, and reaffirm my need for care (see appendix ). Example 5. Using psychotic symptoms to mask restriction During three separate hospitalisations, I was taken to emergency after ceasing food and water intake and becoming medically unstable. I concealed the severity of my ED by using my pre-existing schizoaffective disorder diagnosis to my advantage, telling psychiatrists, psychologists, and nursing staff that my restriction was due to other factors, such as psychotic symptoms I had experienced in the past which had affected my food intake. This included telling staff I believed I didn’t need to eat, that voices told me not to eat, or that I had no motivation to eat. At the time, however, I was not experiencing active psychosis and was deeply affected by my AN, driven by a wish to die slowly through starvation, and desperate to avoid an ED admission and refeeding protocol. I didn’t respond to prolonged treatment for my ‘psychotic symptoms’ and depression, and kept deteriorating, becoming more suicidal and increasingly medically unwell. Suggested treatment support In these situations, it would have helped to have: A careful, comprehensive clinical evaluation of my history, including my history of: Suicidality, suicide attempts, stressors and protective factors, methods of planning, thoughts and feelings about suicide. Voice-hearing in psychosis and the eating disorder voice. Experiences of appetite, food intake, and triggers for restriction. The meaning behind self-starvation to me (i.e., symbolism, connection to suicide, punishment). Maladaptive schemas/beliefs (i.e., worthlessness, hypervigiliance for and expectation of mistrust/abuse, beliefs about the need to be acutely unwell in order to ‘warrant’ care or safety). The MDT / psychiatrist/ assessor to say that because of my history of having both an eating disorder and psychosis, my treating team wanted to ask if my struggles to eat might be related to my eating disorder: Did I feel like I didn’t deserve to eat? Did I feel like I wanted to die – such as deciding I wanted to die by not eating? Did I feel like I didn’t deserve help or safety unless I was near death? Did I have a voice telling me I didn’t deserve to eat? (If yes, did the voice sound like it was inside or outside my body? Did it sound like someone else, or like my own voice? ) If my answered indicated the voice was strongly aligned to an ‘eating disorder voice’ (i.e., belief that I didn’t deserve to eat, that I wanted to die through starvation, that I didn’t deserve help or safety, that I didn’t deserve to eat), for the assessor to tell me that I deserved safety and care, and that the MDT would be there to support me, and truly wanted to care for me and work with me to help me be safe. Example 6. Antipsychotic medication refusal due to side effects During multiple hospitalisations, I refused antipsychotic medications due to side effects (sedation, stupor, appetite changes, metabolic impacts, weight gain). This often led to refusing to swallow medication, prolonged admissions, greater medical and psychiatric acuity, forced medication under restraint, seclusion, and further iatrogenic harm. I often felt the potential benefits didn’t outweigh the side effects and that this wasn’t well discussed with me, beyond being told I had to take the medication or being forced to. It was difficult to believe antipsychotics would help when they made me feel physically ill or impaired my movement and control over my body, which was distressing. A stronger therapeutic relationship and collaborative involvement in medication decisions would have helped build trust, reduce paranoia and feeling unsafe, and made me feel more heard in my concerns about physical health consequences and agency. It would have helped to have staff validate my concerns about side effects, explain that they also held concern for my physical and emotional wellbeing, and clearly explain the rationale for medication treatment. Suggested treatment support During one hospitalisation, it helped to have a psychiatrist: Discuss with me the expected response to antipsychotics for me, and the rationale for their use in my treatment for psychosis, ED-related cognition and/or low body weight, or all of these factors. Print out and discuss with me multiple antipsychotics and their side effects. Discuss with me my experience of side effects, how long side effects were likely to last, my options for treating side effects if they became prolonged, and my options for tapering off the medication or changing medication if needed. Discuss with me my different medication options (different antipsychotics, oral/depot administration), no antipsychotic medication, and potential likelihood of shorter or prolonged admission. I was able to come to an informed decision, was collaboratively involved in my treatment (see 96 , 97 – 99 ), and developed more trust with the MDT. In future admissions, and using my advanced psychiatric health directive, I was able to self-advocate for the antipsychotic medication I would tolerate if I was experiencing a psychotic episode, and the antipsychotic medications I would refuse (due to previous non-response or intolerable side effects such as extrapyramidal side effects; akathisia, tardive dyskinesia, dystonia). Collaborative involvement in medication decision-making renewed and supported my sense of trust with the MDT when receiving treatment during psychotic episodes, and led to my acceptance of the antipsychotic treatment I best tolerated during ED admissions when I was experiencing psychotic symptoms. Conclusion This case study, written from a first-person perspective, provides insight into co-occurring psychosis and restrictive eating disorder (schizoaffective disorder and longstanding anorexia nervosa), with PTSD and CPTSD. These experiences demonstrate the need for integrated, personalized care, and the gaps when treatment is not integrated, which creates risk for deterioration, non-response, iatrogenic harm in treatment for the patient and staff, and treatment disengagement. It highlights the potential benefits of helping patients understand their need for treatment; emphasising the importance of their needs and safety; building, nurturing and restoring trust during care; and addressing sensory processing for a patient with ED-psychosis co-occurrence. Disorientation, dissociation, and atypical sensory processing may be significant aspects of ED-psychosis co-occurrence that affect information processing, staff-patient interactions, and treatment experience. Personalized treatment with focus on self-connectedness, sensory grounding, and sense of routine may reduce distress, improve treatment engagement, and foster a more positive treatment experience. As no comprehensive clinical guidance or guidelines for integrated treatment of ED-psychosis co-occurrence currently exist, the examples and suggestions for personalized treatment in this paper may offer valuable insight for supportive care. This case study also emphasises the need for patient involvement in developing clinical guidance and guidelines for integrated treatment and personalized care in future. The experiences and suggestions outlined highlight the potential benefits of integrated clinical care for co-occurring psychosis-ED to include an emphasis on: Compassion, understanding, and patience for the person’s experience of complex symptoms, distress, and perceptual, sensory, and cognitive disturbances (psychotic-based and eating disorder-related). Reassurance and a focus on helping the person feel safe, reaffirming sense of trust, and reminding them of the reason and need for them to be in hospital. This may be particularly helpful for treatment engagement where ED-psychosis co-occurrence may heighten vulnerability for difficulty recognising and accepting need for treatment, particularly early on. A strong trauma-informed approach incorporating simple somatic care to reduce dissociation, interoceptive, exteroceptive, and body image disturbances, and increase body connection (e.g., grounding exercises, sensory soothing). Including occupational therapists may be particularly beneficial, especially for individuals with depersonalisation and derealisation symptoms. Collaboration in treatment and shared medication decision-making to help a person understand their treatment options, support autonomy, and build trust. Consistent, firm and compassionate affirmation that the team wants to provide care and safety, and that the individual deserves safety and support. This may help to reduce paranoia, and encourage treatment engagement, especially for those with anosognosia as part of their ED and psychotic symptoms and strong eating disorder-related cognitions (e.g., belief they are are undeserving or not not ‘sick enough’ for treatment, and/or that their needs are ‘too much’). Personalized development of a ‘reminder list’ (see appendix ) with the person to help them understand the need and purpose of their treatment, reduce disorientation, and increase a sense of safety during care, particularly while experiencing cognitive and perceptual disturbances and/or starvation syndrome. The risk of delayed diagnosis, diagnostic overshadowing, and misdiagnosis in ED-psychosis/psychosis-DE co-occurrence underscores the need for frequent, brief screening for ED symptoms in psychosis patients, especially for those with food refusal, difficulty regulating food intake, weight changes, and body image distress (e.g., perceptual body disturbances, negative body image). If low body weight is present, lack of recognition of its seriousness may indicate ED-related anosognosia [ 42 ]. Frequent brief screening for psychotic symptoms may also benefit ED patients, particularly those with paranoia, a strong ED voice with commands, delusion-like beliefs, and intense body image or sensory processing disturbances. Psychosis and ED co-occurrence warrant further research and development of clinical guidance for integrated treatment. Lived experience perspectives and involvement [ 49 , 100 – 102 ] is essential in the development of such guidance and guidelines, to ensure sensitivity to the varied presentations within ED-psychosis/psychosis-DE co-occurrence and needs of the individuals with these complex experiences. This case study provides an example of how treatment can be personalized for individuals with ED-psychosis co-occurrence or co-occurring trauma-ED-psychosis.
