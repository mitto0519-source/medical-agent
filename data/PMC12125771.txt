Abstract Background A physical inactivity epidemic has been described as one of the greatest public health challenges. It accounts for 6% of deaths and is the fourth most common risk factor for mortality worldwide. Active transport (i.e. cycling and walking) has been identified as a key strategy for combating physical inactivity. Our aim was to explore the barriers to and perceptions of active transport modes in Tehran from the perspective of citizens. Methods This qualitative study was conducted using a phenomenological approach, from April to November 2024. Twenty-five individual interviews and four focus group discussions were conducted with 18 residents of Tehran. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using content analysis in MAXQDA 18 software. Results Participants mentioned walking in their daily lives, but never using a bicycle. Although cycling is not a widely used mode of transport among Tehran residents, it has been reported to be a clean and environmentally friendly mode of transport and an effective vehicle for reducing traffic congestion and air pollution. Walking was also considered a convenient mode of transport that could help save money. Three main themes were identified as barriers to active transport modes: (1) individual-level factors (e.g., lack of skills and confidence); (2) social and cultural-level factors (e.g., incompatibility of women’s cycling with cultural conditions); and (3) environmental-level factors (e.g., poor infrastructure). Conclusion Our study found that there are multiple barriers to cycling and walking as active modes of transport at individual, social and cultural, and environmental levels. Thus, programs or policies promoting active transport modes are effective when they target a multi-component approach. Background The physical inactivity epidemic has been described as one of the greatest public health challenges [ 1 ], with a recent estimate that one out of four adults and three out of four adolescents do not meet the global recommended levels of physical activity [ 2 ]. Physical inactivity is associated with a 20–30% increased risk of mortality [ 3 ], cardiovascular disease, diabetes, obesity, cancer, dementia, and depression [ 4 , 5 ]. Active transport, defined as making journeys in physically active ways, such as walking and cycling, has been flagged as a key strategy to combat physical inactivity [ 6 , 7 ]. Positive consequences for active transport modes, especially walking and cycling, including enhanced health, lower journey costs, reduction in energy use, traffic congestion, road casualties, greenhouse gas emissions, air pollution, and noise pollution, have been well documented [ 4 , 8 – 10 ]. Despite the recognition of these benefits, the rates of physical inactivity are stable or declining in most countries regardless of income [ 11 ]. In 2016, the prevalence of adult’s inactivity level was double in high-income countries (36.8%) compared to low-income countries (16.2%). In most countries, especially the Eastern Mediterranean and American regions, the prevalence rate was higher in women than in men [ 11 ]. In Iran, a 54.7% prevalence of physical inactivity has been reported in adults [ 12 ]. Moreover, evidence shows that in Singapore, cycling is used for only 1–2% and walking for 22% of the daily trips [ 13 ]. In the United States, 0.4% [ 14 ], France and the UK below 3% [ 15 ], Canada 1.5% [ 16 ], and Iran below 1% [ 17 ] of trips are made by bicycles. The situation is different in European countries such as the Netherlands, Denmark, Germany, and Sweden, where they have recorded high participation rates in cycling [ 18 , 19 ]. Extensive effort has been made to promote walking and cycling [ 7 ]. In 2016, the “physical activity strategy for the European Region” identified reducing car traffic and increasing walking and cycling as specific objectives [ 7 ]. In 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the first Global Action Plan on Physical Activity (GAPPA) with the slogan “more active people for a healthier world” to support countries to achieve a 15% relative reduction in the prevalence of physical inactivity by 2030 [ 2 ]. The focus of this action plan, as well as the “investments that work for physical activity” document, was to promote safe and active modes of transportation, including walking and cycling [ 2 , 20 , 21 ]. The first European Master Plan for Cycling Promotion was adopted in 2021 [ 22 ]. Barriers to active transport modes are complex and can differ from country to country [ 23 ]. In some studies, common concerns included physical infrastructure, road safety, traffic, distance, time constraints, and weather conditions [ 10 , 24 – 27 ]. In Iran, studies on active transport modes have mostly focused on cycling using quantitative methods that limit the understanding of contextual aspects [ 25 , 28 – 30 ]. Identifying barriers related to promoting active transport modes is an important first step in helping policymakers and authorities design effective intervention programs. To the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive qualitative study has shed light on the barriers to active transportation modes (walking and cycling) in Iran. To address this gap, this study aimed to explore the barriers to and perceptions of active transport modes in Tehran from the perspective of citizens. Methods Study design This qualitative study was conducted using a phenomenological approach, from April to November 2024, in Tehran, Iran. Semi-structured telephone interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) were used to explore the barriers and Tehran residents’ perceptions of active transport modes. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist was used to report this study [ 31 ] (Additional file 1 ). Study area The study area is Tehran, the capital of Iran and the second most populous city in the Middle East. The city has 22 municipal districts and an area of approximately 750 km², with a population of more than 9.8 million [ 32 ]. The number of daily trips in Tehran is about 20.3 million, with the most common mode of travel being private cars, and cycling represents only 0.3% of all trips [ 33 – 35 ]. Participants and recruitment The study population consisted of all residents living in Tehran. The inclusion criteria were age between 20 and 75 years and living in one of the districts of Tehran. To identify potential participants, purposive sampling with maximum variation (based on age, gender, occupation, socio-economic status, and region of residence) was employed. We chose to use both telephone interviews and FGDs to create rich data and achieve a fuller picture of participants’ perceptions [ 36 ]. Interviews are valuable for the in-depth exploration of individual thoughts. FGDs capitalize on group dynamics to stimulate discussions. In this technique, participants can challenge each other’s perspectives, which often reveals insights that might have been overlooked in the interviews [ 37 , 38 ]. Following coordination with Tehran Municipality, households, as data units, from Tehran city were randomly selected for telephone interviews. For this purpose, Tehran city was divided into five geographical regions (north, south, center, east, and west), and 20 households were randomly selected from each region. Selected households were contacted by telephone, the purpose of the study was explained, and if eligible to participate, they were invited to be interviewed by telephone. For the focus group sessions, sampling was conducted from Neighborhood Houses and organizations affiliated with Tehran Municipality that were established to promote citizens’ participation in social and cultural affairs consistent with neighborhood norms [ 39 ]. Two regions (northern and central) were randomly selected from different regions of Tehran. In each region, a Neighborhood House was randomly selected, and in each Neighborhood House, participants were selected according to the inclusion criteria. All participants provided verbal/written informed consent to participate in the study and audio recording of the interviews. They were also aware that their participation was voluntary, and that they were free to end their participation at any time during the study without explanation. Data collection A semi-structured interview/discussion guide that had been pre-tested in one pilot interview was used to collect data (Additional files 2 and 3 ). No changes were made to the guiding questions in the pilot test. After the initial introductions, the interview/discussion began with a general question about the participants’ perceptions of active transport modes. Participants were then asked to express their opinions on the barriers to active transport modes and suggestions for overcoming these barriers. All telephone interviews were conducted by the lead author (MM, PhD, female, Assistant Professor in Health Education and Promotion with sufficient experience in qualitative research) and lasted on average 30 min. FGDs were held at the Neighborhood Houses in a room designated for community meetings, without someone other than the interviewees, and lasted between 45 min and one hour. Each FGD was facilitated by a moderator (MM), and an assistant moderator (FGh or MHF, PhD candidates in Environmental Health who were trained by the moderator). The assistant moderator took notes during the discussion and ensured that all the participants participated and voiced their perspectives. The researchers and participants had no relationship before the commencement of the study. Demographic characteristics were also collected from each participant at the beginning of each interview/discussion. We terminated data collection after 25 telephone interviews and four FGDs, that is, when ideas and opinions began to be repeated or when no new data emerged about the topic under inquiry. None of the interviews required repetition. Data analysis Data analysis was performed simultaneously with data collection, using the inductive content analysis approach proposed by Graneheim and Lundman [ 40 ]. Interviews/discussions were held in the Persian language, were audio-recorded (following verbal consent), and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were checked against audio recordings by MM, and each transcript was assigned an identification number to ensure confidentiality. Analysis and coding were conducted independently by MM and MY (male professor in epidemiology with experience in qualitative research). First, the transcripts were read several times to obtain a general understanding of their content. Then, the transcripts were again read line by line and semantic units and the initial codes were identified. Next, the codes were constantly compared and arranged into more comprehensive categories, based on their similarities and differences. Finally, the main themes and categories were created [ 41 ]. The coders met frequently throughout the process to address any disagreement. MAXQDA 18 software was used for data management [ 42 ]. The quotations were selected for inclusion in the manuscript and were translated into English. Rigor and trustworthiness Lincoln and Guba criteria were applied to ensure trustworthiness of qualitative results [ 43 ]. Data credibility was ensured through data triangulation by combining telephone interviews and FGDs, multiple data sources (including audio recordings, field notes, observations, and transcripts), and by including various researchers during the analysis process. To increase the reliability or dependability of the data, the research findings were reviewed and approved by an expert in the field of qualitative research, who was not involved in the study. To ensure transferability, a complete description of the research steps was provided to allow the readers to understand the study steps and interactions. Furthermore, the transferability of the data was enhanced by including a variety of participants with different characteristics, including age, gender, occupation, socio-economic status, and region of residence. Confirmability of the data was also achieved by extracting direct quotes and holding several meetings by coders to discuss emerging codes and themes until a consensus was reached. Results Socio-demographic characteristics of participants A total of 25 individual interviews and four FGDs were conducted with 18 residents of Tehran. The socio-demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1 . Twenty-eight individuals refused to participate in this study. Of the 100 individuals contacted for telephone interviews, 49 did not respond, and 26 responded and declined. Two individuals refused to participate in the FGDs. The main reason for refusing to participate was lack of time. General perception of active transport modes During the individual interviews and FGDs, the participants mentioned cycling as a “clean and environmentally friendly mode of transport”. Although they mentioned that cycling was not a widely used mode of transport among Tehran residents, they agreed that it was an effective vehicle for reducing traffic congestion and air pollution. “Cycling is a clean transportation mode. I think that promoting it in a metropolis such as Tehran , which is plagued by traffic problems and air pollution , has significant advantages…” (FGD 1, male, 33 years old). Another participant noted: “… is an effective way to improve transportation and the environment.” (Interview 23, female, 38 years old). The participants further discussed the health benefits of walking, reporting it as a good exercise to maintain good health and fitness and improve mental health. In addition, walking is considered a convenient mode of transport that could help save money. “Similar to any other exercise , walking has many benefits for physical and mental health. People can easily incorporate it into their daily lives.” (FGD 3, female, 49 years old). One of the participants said: “It can help save money.” (FGD 2, male, 45 years old). Some participants also described cycling and walking as ways for housewives to escape from inactivity. As one participant noted, “Half of our society’s population is women. Many of them are housewives , and living in apartments reduces their mobility. I believe that walking and cycling can be solutions to reduce inactivity.” (Interview 14, male, 51 years old). Many participants mentioned walking in their daily lives. They stated that to exercise, recreation, daily shopping, and traveling short distances, they usually choose this active transport mode. Others used a combination of walking and public transportation to commute to work. “… I walk most of the time; for example , I prefer walking for my daily shopping or I usually walk in the park on weekends. I think it’s a very enjoyable option for recreation.” (FGD 3, female, 70 years old). Moreover, most participants stated that they never use bicycles in their daily lives. Only one male participant mentioned using a bicycle to commute to work: “I commute to work by bicycle. Because cycling is one of my favorite activities , and my home is close to my workplace… my colleague advised me not to ride a bike! He said , ‘It’s not safe at all.” (Interview 19, male, 36 years old). However, some participants noted that they might be willing to use active transport modes in their daily activities if the transport network is improved (especially by providing safe paths for cycling and walking and promoting cycling culture). “Necessary infrastructure for cycling and walking should be provided , especially a safe path… I believe that the provision of infrastructure should be accompanied by culturalization , advertising , and encouraging citizens.” (FGD 4, female, 48 years old). “… We do not have enough and appropriate paths for cycling or walking. If this infrastructure is provided , I will personally do it , and at least part of my commute to work , I will use a bicycle… I think if infrastructure is provided , people would welcome cycling and walking.” (Interview 10, male, 38 years old). Barriers to active transport modes We identified three main themes for barriers to active transport modes: individual-level, social and cultural-level, and environmental-level factors (Table 2 ). Individual-level factors Insufficient time Some participants related their reasons for not using active transport modes to insufficient time. They stated that long working hours, having part-time jobs, and family and social commitments can make it difficult to find time to walk or cycle. “I think sometimes we are so busy with work that we do not have enough time to walk or cycle. For example , I have to work from morning to evening , do you think I have time to walk?” (FGD 1, male, 33 years old). High purchase cost The high cost of purchasing a bicycle has also been reported to be a barrier to its use. Some participants affirmed that the cost of purchasing a bicycle was too high for many households, which may prevent its widespread adoption. They stated that strengthening and supporting the bicycle sharing system (BSS) could provide expanded access to cycling without a financial burden. “The cost of purchasing a bicycle is too high. Currently , you must have a good income to purchase a high-quality bicycle… If a bicycle is available to everyone in different places , it may be an incentive to choose it.” (Interview 21, male, 58 years old). Furthermore, some participants who reported bicycles as an expensive thing, described it as “illogical and without economic justification” to purchase a bicycle to ride it only a few times a month or year. “… Well , if I pay X million for a bicycle , how much can I use it? Unfortunately , maybe a few times a month or year. Therefore , I believe that purchasing bicycles is not economically justified… However , if this bike could be used daily , the cost would not be a significant barrier.” (FGD 1, male, 35 years old). Lack of skills and confidence Some female participants also mentioned that cycling, as a form of active transport, requires skill and confidence. They stated that negative past experiences, such as falling, getting injured, or fear of such incidents, caused them to feel anxious and fearful about cycling. They also mentioned that they did not have the opportunity to learn cycling during childhood, or that necessary training was not provided to them. These factors have led to feelings of insecurity and lack of confidence in engaging in this activity. As one participant stated, “Since childhood , I have always been afraid of cycling because of the negative experience of falling and getting injured… I never had the opportunity to properly learn how to cycle…” (Interview 11, female, 46 years old). Physical discomfort Several participants also mentioned a decreased desire to walk or cycle because of physical discomfort (especially knee pain). “Walking is my favorite activity , but I cannot walk or cycle because of the knee pain.” (Interview 12, male, 63 years old). Social and cultural-level factors Lack of a cycling culture Participants highlighted the lack of a cycling culture in Iranian society as an important barrier. Some of them stated that what was formed in the Iranian urban culture was the use of bicycles for recreation and entertainment in childhood rather than as a vehicle. Therefore, the promotion of cycling culture was considered an important determinant. As noted: “It is crucial to promote the culture of bicycles. The use of bicycles as vehicles for transport has not yet been institutionalized in our culture…” (Interview 15, male, 53 years old). Incompatibility of women’s cycling with cultural conditions of the society Another important barrier discussed was the incompatibility of women’s cycling with cultural conditions of the society . Most participants (especially women) stated that although there is no legal prohibition for women to use bicycles in Iranian society, the prevailing cultural and religious conditions show signs at the community level that prevent women from riding bicycles. This perception was largely related to concerns regarding the type of clothing worn during cycling. A few women also mentioned concerns about being teased by others: “I believe that there are restrictions on us in society , especially regarding the type of clothing that we cannot use bicycles as easily as men.” (FGD 2, female, 56 years old) “Unfortunately , in our society , women’s cycling is still not accepted , or they are strict in different ways , or you may be ridiculed…” (Interview 18, female, 43 years old). In addition, some male participants mentioned that in a society where women make up a large proportion of the population, depriving women of cycling is likely to reduce men’s willingness to cycle: “As a man , I would like to go cycling with my wife , but there are restrictions on her cycling… so I personally do not do it (cycling).” (Interview 5, male, 64 years old). Improper culturalization regarding respect for the rights of pedestrians and cyclists Improper culturalization regarding respect for the rights of pedestrians and cyclists was identified as another important barrier. Most of the participants mentioned the traffic of motorcyclists and pedestrians on bike paths, which was associated with an increased risk for the safety of cyclists. Participants also shared their concerns about vehicle traffic or parking (especially motorcycles) on walking paths: “The traffic of motorcyclists and pedestrians on bicycle paths is also a common problem. There is a bicycle path on X Street , but nothing else travels there , except for motorcycles. Culturalization is crucial in this regard… Efforts should be made to address this problem by training and increasing citizens’ awareness of their rights.” (FGD 4, female, 40 years old). One of the participants also said: “I think that many cycling paths are not usable for cyclists , because after traveling a short distance , they will encounter barriers such as motorcycle traffic or pedestrians , and this is one of the important manifestations of the lack of safety…” (Interview 25, male, 37 years old). Another noted: “… On walkways , we must be careful not to be hit by motorcycles. The motorcycle comes in front of and behind us. Last year , I was hit by a motorcycle on the walkway and my shoulder was injured…” (Interview 7, female, 62 years old). Poor advertising Poor advertising has also been identified as a crucial barrier to unwillingness to ride a bike and walk. Most participants wanted wide advertising and education through the media about the benefits, as well as the laws related to active transport. “Advertising about walking and cycling in our country is weak… Increasing awareness of active transport modes can help increase it.” (Interview 11, female, 46 years old). Fuel price Another social barrier to promote active transport modes (especially cycling) was expressed fuel price. A few participants mentioned low fuel prices as a factor influencing the choice of motorized transport (MT) rather than active transport modes. “Our fuel prices are very cheap , which can be a barrier to promoting active transport modes. If fuel is available to people at a reasonable price , they may switch to walking or cycling for short travels.” (FGD 3, male, 40 years old). Environmental-level factors Poor infrastructure Poor infrastructure emerged as the most prominent barrier for most participants, including a lack of cycling and walking paths and lack of connectivity of bike paths. Some participants reported that due to the lack of cycling paths, they had to go to places such as parks or cycling tracks that are considered in some areas of the city. Therefore, their willingness to use this mode of transport is reduced. The participants also considered the construction of fragmented and discontinuous bicycle paths as a “ waste of resources ” because they believed that frequently such paths were not used. “I think the key barrier to cycling and walking is a lack of paths… When there are insufficient paths , cyclists are forced to ride on the road in the middle of cars , which is dangerous. Therefore , if we want to ride a bike , we must go to parks such as X that have cycling tracks…” (FGD 2, male, 45 years old). “… If there were enough and appropriate bike paths , maybe I would let my daughters ride bikes…” (Interview 4, female, 43 years old). “Maintenance of the connectivity of the bike path is the most significant requirement for cyclists. Unfortunately , many of the city’s designed bike paths are not connected , and can be dangerous for cyclists. Therefore , many citizens are less inclined to use bicycles as transportation vehicles.” (FGD 1, male, 38 years old). Participants also agreed that the lack of sidewalks, discontinuous sidewalks, and lack of greenery causes walking to be unattractive to them. “Sidewalks are unsuitable for walking. They are either full of potholes , not connected , or do not have greenery that would encourage me to walk along that path…” (Interview 23, female, 38 years old). Lack of safety Most participants also mentioned a lack of safety as the most significant barrier to active transport mode choice. They stated that they felt unsafe when cycling or walking, owing to the presence of motor vehicle traffic (especially motorcycles) and the potential for accidents. This feeling was largely a result of participants’ perception of drivers’ lesser knowledge of the rights of cyclists and pedestrians, as well as their lack of adherence to traffic norms and laws. “… Sometimes , I wonder when I see a cyclist on the street. How dare they? It’s not safe at all… In Tehran , drivers are reckless and do not watch pedestrians or cyclists out.” (FGD 3, female, 59 years old). “… I wish that the paths were such that cyclists or pedestrians felt safe. We are afraid to walk on the sidewalk because the sidewalks are filled with motorcyclists…” (Interview 12, male, 63 years old). Lack of access Participants reported a lack of access to bicycles as a crucial barrier in reducing the unattractiveness of cycling. Some affirmed that the municipality should provide bicycles of different sizes and in different places so that people can easily access them. Most participants agreed that the BSS could play a crucial role in easy access to bicycles. However, due to the poor implementation of this system in Tehran, including the improper distribution of bicycles and poor advertising, the opposite has occurred. “Easy access to bicycles is important… A few years ago , my son used to ride bicycles that were scattered around the city… Although the municipality’s initiative in launching a bicycle sharing system is commendable , it was unfortunately not implemented properly , and after a while , the bikes were collected from across the city and no one was held accountable.” (FGD 4, female, 47 years old). “… Access to bicycle-sharing has been provided to the central areas of Tehran. I reside in the 22nd geographical area of Tehran , and despite low access to public transport , there is no bicycle sharing in this area. Therefore , we have to use private cars even for short travels… It is essential that the municipality provide access to most areas of Tehran.” (Interview 10, male, 38 years old). Several participants also emphasized increasing access to bicycles through government-provided bicycle purchase loans to the public: “A useful way to make bicycles easily accessible and to promote cycling is to provide loans to people to purchase bicycles…” (Interview 5, male, 64 years old). Distance Travel distance was also reported as a barrier by some participants. They pointed out that the distance between work, shopping, and recreation in many urban areas and where one lives is significant, meaning that individuals must travel to areas with different conditions from where they live throughout the day to perform their activities. Therefore, cycling or walking was not welcomed. However, walking is considered relatively comfortable and easy for short trips. “A significant percentage of our daily trips in Tehran are not possible by cycling or walking because of the long distances… Perhaps they could be chosen for part of the trip. I mean using a combination of public and active transportation.” (FGD 2, female, 64 years old). Air pollution Air pollution in Tehran was also considered a barrier to the use of active transport, especially cycling, by some participants. Participants agreed that when cycling, a person’s intake of pollutants increases due to increased inhalation. Thus, there is a perception that cycling and walking in polluted air is harmful. “Another factor contributing to the reluctance of active transport modes is air pollution in Tehran… For example , when riding a bike in polluted air , one’s heart rate and breathing rate increase , and one takes in more polluted air into the lungs. Therefore , I think that the harm of these activities outweighs their benefits.” (Interview 1, female, 27 years old). Topography Some participants talked about Tehran’s topographic conditions, citing its slope as a barrier to bicycle use. It is noted that Tehran is not conducive to cycling owing to its geographical location and slope, especially from north to south, which contributes to increasing safety concerns. “To promote cycling , the topographic conditions in Tehran should also be considered. Some areas of Tehran have steep slopes and are unsuitable for cycling… There is a risk of accidents… I think cycling is more possible on flat roads and in central-city areas.” (Interview 24, male, 47 years old). Discussion This qualitative study investigated the barriers to and perceptions of active transport modes (cycling and walking) in Tehran from the perspective of citizens. To our knowledge, this is the first study that used qualitative methods to investigate this issue in Iran. A comprehensive understanding of these barriers is an important first step in assisting urban policymakers and transportation planners to support active transport modes. Overall, our results showed that there are multiple barriers to active transport modes at the individual, social and cultural, and environmental levels. Addressing these factors requires multi-component interventions to maximize the uptake of active transport modes. In this study, cycling was described as a clean and environmentally friendly mode of transport, and walking as a convenient mode of transport, which is supported by other studies [ 44 , 45 ]. The participants in our study associated cycling and walking with a reduction of traffic congestion, air pollution, cost savings, improved health, and reduced inactivity. The benefits of active transport modes, such as reduced congestion [ 46 , 47 ], better air quality and reduced pollution [ 6 , 46 ], lower journey costs [ 4 , 46 , 48 ], and health [ 49 – 52 ], have been shown in other studies. Most participants in our study never used bicycles in their daily lives. However, for exercise, recreation, daily shopping, and traveling short distances, they usually choose to walk. Similarly, in a study on the social norms and perceptions of Saudis on active transportation, Hasan et al. (2024) found that few participants reported walking as a mode of transport for their daily commute; they preferred to use private cars. In addition, no participants reported using a bicycle for daily trips [ 53 ]. In the study by Bąk et al. (2019), over one-third of the participants also reported that they never used a bicycle [ 54 ]. However, in the study by Basil et al. (2023), after public service vans, known as matatus, walking and cycling ranked second and third as common modes of transport, with 21.9% of Nairobi citizens using walking, and 8.8% using cycling for daily trips [ 55 ]. Consistent with previous literature, our findings showed that participants perceived physical infrastructure [ 55 – 57 ] and safety concerns [ 49 , 55 , 58 , 59 ] as the predominant barriers to use active transport modes. Cheyne et al. (2015) reported that if the development of high-quality infrastructure and the safety of pedestrians and cyclists are not prioritized, participation in active transport will decline [ 60 ]. The literature further indicates that in countries such as Germany, Japan, and the Netherlands, which represent high levels of cycling, infrastructures, such as protected cycle paths and secure parking facilities, are provided for cyclists. However, in countries such as the USA and Australia, which represent low levels of cycling, cycle-supportive infrastructure is rare, and transport policies are often car-centric [ 19 ]. Our study, like other studies [ 10 , 13 , 55 , 59 , 61 ], highlighted bicycle access as a crucial factor in shaping people’s choice of cycling and emphasized the role of BSS in easy access to bicycles. Providing rental bicycles through the BSS was one of the policies implemented to promote cycling in Iran, first launched in Mashhad in 2012, however the usage rate was low [ 62 , 63 ]. In Tehran, BSS, known as Bdood bike-sharing, was launched in 2018 in the busiest areas of the city (central areas of Tehran, including Districts 6, 7, 11, and 12) but failed a few years after its implementation. Studies conducted on BSS in Iran indicate the inefficiency of these systems under current conditions [ 64 – 66 ]. Participants in our study also reported that long travel distances and air pollution were significant barriers to use active transport modes. Hasan et al. (2024) found that long travel distance is a barrier, which makes people less likely to choose walking or taking a bus for their daily commute [ 53 ]. Rahman et al. (2023) also reported that travel distance is a factor that affects both walking and bicycling [ 67 ]. Furthermore, studies show that the benefits of cycling exceed the risks associated with increased inhaled air pollution doses [ 68 , 69 ]. This study also identified the lack of a cycling culture and the incompatibility of women’s cycling with cultural conditions of society as significant sociocultural barriers to the adoption of cycling as a mode of active transport. As explained by other studies, cultural perspectives and social norms and traditions regarding women’s cycling were found to play an important role in bicycle usage [ 53 , 59 , 61 , 70 ]. It is not surprising that cycling in some societies is viewed as a masculine domain [ 59 ]. Another barrier that we identified was improper culturalization regarding respect for the rights of pedestrians and cyclists. This was supported by a study by Jessiman et al. (2023), which showed that drivers’ lower awareness of cyclists and their failure to accept them on roads or paths increases the risk of accidents [ 4 ]. Other studies also reported poor attitude of road users as a barrier to cycling [ 68 , 71 ]. Furthermore, our findings show that poor advertising can affect the use of cycling and walking as active transport modes. As mentioned by the participants, education through the media about the benefits and laws of active transport is important. Previous studies have also shown that awareness of the social, health, and environmental benefits of cycling and walking plays an important role in increasing their use [ 44 , 72 , 73 ]. Our findings also highlight low fuel prices as a factor that encourages the use of MT and reduces the attractiveness of active transport modes, especially cycling. Fuel prices play a crucial role in shaping travel behavior, and previous studies has showed that an increase in fuel prices led to a higher probability of bicycle use [ 74 – 76 ]. In this study, the participants discussed some individual-level factors (including insufficient time, physical discomfort, high cost of purchasing a bicycle, and lack of skills and confidence) reducing their willingness to use active transport modes. A narrative review of the benefits, risks, barriers, and facilitators of cycling found that a lack of skills and confidence, physical discomfort, and cost were key barriers to cycling [ 68 ]. The results of the study by Mogaji et al. (2022) also showed that lack of skills and confidence can discourage people from cycling [ 73 ]. Evidence also shows that in countries where cycling infrastructure is good, individual-level factors can play a more important role than environmental-level factors in predicting cycling behavior [ 77 , 78 ]. Overall, the findings of this study provide implications for urban and transportation policymakers and planners in Iran and other contexts. We suggest that they consider the findings of this study when planning and implementing interventions regarding active transport modes. Additionally, this study suggests a need for interventions to promote and increase the use of active transport modes in Iran. When designing and implementing these interventions, it is crucial to invest in physical infrastructure (such as increasing and connecting cycling and walking paths, improving sidewalks, and creating greenways), develop strategies to improve pedestrian and cyclist safety, reform culture and reduce gender disparities for cycling, implement education policies, raise awareness of the benefits of cycling and walking, and provide legal protection for active transport modes. Strengths and limitations The strength of this study is the combination of telephone interviews and focus groups, which provides in-depth insight into the individuals’ experiences and perceptions. We also believe that this is the first qualitative study to investigate the barriers to active transport modes using a diverse group of Tehran residents. Nevertheless, the study has limitations. First, juveniles were not included in this study, and this age group may provide different perspectives and needs related to the use of active transport modes in Tehran. It is recommended that this be considered as a part of further research. Second, the sample of this study consisted of the age group between 20 and 70 years residing in Tehran, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to a wider population. Conclusion Our study showed that most participants walked in their daily lives and never cycled. Our study also showed that the decision to cycle and walk as active transport modes is influenced by factors at individual, social and cultural, and environmental levels. Therefore, programs or policies promoting active transport modes are effective when they target a multi-component approach.
