Abstract The purpose of this study was to explore the structural relationships among Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, Achievement Emotions Negative, and COVID-19-Induced Negative Psychological States in Chinese adolescents. The study aimed to identify key psychological and emotional factors that may mitigate the negative psychological impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023), with a focus on how physical literacy and related constructs influence adolescents’ psychological well-being. Given the significant psychological challenges faced by adolescents during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023), this study seeks to understand how physical literacy, mental toughness, and emotional states interact to influence negative psychological outcomes. A cross-sectional design was adopted, and data were collected from 729 secondary school students in Lanzhou, China, using validated questionnaires to assess Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, Achievement Emotions Negative, and COVID-19-Induced Negative Psychological States. Structural equation modeling conducted using Mplus 8.0 software revealed that the initial hypothesized model showed good fit to the data (CFI = 0.953; TLI = 0.948; SRMR = 0.041). The results indicated that Physical Literacy positively predicted Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, and Achievement Emotions Positive, while negatively predicting Achievement Emotions Negative and COVID-19-Induced Negative Psychological States. Additionally, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, and Achievement Emotions Positive were found to negatively predict COVID-19-Induced Negative Psychological States, whereas Achievement Emotions Negative positively predicted COVID-19-Induced Negative Psychological States. In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive model that elucidates the interplay between physical literacy, mental toughness, emotional states, and negative psychological outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023). The findings offer valuable insights for designing interventions to enhance adolescents’ psychological resilience and emotional well-being during large-scale public health crises akin to COVID-19. Introduction Since the initial outbreak of COVID-19 in December 2019, the virus has exhibited remarkably high transmission rates, causing significant impacts on countries across the globe. The World Health Organization’s (WHO) COVID-19 Dashboard data from 2022 indicated that the total number of confirmed global cases surpassed 600 million by September. The ongoing mutations of the virus have sustained elevated numbers of confirmed and suspected cases, leading to prolonged coexistence with the virus. This situation has resulted in substantial psychological distress and anxiety among individuals worldwide, disrupting normal lives 1 , 2 . Despite a decline in the severity of COVID-19 over time and improvements in epidemic prevention and control worldwide, complete eradication of the virus has not been achieved. It continues to pose a substantial threat to humanity, as noted by the WHO in 2023. The risk of new variants emerging and future waves of infections remains a real concern 3 . Recent data from the WHO’s COVID-19 Dashboard, as of 23 February 2025, shows that 94,614 new cases were reported globally in the last 28 days, representing a decrease of 59,657 cases compared to the previous 28-day period 4 . According to the WHO’s 2025 Global Health Report (World, 28 days to 30 March 2025), although COVID-19 cases have declined 84,654 since peak incidence, pandemic-related mental health burdens persist globally, with adolescents showing increase in anxiety disorders compared to pre-pandemic levels, it is estimated that 4.4% of 10–14-year-olds and 5.5% of 15–19-year-olds experience an anxiety disorder 4 , 5 . Despite this decline, COVID-19 continues to pose a significant public health challenge, with countries such as Brazil and the Russian Federation reporting 38,300 and 28,000 cases, respectively, during the same period 4 . The outbreak’s trajectory has shown unpredictable fluctuations in weekly confirmed case numbers worldwide since its inception, with a significant surge occurring in December 2022. Within just four weeks, this outbreak contributed to an increase of 97,976,070 cases globally 3 . Numerous psychological effects have been studied in relation to the virus outbreak, spanning from personal to national and international levels 6 . On an individual level, people have experienced heightened fears of illness or death, a sense of helplessness, and the stigma associated with the virus, which have collectively given rise to severe mental health crises 7 – 9 . The WHO has additionally revealed that the global prevalence of anxiety and depression has surged by 25% due to the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023) 10 . It is evident from these findings that the threat posed by COVID-19 persists. To adequately address the potential for future recurrences of the pandemic, it is imperative to comprehensively understand the diverse psychological impacts of COVID-19 and develop effective response strategies. Adolescents face unique challenges compared to working adults, often exhibiting emotional instability under pressure. Their psychological characteristics distinguish them from the general population and even other young individuals, placing them at a higher risk for psychological issues 9 , 11 , 12 . The external environment’s influence makes them more susceptible to problems like anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) 13 . Their academic burdens and challenges are more pronounced, intensifying concerns about academic performance and future job prospects 14 . Unlike adults, their relative lack of experience in time management makes them more vulnerable to the negative effects of remote learning 15 . A significant portion of students’ social and interpersonal interactions occur on campus, and the isolation imposed by COVID-19 severs many of these ties. This isolation leads to heightened loneliness, declining mental health, and this impact can persist for an extended period, not dissipating quickly even as the epidemic concludes 16 . Furthermore, in terms of independence and social identity, the lingering negative effects of COVID-19 can endure 17 . Addressing the psychological challenges faced by adolescents due to the COVID-19 epidemic has emerged as a pressing research concern 18 . Research has also demonstrated that self-efficacy serves as a negative predictor for symptoms such as depression and anxiety 20 . Method Participants The study was granted ethical approval by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM) under the code USM/JEPeM/22,040,247, and followed strictly the procedure from the Declaration of Helsinki. Parental consent was obtained for all participants under the age of 18, and the study adhered to ethical guidelines outlined by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM). This cross-sectional study was conducted in secondary schools located in Lanzhou city, Gansu Province, China. Five schools were randomly selected from a comprehensive list of educational institutions in Lanzhou. Within each chosen school, two to three classes were randomly selected for participation. All eligible students were invited to participate, resulting in a total of 780 participants in the study. After screening the submitted questionnaires, 729 students met the criteria. The average age of the participants was 16.2 years (SD = 1.1), with 493 (67.6%) being male and 236 (32.4%) being female. All participants were of Chinese nationality. The participants in this study were secondary school students in Lanzhou, China, who were enrolled in regular physical education (PE) classes as part of their school curriculum. While the students were not specifically selected based on their athletic involvement, the study aimed to explore how physical literacy and athletic mental energy, as developed through school-based PE programs, influence their psychological well-being during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023). Inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: enrollment in a secondary school in Lanzhou city, age between 14 and 18 years, and proficiency in reading and communicating in Chinese. Students attending specialized schools were excluded from participation, as were those with visual, auditory, or other impairments that hindered their ability to independently complete the questionnaire. Instrument Stress and anxiety to viral Epidemics-6 (SAVE-6) The SAVE-6 questionnaire, developed by Chung et al. (2021) in South Korea, is designed to gauge the stress and anxiety reactions of the general public in response to COVID-19 42 . Consisting of six questions, it addresses key symptoms of stress and anxiety triggered by the pandemic. Example items include: (1) I am worried that I might get infected with COVID-19. (2) I feel anxious when I think about COVID-19. Following its use in Korea, the SAVE-6 scale demonstrated favorable internal consistency (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.815). The scale has been recognized as a reliable, valid, and straightforward measurement tool suitable for assessing stress and anxiety in the general population. Fear of COVID-19 scale (FCV-19) The Fear of Coronavirus Scale (FCV-19) was created by Ahorsu et al. (2022) to assess the fear, concern, and anxiety experienced by individuals towards COVID-19 43 . This questionnaire includes seven items that probe public apprehension about the virus (item-total correlation ranging from 0.47 to 0.56). Example items include: (1) I am afraid of losing my life because of COVID-19. (2) I feel nervous when I watch news about COVID-19. The scale exhibits solid psychometric properties, boasting acceptable reliability metrics such as internal consistency (α = 0.82) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.72). With its robust reliability and validity, the FCV-19 Scale proves valuable in evaluating COVID-19-related fears among the general public. Athletic mental energy scale (AMES) The Athletic Mental Energy Scale (AMES), founded by Lu et al. (2018), is tailored to assess Mental Energy 25 . This questionnaire comprises 18 items that respondents rate based on their feelings, selecting from six levels (ranging from “completely so” to “completely not”). Example items include:1. I feel full of energy during physical activities. 2. I am motivated to push myself during sports. Each response is assigned a corresponding score (6 − 1). Upon utilization in Taiwan and Malaysia, the scale demonstrated high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.95) and proved effective as a mental energy measurement tool. Mental toughness questionnaire (MTQ) The Mental Toughness Questionnaire-48 (MTQ-48), developed by Clough et al. (2002), serves as a measure of mental toughness 27 . Comprising 48 questions, this questionnaire requires participants to rate their agreement on a five-point scale (ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). Example items include: 1. I can handle pressure well in difficult situations. 2, I remain focused even when faced with setbacks. Scores are assigned to each response (1–5), collectively representing participants’ mental toughness levels. The MTQ-48 has gained widespread use as a reliable measurement tool for assessing mental toughness. Portuguese physical literacy assessment questionnaire (PPLA-Q) The Portuguese Physical Literacy Assessment Questionnaire (PPLA-Q), introduced by Mota et al. (2021), is tailored for assessing physical literacy among secondary school students 26 . The questionnaire encompasses three cognitive, psychological, and social modules. In this study, the psychological module (consisting of 46 Likert-type items) is chosen. PPLA-Q assesses four factors within the psychological domain: motivation, confidence, emotional regulation, and physical regulation. For example, the confidence factor includes items such as ‘I feel confident in my ability to participate in physical activities,’ while the emotional regulation factor includes items like ‘I can manage my emotions during physical activities.’ Participants evaluate their feelings on a five-point scale (ranging from “not at all” to “totally”), with scores assigned (0–4). To adapt the PPLA-Q for use in China, the questionnaire was translated into Mandarin by a bilingual expert and back-translated into Portuguese to ensure accuracy. The translated version was then reviewed by a panel of experts in physical education and psychology to ensure cultural relevance and clarity. The questionnaire has proven its effectiveness in measuring physical literacy among secondary school students in Portugal and demonstrated good reliability in the Chinese context (Cronbach’s α = 0.89). Achievement emotions adjective list (AEAL) The Achievement Emotions Adjective List (AEAL), developed by Raccanello et al. (2022), is a succinct self-report questionnaire designed to assess ten achievement emotions 44 . These include enjoyment, pride, hope, relief, relaxation, anxiety, anger, shame, hopelessness, and boredom. Example items include: (1) I feel proud when I achieve my goals. (2) I feel anxious when I think about failing. With favorable internal consistency demonstrated among Swiss secondary school students (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.78), the AEAL is regarded as a simple and effective tool for evaluating emotions among secondary school students. Statistical analysis Descriptive analysis was employed to assess the participation rate of undergraduate students in the curricular program. Numerical variables were characterized by their mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The data analysis was conducted using Mplus version 8. For inferential statistics, Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) analysis was utilized to investigate the structural connections between the variables. To account for potential deviations from normality, the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLR) was applied in the analysis. MLR was selected due to its robustness against non-normality and the non-independence of observations 45 . To assess the model’s adequacy, various fit indices were employed. These included criteria such as a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) below 0.05, a standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) below 0.08 with a p-value less than 0.05, a Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) exceeding 0.95, and a comparative fit index (CFI) surpassing 0.95 46 . In the context of the initial hypothesized Structural Equation Model (SEM), a significance level of p-value less than 0.05 was chosen to identify any significant pathways. Discussion The aim of this study was to explore the structural relationships among Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, Achievement Emotions Negative, and COVID-19-Induced Negative Psychological States within the hypothesized model. Upon a thorough examination of the study’s outcomes, the significant structural relationships revealed by the hypothesized model provided valuable insights into the intricate interactions between these factors. The confirmatory structural equation modeling analysis supported the connections between Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, Achievement Emotions Negative, and Achievement Emotions Negative, suggesting that variations in these factors may directly or indirectly impact individuals’ Achievement Emotions Negative to varying extents. Specifically, the research findings demonstrated that students’ Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, and Athletic Mental Energy exerted significant negative influences on Achievement Emotions Negative. This discovery implies that higher levels of Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, and Athletic Mental Energy may contribute to mitigating Achievement Emotions Negative, underscoring their importance in assisting individuals to better cope with challenging circumstances and maintain psychological well-being. Our findings align with Edwards et al. (2017) 22 on Physical Literacy’s mental health benefits but extend prior work by demonstrating Mental Toughness’s mediation role, supporting Hanson &amp; Kim’s (2007) dynamic model 24 . Notably, while Bruffaerts et al. (2018) characterized Mental Toughness as a stable trait, our Chinese adolescent sample showed its malleability through Physical Literacy—a divergence potentially explained by cultural differences in resilience development (e.g., collectivist values emphasizing effort over innate ability this reinforces Physical Literacy’s potential as an intervention target for Mental Toughness enhancement 13 .Our findings align with previous research demonstrating that physical literacy is positively associated with mental toughness and emotional well-being 22 . However, unlike some studies that suggest Mental Toughness is primarily an innate trait, our results indicate that Mental Toughness can be developed through physical literacy, supporting the dynamic model of mental toughness 24 . The mediation role of Mental Toughness challenges trait-based perspectives 13 . Our results support Hanson &amp; Kim’s (2007) dynamic model 24 suggesting Physical Literacy can cultivate Mental Toughness—particularly in collectivist cultures where effort-based growth mindsets prevail 47 . This has practical significance for designing school-based Physical Literacy programs to foster resilience. Furthermore, intriguing trends emerged from the perspective of achievement emotions. Notably, Achievement Emotions Positive exhibited a significant negative impact on Achievement Emotions Negative, suggesting that positive emotional experiences might alleviate Achievement Emotions Negative to some degree when facing challenges. Conversely, Achievement Emotions Negative showed a significant positive impact on Achievement Emotions Negative, indicating that greater levels of negative emotions could intensify negative psychological states during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023). In summary, this study has shed light on the mechanisms through which Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, and Achievement Emotions Negative factors influence individuals’ Achievement Emotions Negative, enhancing our understanding of and response to the psychological impacts of COVID-19. These findings provide valuable foundations for devising relevant intervention strategies and supporting individuals’ mental well-being. Moreover, the results of the mediation analysis have illuminated the pivotal roles played by the intermediary variables in the relationship between Physical Literacy and Achievement Emotions Negative. Specifically, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, and Achievement Emotions Negative functioned as key mediators in conveying the impact of Physical Literacy on Achievement Emotions Negative. This indicates that these factors not only directly affect Achievement Emotions Negative but also modulate the relationship between Physical Literacy and Achievement Emotions Negative, thereby influencing COVID-19-induced negative psychological states. Delving deeper into the mediation effects, it was observed that Mental Toughness, as an intermediary variable, contributed to explaining the influence of Physical Literacy on Achievement Emotions Negative. This suggests that mental toughness might play a crucial role in individuals’ adaptation to adverse situations, potentially mitigating the negative psychological impact of COVID-19. Similarly, Athletic Mental Energy, as another intermediary variable, played a role in transmitting the impact of Physical Literacy on Achievement Emotions Negative, highlighting that enhancing athletic mental energy could potentially alleviate psychological distress induced by COVID-19. Additionally, the two types of achievement emotions, Achievement Emotions Positive and Achievement Emotions Negative, as intermediary variables, also elucidated the impact of Physical Literacy on Achievement Emotions Negative. The mediating effect of positive achievement emotions indicated that cultivating positive emotional experiences might equip individuals with psychological resources to cope with negative circumstances, thus alleviating COVID-19-induced psychological distress. In contrast, the mediating effect of negative achievement emotions revealed that when confronted with challenges and difficulties, heightened negative emotions could exacerbate negative psychological states during the peak pandemic period (2020–2022). The results of the mediation analysis have provided deeper insights into the roles of Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, and Achievement Emotions Negative in influencing the process of Achievement Emotions Negative, offering crucial leads for comprehensively understanding and addressing the psychological repercussions of COVID-19. These findings contribute not only to theoretical refinement but also offer substantial guidance for the formulation of relevant intervention strategies. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations in this study. First, all data collection was confined to Lanzhou City in Gansu Province, China, thus limiting the generalizability of the model to middle school students in other regions of China. Second, all survey results relied on participants’ self-reports, lacking support from alternative forms of evidence. Self-report data may be susceptible to self-cognitive biases that could impact data accuracy. Lastly, participants may have been influenced by social desirability, potentially leading to responses aligned with perceived social expectations 48 . Thus, efforts were made to encourage participants to respond truthfully to mitigate this influence. Strengths and limitations This study has several notable strengths. First, the large sample size ( N = 729) enhances the statistical power and reliability of our findings. Second, the use of advanced structural equation modeling (SEM) allows for simultaneous examination of complex relationships among multiple constructs. Third, all measurement tools demonstrated good reliability in our Chinese adolescent sample (e.g., PPLA-Q α = 0.89; AMES α = 0.95), supporting the validity of our assessments. Finally, this is among the first studies to systematically examine the mediating roles of Mental Toughness and Athletic Mental Energy between Physical Literacy and COVID-19-related psychological outcomes. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the observed relationships. Second, reliance on self-report measures may introduce common method bias, despite our efforts to ensure anonymity and truthful responding. Third, the sample was drawn exclusively from Lanzhou, China, which may limit generalizability to other cultural contexts or age groups. Fourth, while we controlled for key demographic variables, other potential confounders (e.g., socioeconomic status, prior mental health history) were not assessed. Lastly, the study was conducted during a specific phase of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023), and the findings may not fully generalize to other public health crises. Future research directions emerging from these limitations include: (1) longitudinal studies to establish temporal precedence and causal relationships; (2) multi-method assessments combining self-reports with behavioral or physiological measures; (3) cross-cultural replications to test the universality of these findings; and (4) intervention studies examining whether enhancing Physical Literacy leads to improvements in Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, and psychological resilience. Conclusion Through the analysis of confirmatory structural equation modeling, we have established the connections among Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, Achievement Emotions Negative, and Achievement Emotions Negative, underscoring their roles in individual psychological states. The research findings reveal negative influences of Physical Literacy, Mental Toughness, and Athletic Mental Energy on Achievement Emotions Negative, indicating that positive changes in these factors can alleviate psychological distress caused by COVID-19. Achievement Emotions Positive demonstrates a negative impact on Achievement Emotions Negative, suggesting that fostering positive emotions can mitigate the psychological strain induced by COVID-19. However, Achievement Emotions Negative exhibits a positive impact on Achievement Emotions Negative, potentially exacerbating negative psychological states in individuals. Furthermore, the mediation analysis highlights the crucial role of intermediary variables in the Physical Literacy-Achievement Emotions Negative relationship. Mental Toughness, Athletic Mental Energy, Achievement Emotions Positive, and Achievement Emotions Negative as intermediary variables influence the COVID-19-induced negative psychological state by moderating the relationship between Physical Literacy and Achievement Emotions Negative. In conclusion, this study offers substantial insights for understanding psychological responses to large-scale public health crises. Nurturing positive physical literacy, mental toughness, and emotions can aid individuals in effectively confronting challenges and maintaining psychological well-being. These research findings provide valuable guidance for formulating intervention strategies and supportive measures.
