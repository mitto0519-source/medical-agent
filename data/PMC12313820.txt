Abstract Aim Suicidality remains a significant clinical concern for individuals with attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) receiving treatment with ADHD medications, especially children and adolescents. This retrospective cohort study examined the association between suicidal ideation and the initiation of ADHD medications among children and adolescents with ADHD. Methods Using children's mental health registry data from the National Center for Global Health and Medicine, this study included 431 patients aged 5–15 years diagnosed with ADHD and recorded their use of ADHD medications up to 5 years after the diagnosis. The baseline assessment of suicidal ideation was based on Item 10 of the Depression Self‐Rating Scale for Children. The primary outcome was the incidence of the initiation of ADHD medications within 3 months after the diagnosis of ADHD. Results At baseline assessment, 143 (33.2%) of 431 patients with ADHD had suicidal ideation. Multivariable logistic regression models revealed that suicidal ideation, older age, and more severe ADHD symptoms were all associated with the initiation of ADHD medications within 3 months after the diagnosis. Conclusion These results suggest that among children and adolescents with ADHD, caution and vigilant monitoring for suicidal ideation are crucial before initiating ADHD medications; follow‐up is necessary according to the severity of their condition. attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder psychopharmacology suicidality suicidal ideation None Assessment of suicidal ideation The baseline assessment of suicidal ideation was based on the Depression Self‐Rating Scale for Children (DSRS‐C), specifically Item 10 (“I think life isn't worth living,” scored from 0–2 points), which determines presence or absence of suicidal ideation. 35 In this study, suicidal ideation at the baseline assessment was collapsed into a single binary measure: 0 = no suicidal ideation (score of 0: never ), and 1 = with suicidal ideation (score of 1: sometimes or 2: most of the time ). Outcome measures The incidence of the initiation of ADHD medications was examined at five time points, specifically at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 18 months after the diagnosis of ADHD. Four ADHD medications approved in Japan were used for the outcome in this study: methylphenidate (osmotic‐release oral system formulation), lisdexamfetamine, guanfacine, and atomoxetine. The initiation of ADHD medications was collapsed into a single binary measure: 0 = no initiation of ADHD medications, and 1 = initiation of ADHD medications. Covariates The severity of ADHD symptoms was evaluated using the Japanese version of the ADHD‐Rating Scale‐IV (ADHD‐RS‐IV). 36 The ADHD‐RS‐IV consists of 18 items each rated from 0–3, with the highest possible score of 54 indicating a condition of the highest severity. 37 Additionally, the severity of depression was evaluated using the DSRS‐C. 38 The DSRS‐C is a self‐reported questionnaire that measures severity of depressive symptoms in the preceding week across 18 items each rated from 0–2 (i.e., never = 0, sometimes = 1, most of the time = 2), with the highest possible score of 36 indicating the highest severity of depression. To address the confounding effects of other variables that have been associated with the initiation of ADHD medications in previous children's mental health registry studies, 39 , 40 the following covariates evaluated at the baseline assessment were included as a single binary measure: age, gender, comorbid psychiatric disorder, history of abuse, refusal to attend school, history of suicidal behaviors, ODD symptoms, antisocial behaviors, and child‐to‐parent violence. Participants were considered to exhibit “antisocial behavior” if they engaged in illegal activities, such as smoking, drinking, illicit drug use, shoplifting, or causing injury to others. 39 ODD symptoms were assessed using the Oppositional Defiant Behavior Inventory (ODBI), 41 which comprises 18 items rated from 0 to 3 based on DSM‐IV‐TR diagnostic criteria for ODD, with a maximum score of 54. An ODBI score greater than 20 is indicative of ODD 42 ; therefore, ODD symptoms at baseline were categorized into a binary measure: 0 = no ODD symptoms (score &lt; 20), 1 = presence of ODD symptoms (score ≥ 20). Statistical analysis Demographic and clinical variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and as frequency and percentage for binary and categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p &lt; 0.05 (two‐tailed). To examine the relationships between ADHD symptoms and either the DSRS‐C total score or the DSRS‐C suicide item (Item 10), Spearman's rho correlation coefficients were calculated. Stepwise logistic regression analysis was performed to determine whether the incidence of the initiation of ADHD medications independently predicted suicidal ideation or other demographic and clinical variables. This involved univariate regression analyses for the participants' sociodemographic and clinical variables, in which the identified significant factors underwent stepwise logistic regression analysis. All statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac (Version 28.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). RESULTS Characteristics of the study participants Among 431 children and adolescents with ADHD at baseline assessment (mean [SD] age, 9.8 [2.4] years; 333 males [77.3%]), 143 (33.2%) had suicidal ideation. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1 . There were no significant correlations between the DSRS‐C total score and the ADHD‐RS total score ( ρ = 0.06, p = 0.24), the Hyperactivity–Impulsivity subscale score ( ρ = 0.02, p = 0.73), or the Inattention subscale score ( ρ = 0.09, p = 0.05). Similarly, there were no significant correlations between the DSRS‐C suicide item (Item 10) and the ADHD‐RS total score ( ρ = −0.004, p = 0.94), the Hyperactivity–Impulsivity subscale score ( ρ = 0.009, p = 0.86), or the Inattention subscale score ( ρ = −0.02, p = 0.75). Predictors of the initiation of ADHD medications during the follow‐up periods The median follow‐up time was 21 (range, 0–72) months. Significant differences in the incidence of the initiation of ADHD medications within 3, 6, 12, and 18 months were observed between patients with and without suicidal ideation (Table 2 ). Multivariable logistic regression models revealed that the initiation of ADHD medications within 3 months was associated with age, severity of ADHD symptoms, and suicidal ideation (Table 3 ). Thus, for children and adolescents with ADHD, a greater degree of suicidal ideation was associated with greater odds of initiating ADHD medications within 3 months of the ADHD diagnosis. In addition, suicidal ideation was also associated with greater odds of being prescribed with ADHD medications within 6, 12, and 18 months after the diagnosis (Table S1 ). DISCUSSION This study investigated whether suicidal ideation was associated with a delay in the initiation of ADHD medications among children and adolescents with ADHD. Specifically, the initiation of ADHD medications was examined at five time points (i.e., 1, 3, 6, 12, and 18 months) after the diagnosis of ADHD. Contrary to our hypothesis, a greater degree of suicidal ideation was associated with greater odds of initiating ADHD medications within 3 months after the diagnosis. Our findings emphasize the necessity for caution and vigilant monitoring of suicidal ideation among children and adolescents with ADHD who are receiving treatment with ADHD medications. These results may reflect real‐world use of ADHD medications. Our findings are partly consistent with those of a previous multicenter, 1‐year prospective observational study by Hong et al. in Central Europe and East Asia. 31 Their study included 1068 children and adolescents newly diagnosed with symptoms of ADHD and investigated the initiation of treatment (i.e., pharmacotherapy and/or psychotherapy) at baseline. Older age and a higher severity of ADHD symptoms were significantly associated with the initiation of treatment. One strength of our study was the use of real‐world data regarding the initiation of ADHD medications. Although most clinical guidelines for ADHD recommend pharmacotherapy as the first‐line treatment for ADHD, 12 , 13 , 14 the Japanese clinical guidelines for ADHD recommend nonpharmacological treatments (i.e., the first‐line treatment is school environment management and/or psychosocial treatment, and the second‐line treatment is pharmacological treatment). 15 Unfortunately, the impairment from ADHD symptoms remains a concern despite the implementation of the 2018 National Institute for Healthcare and Excellence (NICE) guidelines, which suggested that medication should be offered after environmental modifications, such as reducing noise or distractions. 43 In this study, however, those with suicidal ideation received more pharmacological treatment than those without suicidal ideation. One possible explanation for our findings is that internalizing or externalizing problems, which are associated with irritability, 44 in children and adolescents with hidden suicidality are treated by clinicians with ADHD medications. Although not listed among its core symptoms, irritability or emotional dysregulation are considered common features associated with ADHD. 3 In addition, evidence suggests that ADHD medications, compared to placebo, exert positive effects on irritability or symptoms associated with ODD or CD. 45 , 46 Another possibility is that the treating clinician may have better access to pharmacological treatments that can quickly control both ADHD symptoms and suicidality. However, nonpharmacological treatments, such as environment management, psychosocial treatment, or environmental modification, may be too time‐consuming for both clinicians and families who are in desperate need of a quick solution. Another notable aspect of this study is its retrospective cohort design at a single center, which is the only national center that has a ward specializing in child and adolescent psychiatry. Hence, it is assumed that the study cohort had severe symptoms of ADHD and externalizing or internalizing symptoms. However, a previous population‐based case series found that a higher risk of suicidality before pharmacological treatment might reflect emerging psychiatric symptoms that lead to medical consultations, which can lead to the initiation of ADHD treatment. 47 Our study suggests that ADHD possesses the link among ADHD symptoms, externalizing and internalizing symptoms, and suicidal ideation; this is therefore being associated with the increased use of ADHD drugs in children and adolescents. Although rare, several clinical guidelines for children and adolescents with ADHD note an increase in suicidal ideation with atomoxetine, which is noted by a black box warning issued by the Food and Drug Administration. 12 , 13 , 14 These guidelines recommend carefully monitoring for suicidal ideation, especially in the early treatment phase. Moreover, clinicians are wary of the potential side‐effects of long‐term medication use. 48 A previous cross‐sectional survey reported that pediatricians and psychiatrists were more likely to prioritize ADHD symptoms while prescribing stimulant medications, but were less responsive to parents' concerns about adverse effects. 49 The findings of our study highlight the need for educational efforts among clinicians, hopefully to improve the recognition of suicidality in children and adolescents with ADHD. Although suicidal ideation is a strong predictor of suicidality, there was no statistically significant difference in the history of suicidal behavior between the groups with and without suicidal ideation (data not shown; Fisher's exact test, p = 0.09). ADHD has recently been recognized as a major risk factor for suicide. 10 Our results suggest the need to evaluate the severity of ADHD symptoms and suicidality as distinct psychopathological dimensions. Further research on interventions that target pathways linking ADHD symptoms to suicidality among children and adolescents can help optimize the initiation of pharmacological treatment in this population. This study has several limitations. First, despite the relatively large sample size of this study, the generalizability of our results can be further improved by conducting replication studies across multiple centers or using large data sets. Second, suicidal ideation was assessed using Item 10 of the DSRS‐C, but the reliability and validity of this scale for the assessment of suicidal ideation has not been fully investigated. As a result, we could not entirely control for the influence of depressive symptom severity on suicidal ideation. If possible, standardized rating scales for suicide risk (e.g., the Columbia Algorithm of Suicide Assessment) should be used for the management of adverse events during ADHD medication treatment. 50 Recently, however, the US Preventive Services Task Force reported that in children and adolescents, there is insufficient evidence regarding the benefits and harms of screening for suicide risk. 51 Third, because the DSRS‐C is a self‐rating scale, it may have been difficult for younger children to complete accurately. Fourth, the participants of our study had various comorbidities (e.g., autism spectrum disorder, tic disorder, depression), but there was an insufficient number of participants for conducting sub‐analyses for each disorder. Fifth, this study included only 431 out of 3018 eligible cases, which may indicate a significant sample bias. Finally, we did not have access to the data on other psychotropic medications (e.g., antipsychotics or antidepressants), which could be a potential explanatory factor for the initiation of ADHD medications. Further studies are needed to replicate our findings among populations with specific comorbid psychiatric disorders and those that use other psychotropic medications. In conclusion, suicidal ideation is associated with increased ADHD medication use within 3 months after the diagnosis of ADHD in children and adolescents. Our study suggests that ADHD possesses the link among ADHD symptoms, externalizing and internalizing symptoms, and suicidal ideation, which are all associated with an increased use of ADHD medications. Suicidal ideation is a serious risk for children and adolescents with ADHD medications. Hence, this population should be carefully monitored for suicidal ideation before the initiation of ADHD medications and followed up according to the severity of their condition.
