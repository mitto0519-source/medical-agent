Abstract Background We have previously shown that treatment with recombinant human growth hormone (GH) influences testicular growth in children with GH deficiency (GHD) and have suggested that GH plays a role in testicular growth in childhood. Little evidence is available on testicular function in post-pubertal GHD patients. Objective This prospective controlled study was undertaken to evaluate testicular function in patients with GHD. Patients and Methods Post-pubertal patients with non-syndromic GHD over the age of 16 years were enrolled. Each patient underwent to the assessment of serum levels of gonadotropins and total testosterone (TT), conventional sperm parameters, and testicular volume (TV) measured by ultrasound examination. Age-matched healthy subjects served as controls. Patients with disorders capable of interfering with testicular function were excluded. Results 26 patients with GHD and 25 age-matched post-pubertal controls were enrolled. They did not differ in serum luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, and TT levels. However, GHD patients had lower semen volume, total sperm count, progressive motility, and total motility values, and a higher prevalence of oligozoospermia compared to controls. No difference was found in sperm concentration and normal morphology. Importantly, GHD patients had lower TV, and a higher prevalence of testicular hypotrophy. Conclusion This is the first evidence of mildly impaired sperm parameters and TV in GHD patients compared to healthy controls. The integrity of the GH-IGF1 axis in prepuberty is important for achieving normal testicular function in adulthood. Evaluating testicular growth over time in GHD children and measuring TV and sperm parameters in postpubertal GHD boys is advisable. Introduction Infertility is a widespread issue worldwide. A global epidemiological study conducted across all continents estimated that approximately 48.5 million couples were infertile in 2010 [1] . In nearly half of these cases, male infertility was a contributing factor, either independently or alongside female infertility. Most infertile men exhibit abnormalities in sperm parameters [2] . In addition to its high prevalence, a significant proportion of male infertility cases remain undiagnosed despite extensive diagnostic evaluation. This condition is referred to as idiopathic infertility, and in large clinical series, it is estimated that up to 60 % of cases fall into this category, according to some studies [3] . Furthermore, sperm concentration and total sperm count have declined over the last 40 years, with a more significant downward trend observed since the 2000 s [4] . However, it is challenging to draw definitive conclusions, as semen analyses conducted over this period may not have adhered to standardized criteria, such as those outlined in the current WHO guidelines. Given the concerning epidemiological trend, it is essential to gain a deeper understanding of the factors driving changes in sperm parameters. This knowledge could help reduce the prevalence of idiopathic male infertility and facilitate the development of targeted treatments that address the recently identified pathophysiological mechanisms. Some evidence indicates that testicular dysfunction can arise early in life, at least in a percentage of cases with idiopathic infertility. Bilateral testicular hypotrophy has been observed in around 14 % of apparently healthy young Italian adults aged 18, and is associated with high-risk lifestyle behaviors [5] . Recently, the detrimental effect of obesity on testicular development during childhood and adolescence has been highlighted [6] , with several mechanisms proposed to explain this link [7] . The study of testicular structure and function in prepubertal age can help to acquire new insights to better understand the factors responsible for its growth in the first two decades of life. In children, the testis is predominantly composed of immature Sertoli cells (SCs) capable of actively proliferating [8] . This proliferation is enhanced by increased follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels at mini puberty [9] . When puberty begins, SCs switch to the mature state under the effects of intratubular testosterone and lose the ability to proliferate [10] . The extent of SC proliferation during childhood is important for adult fertility because, after puberty, each SC can promote the differentiation of a finite number of germ cells [11] – specifically, 4 in adolescence and 2 in adulthood [12] . Therefore, monitoring testicular volume (TV] from childhood to quantify the expansion of the immature SC pool could be useful for early identification of testicular dysfunction [13] . The development of SCs relies on various factors. Although the main hormone involved is FSH [14] , other factors have also been identified over time. These include activin A, glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor, insulin, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1), epidermal growth factor, fibroblast growth factor, retinoic acid, transforming growth factor-α, interleukin-1, and thyroid hormones [15] , [16] , [17] , [18] , [19] , [20] . In this regard, insulin and IGF1 are important for SC development. Indeed, an in vivo study demonstrated that FSH requires activation of the IGF1 signaling pathway to promote adequate SC growth [21] . Using porcine neonatal SCs, we previously found that these cells did not proliferate when incubated with FSH or growth hormone (GH) alone, whereas SCs proliferated in vitro when incubated with IGF1 alone, reaching the maximum increase by co-incubating them with FSH and IGF1 [22] . That FSH and IGF1 have a synergistic effect seems to be supported by several studies which indicate the existence of a crosstalk between the two hormones [23] . In particular, the insulin receptor substrate has been suggested as the hub linking between the FSH and IGF1 pathways [24] . Further indicator of the importance of the IGF1 signaling pathway for gonadal function is given by the “physiologically acromegalic” levels that IGF1 reaches in peripuberty, leading to the hypothesis that high levels of this hormone are important for the maturation of gonads and sexual organs at this stage [25] . In fact, the peak of IGF1 occurs two years after the pubertal spurt, indicating that IGF1 could be involved not only in stature growth but also in the growth of the gonads that acquire functional capacity in this period of life [25] . Although extensive data link IGF1 to testicular development in vitro and in experimental animals, little information is available in humans [23] . In this context, GH deficiency (GHD) may represent a suitable model to evaluate the effects of the GH-IGF1 axis on testicular growth in humans. GHD is a rare disease, characterized by insufficient production of GH by the pituitary gland [26] . In children, the main sign is short stature; the diagnosis is made with the lack of response to stimulation tests, and treatment is based on the administration of recombinant human GH (rhGH) [26] . Children with GHD have low serum IGF1 levels due to insufficient GH production. Treatment with recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) can directly raise IGF1 levels, which in turn stimulates the growth of various organs and tissues, including the testes [27] . We have previously shown that rhGH treatment influences testicular growth and the onset of puberty in children with GHD by increasing serum IGF1 levels, and have suggested that the GH-IGF1 axis plays a role in testicular growth during childhood [27] . However, it is not known whether GHD status in childhood has any impact on the adult testicular function and spermatogenic potential. With these premises, we evaluated testicular function using a prospective controlled study design in postpubertal GHD patients previously treated with rhGH. To accomplish this, serum gonadotropin and testosterone levels, sperm parameters, and TV were assessed in a previously investigated and treated GHD cohort [27] and compared with those of healthy peers. Patients and methods Experimental protocol This prospective controlled study was conducted on post-pubertal GHD patients, who were diagnosed and followed-up at the Pediatric Endocrinology Unit, University-Teaching Hospital Policlinico “G. Rodolico-San Marco” (Catania, Italy), from May 2002 to December 2019. They underwent treatment with rhGH, which was discontinued upon reaching the therapeutic target. Patients satisfying the study inclusion criteria were recalled from the Division of Endocrinology, Metabolic Diseases and Nutrition of the same hospital, and their testicular function was assessed by measuring gonadal hormones and performing sperm analysis and scrotal ultrasound, from April 2021 to April 2023. Age-matched healthy postpubertal men who attended the Division of Endocrinology, Metabolic Diseases and Nutrition for an andrological check-up served as controls. The study outcomes were serum luteinizing hormone (LH), FSH, and total testosterone (TT) levels, conventional sperm parameters, and TV measured by ultrasound examination. Patient selection We enrolled patients older than 16 years diagnosed with GHD and who had been treated with rhGH for disease management at an initial dose of 25 µg/kg/day during childhood, with adjustments made within a range of 25–50 µg/kg/day [27] , as per the guidelines [28] . Their treatment was discontinued upon reaching the therapeutic target (growth velocity &lt; 2–2.5 cm/year) [29] at the Pediatric Endocrinology Unit. At the time of enrolment in the present study, they had to be postpubertal (Tanner 5) and be able to masturbate to collect a semen sample. Syndromic or neurodevelopmental diseases, genetic disorders, celiac disease, thalassemia, systemic disorders (e.g., kidney and/or liver diseases), head or testicular trauma, tumors, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine diseases (hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, Cushing syndrome, and hypopituitarism), and varicocele of grade higher than II according to the Dubin and Amelar scale [30] were considered a reason for exclusion. A group of healthy postpubertal men matched for age, without relevant comorbidities or testicular disorders, served as controls. They were enrolled among those who consulted our Division for andrological control. Men with systemic (liver, kidney, cardiac diseases, etc.) or andrological (varicocele, urogenital infections, previous cryptorchidism, testicular trauma or tumors, hypogonadism, etc.) diseases were excluded. Hormone measurements The measurement of hormones was performed by venous sampling carried out from the antecubital vein at 8:00–8:30 a.m., on an overnight fast. All measurements were made at the central laboratory of the University-Teaching Hospital Policlinico “San Marco-G. Rodolico” (Catania, Italy) by electrochemioluminescence. Reference values were: 1.14–8.75 IU/L for LH, 0.95–11.95 IU/L for FSH, and 2.5–8.77 ng/mL for TT. Scrotal ultrasound TV was assessed by scrotal ultrasound. To measure testicular diameters, the Mylab 25 ultrasound instrument (Esaote SpA, Genoa, Italy) equipped with a linear probe (7.5 MHz) was used. TV was calculated using the ellipsoid formula (length x width x thickness x 0.52). Values between 15 and 25 cm 3 were considered normal [31] . Ultrasound examination was also used to evaluate the testicular texture, echo structure, and vascularization (using Doppler signal). Semen analysis Semen analysis was performed by two qualified Seminologists of the Division of Endocrinology, Metabolic Diseases and Nutrition, University-Teaching Hospital Policlinico “G. Rodolico-San Marco” (Catania, Italy), strictly following the 2021 guidelines of the World Health Organization (WHO) [32] . The seminal fluid was collected in a sterile container by masturbation, after 2–7 days of ejaculatory abstinence. After liquefaction, 10 µL of well-mixed undiluted sample was placed in a clean Neubauer counting chamber, maintained at 37 °C, covered with a glass coverslip, examined at 200x or 400x magnification, and then diluted for sperm counting. Sperm motility was characterized as progressive and non-progressive by random scanning of 200 spermatozoa. The sum of both was used to calculate total motility. Morphology was assessed on 200 spermatozoa using the Papanicolaou staining. Finally, the peroxidase assay was used to evaluate the concentration of leucocytes and to distinguish them from other round cells such as immature germ cells [32] . Statistical analysis Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for non-skewed variables, while non-normally distributed continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). The distribution of values was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Student’s t -test was used to retrospectively compare testicular growth in terms of TV during childhood and adolescence of enrolled GHD patients to published normative values in a healthy cohort [33] . Comparison of study endpoints in postpubertal patients and controls was performed using Student t -test for independent samples or Mann-Whitney U test for normally and non-normally distributed variables, respectively. The difference in the percentage of patients and controls with hormonal values ​​outside the reference range, low TV, or alterations in sperm parameters was calculated using the Chi-squared test. In particular, a low TV was considered for values &lt; 12 mL, a condition of oligozoospermia for values of sperm concentration &lt; 15 mil/ml and total sperm count &lt; 39 mil/ejaculate, asthenozoospermia for progressive motility &lt; 32 % and/or total motility &lt; 40 %, and teratozoospermia for percentage of spermatozoa with normal morphology &lt; 4 %. Statistical analysis was performed using MedCalc Software Ltd. (Ostend, Belgium), version 19.6–64-bit. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Ethical approval This study was conducted at the Division of Endocrinology, Metabolic Diseases and Nutrition of the University Teaching Hospital Policlinico “G. Rodolico-San Marco”, University of Catania (Catania, Italy), in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All participants provided informed consent for the analysis of their personal and clinical data contained within the electronic medical records utilized in our Division, following a comprehensive explanation of the study’s objectives and the nature of all procedures involved that are commonly used in clinical practice. Results Descriptive analysis The initial cohort for this study consisted of 115 adolescents and young adults with GHD that were enrolled in our previous study [27] . Of these, 26 Caucasian patients fulfilled the study inclusion criteria and were enrolled. The remaining 67 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Finally, 25 Caucasian adolescents and young adults were included in the control group ( Fig. 1 ). At enrollment, the patient group had a mean age of 18.8 ± 3.3 years, while the control group had a mean age of 20.1 ± 3.6 years (p = 0.18). During childhood, all patients were treated with rhGH, at a dose ranging from 25 to 40 µg/kg/day (a mean of 27.6 ± 3.2 µg/kg/day). They started the treatment at a mean age of 11.1 ± 2.14 years when all were at Tanner stage 1. For the current caseload, the reasons for starting therapy after the age of 10 were late presentation at the center and lack of compliance. The mean treatment duration was 51.3 ± 23.2 months (range: 13–95 months). Age at discontinuation of rhGH for having achieved the target height was 15.0 ± 4.0 years. Testicular growth over time of GHD patients enrolled during treatment is shown in Fig. 2 , with means and SD detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Consistent with our previous study [27] , the testes of children and adolescents with GHD enrolled in the present study showed lower volumetric growth than to normative values [33] . Hormone measurements No significant difference in serum of LH, FSH, and TT levels was found between GHD patients and the control group ( Table 1 ). Nevertheless, 2 (7.7 %) GHD patients showed TT lower than 2.5 ng/dL, 2 (7.7 %) had abnormal FSH values (one below and one above the reference range), and one (3.4 %) had LH below the reference range. No abnormalities were found in the control group. Sperm parameters Patients with GHD had significantly lower semen volume, total sperm count, and progressive and total motility than the control group. No significant difference was observed in sperm concentration and the percentage of spermatozoa with normal morphology ( Fig. 3 ). Four of 26 GHD patients (15.4 %) had oligozoospermia, 15 (57.7 %) had asthenozoospermia, and 2 (7.7 %) had teratozoospermia. Within the control group, there were no cases of oligozoospermia or teratozoospermia, while 8 men (30.8 %) had asthenozoospermia. The prevalence of oligozoospermia was significantly higher in GHD patients compared to controls (15.4 % vs. 0 %, p = 0.04). In patients with GHD compared to controls, a trend towards an increase in the prevalence of asthenozoospermia was found (57.7 % vs. 30.8 %, p = 0.051), and no difference in the prevalence of teratozoospermia (7.7 % vs. 0 %, p = 0.15). Testicular volume Right, left, and total TV (given by the sum of right and left volume) were significantly lower in the group of patients with GHD compared to controls ( Fig. 4 ). Twelve out of 26 GHD patients (46.2 %) showed one or both TV lower than 12 ml, while only 3 cases (11.5 %) were found in the control group, indicating a significantly higher prevalence of testicular hypotrophy in GHD patients compared to controls (p = 0.0059). Discussion This prospective controlled study was undertaken to evaluate testicular function in postpubertal patients with GHD who had been treated with rhGH in their childhood and who had already discontinued the treatment at the time of enrollment since the achievement of their target height. Consistent with published data [27] , their TV during the first two decades of life demonstrated a slower growth trajectory compared to previously published normative values [33] . Whether this is associated with worse testicular function in adulthood has never been explored. On this basis, we herein reported the evidence of mildly impaired gonadal function in these subjects. Indeed, GHD patients showed significantly lower semen volume, total sperm count, progressive and total motility, and TV compared to age-matched controls, in the absence of any difference in the serum levels of LH, FSH, and TT. Noteworthy, the prevalence of oligozoospermia and testicular hypotrophy was significantly higher in the GHD group compared to controls. Data reported to date on testicular function in adult patients with GHD are scarce. An in vivo study demonstrated the efficacy of recombinant bovine GH treatment in improving sperm motility in adult rats with GHD, which was not observed with the administration of placebo [34] . Similarly, another study showed a lower sperm concentration, motility, and percentage of spermatozoa with normal morphology in GHD rats compared to the healthy ones [35] . Furthermore, treatment with IGF1 increased sperm motility and the percentage of spermatozoa with normal morphology in GHD rats [36] . This indicates the positive effect of the replacement of this hormone on sperm parameters in case of its deficiency. Despite these data, the evaluation of spermatogenesis in patients with GHD has not been explored extensively. A single dated study conducted on 11 patients with acquired GHD evaluated the effects of rhGH treatment on sperm parameters, finding only a significant improvement in semen volume compared to pretreatment values [37] . However, the design of this study differs significantly from ours, as it involves acquired GHD. In these cases, patients were not exposed to GH/IGF1 deficiency during childhood, a critical period for SC proliferation. Furthermore, a comparison with a healthy cohort was not performed and the effects of GH therapy (rather than GHD status) were evaluated. In the 1990 s, a series of studies were conducted to evaluate the role of GH on spermatogenesis. Notably, GH has been administered to non-GHD adults with severe idiopathic oligozoospermia without showing any improvement in sperm count [38] , [39] , [40] . These data probably dampened the initial enthusiasm regarding the role of GH on spermatogenesis and possibly led to believe that deficiency of the GH/IGF1 axis had no repercussions on spermatogenesis. However, current knowledge has allowed us to better understand the physiology of testicular development in pediatric age, as well as the importance of a correct expansion of the Sertolian pool for adequate spermatogenesis in adulthood [10] , [12] , [13] . According to preclinical evidence, the GH-IGF1 axis appears to have an important role in influencing the proliferation of immature SCs and testicular development during childhood [21] , [22] , [23] . The data in humans appear to point in the same direction. Interestingly, a prospective, randomized, controlled study of 124 short non-GHD children treated with rhGH showed a notable increase in TV in the treated group [41] . Similarly, a retrospective cross-sectional study from our group reported a significant TV growth over time in GHD children treated with rhGH, compared to age-matched GHD patients but not yet treated. In multivariate analysis, the percentage of TV increase was influenced by the dosage and duration of treatment [27] . Further suggesting the importance of the integrity of the GH-IGF1 axis in promoting adequate testicular growth during childhood, delayed puberty and poor progression of testicular growth are common features in patients with Laron syndrome, a genetic disease characterized by insensitivity to the GH [42] . The following practical clinical messages emerge from this study. Evaluation of patients with GHD should include testicular volumetric growth over time during developmental age and the evaluation of andrological health in postpubertal age (sperm parameters and TV). Furthermore, continuation of GH treatment in childhood-onset GHD until both final height and maximal TV have been achieved should be pursued. A reassuring message also comes from the data of this study. Although conventional sperm parameters and TV were found significantly reduced compared to healthy controls, we found no major alterations. This could be ascribed to the fact that these patients had been adequately compensated with rhGH replacement therapy during childhood. However, the prevalence of testicular hypotrophy and oligozoospermia was significantly higher in GHD patients compared to controls. Finally, the data allow us to speculate the role of the GH-IGF1 axis during the age of testicular development for the future function of the male gonad. The alarming prevalence of idiopathic forms of infertility [3] and the finding of testicular hypotrophy in a high percentage of apparently healthy 18-year-old boys [5] justify the importance of adopting measures for the primary prevention of male infertility. In children with stunted testicular growth, the function of the GH-IGF1 axis should be evaluated. Reasons to take our findings with caution include the low number of patients enrolled. Unfortunately, often due to cultural preconceptions, many young people who can undergo semen analysis refuse to do so. This underlines the importance of greater awareness in performing semen analysis of patients with GHD, especially in peripubertal age. Other factors that further limit teenagers' ability to undergo semen analysis include limited access to healthcare, financial constraints, embarrassment, and variability in sexual maturity among adolescents and young adults. Furthermore, the normative testicular growth values we used (see Fig. 1 ) came from a study conducted on a predominantly Caucasian cohort (93 %) in the Netherlands [33] . Although we cannot exclude a difference in testicular growth curves in healthy Italian boys, normative data are not yet available in the Italian population. In addition, although the WHO recommends obtaining two semen analyses to account for individual variability, the present study conducted statistical analysis using only the single semen sample provided by the enrolled patients and controls, as a second sample was unavailable for most participants. On the other hand, the prospective design of the study and the methodology used to evaluate the outcomes represent the strengths of the study. Semen analysis carried out according to the World Health Organization criteria, conducted by two expert semiologists at our Andrological Center, recognized for excellence and accredited by the European Academy of Andrology [43] makes semen parameters reliable. Finally, another strong point is that the TV was measured by the same operator (A.C.) using ultrasound, thus avoiding the overestimation of the data that is generally obtained with the Prader orchidometer [44] , but also issues related to inter-operator variability of measurement. Conclusion In conclusion, we previously reported that testicular growth over time is influenced by the dose and duration of rhGH treatment in a cohort of GHD boys [27] . The present study prospectively evaluated testicular function in postpubertal patients with GHD. By enrolling 26 GHD patients and 25 age-matched healthy controls, we found that semen volume, total sperm count, progressive and total motility were significantly lower and the prevalence of oligozoospermia in GHD patients was higher than in controls. Also, TV was significantly lower, and the prevalence of testicular hypotrophy was significantly higher, in the absence of significant difference in serum levels of gonadotropins and TT. This represents the first evidence indicating the presence of abnormal TV and mildly impaired spermatogenesis in GHD patients. Based on these data, it is advisable to evaluate testicular growth over time in GHD children, and to measure TV and sperm parameters in postpubertal GHD boys. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings. Funding statement The authors declare no funding. Attestation Statement.
