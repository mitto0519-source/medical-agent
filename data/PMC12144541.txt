This condition typically affects individuals who have travelled from low altitude to high altitude (≧2500 m a.s.l.) or who have lived at high altitude for a long period of time. Chronic plateau disease can be diagnosed based on the Qinghai Diagnostic Criteria for Chronic Plateau Disease published by the 6th International Conference on Plateau Medicine and Hypoxia Physiology in 2004. Plateau heart disease is characterized by an average pulmonary artery pressure of 30 mmHg or a systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 50 mmHg, along with right ventricular hypertrophy and moderate hypoxemia, but without erythrocytosis (where hemoglobin levels are below 19 g/dl for females and 21 g/dl for males). However, it is challenging to distinguish between plateau heart disease and plateau chronic pulmonary heart disease in clinical practice. This paper reports a case of plateau heart disease who had recurrent symptoms of chest tightness and shortness of breath for six years but was misdiagnosed as chronic pulmonary heart disease. As such, this paper may contribute to a better understanding of this disease and its differential diagnosis from chronic pulmonary heart disease. CLINICAL INFORMATION General information A 49-year-old male patient developed chest tightness and shortness of breath six years ago, which became worse after activity, and coughed up a small amount of white foamy sputum intermittently. However, the patient had no obvious chest pain or precordial pain. The patient was diagnosed as chronic pulmonary heart disease in several hospitals and received irregular oxygen therapy at home and Lung Tonic Pill (a Chinese patent medicine for lung diseases) and other drugs. One month ago, the patient experienced right-sided chest pain after activity, which was intermittent and dull and accompanied by general fatigue, chest tightness and shortness of breath. As the symptoms did not improve after taking theophylline delayed-release tablets and buckwheat capsules, the patient was referred to Qinghai Provincial People's Hospital. The patient had a history of hypertension for 6 years with the highest blood pressure being 190/130 mmHg. The long-term oral administration of “felodipine extended-release tablets 5 mg” produced no satisfactory control of blood pressure. The blood pressure at the time of admission was 158/91 mmHg, which remains above the normal range of 120/80 mmHg. His eyelids and conjunctiva were congested, and his lips were mildly cyanotic. Cyanosis of the extremities, clubbing of the fingers (Figs. 1 and 2 ). The thorax was symmetrical and both lungs were normal on palpation, percussion was clear, lungs showed coarse respiratory sounds with no obvious dry or wet rales or pleural friction sounds. The heart boundary was not large and a pronounced second heart sound can be detected in the area where the pulmonary valve is auscultated. No pathological murmur was heard in each valve auscultation area, and no edema was observed in in either lower limb. The examination in 2020 showed that the hemoglobin level was 201 g/l and the lung function was normal, but no other information was provided. The medical history revealed that the patient was born in Hu zhu County (&gt;2500 m a.s.l), Qinghai Province, China, and lived there for a long period of time, but he had travelled to and from Chaka Salt Farms (3059 m a.s.l) many times in his youth over a period of 10 years. The patient complained of chest tightness, shortness of breath and cyanosis once at high altitude, which were relieved by oxygen inhalation. The patient has a 15-year smoking history (15 cigarettes a day) and quit smoking 6 years ago. The medical history suggested that the disease was present for at least 6 years and was misdiagnosed as chronic pulmonary heart disease caused by bronchopulmonary, pulmonary vascular and thoracic diseases. He was admitted to the hospital to undergo lung function tests, chest CT, pulmonary angiography and polysomnography. Diagnosis and differential diagnosis Diagnosis Plateau heart disease Plateau pulmonary hypertension Cardiac function class II Type I Respiratory Failure Hyperuricemia Hypertension Diagnostic principles: The patient is a middle-aged man and has a chronic course of the disease. The condition is recurrent with main symptoms of chest tightness and shortness of breath that become worse after activity. Cardiac ultrasound suggests moderate pulmonary hypertension. Chest CT suggests no apparent emphysema. Pulmonary angiography also suggests no abnormalities. Therefore, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), bronchial asthma, pulmonary embolism and sleep apnoea hypoventilation syndrome could be excluded. High altitude related hypertension is confirmed because the patient has worked at high altitude (3000 m) for 10 years and lived at middle and high altitude (2500 m) for a long time. Differential diagnosis Plateau heart disease should be differentiated from plateau chronic pulmonary heart disease, hypertensive heart disease, and plateau congenital heart disease. Treatment Oxygen therapy (low-flow oxygen,2 l/min) and intermittent diuresis (spironolactone 20 m/day and furosemide 20 mg/day) were prescribed to reduce cardiac afterload, and alisentan (5 mg/day) was prescribed to reduce pulmonary arterial pressure after admission. Treatment outcomes and follow-up After treatment, the symptoms of chest tightness and shortness of breath were relieved. The patient was advised to take oral diuretics intermittently for a long period and oral anolisentan regularly, and followed up to monitor heart, liver and kidney function and blood pressure. DISCUSSION Pulmonary hypertension is a disease characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling and elevated pulmonary arterial pressure, which is associated with a variety of cardiopulmonary diseases, such as congenital heart disease, left heart disease, obstructive lung disease, and restrictive lung disease, etc. The diagnostic criterion for pulmonary hypertension is that the mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) is greater than or equal to 25 mmHg, measured by right heart catheterization, in the resting state at sea level. The diagnostic criterion is a mPAP greater than or equal to 25 mmHg measured by right heart catheterization at sea level at rest, and the diagnostic criterion is a mPAP greater than or equal to 25 mmHg measured by right heart catheterization at sea level at rest. According to the 2022 ESC/ERS Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pulmonary Hypertension classification criteria [ 1 ], pulmonary hypertension due to lung disease and/or hypoxia is categorized in the third group of pulmonary arterial hypertensive disorders, in addition to the inclusion of other coexisting pulmonary disorders of both obstructive and restrictive lung disease pulmonary dysplasia, and nonpulmonary diseases, especially high-altitude pulmonary hypertension, also need to be given attention. Considering the environmental specificity of the diagnosis of plateau pulmonary hypertension, i.e., in a nonsea level state, there is a difference in its diagnostic criteria, so the Sixth International Academic Conference on Plateau Medicine and Hypoxia Physiology in 2004 promulgated the “Qinghai Diagnostic Criteria for Chronic Plateau Disease” [ 2 ], which formulated the diagnostic criteria for plateau pulmonary hypertension: living at an altitude of more than 2,500m above sea level, with a mean pulmonary artery pressure of &gt;30 mmHg or pulmonary artery systolic pressure &gt;50mmHg, right ventricular hypertrophy with moderate hypoxemia and no erythrocytosis (HGB &lt;19 g/dl in women and &lt;21 g/dl in men) are the main points of diagnosis of plateau heart disease. In terms of the diagnostic criteria for pulmonary hypertension, it has a stricter stratification level for the pulmonary arteries. As the mechanism of plateau pulmonary hypertension is still unclear, many studies have focused on the pathophysiological processes of pulmonary hypertension caused by low pressure hypoxia in the plateau area. In the plateau environment, although the oxygen content of the air is still close to 21%, the decrease in air pressure leads to a decreasing trend in the partial pressure of inspired air oxygen and alveolar oxygen partial pressure that is positively correlated with altitude, and the most important physiological alterations that occur at this time are an increase in the hypoxia ventilation reaction (HVR) and hypoxia pulmonary vasoconstriction (hypoxia pulmonary vasoconstriction HPV), a physiological response aimed at decreasing perfusion in poorly ventilated lung regions and redistributing more blood flow to well oxygenated regions, as well as hypoxic stimuli leading to vascular remodeling of the middle and small pulmonary arteries. The pulmonary hypertension caused by acute hypoxia in plateau can be quickly normalized after the reduction to plain and restoration of oxygen supply. However, if the tissues of the body are in the state of hypoxia for a long period of time, it will have different degrees of adverse effects on the human body's various systems, especially in the regulation of cardiovascular disease, prolonged hypoxia will lead to the contraction and reconstruction of pulmonary blood vessels, resulting in an increase in the resistance of the pulmonary circulation and the elevation of the pulmonary arterial pressure, which will lead to the occurrence of hypoxic pulmonary hypertension. At present, there are about 140 million people living in the plateau above 2,500 m above sea level all over the world [ 3 ], and studies have shown that chronic hypoxemia exists in healthy people in the plateau, and some healthy people with nonorganic diseases will develop chronic highland disease [ 4 ], and multiple factors such as gender, age, altitude, labor intensity, and genetic factors all affect the development of chronic highland disease [ 5 ]. Epidemiological studies on the development of plateau pulmonary hypertension are still limited, and the prevalence of pulmonary hypertension has been found to be between 6 and 35% in highland populations living at altitudes above 3250 m [ 6 ], and in the Andes, 5–10% of high-altitude healthy residents have varying degrees of pulmonary hypertension [ 7 ], while the Tibetan plateau residents have mainly focused on the study of the adaptation of the Tibetans [ 8 ], which concluded that their chronic plateau disease [ 9 ] is a chronic condition of the Tibetan population. The incidence of other lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in the plateau region is also not few [ 9 ], and the incidence of this disease combined with pulmonary hypertension was found to reach 29.13% in the study of 127 patients with COPD in the plateau [ 10 ]. In clinical work, the most difficult to distinguish is the plateau chronic pulmonary heart disease and plateau pulmonary hypertension, both have pulmonary hypertension and its later right heart insufficiency, very easy to diagnostic confusion. Plateau chronic pulmonary heart disease is characterized by increased pulmonary vascular resistance due to bronchopulmonary tissue, thoracic, or pulmonary vascular disease, resulting in pulmonary hypertension, which in some patients is followed by structural or (and) functional changes in the right ventricle. Such forms of pulmonary hypertension are affected by a combination of environmental factors, underlying lung disease and disease severity in the highlands, and the pathogenesis is characterized by different features than in the plains, e.g., hypoxemia is more severe than in the plains [ 11 ]. The most common form of pulmonary hypertension in the plateau region is that caused by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and the lung function index FEV1 can reflect the degree of impairment of lung function in COPD, and the low FEV1% increase in the plateau region may be a risk factor for COPD combined with PH in this area. In this case report, the patient's pulmonary function indexes were not abnormal for many times, and no lung parenchymal or pulmonary vascular lesions were found in chest CT and pulmonary arteriography, so pulmonary hypertension due to chronic lung diseases could be excluded, mainly because of the combination of the patient's 10-year history of working at high altitude and living at medium and high altitude for a long time, and the patient's history was provided in the repeated inquiries to the fact that he had repeatedly experienced acute plateau reaction when traveling to and from medium-high and high-altitude areas during this period. Acute plateau reaction, i.e., severe cyanosis of the lips and face, chest tightness, shortness of breath, etc., which could be relieved by oxygen intake, was firstly considered as the diagnosis of plateau pulmonary hypertension. The gold standard for the diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension is hemodynamic assessment based on right heart catheterization, but compared to cardiac ultrasound, Doppler echocardiography is also mostly applied in national and international studies on plateau pulmonary hypertension because of its invasiveness and inability to achieve dynamic continuous monitoring. Echocardiographic assessment of plateau pulmonary hypertension and its right ventricle may be done by the following indices: peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV), tricuspid annular contraction excursion (TAPSE), right ventricular fractional area change (RV-FAC), right ventricular free wall strain, and tricuspid annular velocity (S), as well as potential right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF). For the detection of pulmonary systolic pressure the tricuspid regurgitant pressure difference estimation method is commonly used to calculate the SPAP, which is calculated by the formula SPAP = 4 × (tricuspid regurgitant early peak velocity) 2 + right atrial pressure. There has been a small-scale investigation in Tibet for plateau heart disease patients and chronic pulmonary heart disease patients echocardiography [ 12 ], did not make a clear comparison of pulmonary artery systolic pressure, but found that plateau pulmonary heart disease patients with fuzzy lungs, and small hearts are common, emphysema and chronic fibrosis is clearly unclear, to the right atrial chamber enlargement is dominant. On the other hand, the lungs of patients with plateau heart disease were basically clear, clinical symptoms were not obvious, emphysema and chronic fibrosis were not obvious, and the heart was enlarged. At present, there are no drugs that are clearly and definitely capable of treating high altitude pulmonary hypertension. The treatment of choice most often is ex-situ treatment; given the important role of hypoxia in the pathogenesis of hypoxic pulmonary hypertension, relocation of residence to a lower altitude may improve pulmonary hypertension and possibly cure the disease. Similarly, in most temporary high-altitude residents, symptoms of pulmonary hypertension subside after returning to sea level Mean pulmonary arterial pressure in patients rises again [ 5 ]. Current antihypertensive drugs which act on pulmonary arterial vasodilation include prostaglandin receptor agonists, endothelin receptor antagonists and phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, which are less effective on reducing pulmonary vascular remodelling [ 13 ]. However, it can have a dilatory effect on the initial pulmonary vasoconstriction. The patient developed respiratory failure and cardiac insufficiency due to plateau heart disease. To address this, long-term oxygen therapy at home and regular oral anlisentan (5 mg/day) were prescribed. Anlisentan is a highly selective endothelin A receptor antagonist with a minimal damage to hepatic function. The patient is also advised to live at low altitude. Regular monitoring of pulmonary artery pressure and oxygenationis necessary throughout the treatment. In conclusion, it is important to better understand the pathogenesis, treatment and prognosis of chronic pulmonary heart disease occurred in the plateau area.
