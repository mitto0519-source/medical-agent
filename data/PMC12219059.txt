Introduction Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the most common mental disorders that affects at least 5% of the adult population worldwide 1 , 2 . It is characterized by depressed mood, anhedonia and reduced drive, as well as changes in body weight, sleep difficulties, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, and suicidality, among other symptoms 1 , 3 . The diagnosis of depression requires five or more of these symptoms to be present within a 2-week period, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) and International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10 and ICD-11). The main risk or predisposing factors for depression include genetic variants, environmental factors like psychosocial stress, and female sex, with gene-environment interactions being an important consideration 4 . Depression is linked to both structural and functional abnormalities in the brain. Proposed neurobiological factors include monoamine neurotransmitters, specifically serotonin, noradrenaline, and dopamine; increased neuroinflammation. Additional mechanisms involve structural changes (i.e., cortical thickness, altered blood-brain barrier permeability); neuronal and neuroplasticity dysfunction (with a reduction of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)), and dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis which leads to elevated cortisol levels 1 , 5 – 8 . Classically psychopharmacological antidepressant treatments primarily involve targeting the serotonergic and noradrenergic systems 4 . While enhancing serotonin neurotransmission has proven effective for many individuals 9 , 10 , and recent findings suggest intestinal serotonin as a novel target to modulate anxiety- and depressive-like behaviours 11 , the heterogeneous nature of depression suggests that dysfunction in a single neurotransmitter could not fully explain its pathophysiology 12 . Notably, about 20–30% of the affected individuals will not reach remission after the first trial with an antidepressant, as shown for example in the STAR*D trial 13 . Currently, glutamatergic modulators such as Esketamine or Dextromethorphan-Bupropion are approved to treat MDD, highlighting the potential of N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA)-targeted therapies 14 . In addition, tricyclic antidepressants and electroconvulsive therapies upregulate the neurotrophic BDNF, which is reduced in depression 15 , 16 . However, when combining psychopharmacological, psychological approaches and using biological therapies, such as non-invasive brain stimulation, about 10% of patients still do not achieve sufficient improvement 17 – 19 . Hence, there is a significant unmet medical need to explore more individualized precision alternatives to treat depression, and one emerging area of interest is the gut microbiota. The gut microbiota has emerged as a key regulator for mental disorders through the bidirectional communication pathway known as the microbiota-gut-brain axis 5 , 20 . There is growing evidence highlighting microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and indoles derived from tryptophan as key effectors of the gut microbiota, which communicate directly or indirectly with the brain 21 – 27 and may also play a role in modulating depression. Furthermore, biological depression research extensively relies on rodent models and while these are valuable tools for studying the neurobiological basis of depression, they cannot fully replicate all facets of the complex and heterogenous nature of human mental disorders 28 . This limitation likely contributes to the fact that ~90% of drugs for mental disorders that show promise in preclinical trials ultimately fail in humans 29 . The lack of suitable in vitro and in vivo models, as well as the limited access to human brain cell types, presents significant challenges in studying the mechanisms underlying these diseases. This translational gap highlights the urgent need for more human-based research to evaluate the therapeutic potentials of novel targets, such as the gut microbiota in depression. In this context, human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) offer an innovative new solution to this dilemma. The aim of this narrative review is to define the current state-of-the-art with an emphasis on the alterations and potential effects of gut microbiota, and especially, microbial metabolites and their potential effects. We also explore the advantages and disadvantages of hiPSC technology and to identify the challenges and opportunities where they can serve as an innovative and effective new tool for studying the host-microbiome relationship in depression. Methodology In this narrative review, we searched PubMed and Web of Knowledge (from 2014 onwards) using the following search strategies: “Depression” OR “MDD”, together with (AND) “microbiota” OR “Germ-free” OR “microbial metabolites” OR “microbiome” OR “microbiota-gut-brain axis” OR “prebiotic” OR “probiotic” OR “psychobiotic” OR “symbiotic” OR “diet” OR “fibre” OR “Short-chain fatty acids” OR “Bile acids” OR “indoles” OR “tryptophan”. We included clinical, and preclinical trials focused on MDD or depressive-like behaviours as well as studies that confirm an amelioration of depressive hallmarks with microbiota-related treatment to have a general understanding of the current state of art in the relationship between microbiota and the brain in depression. In this case, we excluded studies with only healthy participants, or with comorbidities and very small studies with n &lt; 20 from our review. Additionally, we reviewed “induced pluripotent stem cells” OR “iPSC” OR “hIPSC” OR “stem cells” along with (AND) “microbiota” OR “microbial metabolites” OR “microbiota-gut-brain axis”, “depression” OR “MDD”, NOT (review). We also excluded previous studies conducted in languages other than English, ensuring that all authors could read and comprehend the manuscripts. Alteration of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in depression The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as an important modulator of cognition, anxiety and mood via the gut-brain axis framework 30 – 32 . The microbiota-gut-brain axis is a bidirectional network of communication between the microbiota, the gut and the brain involving neural, immune, endocrine, and metabolic pathways 20 . Growing evidence indicates alterations in the gut microbiota of patients with depression compared to healthy individuals. Research has evolved from initial reliance on compositional assessments to investigations of causality and functional alterations linked to specific mechanisms (Fig. 1 ). However, confounding variables, such as antidepressant treatment, geographic variation, individual differences, or diet, are often overlooked, making it challenging to reach a universal consensus. Subset-specific observations, such as low testosterone levels or hormonal fluctuations in postmenopausal individuals, have been shown to influence depression and modulate gut-brain axis communication 33 – 35 . As mentioned, diet plays a critical role in shaping the gut microbiota, and dietary changes observed in depression may contribute to alterations in gut microbiota composition 36 , 37 . However, it is also important to note that one of the core symptoms of depression is anhedonia or inability to experience pleasure, which can also disturb the reward-seeking behaviours and result in altered dietary habits. Chronic stress exposure, another risk factor of depression, is also a key contributor to changes in appetite regulation 38 . While half of the individuals with MDD typically experience a reduction in appetite, approximately one-third report an increase, highlighting the heterogeneity of the disorder 39 . These appetite changes are associated with distinct physiological mechanisms: decreased appetite correlates with elevated cortisol levels, while increased appetite is associated with immunometabolic dysregulation, such as insulin resistance or reduced ghrelin levels 40 . Conflicting evidence exists regarding the association between depression and unhealthy dietary intake. On one hand, depression has been linked to unhealthy dietary intake and poorer dietary quality 41 . Specifically, individuals with mild depressive symptoms exhibit dysregulation in fat taste perception 42 . Among students, depressive symptoms are linked to less frequent consumption of fruits, vegetables, and meat 43 . In addition, antidepressant treatment can also modulate taste thresholds for sweet, bitter and sour flavours 44 . However, while high-sugar and fat diets have been associated with a higher incidence of depression, studies have found no evidence of reverse causation. MDD does not lead to increased sugar or fat intake 45 , 46 . On the other hand, randomized controlled trials of dietary interventions such as SMILE improved the symptomatology of MDD patients 47 . Hence, lifestyle factors like dietary preferences should be considered in gut microbiota interventions for depression and as a factor influenced by the disease itself. Antidepressants or other psychotropic drugs should also be considered in human trials, given the reciprocal interaction between xenobiotics and the gut microbiome 48 – 51 . When considering the treatment within their analysis, a study found that antidepressant treatment increased the prevalence of the genera Butyricicoccus and Bacteroides 52 . In addition, another research showed a different metabolite enrichment profile depending on whether the group was treatment responders or non-responders 53 . These studies consistently demonstrate an association between depression and alterations in the gut microbiota, even after accounting for the effects of antidepressant treatment, reinforcing the connection between the two. Despite these challenges, some consistent findings have emerged 54 , 55 . For example, microbial composition of MDD patients showed an enrichment in pro-inflammatory bacteria such as Escherichia-Shigella , Desulfovibrio, Eggerthella, Hungatella, Enterobacterales with Eggerthella being more likely to be causally associated with MDD. In contrast, depleted levels of Faecalibacterium , Ruminococcus , Coprococcus and Dialister 56 – 59 (Table 1 ). Notably, the human gut microbiota and the immune system are intricately linked with microbial education and priming of the immune system 36 , 60 . Compositional data analysis reveals that microbial-related inflammation and dysregulation of glutamate, monoamines, proline, and GABA neurotransmission, as well as butyrate degradation pathways have been linked to the pathophysiology of depression 61 , 62 . Building on these observations, faecal microbiota transplantation from depressed patients to microbiota-depleted rodents has been used to study this interaction in the context of establishing potential causality 63 , 64 . In these preclinical models, colonization of microbiota-depleted mice transferred depression-like behaviours (Table 2 ). The microbiota composition in recipient transplanted rodents was similar to that of the donor humans with depression, with increased levels of Eggerthella, Holdemania, Gelria, Turicibacter, Paraprevotella , and Anaerofilum , and decreased Dialister, Ruminococcus, Lactobacillus and Akkermansia 61 , 65 , 66 . These changes in gut microbiota composition were associated with a pro-inflammatory profile, reduced neurogenesis, impaired gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and glutamine metabolism. They were also linked to decreased SCFA-producing species, and altered tryptophan and kynurenine pathway and lipid metabolism, including bile acid metabolites 65 – 68 . Taken together, these studies highlight depression-associated changes in gut microbiota in driving depressive-like hallmarks, emphasizing gut microbiota as a promising therapeutic target for depressive disorders, and a list of metabolites that could serve as the potential link between the gut and the brain. However, further validation using gut microbiota and microbial metabolites is needed to confirm this potential. Targeting metabolite alterations: advancing gut microbiota interventions for depression-like phenotypes The administration of live microorganisms that confer a health benefit when consumed in adequate amounts 69 , known as probiotics, has been shown to have an impact ameliorating different diseases, including depression 70 (Table 3 ). Traditional probiotics, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species, have a well-established history of use and are among the most extensively studied for their effects on conditions like depression-like behaviour. In contrast, next-generation probiotics represent recently identified bacterial strains discovered using advanced analytical tools and techniques that compare microbiota compositions between healthy and diseased individuals, in which we found Akkermansia muciniphila and Faecalibacterium 71 . In preclinical trials, several animal models are employed to study and replicate depression-like behaviours. These models typically involve exposure to chronic stress, which is critical for inducing behaviours analogous to depressive symptoms in humans. Commonly used models include chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS), as well as restraint stress, adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH) or corticosterone administration, and early-life stress models, such as maternal separation 72 , 73 . The behavioural readouts in these models are designed to assess core symptoms of depression, utilizing various tests that target specific aspects of depressive behaviour 74 (Box 1 ). The administration of probiotics has shown promise in studies exploring their potential role in alleviating depression-like and anxiety-like behaviours, shown through various behavioural tests 75 – 82 . This intervention primarily modulates functional alterations within the gut microbiota, with changes in composition also occurring, leading to subsequent shifts in microbial metabolite production. For instance, SCFA levels were increased in both colon and faeces following probiotic administration. However, it has been reported an overall increase in SCFAs, with a specific reduction in acetate production 79 . Notably, studies have observed a reduction in tryptophan metabolism and kynurenine 75 , 78 , 80 , while also showed increased tryptophan synthesis 77 , 78 , contributing to the restoration of the kynurenine/tryptophan metabolic balance 24 . These interventions consistently influence neurotransmitter pathways, enhancing levels of serotonin (5-HT), dopamine (DA), and GABA in key brain regions like the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, regions typically impacted in stress-induced depression models 75 , 77 – 79 , 81 . Additionally, probiotic treatments reduce the activation of microglia, and neuroinflammation and increase BDNF, a critical protein for neuroplasticity, as well as c-FOS, a marker of neuronal activation and plasticity 75 – 77 , 79 , 80 . Prebiotics, which stimulate the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, synbiotics (probiotics combined with prebiotics) and certain dietary components has demonstrated effectiveness in alleviating depression-like behaviours (Table 3 ). Among these, fructooligosaccharides (FOS) and galactooligosaccharides (GOS), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are commonly studied. Interestingly, these treatments lead to increase levels of beneficial bacteria, such as Akkermansia, Lactobacillus and Ruminococcus , which results in higher SCFA concentrations, and restored tryptophan metabolism 83 – 85 . Similarly, other bioactive compounds, and natural products can also revert the depression-like phenotype and modify microbiota composition and metabolic production 86 – 89 . Like probiotics, these interventions also enhance BDNF, normalize neurotransmitter levels, and reduce neuroinflammation and corticosterone in key brain areas 83 , 85 – 88 , 90 , 91 . Apart from SCFA and tryptophan metabolites, secondary bile acids, which are metabolized by the gut microbiota, such as tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), taurine-hyodeoxycholic acid (THDCA), taurine-deoxycholic acid (TDCA), taurine-α muricholic acid (TMCA-α), and taurine-cholic acid (TCA), are found enhanced following PUFA treatment 84 . In summary, the administration of bacteria associated with depression and dietary components has been shown to reverse the effects of chronic stress and depression-like phenotypes, as well as gut microbial abnormalities in rodents. These studies highlight the significant role of the gut microbiota in influencing the CNS through various gut-brain axis pathways. This includes the regulation of neurotransmitters such as GABA, which was previously shown to modulate depressive-like behaviours via vagal nerve-dependent mechanisms 92 . Furthermore, the gut microbiota can modulate the immune response, reducing systemic and neuroinflammatory markers, and regulate the HPA axis, leading to decreased cortisol or corticosterone levels 8 , 93 . Therefore, harnessing probiotics, prebiotics, fermented food and other dietary compounds has been shown to be effective as a microbial intervention in depression in preclinical studies. However, further validation in humans using gut microbiota and microbial metabolites is needed to confirm this potential. Box 1 Behavioural readouts Despair-like behaviour: measured by Forced swim test (FST), and Tail suspension test (TST). Decreased attempts to escape, measured as immobility, are interpreted as despair or helplessness but may also capture stress-coping. Anhedonia: a reduced ability to experience pleasure, is often assessed through the Sucrose Preference Test (SPT) or the Female Urine Sniffing Test (FUST), which measure interest in naturally rewarding stimuli. Social Behaviour: evaluated through the Social Interaction Test (SIT) to detect reductions in social engagement. Anxiety-like behaviour: assessed with the Open Field Test (OFT), where time spent in exposed areas reflects anxiety-related responses. Human studies using gut microbiota as an adjuvant treatment in depression Only a limited number of studies using gut microbiota interventions have been completed in clinical trials in depression (Table 4 ). Administration of probiotics such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus can improve cognitive functions in patients with MDD with 94 – 96 or without SSRI treatment 97 , 98 . Shifts in microbiota composition were also observed with an enhancement of the abundance of Faecalibaculum , Ruminococcus , and Coprococcus . Interestingly, functional analysis showed an enhancement of metabolic pathways, mainly explored tryptophan-derived metabolites 94 , 95 , which could be due to the relevance of its pathways on depression 24 , 34 , as well as an increase in neurotransmitters such as serotonin, and glutamatergic and gabaergic synapses 96 . In addition, administration of probiotics could also reduce inflammation in MDD patients 98 , which is one of the main hallmarks of the disease and can be correlated to metabolomic alterations in MDD 99 . Many preclinical and clinical studies have lacked functional analyses, which are crucial for deepening our understanding of the pathways linking the microbiota to depression. Despite this limitation, microbial metabolites consistently emerge as key factors altered in these studies. From this research, we can identify a target list of metabolites, such as SCFA, tryptophan-derived metabolites, and bile acids, that serve as direct or indirect key link between the gut microbiota and brain function. These findings pave the way for developing new treatment strategies that incorporate these interventions. Microbial metabolite interventions in models of depression-like phenotypes Microbial metabolites are molecules produced by the gut microbiota that have been detected in the circulation and at organs distal to the gut including the brain. There is a large list of metabolites alterations that require systematic evaluation, but to date this has often been done using in vivo approaches in models of depression-like phenotypes. The main microbial metabolites are categorised SCFAs, tryptophan derived-metabolites and bile acids which interact with host receptors, including those in microglia and neurons, where they exert neuroprotective effects and help mitigate neuroinflammation 100 – 102 . Supplementation with these metabolites have been conducted to understand if they can alleviate depression-like behaviours (Table 5 ). Short-chain fatty acids SCFAs are produced as a result of microbial fermentation of dietary fibres. The main SCFA are acetate, butyrate, and propionate, and they have been detected within the gut lumen, circulating bloodstream, and central nervous system (CNS) 22 . Intracellularly, SCFAs are converted into acetyl-CoA, fueling the citric acid cycle and promoting cellular functions like energy production, mTOR activation, and fatty acid synthesis, thus impacting cellular differentiation. Common SCFA-producing gut microbes include Akkermansia , Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Ruminococcus , Blautia , Bacteroides , Roseburia , Prevotella , and Clostridium , each contributing to the extensive network of microbial and host interactions within the gut and beyond. SCFAs play a role in regulating physiological and neurological processes, affecting blood pressure, circadian rhythms, microglia maturation, astrocyte gene expression, synaptic plasticity, and gut and blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity 103 – 106 . Their influence extends to gene expression associated with immune, metabolic, and epigenetic pathways, as shown in primary glial cultures 27 . SCFAs have been shown to be neuroprotective, highlighting their potential as therapeutic agents in neuroinflammatory diseases 107 , 108 . In human trials, these metabolites have been shown to lower cortisol responses to psychosocial stress, although not when butyrate was administered alone 109 , 110 . Evidence from rodent assays of depression-like behaviour suggests that SCFA supplementation, especially butyrate and propionate, can alleviate depressive symptoms, reduce corticosterone levels, and modulate tryptophan metabolism. This might be mediated by the effect of SCFAs in reducing neuroinflammation, promoting neuroplasticity and neurogenesis by increasing markers like BDNF and activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein (Arc) 111 – 113 . SCFA can bind G protein-coupled receptors GPR43 (free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2)) and GPR41 (FFAR3), as well as the less common GPR164 and GPR109a (also known as OR51E1 and HCAR2 respectively). These interactions facilitate signalling cascades across different body cell types enabling selective cellular regulation 114 . They can also inhibit histone deacetylases (HDACs), further affecting epigenetically gene expression. Interestingly, both type of receptors were modulated after the supplementation of SCFA in the prefrontal cortex in animal models 113 , 115 . Remarkably, it has been shown that inhibition of HDAC could ameliorated depression-like behaviours and reversed the decrease in BDNF 116 . This indicates that SCFAs may have promising implications for mood regulation and mental health, extending well beyond their established role in gut health. However, studies have yet to specifically investigate SCFA effects in patients with MDD. Tryptophan metabolites Tryptophan is an essential amino acid that cannot be synthesized by mammals and must be obtained from dietary sources such as fish, milk, oats, cheese, poultry, bananas, and chocolate 73 . Tryptophan can be metabolised to serotonin, kynurenine and indole pathways, all regulated by gut microbiota, and has been associated with neuropsychiatric pathologies such as MDD 117 . Several bacterial genera, including Lactococcus , Lactobacillus , Streptococcus , Escherichia coli , and Klebsiella , are capable of producing serotonin from tryptophan 118 . The metabolism of tryptophan via the kynurenine pathway is driven by indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which is activated in response to immune stimuli. Beyond the kynurenine pathway, gut bacteria transform tryptophan into various bioactive metabolites, including tryptamine, indole, and other indole derivatives, which interact with host cellular receptors to modulate physiological functions 119 . For example, bacteria such as Clostridium , Ruminococcus , Blautia , and Lactobacillus convert tryptophan into tryptamine, while indole production is facilitated by the enzyme tryptophanase (TnaA) in Escherichia coli , Clostridium , and Bacteroides species. Other derivatives, like indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), are synthesized by bacteria such as Clostridium , Bacteroides , and Bifidobacterium . Strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium convert tryptophan to indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), which Clostridium and Peptostreptococcus can further transform into indolepropionic acid (IPA) 120 . Interestingly, indole, IPA, and ILA improved intestinal barrier function, anti-inflammatory effects, as well as neuroprotective effects in rodents 121 – 124 . In addition, altered tryptophan metabolism was also associated with intestinal disorders, and neuropsychiatric disorders 117 , 125 , 126 . Within the central nervous system, kynurenic acid, a product of this pathway, has been shown to possess neuroprotective properties, whereas quinolinic acid is known for its excitotoxic effects as an NMDA receptor agonist 127 . As potential interventions, tryptophan-rich diets have been shown to improve depression-like behaviours in mice 128 , and intraperitoneal administration of IAA has alleviated depression-like symptoms, increased BDNF expression, and promoted the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria 129 . In addition, oral supplementation of ILA, which was reduced in MDD patients, shown antidepressant effects by reducing neuroinflammation through the activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) 130 . This receptor is engaged by many tryptophan metabolites, particularly indole derivatives, influencing gene expression linked to immune responses, energy metabolism, and microbial defense 131 . Interestingly, knocking out intestinal AhR expression in rodents reduces anxiety in behavioural tests and improves spatial learning, highlighting the role of the receptor in gut-brain interactions 132 . Bile acids Bile acids are essential components of bile, with key roles in lipid absorption and regulation of metabolic and inflammatory pathways 133 . Bile acids are first synthesized from cholesterol in the liver, known as primary bile acids, such as cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), conjugated with taurine or glycine, producing TCA and taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA) 134 . Most bile acids are reabsorbed in the ileum and recirculated to the liver. However, a portion reaches the colon, where the gut microbiota further metabolizes these primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, such as deoxycholic acid (DCA), Lithocholic acid (LCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA). Taurine conjugation of UDCA forms tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) 135 . Genera such as Bacteroides , Bifidobacterium , Clostridium , Eubacterium , Lactobacillus , and Fusobacterium are known to participate in this transformation 135 . Supplementation of CDCA and TUDCA produce antidepressant-like effects and reduced systemic inflammatory response in preclinical models 136 , 137 . TUDCA ameliorates colonic inflammation 138 , and also present anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties in the brain of mice 139 . Bile acids act as signalling molecules, binding to receptors like the G protein-coupled bile acid receptor (TGR5) and the nuclear hormone receptor farnesoid X receptor (FXR). These interactions initiate cellular responses that are crucial for lipid metabolism, electrolyte transport, and immune regulation, underscoring the wide-ranging influence of bile acids on host physiology 140 . Interestingly, the depletion of FXR increased serum and brain bile acids levels and alleviated depressive-like outcomes, whereas an overexpression of it produced the opposite effects 141 , 142 . Direct evidence supporting the involvement of gut microbiota-derived metabolites in regulating brain function is currently limited. To study this relationship, primary cell lines from rodents, such as microglia or neuronal cells, have been used to examine the effects of gut microbiota and microbial metabolites on cellular functions 143 . Additionally, in vitro models with established cell lines, such as SH-SY5Y, a human-derived neuroblastoma cell line that can be differentiated into neuron-like cells, have also been employed for similar investigations 144 , 145 . While valuable, these models are far removed from accurately representing the pathophysiology of depression. A similar challenge arises with animal models. Despite their role in advancing our understanding of gut-brain axis interactions in depression, the extensive use of rodent models, particularly males, cannot fully replicate the complexity of human mental disorders 28 . For instance, similar to what happens with new pharmacological antidepressants, microbial interventions that improve depressive-like behaviours in rodents often fail to translate into improvements in human depressive outcomes 95 , 96 , 98 . Therefore, there is an urgent need for further human-based research to explore the potential role of gut microbiota as an intervention for depression. Given the limited accessibility of the human CNS in vivo, hiPSCs offer a unique and valuable model to study these mechanisms. These models provide enhanced cellular and molecular insights, which are critical for the development of improved therapeutic options for clinical management of depression. HiPSCs have already been used to investigate the pathological characteristics of various psychological disorders and to assess the direct impact of gut microbiota and microbial metabolites as potential treatments for depression 146 – 148 . Human induced pluripotent stem cells The hiPSC technique, developed less than two decades ago, involves collecting primary somatic cells from humans and transdifferentiating them into any cell type 149 , 150 (Fig. 2 ). Somatic cells such as fibroblasts or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) can be obtained through skin biopsies or blood extractions, respectively. After culturing these cells, they can be reprogrammed using a non-integrating system that uses viral vectors to reprogram somatic cells into iPSCs. The set of vectors most commonly used includes OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, and MYC 151 , as well as OCT4, SOX2, NANOG, and LIN28 152 . Once reprogrammed, these cells can be differentiated into any cell type. To study depression, the cells are typically differentiated into brain-relevant cell types, such as neurons, microglia, or astrocytes, as these are the most affected cell types in the condition, according to the best of the current knowledge 153 . In addition, hiPSC-derived cells can self-organise into a more complex model of 3D structure, called organoids, which have demonstrated the ability to mimic native organs in terms of gene and protein expression, metabolic functions, microscale tissue architecture, and cellular composition 154 . These cells offer an innovative platform for studying the mechanisms underlying complex human pathologies, such as depression. However, it is essential to consider both their advantages and limitations (Fig. 3 ). HiPSCs provide a human-based model that enables more direct clinical translation. Unlike established cell lines or in vivo models, they retain the unique human genetic background of the patient, which is crucial for investigating the variability and heterogeneity of MDD, particularly regarding symptoms and treatment responses. Moreover, hiPSCs offer a valuable tool for studying human brain function, which is otherwise difficult to access in research. They serve as an alternative or complementary approach to linking cellular findings with behavioural alterations observed in vivo, ultimately also facilitating clinical translation. Additionally, hiPSCs provide a platform for exploring new treatments and gaining deeper insights into underlying mechanisms of action. Finally, this human-based model aligns with the 3R principles in animal research (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), promoting more ethical and efficient scientific approaches. In contrast, this technology also faces several challenges that must be addressed. Mass production of hiPSCs remains a significant obstacle due to the complexity of the methodology, the lack of standardized protocols, and the requirement of regulatory guidelines, which are essential to advancing hiPSC toward clinical application. Additionally, the heterogeneity of these cells, driven by factors such as the cell line, genetic background, passage, and protocol used, is valuable for studying patient-specific characteristics, but further complicates progress toward large-scale production. To promote better reproducibility, not only should routine validation steps be performed, but also the implementation of universally accepted guidelines is crucial 155 . Moreover, hiPSC-derived cells often fail to reach full maturation, which highlights the importance of understanding the morphological, transcriptional, and functional characteristics of these cells 156 . This limitation, while presenting challenges for studying age-related conditions, makes hiPSCs especially valuable for investigating human developmental disorders 157 , 158 . Finally, the unique characteristics of these cells pose other complex challenges such as graft rejection from the host and an increased risk of tumorigenicity 159 . To address these challenges, bioengineering modifications, along with the implementation of automated methods. Integration of machine learning, are crucial to ensuring consistency and reliability during the final stages of production and application. Overcoming these barriers holds great promise for accelerating research progress and expanding the discoveries that can be made using these models 149 . Despite these challenges, hiPSC replacement therapies have already been successfully applied in clinical trials involving retinal disease 160 , type 1 diabetes mellitus 161 , and hematopoietic stem cells transplantation 162 , among others 163 – 166 . In the context of depression, this advance tool has recently begun to be explored. Human induced pluripotent stem cell in depression To use hiPSC models as valuable tools for studying MDD, different iPSCs have been derived to recapitulate GABAergic interneurons, serotonergic, glutamatergic, or dopaminergic neurons. Ensuring the reliability of these models requires the confirmation of their functionality 167 , neuronal subpopulation identification, and the inclusion of functional assays, such as assessing their ability to establish functional synapses 168 . Neurons generated from hiPSCs of individuals with MDD have offered crucial insights into the mechanisms contributing to depression. For instance, cutting-edge research has shown that hiPSC-derived neuronal cells from MDD exhibit abnormal activity related to decreased serotonergic receptor 169 , mitochondrial dysfunction 170 , and altered bioenergetics and electrophysiological properties 171 compared to hiPSC from controls. These cells can also be manipulated to present different variant polymorphism such as underexpression of BDNF, characteristic of the disease 172 . Using this technique, studies comparing iPSCs from remitters and non-remitter patients after SSRI treatment have demonstrated that neurons derived from non-remitter patients exhibited serotonin-induced hyperactivity due to the upregulation of excitatory serotonergic receptors, a response not observed in neurons from healthy individuals or remitter patients 173 . In addition, to model the disease, hiPSC can also be derived from healthy donors 174 or established human fibroblasts 175 into i5HT serotonin neurons, which is particularly interesting as these types of neurons are dysregulated in depression, and are the target of commonly used antidepressants. i5HT cells are very similar to serotonergic neurons in vivo, exhibiting active serotonergic synaptic transmission, Ca 2+ -dependent release of serotonin and selective uptake of serotonin, which can be measured in real-time. This demonstrates that serotonin release is influenced by both the intensity of stimulation and tryptophan preloading 174 . Human microglia and astrocytes from stem cells, which are phenotypically and functionally comparable with primary cultures, have also been used to study the neuroimmune interactions involved in MDD 176 , 177 . To model MDD, these cells can be treated with cortisol or dexamethasone, a glucocorticoid receptor agonist, for at least 3 days to mimic chronic stress exposure. After the treatment, the cells revealed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) regulated uniquely by chronic cortisol exposure compared to acute 177 , 178 . HiPSCs differentiated into brain microvascular endothelial cells-like cells have been used to study BBB integrity, which is found altered in chronic stress and depression models, allowing the study of different personalized applications to enhance the barrier function 179 , 180 . HiPSC models have been used to understand treatment-resistant depression (TRD) or difficult-to-treat-depression better and promote new treatment for those cases. Two hiPSC lines (UNIBSi012-A and UNIBSi013-A) were generated from patients with treatment-resistant depression, providing valuable resources for studying TRD mechanisms 181 . Additional research has explored new treatments for the disorder, such as Ropinirole and Pramipexole, dopamine agonists acting on dopaminergic neurons derived from hiPSCs 182 , and the effects of ketamine on hiPSC-derived neuronal progenitors and dopaminergic neurons 183 , 184 . These models provide patient-specific in vitro systems, overcoming limitations of animal and post-mortem brain analyses. While such traditional models offer valuable insights, they lack the patient specificity and the human-based model necessary to capture the complexity and interindividual variability in MDD. This human-based model is essential for advancing in microbiome science in this disorder. While studies have identified microbiome-related targets, including microbial species and metabolites linked to MDD and FMT from humans to rodents (see Section “Alteration of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in depression” in this review), and microbiome-based therapies such as probiotics, prebiotics, and metabolite treatments in clinical and preclinical studies (see Sections “Targeting metabolite alterations: advancing gut microbiota interventions for depression-like phenotypes”, “Human studies using gut microbiota as an adjuvant treatment in depression” and “Microbial metabolite interventions in models of depression-like phenotypes”), validating their effects in human-relevant systems and understanding their impact in the brain remains a challenge. By integrating hiPSC technology with microbiome research, we can refine our understanding of the gut-brain axis and accelerate the development of targeted, patient-specific interventions. Human induced pluripotent stem cell and gut-brain interventions Emerging research has begun to incorporate hiPSCs and hiPSC-derived organoids, which are more complex models that include multiple cell types, to advance the study of microbiota-host interactions in the gut-brain axis. For instance, butyrate supplementation in hiPSC-derived intestinal organoids has been shown to mitigate oxidative stress associated with barrier function impairments and inflammatory responses 185 . Additionally, the probiotic Limosilactobacillus reuteri strain DS0384 was found to accelerate the maturation of foetal intestinal organoids via the metabolite N-carbamyl glutamic acid, offering protection against colitis 186 . Recent studies have also emphasized the effects of dietary compounds and microbial metabolites on brain cell types to deepen the understanding of gut-brain interactions using hiPSCs. For example, it has been demonstrated that adding propionate and butyrate to induced neuronal cells from multiple sclerosis patients restored damaged neurites, highlighting the direct impact of these SCFAs on human neurons 187 . Similarly, treatment of hiPSC-derived neurons with sodium butyrate improved mitochondrial function, reduced neuronal apoptosis, and corrected Parkin-mediated mitophagy dysregulation by inhibiting HDAC8 188 . Furthermore, supplementation with gut microbial-derived metabolites in hiPSC-derived neurons has been linked to enhanced neural growth, maturation, and synaptic plasticity 189 . The addition of PUFAs to induced astrocytes was shown to increase the production of brain-derived BDNF and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), as well as enhance CREB activity, mirroring an antidepressant effect 190 . These findings emphasize the potential of gut microbiome-targeted therapies for conditions related to oxidative stress, inflammation, neurodegenerative diseases, and mood disorders. The ability of microbial metabolites to enhance neural growth, synaptic plasticity, and neurotrophic factor production opens new pathways for developing microbiota-based treatments for these disorders. However, further research is needed using hiPSCs, which have an immense translational potential, to deepen understanding of the impact of gut microbiota on neuropsychiatric conditions. Conclusions and outlook The growing body of evidence showing a link between gut microbiota and mental health disorders, including depression, highlights the potential of microbial interventions in addressing these conditions. However, these studies raise the question: is the gut microbiota an unwitting player or do they precede the onset of depression and drive the development of symptoms? Faecal microbiota transplantation interventions along with multi-omics analyses have been used to examine this host-microbiome association in depressive symptoms 64 , 191 , 192 . Despite these promising findings, there are also some important limitations to consider. These include the inter-individual variability in the taxonomic composition of human metagenomes, which makes it difficult to generalize findings across populations, as well as the lack of global representation needed to better understand the diversity of the gut microbiome worldwide 193 – 195 . While these challenges still need to be addressed, hiPSC technology offers a complementary and transformative platform to investigate the effects of gut microbiota on the central nervous system. Although still in its infancy, especially in the context of microbiome research, hiPSC technology represents a promising avenue for unravelling microbiome-gut-brain interactions. As this field advances, hiPSC-based models are expected to evolve toward more personalized and mechanistic insights, ultimately supporting the development of targeted therapeutic interventions. However, hiPSC in vitro models also present certain limitations. While microbial metabolites can impact directly on the brain 21 , in vitro models cannot fully capture the complex interconnections present in whole-body systems, and miss the indirect effects mediated by gut microbiota through other physiological pathways. However, these models are highly effective for studying precise signals and mechanisms influenced by specific interventions, without the confounding factors present in more complex systems. In addition, the epigenetic memory persists in hiPSCs but is lost in subsequently derived germline cells, which may limit the study of microbial metabolite interventions 196 . Building on these advances, future directions in bioengineering are focusing on developing hiPSC-derived organ-on-a-chip platforms 197 . These microfluidic systems integrate 3D organotypic cultures with continuous medium perfusion, allowing precise regulation of microenvironmental conditions to mimic individual pathophysiological states by dynamically adjusting physicochemical parameters based on personal health data. In addition to reproducing essential functional aspects of tissue and organ physiopathology, these platforms offer efficient tools for testing therapeutic agents. Organs-on-a-chip have already been employed to model various organs and tissues, simulating processes such as substance transport across microanatomical barriers like the BBB and supporting the culture of intestinal microbiota, which could model the microbiota-gut-brain axis to develop new strategies involving the microbiota in depression 198 – 201 . In summary, we have highlighted the growing evidence of gut microbiota modulation in depression, and how hiPSC-derived models can effectively explore the intricate relationship between the microbiota and the brain (Fig. 4 ). These cells hold significant potential for advancing our understanding of depression specifically and offer promising avenues for developing novel therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the gut microbiota to enhance mental health in general.
