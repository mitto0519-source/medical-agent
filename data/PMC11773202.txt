Summary Background Suicide is a leading cause of death for young people. Half of young adult suicides are by firearm, and many involve alcohol consumption. We aimed to assess the impact of the minimum legal drinking age (MLDA) of 21 years on rates of alcohol positive firearm and non-firearm suicide. Methods We used a regression discontinuity approach with data from the National Violent Death Reporting System (NVDRS) 2005–2021, among U.S suicide decedents in 14 states aged 16–35 years. The primary outcomes were the change in the proportion of tests positive for alcohol and the change in rates of alcohol positive suicide per 100,000 population at the MLDA. Findings Across 14 states and 169 state-years, there were 434 alcohol positive suicides at age 20 and 684 alcohol positive suicides at age 21. The percentage of suicides with a positive alcohol test increased from 29.5% at age 20 to 38.7% at age 21 (p &lt; 0.001), with a larger increase among firearm suicides relative to non-firearm suicides. Accounting for age-related trends, the total alcohol positive suicide rate increased by 1.52 per 100,000 (p &lt; 0.001) at the MLDA, mostly due to an increase in alcohol positive firearm suicide. Compared to the expected rate in the absence of the MLDA, there was a 47.5% (95% CI 24.1%–81.8%) excess in firearm suicides, equivalent to 137 (95% CI 82–191) excess deaths. There was no discontinuity in the rate of alcohol negative firearm suicides at age 21. Blood alcohol content was not affected by the MLDA but was higher for firearm suicides across all ages. Interpretation There was a large increase in the rate of alcohol positive suicide at the minimum legal drinking age (MLDA), driven by firearm suicide. Policies and programmes should aim to weaken the link between alcohol and firearms among those at or just above legal drinking age to prevent suicide. Research in context Evidence before this study Alcohol ingestion just prior to death appears to be more common among suicide decedents who use firearms than those who use other suicide methods. In the U.S, the minimum legal drinking age (MLDA) was set at 21 years across all states in 1984. Evidence indicates that raising the MLDA to 21 years of age has reduced alcohol consumption and road traffic accidents. Studies using panel data models have found that in states that decreased their MLDA from 21 to 18 (prior to 1984), suicide rates increased over later years. But these studies did not measure the presence of alcohol in suicides, differentiate between firearm and non-firearm suicides, or account for the fact that in some states the MLDA is also the age at which handguns become legal to purchase. Added value of this study The current study uses a quasi-experimental approach with 15 years of linked individual-level suicide, alcohol toxicology, and population data to find that rates of alcohol positive suicide increased at the MLDA of 21 years old, mostly due to an increase in firearm suicide. There was no discontinuity in the rate of alcohol negative firearm suicides at the MLDA. Blood alcohol content was not affected by the MLDA but was higher for firearm suicides across all ages. Implications of all the available evidence MLDA laws limit the harmful effects of alcohol among young adults. Concurrent access to alcohol and firearms at age 21 increases risk of suicide. Policies and programmes should aim to weaken the link between alcohol and firearms among those at or just above legal drinking age to prevent suicide. Introduction In 2021, suicide was the second leading cause of death for young people aged 10–34 years in the United States (U.S). 1 Half of suicides in this age group involve firearms. 2 Post-mortem studies have found alcohol in the blood of approximately 30–40% of US suicide decedents overall, including 41% of suicides in white teenagers. 3 , 4 Alcohol ingestion just prior to death appears to be more common among suicide decedents who use firearms or hanging than those who use other suicide methods, 5 with the proportion of suicides involving firearms (relative to other means) increasing with blood alcohol content (BAC) up until very high levels are reached, after which firearms no longer predominate. 6 In the U.S, the National Minimum Drinking Age Act (NMDAA) of 1984 set the minimum legal drinking age (MLDA) at 21 years across all states. According to the U.S Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, raising the MLDA to 21 years of age across all states has reduced alcohol consumption and road traffic accidents among young adults. 7 More limited evidence suggests that MLDA laws also affect suicide rates. A U.S analysis of 200,000 suicide decedents born between 1949 and 1972 found that suicide rates among women, but not men, increased on average by 12% in states that decreased their MLDA below 21. 8 A fixed effects analysis of U.S. suicide mortality data from 1970 to 1990 found that states lowering the MLDA from 21 to 18 years of age had an 8% higher suicide rate among 18–20-year-olds, compared with states where the MLDA remained 21 years of age. In this same study, the suicide rate was 6% higher among 22–23 year olds in states that decreased their MLDA, but no difference was observed in the rate of suicide among 15–17 year olds. 9 The existing literature on the impact of the MLDA on suicide has relied on panel data methods investigating pre-post changes in the MLDA prior to or around the NMDAA, and, for all the advantages of this approach, is subject to potential bias from unobserved time-varying confounders. 10 In addition, studies have not measured the presence of alcohol in suicides, differentiated between firearm and non-firearm suicides, or accounted for the fact that in some states the MLDA is also the age at which handguns become legal to purchase, potentially affecting suicide risk. 11 The current study uses a quasi-experimental approach with 15 years of linked individual-level suicide and alcohol toxicology data. We investigate how the MLDA of 21 years of age has impacted alcohol positive firearm and non-firearm suicide rates from 2005 to 2021, excluding states where handguns become legal for purchase at age 21. Methods Data sources The CDC established the National Violent Death Reporting System NVDRS in 2003; it captures all deaths in participating states that result from homicide, suicide, legal intervention, unintentional shooting, and injuries of unknown intent. 12 NVDRS has been fully described elsewhere. 13 NVDRS captures the violent deaths in the states which fully participate. 14 In 2005, 16 states participated and as of 2021, all 50 states participate. Routinely collected data includes information on individual demographics, toxicology results and death circumstances, including means of suicide. The number of years states have participated in NVDRS varies, with more states participating over time. We included any state that had at least one year of complete data on suicides. We linked NVDRS suicide data to the CDC WONDER dataset estimating population size, 15 for all states and years for which NVDRS suicide data were available. We obtained population size by gender and ethnicity for the same states and years. We obtained data on the legal age for handgun purchase and possession across states from the Giffords Law Center database. 16 Outcomes The primary outcomes were the change in the proportion of tests positive for alcohol (Yes/No) among suicides, and the change in rates of alcohol positive suicide per 100,000 population at the MLDA. We investigated firearm and non-firearm suicides separately. We aggregated suicides at each year of age, using NVDRS data from 2005 to 2021. Data were collected on whether blood was tested for alcohol at the time of death and whether alcohol was present in the blood. Data were collected on means of suicide: firearm vs. non-firearm. We calculated suicide rates by dividing the number of suicides observed in NVDRS by the size of the population. Secondary analyses examined the mean BAC in suicides with a positive alcohol test, measured in mg/dl, and the change in the rate of alcohol negative suicide per 100,000 population at age 21. We excluded states where a) handguns became legal to purchase at age 21, including only states where they can be purchased from age 18. b) &lt;50% of suicides reported were tested for alcohol on toxicology, and (c) they did not have complete data for at least one year in NVDRS (i.e., incomplete coverage across the population). We also excluded all decedents with no test for alcohol and suicides where the weapon type was unknown (0.2% of suicides). Statistical analysis We used a regression discontinuity (RD) design to exploit the natural experiment at age 21, when all Americans become legally able to purchase alcohol. To isolate the effect of the MLDA we compared alcohol positive firearm and non-firearm suicide rates below and above age 21. We first identified whether outcomes at age 20 and 21 differed significantly using the Z-test for proportions. We then undertook an intention-to-treat analysis through a sharp RD design, 17 where the probability of intervention changed from 0 to 1 at age 21, to understand the real-world effect of the MLDA. Our RD coefficients calculated the level change in the regression line moving from age 20 to 21, accounting for age-related trends either side of this. We also calculated the difference between the observed suicide rate at age 21 and that predicted in the absence of the MLDA, based on the pre-intervention trend. There is no widely agreed-upon method for selection of the optimal bandwidth (i.e., age window) but a minimum recommended number of four values either side of the threshold in studies with discrete running variables. 18 Given that our data was negatively skewed we included more observations from above the MLDA and to balance the trade-off between bias and statistical power in RD 19 we focused on those aged 16–35 years old (i.e., 5 years below the cut-off to 15 years above). Our method assumed that confounders were similarly distributed either side of the age threshold. 20 The basic RD model was as follows: Y a(st) = α + β 1 T a(st) + β 2 Z a(st) + β 3 Z a(st) (2) + β 4 TZ a(st) + ε We used this approach to fit three separate models for total, firearm, and non-firearm suicide rates. Y a(st) represents the alcohol positive (total, firearm, or non-firearm) suicide rate per 100,000 for a particular age year (Y a ) in a set of states (Y s ) over the study period (Y t ). The treatment variable is denoted by T a(st) and equals one for age years 21 and above, and zero otherwise. The coefficient β 1 , our coefficient of interest, indicates the difference in suicide rate between age 20 and 21. Age in years, our running variable, is represented by Z a(st) , with coefficient β 2 and centered at zero. To allow for non-linear trends we included a quadratic term of the running variable, denoted by Z a(st) (2) with coefficient β 3 . To allow the regression slope to vary on either side of the age cut-off, we included an interaction term between our treatment and running variables, denoted by TZ a(st) , with coefficient β 4 . We used two tailed tests of statistical significance with α set at 0.05. We used standard errors clustered on the running variable to account for clustering of individuals at each value. 18 We used the same approach to model the impact of the MLDA on mean BAC (including a subgroup analysis for BAC among males only) and on alcohol negative suicide rates. We added an additional term to control for the proportion of suicides tested for alcohol through sensitivity analysis. We used scatter plots of outcomes and covariates against the running variable to visualize discontinuities at the MLDA. 21 We compared gender and ethnicity characteristics between the 20-year-old and 21-year-old cohorts from included state-years. For this, we calculated the proportion of the overall population at each age year with each characteristic. We repeated our RD model for population subgroups which were significantly different between age 20 and 21 and included at least 50% of the overall population. Further robustness checks included adjusting for the proportion of suicides tested for alcohol at each age year, using narrower age windows, repeating regression models without a quadratic term, using a non-parametric model with a local randomization approach, and repeating the analysis in a) all states where &gt;50% of suicides were tested for alcohol, regardless of handgun minimum age laws (n = 28), b) states where &gt;50% of suicides were tested for alcohol and either handgun possession or purchasing was legal prior to age 21 (n = 18) and c) states where &gt;50% of suicides were tested for alcohol and NVDRS suicide data was collected every year between 2005 and 2021 (n = 8). Results Out of 26 states where the purchase of handguns was legal prior to the age of 21, Texas was excluded as it only partially participated in NVDRS from 2020. A further 11 states were excluded due to &lt;50% of reported suicides across the study period being tested for alcohol on toxicology (Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Indiana, Maine, Mississippi, New Hampshire, Oregon, Pennsylvania, South Dakota and Tennessee). Across 14 remaining states and 169 state-years ( Supplementary File, Tables S1 and S2 ) there were 6807 alcohol positive firearm suicides and 5697 alcohol positive non-firearm suicides among 16–35-year-olds. Among 20-year-olds there were 434 alcohol positive suicides (232 firearm and 202 non-firearm) and among 21-year-olds there were 684 alcohol positive suicides (425 firearm and 259 non-firearm). Of all suicides in these states among 20-year-olds, 72.8% were tested for alcohol and among 21-year-olds 70.6% were tested for alcohol ( Supplementary File, Table S3 ). Across included state-years, differences in gender and ethnicity were all within 0.4% of each other, although the large population size meant that differences of more than 0.1 percentage points were statistically significant ( Supplementary File, Tables S4 ). Demographics remained similar across the 16–35-year-old age range ( Supplementary File, Figs. S1–S6 ). Table 1 shows that the percentage of suicides tested with a positive alcohol result increased from 29.5% at age 20 to 38.7% at age 21 (p &lt; 0.001). The absolute increase in test-positivity was 10.5% for firearm suicides and 6.2% for non-firearm suicides, and the relative increase in test positivity was 31.5% for firearm suicides and 23.6% for non-firearm suicides. In addition, the total alcohol positive suicide rate increased from 3.43 per 100,000 at age 20 to 5.35 per 100,000 at age 21 (p &lt; 0.001), predominantly driven by the firearm suicide rate which increased from 1.83 to 3.33 per 100,000 (p &lt; 0.001). Fig. 1 shows that rates of alcohol positive suicide increased with age for firearm and non-firearms, with overlapping confidence intervals, prior to the MLDA. At the MLDA, there was a large increase in the rate of firearm suicide but not non-firearm suicide. Accounting for age-related trends, the total alcohol positive suicide rate increased by 1.52 per 100,000 (p &lt; 0.001) from age 20 to 21 ( Table 2 ). The alcohol positive firearm suicide rate increased by 1.37 per 100,000 (p &lt; 0.001) from age 20 to 21, while the alcohol positive non-firearm suicide rate increased by 0.15 per 100,000 (p = 0.04). Comparing the observed rate at age 21 to that expected in the absence of the MLDA, there was a 28.6% (95% CI 19.7%–39.0%) excess in total alcohol positive suicides, equivalent to 153 deaths (95% CI 113–192), and a 47.5% (95% CI 24.1%–81.8%) excess in alcohol positive firearm suicides, equivalent to 137 deaths (95% CI 82–191). In subgroup analyses, males saw a larger absolute increase in total alcohol positive suicides of 2.58 per 100,000 (95% CI 1.93–3.22) and alcohol positive firearm suicides of 2.34 per 100,000 (95% CI 1.73–2.96). Relative increases in alcohol positive suicides were similar between males and the overall population. Comparing the observed rate at age 21 to that expected in the absence of the MLDA, there was a 28.4% (95% CI 16.7–42.7%) excess in total male alcohol positive suicides and a 43.9% (95% CI 18.7%–82.5%) excess in male alcohol positive firearm suicides. We found similar results on sensitivity analyses ( Supplementary File, Table S5 ). There was no significant discontinuity in the alcohol negative total or firearm suicide rate, with a significant decrease in the alcohol negative non-firearm suicide rate ( Supplementary File, Fig. S7 and Table S6 ). Fig. 2 shows that among those who tested positive for alcohol, mean BAC increased with age for both firearm and non-firearm suicides, until the late twenties. Mean BAC was consistently higher for firearm compared to non-firearm suicides. Repeating our RD model with mean BAC as the outcome revealed that there was no significant difference across the MLDA in the overall population or in the male subgroup of the population ( Supplementary File, Fig. S8 and Table S7 ). Discussion At the population level, legal access to alcohol at the MLDA led to a large and significant increase in the rate of alcohol positive suicide, driven by an increase in alcohol positive firearm suicide. Rates of total and firearm suicides where alcohol was not involved were similar across the MLDA. Blood alcohol content among alcohol positive suicides was not affected by the MLDA but was higher for firearm compared to non-firearm suicides across all ages. To our knowledge this is the first study to estimate the impact of the U.S MLDA through an RD approach. A 2013 Canadian RD study found a 51.8% increase in alcohol-related attempted suicide at the MLDA (age 19). 22 We found a similar increase for deaths among firearm but not non-firearm suicides. Our results are not directly comparable, since the study in Canada included only those presenting to hospital emergency departments, and suicide attempters and completers show demographic, personality, and clinical differences. 23 In addition, in Canada, an estimated 16% of suicides involve a firearm, 24 compared to over 50% in the U.S. 2 We found a large increase in the rate of alcohol positive suicide due to firearms but not other means at the MLDA. Using the same model to account for age-related trends, the rate of alcohol negative suicide due to firearms did not change significantly across the MLDA. Alcohol reduces inhibitions and impairs judgement. Drinking can promote acutely stressful and emotional situations. 25 While some suicides involve extensive planning, many do not, and the acute period of heightened risk for suicidal behavior is often only minutes long. 26 , 27 Firearms provide a highly lethal and often easily accessible method of suicide, 28 which may explain why individuals are more likely to have alcohol in the blood when they attempt and complete suicide by firearm relative to other means. Our study is subject to several limitations. First, we used a cross-sectional ecological design. Like previous studies in this area, 29 we were unable to follow individuals, or understand whether a decision to die by suicide came before or after the consumption of alcohol, limiting conclusions about the mechanism through which alcohol contributed to suicide. In addition, since the NMDAA has been uniform across states since 1984, we were not able to validate our results with a longitudinal pre-post study comparing suicide rates in populations with different MLDAs. Despite its cross-sectional nature, the RD design provides high-quality evidence relative to other quasi-experimental methods. 30 Second, we did not have complete data on date of birth, limiting our ability to compare similar individuals very close to either side of the MLDA. To address this limitation, we tested for balance in selected confounders across age years, performed subgroup analyses, narrowed the selected age years for analysis, and weighted age years closer to the cut-off through a non-parametric test. Third, although we excluded data from states with low alcohol testing rates, some suicides in the included states were not tested for alcohol. We also assume that alcohol testing practices among suicide victims do not change significantly between ages 20 and 21. We found similar rates of alcohol testing between these ages and performed a sensitivity analysis adjusting for alcohol testing rates to mitigate this risk. Fourth, although the MLDA remained the same in states over time, there may have been time-varying factors correlated with our exposure or outcome, that we were unable to account for, such as changes in firearm policies and/or ownership at the state level. Finally, while we used a national data system, our results are not directly generalizable as several states were excluded based on limited data on suicides and/or alcohol testing in suicides. In addition, not all states contributed equally to our dataset, with the number of states participating in NVDRS increasing over time. The MLDA has been uniform across the U.S since 1984 but some have recently proposed decreasing it. In March 2023, an Illinois lawmaker put forward a bill to reduce the MLDA to 18 years of age. 31 Our findings suggest that an increase in legal access to alcohol among young adults could increase rates of firearm suicide. Public health policies and programs must address risks presented by concurrent access to alcohol and firearms. Advocacy, education, and harsh legal penalties have changed social norms related to drunk-driving over past decades. 32 Yet only a minority of states prohibit concealed carry in bars, nightclubs, sporting arenas, and other places where alcohol is consumed. 33 Some states require people to obtain a permit or license, pursuant to a background check, to acquire or possess firearms. But these checks are largely unaffected by an individual's alcohol-related risk profile unless they have a criminal or mental health history. 34 Laws on whether those caught driving under the influence (DUI) can own and purchase firearms varies across states, with many requiring a severe (felony) DUI offense before restricting firearm access. 35 MLDA legislation is a critical component of public health policy mitigating the harms of young adult alcohol consumption. It is important to understand the impact of this legislation on suicide, which is one of the leading causes of preventable death in this population. Our study demonstrates that there is a large increase in alcohol positive firearm suicide immediately after the MLDA. Across all ages, firearm suicide decedents testing positive for alcohol have higher levels of alcohol in their blood compared to non-firearm suicide decedents. Policies and programmes should aim to weaken the link between alcohol and firearms among those at or just above legal drinking age to prevent suicide.
