ABSTRACT Objectives The reciprocal relationship between oral health and mental health, and the underlying mechanisms remain underexplored. This study examined whether formal social engagement mediates the relationship between oral health and mental health in both directions. Methods Cross‐lagged panel models with two‐wave mediation were applied to data from 3860 older adults, aged 65 years or above, in the two waves of the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging conducted in 2018 and 2020. Oral health was measured using the 12‐item Geriatric Oral Health Assessment Index, and mental health was assessed with the 10‐item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. Results The analysis identified a reciprocal relationship between oral health and depressive symptoms, with depressive symptoms exerting a stronger effect on oral health than vice versa. Formal social engagement partially mediated the effect of oral health on depressive symptoms, but not the other way around. Conclusions The findings reaffirm the interplay and interdependence of oral health and mental health among older adults. Comprehensive strategies that integrate social engagement opportunities are imperative for developing interventions targeting older adults with compromised oral health. cross‐lagged panel models depressive symptoms oral health social engagement South Korea Hanyang University HY‐ 202400000003952 4 Discussion This study aimed to clarify the reciprocal relationship between oral health and mental health in later life and the mediating role of social engagement. Our analyses of two waves of KLoSA data showed that oral health and depressive symptoms were reciprocally related, with social engagement partially mediating the pathway from oral health to depressive symptoms, but not vice versa. As essential aspects of healthy aging, oral health and mental health among older adults have increasingly become focal points for both research and policy [ 3 ]. However, previous studies have often examined the relationship between oral health and mental health unidirectionally [ 9 , 10 ]. Moreover, existing reviews tend to focus on biological and behavioral mechanisms, overlooking potential social factors [ 5 , 6 ]. This study extends the literature by empirically confirming the bidirectional nature of the relationship between oral health and depressive symptoms among older adults, and highlights the explanatory role of social engagement. The findings align with recent empirical studies documenting the bidirectional link between oral health and depressive symptoms [ 7 , 16 ], highlighting the need to consider the interdependence of these two health domains in later life. The analyses further revealed that the impact of depressive symptoms on subsequent oral health was stronger than the reverse effect, emphasizing the potential vulnerability of oral health among older adults experiencing poor mental health and the importance of offering accessible dental care services for this group [ 6 , 13 ]. At the same time, the significant, albeit smaller, role of oral health in protecting mental health reinforces the value of integrated approaches to addressing these interconnected aspects of health [ 5 , 37 ]. This study further elucidated a mediating mechanism of social engagement linking oral health and mental health. Joury et al. [ 5 ], in their comprehensive review, suggested that oral health influences mental health primarily through (1) neuroinflammation, (2) chronic psychological stress, including a decrease in quality of life, and (3) poor nutrition. Conversely, mental health impacts oral health via (1) negative health behaviors, (2) poor dental care utilization, (3) impaired stress and immune responses, and (4) side effects of psychotropic medications [ 5 ]. Complementing and expanding this body of knowledge, the present study verified the relevance of social engagement in partially explaining the pathway from oral health to depressive symptoms. Older adults with better oral health may find it easier to engage socially, which in turn protects their mental health. Given the interconnectedness between social engagement, dental care utilization, and stress‐buffering, more research is warranted to further refine these mechanisms by exploring, for example, when and to what extent specific mechanisms operate and how they interact [ 5 , 6 ]. The hypothesized pathway from depressive symptoms to oral health via social engagement was not supported. Specifically, no significant association between baseline depressive symptoms and subsequent social engagement was identified (Model 3 in Table 2 ). This finding diverges from studies that emphasize the health selection perspective, whereby better health facilitates social engagement [ 22 ]. However, the finding that social engagement in 2018 significantly predicted lower depressive symptoms in 2020 (Model 1 in Table 2 ) lends support to the social causation perspective, suggesting that preceding social conditions influence subsequent health. These findings align with a recent study probing the causal direction between social engagement and mental health [ 19 ], reaffirming that social engagement may be more consequential for mental health than vice versa, at least among older adults in Korea. These findings carry critical implications for public health policies, clinical practices, and community‐based interventions aimed at improving oral health and mental well‐being among older adults. By integrating oral and mental healthcare services with structured social engagement opportunities, practitioners and policymakers can address multiple health determinants simultaneously. Policymakers should consider expanding financial support and insurance coverage for oral healthcare, given its influence on social engagement and mental health. Subsidizing basic dental procedures, dentures, and implants can promote more frequent social interaction, in turn helping protect older adults' mental health [ 27 , 38 ]. Public health campaigns should also aim to normalize discussions around dental and mental health issues among older populations and encourage proactive care seeking to disrupt potential vicious cycles. Clinical practitioners—including primary care providers, dentists, and mental health professionals—can collaborate to identify and address psychosocial challenges stemming from the reciprocal relationship between oral and mental health [ 5 , 6 ]. This might involve depression screening in dental settings, referrals to mental health services, or basic oral check‐ups for individuals presenting with poor mental health [ 13 ]. To ensure equitable access, municipalities could allocate funding for mobile dental clinics or home‐visit services for older adults with mobility limitations or severe mental illnesses [ 14 ]. Community‐based interventions can integrate dental clinics or screening services into senior centers, reducing logistical barriers while fostering preventive dental care and social participation [ 14 ]. Such programs might offer group‐based oral health education and peer support, tailored to older adults' mobility levels and possible dental discomfort or embarrassment. Overall, these findings underscore the need for holistic strategies that address oral health, mental health, and social needs in aging populations. This study has several limitations. First, our analyses relied on only two waves of data, limiting our ability to use more advanced approaches (e.g., random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel models) that differentiate within‐individual from between‐individual variations [ 39 ]. Consequently, the results may be influenced by unobserved biological or lifestyle factors (e.g., systemic inflammation, hormonal dysregulation, medication side effects, or oral hygiene behaviors) or social factors (e.g., stressful life events, neighborhood conditions) known to affect oral and mental health, although a wide range of covariates were controlled for [ 5 ]. To mitigate concerns regarding unobserved time‐invariant confounders, robustness checks were conducted using fixed‐effects models, which largely supported our main findings: changes in oral health and social engagement were associated with changes in depressive symptoms (results available upon request). Nevertheless, future research with multi‐wave longitudinal data and more sophisticated statistical methods would enhance understanding of the reciprocal relationships observed here. Second, the 2‐year interval between waves may not adequately capture short‐term reciprocal effects. For instance, oral health problems, such as dental pain or functional limitations, may have more immediate psychological impacts than our data could detect. Future studies with shorter measurement intervals could help clarify these temporal dynamics. Third, the reliance on self‐reported measures for oral health, mental health, and social engagement, rather than clinical assessments or objective logs, may have introduced reporting bias. Fourth, five out of 12 items in the GOHAI were designed to capture psychosocial difficulties resulting from oral health issues, potentially introducing endogeneity when examining their relationship with social engagement and depressive symptoms. However, sensitivity analyses excluding these five psychosocial items corroborated our primary findings, showing a reciprocal relationship between oral health and depressive symptoms, as well as a partial mediation of social engagement on the effect of oral health on depressive symptoms (results available upon request). Fifth, while statistically significant, the mediating effect of social engagement was relatively small, indicating the need for further studies on additional mechanisms, such as peer‐based dental health behaviors. Lastly, findings based on a Korean sample may have limited generalizability, given that healthcare systems, welfare policies, and cultural norms could shape oral health, depressive symptoms, and social engagement differently across diverse sociocultural contexts [ 40 ]. 5 Conclusion Despite these limitations, this study sheds light on the interconnected nature of oral and mental health among older adults. It contributes to the literature by empirically confirming the reciprocal relationship between oral health and depressive symptoms, and identifying the partial mediating role of social engagement in the pathway from oral health to mental health. The findings highlight the importance of maintaining good oral health and mental well‐being in later life, as each plays a crucial role in protecting the other. Policymakers and practitioners should consider integrating formal social engagement opportunities into oral and mental health promotion strategies, potentially through comprehensive community‐based screening programs addressing both dental and psychosocial needs. Future research should revisit the reciprocal relationship and underlying mechanisms across diverse sociocultural settings. Employing multi‐wave longitudinal data over extended periods is needed to further elucidate these complex dynamics and their implications for healthy aging.
