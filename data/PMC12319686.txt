Abstract Background Emotional and behavioral problems (EBPs) in early adolescence are a growing public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Indonesia. The COVID-19 pandemic has further disrupted adolescent development, highlighting the need to identify both internal and external contributing factors to these issues. Objective This study aimed to assess the prevalence of emotional and behavioral problems among early adolescents in Central Java, Indonesia, and to identify internal and external factors associated with these problems during the post-pandemic transition from hybrid to offline learning. Methods A cross-sectional study was conducted from May to June 2023 involving 256 adolescents aged 10–14 years in Central Java. Emotional and behavioral problems were measured using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire – Self-Report (SDQ-SR). Internal factors were assessed using the Reduced Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (rMEQ) and the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire for Children and Adolescents (ERQ-CA). External factors were measured through sociodemographic questionnaires and the Revised Family Communication Pattern (rFCP) Scale. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample. Associations were analyzed using multiple logistic regression, and model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R 2 . Results The prevalence of adolescents with abnormal total difficulties scores was 15.6%. Among SDQ subscales, emotional problems showed the highest abnormal prevalence (30.1%), followed by peer problems (10.9%), conduct problems (10.5%), and hyperactivity-inattention (4.3%). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that adolescents with an eveningness chronotype were significantly more likely to experience EBPs (OR = 21.11, 95% CI: 5.82–76.63, p &lt;0.001). Similarly, low emotional regulation increased the risk (OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 2.18–11.80, p &lt;0.001). Among external factors, low maternal education (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.12–1.94, p = 0.015) and low intrafamilial communication (OR = 3.35, 95% CI: 1.45–7.74, p = 0.004) were also significant predictors. The model showed good fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ 2 = 6.050, p = 0.642) and explained 40.6% of the variance in EBPs (Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.406). Conclusion Emotional and behavioral problems remain a concern among Indonesian early adolescents in the post-pandemic, with emotional symptoms being the most prevalent. Internal risk factors, such as eveningness chronotype and low emotional regulation, as well as external factors like maternal education level and intrafamilial communication, were significantly associated with these issues. These findings suggest a need for early mental health screening and targeted interventions. For nursing practice, this highlights the role of nurses in school and community settings in promoting emotional regulation, strengthening family communication, and delivering health education tailored to familial and developmental contexts. Background Emotional development during adolescence is a highly complex process, influenced by a range of internal and external factors. Internally, adolescents undergo significant hormonal changes that impact their emotional regulation and mood stability (Ho et al., 2022 ; Pfeifer &amp; Allen, 2021 ). These physiological transformations during puberty are closely linked to increased vulnerability to emotional and behavioral issues, including symptoms of depression, anxiety, and various behavioral disorders (Luo et al., 2024 ). Externally, adolescents experience substantial shifts in their social relationships, particularly with parents, peers, and teachers, which can further impact their emotional well-being (Petersen, 2021 ). In Indonesia, emotional and behavioral problems are officially categorized as mental health issues. According to the 2023 Basic Health Research, individuals aged 15–24 years exhibited a prevalence of 2.8%, exceeding the overall population average of 2.0%. In Central Java, one of the country’s most populous provinces, mental health issues in this age group range from 1.5–2%, placing it in the upper third among Indonesian regions (Astutik et al., 2021 ). However, national data on the prevalence of mental health problems in individuals under 15 years of age remains unavailable. Globally, the COVID-19 pandemic has intensified emotional and behavioral problems among adolescents. The resulting disruptions, such as school closures, social isolation, and increased family stress, have significantly contributed to a rise in anxiety, depression, and behavioral regulation difficulties (Ammar et al., 2021 ). In Pakistan, children and adolescents in isolation reported symptoms including depression, low self-esteem, emotional dysregulation, anger issues, and social withdrawal (Imran et al., 2020 ). Similarly, in Indonesia, the abrupt shift from online to hybrid and then offline schooling in 2022 posed additional stressors as adolescents struggled to readjust to traditional school environments and expectations. Comparable trends have been observed internationally; for example, Finnish adolescents experienced increased emotional distress due to prolonged uncertainty and isolation (Hemberg et al., 2024 ), and Italian youth with attention and hyperactivity issues reported worsening symptoms during lockdowns (Melegari et al., 2021 ). Despite these findings, research on emotional and behavioral problems among early adolescents, especially at the community level in Indonesia and Southeast Asia, remains limited. The pandemic’s effect on the education system highlighted systemic unpreparedness, as both adolescents and parents struggled to adapt to online learning formats (Zaharah et al., 2020 ). In rural areas, students reported six major challenges during lockdowns: inadequate technology, unfamiliar academic environments, digital disconnection, physical health concerns, media distractions, and digital illiteracy (Srinivasan et al., 2021 ). Parental involvement also increased during this period, with 39.9% of parents reporting moderate to severe anxiety, which in turn heightened anxiety in their children (Lee et al., 2021 ). Risk factors for emotional and behavioral problems are broadly categorized into internal and external domains. Internal factors include gender, age, physical activity, personal or family history of mental health disorders (Jusiene et al., 2022 ), disturbances in circadian rhythms (Sun et al., 2021 ), intellectual functioning (Hulsmans et al., 2021 ), and difficulties with emotional regulation (te Brinke et al., 2021 ). Puberty-associated shifts in circadian rhythm, if misaligned with environmental or lifestyle patterns, can disrupt sleep and impair emotional stability and behavioral control. Emotional regulation plays a central role in how adolescents respond to emotional experiences, and deficits in this area are a common root of behavioral and emotional problems. External risk factors come from the broader social environment, including family dynamics, peer relationships, and school settings. Other external influences include socioeconomic status (Jusiene et al., 2022 ), poor communication within the family, parental mental illness (Jusiene et al., 2022 ), parental substance use disorders (Frans et al., 2022 ), and a history of abuse (Kim et al., 2023 ). Among these, intrafamilial communication stands out as both a risk and protective factor—healthy dialogue supports emotional well-being, while dysfunctional patterns contribute to emotional and behavioral disturbances. These emotional and behavioral problems can significantly affect adolescents’ academic performance and social adjustment. Challenges in school, including poor academic achievement, difficulty adapting, and even dropping out, are often linked to unresolved emotional issues (Branje &amp; Morris, 2021 ). For instance, in the 2017/2018 academic year, Indonesia reported 10.1 million junior high school students, among whom Central Java recorded 2,599 students repeating a grade and 4,891 dropping out (Kementerian Pendidikan Kebudayaan Riset dan Teknologi, 2022 ). Left unaddressed, these problems can escalate into more severe issues, such as juvenile delinquency—a growing concern in Indonesia. While studies on the prevalence and determinants of emotional and behavioral problems exist in countries such as the United Kingdom (Nonweiler et al., 2020 ), China (Qin et al., 2021 ), Turkey (Ma et al., 2021 ), Japan (Takahashi &amp; Honda, 2021 ), Chile (Ma et al., 2021 ), and Italy (Esposito et al., 2021 ), research in Southeast Asia, particularly Indonesia, remains scarce. The only known study on this topic in Indonesia was conducted in the Yogyakarta province during 2021, when schools were still operating online (Sutomo et al., 2022 ). Other Indonesian studies have primarily focused on related variables like anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation rather than broader emotional and behavioral problems (Akbar &amp; Yenny, 2022 ; Anindyajati et al., 2021 ; Putra et al., 2022 ). Given the rising prevalence of emotional and behavioral issues among adolescents, especially during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, this is an area of growing importance for nursing professionals. Nurses, particularly those in pediatric, mental health, and community roles, are well-positioned to support early detection, prevention, and intervention efforts. Thus, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and key determinants of emotional and behavioral problems among early adolescents in Indonesia. Methods Study Design This study employed a cross-sectional design to investigate emotional and behavioral problems among early adolescents in Banyumas, Central Java, Indonesia. Sample/Participants The target population comprised junior high school students aged 12 to 16 years, enrolled in both public and private schools in Banyumas Regency. For the 2020/2021 academic year, the total student population in this group was 23,414. The required sample size was calculated using the binomial proportion formula (Ennis &amp; Bi, 1998 ), resulting in 256 respondents. This calculation was based on an estimated proportion (p) of 0.2 and q = 0.8, referencing the prevalence of mental and emotional health problems in early adolescents as reported by Tjongjono et al. ( 2019 ). The inclusion criteria included students who were actively enrolled in the current semester and attending non-boarding schools. The exclusion criteria included students who did not reside with their parents. A multistage sampling technique was employed. Initially, stratified random sampling was used to differentiate urban and rural sub-districts. Of the 27 sub-districts in Banyumas, four are classified as urban, and 23 are classified as rural. From these, two urban and three rural sub-districts were selected purposively. Within each selected sub-district, schools were randomly chosen. Finally, proportionate sampling was used within each school to recruit participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Instruments Emotional and behavioral problems were assessed using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire Self-Rated (SDQ-SR), developed by Goodman ( 2001 ) and translated into Indonesian by Wiguna et al. ( 2020 ). The Indonesian version of the SDQ-SR has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.773. Use of the SDQ-SR was authorized in accordance with ethical research standards. Internal risk factors included gender, age, chronotype, emotion regulation, and a history of bullying. External risk factors encompass parental age, parental education level, parental history of substance use, and intrafamilial communication. Chronotype was measured using the Reduced Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (rMEQ), developed by Adan and Almirall ( 1991 ). This instrument was culturally adapted and translated into Indonesian by the research team, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.847. Permission for its use was obtained via email from the original authors, Prof. Ana Adan and Helena Almirall. Emotion regulation was measured using the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), developed by Gross and John ( 2003 ) and translated into Indonesian by Radde et al. ( 2021 ), with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.951.Intrafamilial communication was assessed using the Revised Family Communication Pattern (rFCP) by Koerner and Fitzpatrick ( 2002 ), translated into Indonesian by Rizkyanti et al. ( 2021 ), with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.864. The ERQ and rFCP instruments were used for academic, non-commercial research purposes, with proper citation and without modification, in accordance with their licensing terms. Additional risk factors, such as parental smoking and alcohol use, as well as bullying history, were assessed through closed-ended questions included in the questionnaire. All instruments were compiled into a single questionnaire packet. The average completion time was approximately 30–45 minutes. Data Collection Data were collected from April to May 2022. At each research site, respondents were gathered in a designated hall to complete the questionnaire collectively. Prior to data collection, informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from the adolescents themselves. Only those who provided both consent and assent were included in the study. Research assistants were present during the administration of the questionnaires to assist with any technical or comprehension issues that may have arisen. After completion, all instruments were collected and reviewed by the research team to ensure completeness before proceeding with analysis. Data Analysis Data were processed using SPSS Statistics 23.0. Each instrument was scored based on its specific scoring guidelines (SDQ-SR, rMEQ, ERQ, and rFCP). Variables requiring transformation were appropriately coded for analysis. Descriptive statistics (univariate analysis) were used to summarize respondent characteristics and the prevalence of emotional and behavioral problems. Binary logistic regression was conducted to examine the association between internal and external risk factors and the occurrence of emotional and behavioral problems. Ethical Considerations Ethical approval was granted by the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee (MHREC) of FKKMK-UGM, with approval number KE/FK/0659/EC/2022. All procedures strictly adhered to national and international ethical guidelines for research involving minors, including principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant local regulations. Parental or guardian consent and adolescent assent were obtained after providing comprehensive, age-appropriate information regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality safeguards, and the voluntary nature of participation. Consent and assent were formally documented and securely stored before the commencement of data collection. In addition, a research permit was obtained from the relevant local authorities to ensure compliance with institutional and governmental research policies. All research assistants involved in data collection underwent structured training sessions focused on ethical conduct, culturally sensitive communication, and standardized administration of study instruments. Results Characteristics of Indonesian Early Adolescents Among the 256 respondents, the majority (93.8%) had morningness or intermediate chronotypes. Most participants (79.3%) demonstrated high levels of emotional regulation. Regarding parental education, 87.9% of fathers and 89.8% of mothers had primary or secondary education levels. A large proportion of fathers smoked (69.1%) and 7.4% consumed alcohol, whereas 98.8% of mothers did not smoke, and none reported alcohol consumption. Furthermore, 58.6% of adolescents reported high levels of intrafamilial communication ( Table 1 ). Prevalence of Emotional and Behavioral Problems Table 2 illustrates that most respondents were in the normal and borderline categories across the five subscales: emotional problems, conduct problems, hyperactivity-intention, peer problems, prosocial, and total difficulties. The highest prevalence of abnormal categories was found in emotional problems (30.1%). Risk Factors for Emotional and Behavioral Problems Table 3 shows that both chronotype and emotional regulation were significant internal risk factors. Adolescents with eveningness chronotypes were 21.11 times more likely to experience emotional and behavioral problems than those with morningness. Similarly, adolescents with low emotional regulation had a 5.08 times higher risk. Among external factors, the mother’s education level and intrafamilial communication were statistically significant. Adolescents whose mothers had low education levels had a 1.34 times greater risk, and those reporting low intrafamilial communication had a 3.35 times higher risk of emotional and behavioral problems. Model evaluation using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good fit (χ 2 = 6.050, df = 8, p = 0.642). The Nagelkerke R 2 value of 0.406 suggests that the model explains approximately 40.6% of the variance in emotional and behavioral problems. Discussion Summary of the Findings To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively assess the prevalence and associated internal and external factors of emotional and behavioral problems among early adolescents in Indonesia. Our results revealed that emotional problems were the most prevalent difficulty among adolescents (30.1%), followed by total difficulties (15.6%), peer problems (10.9%), and conduct problems (10.5%). This rate is slightly higher than that reported by Wiguna et al. ( 2020 ), possibly reflecting the psychosocial impact of post-pandemic school transitions and adolescent development stage. Several key risk factors were identified. Internally, chronotype and emotional regulation were significantly associated with emotional and behavioral problems. Adolescents with an eveningness chronotype were more than 21 times as likely to experience such difficulties, aligning with prior research linking eveningness to insomnia, poor academic performance, substance use, and higher psychological distress (Kansagra, 2020 ; Karan et al., 2021 ; Martinez-Cayuelas et al., 2022 ). The disruption of sleep and activity patterns during the COVID-19 pandemic, including increased internet use and shifting school routines, may have contributed to these outcomes (Helgadóttir et al., 2023 ; Kokka et al., 2021 ). Low emotional regulation also increased the likelihood of psychosocial problems. Emotion regulation is essential during adolescence to manage stress and interpersonal challenges, and it develops through early familial interactions, particularly maternal responses to a child’s emotions (Perry et al., 2020 ). This is especially relevant in a predominantly female sample, as hormonal changes and earlier pubertal onset among girls influence emotional reactivity (Demir et al., 2023 ; Lustig et al., 2021 ). Poor emotional regulation has been linked to adolescent anxiety, depression, and diminished academic performance (Gao et al., 2022 ; Kokkinos &amp; Vlavianou, 2021 ). Externally, maternal education and intrafamilial communication significantly influenced adolescent mental health. Lower maternal education was associated with increased emotional and behavioral difficulties, possibly due to less effective parenting and limited access to mental health resources (Goodman et al., 2020 ). Furthermore, adolescents who reported poor communication with family members were over three times more likely to have psychological problems, consistent with other findings that underscore the protective role of family dialogue during crises (Molu et al., 2022 ; Yusuf et al., 2021 ). The timing of this study, during the post-pandemic educational transition, is crucial. Indonesia’s shift from online to hybrid, and finally to fully offline learning, disrupted adolescents’ routines, heightened school stress, and reduced parental involvement (Bülow et al., 2021 ; Zaharah et al., 2020 ). While remote learning offered flexibility, returning to strict school rules and reduced family presence contributed to anxiety and adjustment difficulties. Emotional issues such as anxiety persisted, particularly during this period of readjustment (Eirich et al., 2022 ; Mahfouz, 2020 ). Peer and conduct problems also emerged as significant issues. The resumption of in-person interactions reactivated peer-related stressors such as rejection, conflict, and loneliness, which are linked to self-harm and depressive symptoms (Cheek et al., 2020 ; De Luca et al., 2022 ). Conduct problems, although less prevalent than in clinical samples (Wiguna et al., 2020 ), remained relevant, particularly as adolescents readjusted to external authority and disciplinary norms. These behavioral issues may also be influenced by pandemic-related increases in screen time, a factor not directly measured in this study but widely documented (Morita et al., 2022 ). Limitations of the Study Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal interpretation of the relationships observed. While the associations between risk factors and emotional or behavioral problems are significant, longitudinal studies are needed to establish directionality and long-term effects. Second, the study relied on self-report questionnaires, which are susceptible to social desirability and recall bias. Adolescents may underreport or misinterpret their emotional states, sleep habits, or family dynamics. The inclusion of parent or teacher reports in future studies could help triangulate findings and minimize reporting bias. Third, the sample was geographically limited to a regency in Central Java, which may affect the generalizability of the results to other Indonesian regions with different sociocultural or economic conditions. Adolescents in rural or urban areas may face distinct stressors, necessitating broader regional sampling in future research. Fourth, although the study took place during a period of educational and social transition following the COVID-19 pandemic, the effects of pandemic-related stressors (e.g., parental job loss, family illness, or digital access inequalities) were not comprehensively analyzed. Including such variables could provide richer insight into the complex interplay of pandemic impacts on adolescent mental health. Finally, while data collection was supervised by teachers, conducting it in a communal setting may have affected concentration and response accuracy. Additionally, several potential confounders, including socioeconomic status, parental mental health, and access to healthcare services, were not measured but could significantly influence adolescent outcomes. Implications of the Study for Nursing Practice The findings of this study have substantial implications for community and school nursing practice. Nurses are uniquely positioned to address adolescent emotional and behavioral problems through early screening, targeted health education, and advocacy. First, chronotype assessment and sleep education should be integrated into school health programs. Understanding adolescents’ sleep patterns can help tailor interventions that improve mental health, attention, and behavior in the classroom. Second, emotional regulation training, such as mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral strategies, and social-emotional learning (SEL) programs, should be prioritized. Nurses can deliver or facilitate such programs and help build adolescents’ coping skills during periods of stress and change. Third, the study highlights the need for family-based interventions. Nurses should engage parents, particularly mothers, in educational sessions that emphasize effective communication, emotional support, and adolescent development. They can also help reduce maternal stress by advocating for broader structural supports, such as school-parent partnerships and flexible work policies. Finally, interprofessional collaboration among nurses, teachers, psychologists, and policymakers is essential. Coordinated mental health services in schools and communities can ensure that adolescents experiencing difficulties receive timely support. Nurses can lead advocacy efforts for adolescent mental health resources, especially in underserved regions. Conclusion This study highlights several significant internal and external risk factors associated with emotional and behavioral problems in adolescents. Evening chronotype and low emotional regulation were strongly linked to higher odds of abnormal outcomes, even after adjusting for other variables. Among external factors, low maternal education and poor intrafamilial communication also showed consistent and significant associations in multivariate models. These findings emphasize the importance of both individual traits and family environment in adolescent mental health, suggesting that interventions targeting emotional regulation skills and family communication could be particularly effective in prevention strategies. This study reinforces the need for holistic, preventive, and family-centered nursing strategies to address adolescent mental health. By targeting modifiable internal and external risk factors, nursing practice can play a transformative role in promoting adolescent well-being during and after transitional periods, such as the post-pandemic era.
