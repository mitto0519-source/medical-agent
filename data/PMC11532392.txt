The effect of frailty transition and burden on the risk of all-cause mortality in South Korea remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the risk of all-cause mortality using the most recent frailty index (FI), changes in FI, and frailty burden. We analyzed data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (2013–2020). A total of 3,134 participants aged 53–87 years with a computable FI based on the osteoporotic fracture index during their initial visit. The FI was updated biennially during re-examinations and changes between the initial and last visits were categorized into four groups: (1) improved or maintained to non-frail, (2) worsened to pre-frail, (3) improved or maintained to pre-frail, and (4) worsened or maintained to frail. We used the Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for age, sex, education, lifestyle factors, and diseases. During the follow-up, 218 participants died. Compared to those who were robust at the last visit, pre-frailty and frailty were associated with an increased risk of all-cause death. Of those who visited &gt; 2 times, 62.3% improved or remained robust, and had a decreased risk of all-cause death. Those with &gt; 63% of pre-frailty or frailty burden significantly higher risk of death, with &gt; 60% increase compared to their non-frail counterparts. Maintaining or achieving robustness is associated with a decreased risk of mortality. To prevent premature death and extend healthy life expectancy, identifying biological aging through surrogate measures and implementing interventions to maintain or achieve a robust health status are needed. Amid the global population rapidly aging and growing emphasis on extending healthy life expectancy rather than life, frailty has emerged as a critical public health issue that demands focus and resolution 5 . Frailty is potentially reversible and preventable over time 6 , 7 , thus effective evidence-based strategies focusing on delaying and reversing the progression of frailty are necessary to reduce the associated burden. Many epidemiological studies have suggested various frailty indices (FI), defined by an accumulation of health deficits, to assess frailty status 1 , 8 – 10 . These indices have shown a robust and independent association between frailty and mortality 11 , 12 . Additionally, a longitudinal study using four population-based cohort studies showed that FI changes predicted mortality independent of baseline FI differences 13 . Time-varying FI using longitudinal repeated measurements was also associated with all-cause, cancer, and cardiovascular mortality and suggested the importance of efforts to prevent frailty progression or reversal 14 . The National Health in Aging Trends Study showed that a decrease in FI over one year, measured using a comprehensive 40-item deficit accumulation frailty index covering various domains such as comorbidities, activities of daily living, physical tasks, cognition, and performance testing, was not associated with a reduced mortality risk 15 . As FI changes over time, the time-updated FI (the latest FI measurement available) over multiple follow-up measurements may provide a greater prognostic value than baseline FI. However, previous studies have mostly relied on FI assessed only at baseline 13 – 15 . Furthermore, studies on the relationship between the transition of frailty and mortality, association between reversal of frailty and reduction of mortality risk, and impact of frailty burden during follow-up on the risk of mortality remain unclear. While other frailty assessment tools consider multiple factors 1 , 8 – 11 , such as physical function, capacity in daily living, cognitive function, comorbidities, and other health-related aspects, the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures (SOF) index offers the advantage of being straightforward for self-assessment 17 . Based on these considerations, we used SOF index to assess frailty status and aimed to examine the impact of baseline FI, time-updated FI, changes in FI, and frailty burden on the risk of all-cause mortality in adults in Korea. This will provide the necessary evidence base to support future investment in intervention trials aimed at prevention and management of frailty. Methods Data source Our study was based on baseline and follow-up data from the Ansan-Ansung (urban-rural area) cohort within the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), an ongoing prospective population-based cohort study conducted by the Korea National Institute of Health, Korea. Details of the study design and participants of this ongoing cohort study have been previously reported 16 . Enrollment in the study was based on the communities’ characteristics. This was the most efficient method for recruiting a representative sample of the Korean population. Initially, there were 10,030 participants, aged 40–69 between 2001 and 2002 and followed up every two years. During each face-to-face visit, trained interviewers and staff used standard methods and protocols to collect information on socio demographics, lifestyle factors, medical history, anthropometric measures, and blood and urine tests. Frailty was assessed using the SOF index 17 , which was proposed using three components (irrespective of the intent to lose 5% or more weight, inability to rise from a chair five times without using the arms, and poor energy on the Geriatric Depression Scale). For this study, participants who visited at least once between 8th and 10th waves and had at least one calculable FI were included. Only participants from the Ansung area were surveyed for the items necessary to calculate the FI; participants from the Ansan area were not surveyed for these items and thus could not have a FI calculated. Therefore, participants who died before the 8th wave, those lost to follow-up between the 8th and 10th waves, and participants from the Ansan area were excluded from the analysis (Figure S1 ). Therefore, our study population is as follows: First, participants lost to follow-up between 2013 and 2020 due to mortality or other reasons ( n = 2,850), and those residing in urban areas ( n = 3,433) were excluded. Additionally, participants who were unavailable for FI calculation from 7th to 10th waves ( n = 613) were also excluded. Consequently, a total of 3,134 participants were included in this study. Weight loss for the SOF index at each wave was determined by calculating the difference in weight between the current visit and the visit from two years ago (previous visit). Participants with a weight loss ≥ 5% for blood pressure management were considered to have the intended weight loss. We calculated FI from the 7th (2013–2014) to 10th wave (2019–2020) of the Ansung cohort within KoGES data (1883, 862, 260, and 129 participants were computed at the 7th, 8th, 9 th, and 10th waves, respectively) (Figure S2 ). A total of 3,134 participants aged 53–87 were included. Among them, of the four waves from the 7th to the 10th waves, 1,199 participants were able to calculate FI in all four waves, 941 participants in three waves, 541 participants in two waves, and 453 participants in one wave. The earliest visit when FI could be calculated was considered baseline FI and the last visit time-updated FI. To assess FI transition, we created a time-updated covariate representing the most recently available FI for each participant over the course of the study (Figure S2 ). Ethics statement All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (IRB no. 2021-04-02–1 C-A), and approval date (12/4/2022). All research procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Frailty status, frailty transition, burden of pre-frailty or frailty Subjects who did not meet any of the three criteria of the SOF index were classified as non-frail, those who met only one criterion pre-frail, and those who met two or more criteria were classified as frail. Among participants who had at least two occasions of FI calculation during 2013–2020 in KoGES ( n = 2,681), the transition of frailty was classified into four groups based on the FI change between the initial and last visits as follows: (1) improved or maintained to non-frail (non-frail to non-frail, pre-frail to non-frail, or frail to non-frail), (2) worsened to pre-frail (non-frail to pre-frail), (3) improved or maintained to pre-frail (pre-frail to pre-frail, or frail to pre-frail), and (4) worsened or maintained to frail (non-frail to frail, pre-frail to frail, or frail to frail). The burden of pre-frailty or frailty was defined as the cumulative proportion of visits in which FI exceeded one, indicating the presence of pre-frailty or frailty during the follow-up period. This measure quantified the extent to which an individual experienced a pre-frail or frail state during the study. For instance, one participant had four visits during the follow-up period and exhibited non-frailty at the initial visit, pre-frailty at the second visit, frailty at the third visit, and pre-frailty at the fourth visit; the burden of pre-frailty or frailty was 75%, as three of four visits reflected pre-frail or frail states. Ascertainment of outcome The primary outcome was the all-cause mortality. Mortality data for the participants were obtained from the National Death Records until December 31, 2020. To analyze the relationship between baseline FI and all-cause death, participants were followed-up from their initial visit until the occurrence of death or December 31, 2020 (the end date of the study period), whichever came first. For the association between the time-updated FI, transition of frailty, and burden of pre-frailty or frailty with all-cause death, they were followed up from their last visit until the occurrence of death or the end date of the study period, whichever came first (Figure S3 ). Statistical analysis The study population’s characteristics related to frailty status were recorded at the initial and final visits. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation) and compared using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), whereas categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi-square tests. The rates of all-cause death were computed as the number of deaths during follow-up period divided by the total person-years at risk and were reported as the number of subjects per 1,000 person-years. Kaplan-Meier curves were created for the period between baseline and outcome according to frailty status, and survival distributions were compared using the log-rank test when the proportional hazard assumption was met. This was tested using scaled Schoenfeld residuals 18 . The predictive accuracy of the SOF index was obtained by calculating c-indices based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). The AUC and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using nonparametric methods. The relationship between frailty status at the initial and last visits, frailty transition, and incidence of all-cause death was determined using a Cox proportional hazard regression model. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, education level, smoking, drinking, regular exercise, sweating for at least 30 min/week, sleep time, body mass index (BMI), hand grip strength (HGS) as calculated maximal grip strength, and diseases (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease [CKD], cardiovascular disease [CVD], cancer, dementia and fracture). Restricted cubic spline curves were constructed to determine the cumulative effects of pre-frailty and frailty on the risk of death. All statistical tests conducted were two-tailed, and P &lt; 0.05 represented statistical significance. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (ver. 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) or R 4.2.0 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). The R ( http://cran.r-project.org ) packages “survival,” “ggplot2” and “pROC” were used to conduct the survival analysis. Results The characteristics at the initial and last visit according to frailty status are shown in Table 1 . Among the 3,134 participants from rural areas, 149 (4.8%) and 174 (5.6%) were categorized as frail at the initial and final visits, respectively. The prevalence of frailty at the last visit increased with age: 1.44% and 2.78% in men and women aged &lt; 65 years, respectively, to 6.33% and 13.87% in those 80 years and older, respectively. Therefore, the frailty index showed a right-skewed distribution towards older age ( Figure S4 ). Women had a higher prevalence of frailty than men across all age groups. The prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty was higher in participants who were less educated, underweight (BMI &lt; 18.5 kg/m 2 ), or had lower HGS (Table 1 and Figure S4 ). During the follow-up period, 1,693 (62.3%) participants improved or remained non-frail, 452 (16.9%) worsened to pre-frail, 398 (14.9%) improved or remained pre-frail, and 138 (5.2%) worsened or remained frail (Table 2 ). Participants who worsened or remained frail were older, female, had a lower education level, weaker HGS, less regular exercise, and a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus, CKD and CVD. During the follow-up period from the 7th to the 10th wave (follow-up period from the initial visit, median 6.4 years and IQR, 4.6–7.3 years; from the last visit, median 1.4 years and IQR, 0.5–1.8 years), 218 (7.0%) participants died. As expected, the incidence rate (IR) of death according to frailty status at both the initial and last visits increased with worsening frailty status (at the initial visit: 2.51, 4.53, and 7.13/100,000 person-years in non-frail, pre-frail, and frail individuals, respectively; at the last visit: 8.53, 13.33, and 22.62/100,000 person-years in non-frail, pre-frail, and frail individuals, respectively; Table 3 ). Based on the Kaplan-Meier curves of survival probability for participants during follow-up, those who were frail at the initial and last visits had the lowest survival probabilities compared to non-frail or pre-frail individuals (both log-rank p &lt; 0.001, Figure S5 ). When stratified by sex, the incidence of all-cause death also differed significantly according to the frailty status in both men and women ( Figure S5 ). The F1 C-indices FI at the initial and last visits for death were 0.59 (95% CI, 0.55–0.63) and 0.60 (95% CI, 0.56–0.63), respectively ( Figure S6 ). The predictive ability after the last visit was slightly higher but modest. Nevertheless, compared to non-frail participants, their pre-frail counterparts had a higher risk of death (fully adjusted hazard ratio [95% CI] based on the initial visit 1.47 [1.10–1.97], and last visit 1.37 [1.02–1.85]), and frail participants (fully adjusted hazard ratio [95% CI] based on the initial visit 1.83 [1.11–3.03], and last visit 1.77 [1.13–2.75]) (Table 3 ). In the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, all-cause mortality was significantly different between the frailty transition groups (log-rank p &lt; 0.05, Fig. 1 ). In the adjusted models for age and sex, compared with participants who maintained or improved to non-frail, those who improved or remained pre-frail, and worsened or maintained frailty, had a higher risk of death (HR [95% CI], 1.87 [1.19–2.95] for subjects with improvement or maintenance to pre-frail, and 2.47 [1.42–4.32] for those with worsened or maintained frailty, Table 3 ). After entering all the variables into the model, the risk of all-cause mortality remained significantly higher in participants with improved or maintained pre-frailty and those with worsened or maintained frailty. However, compared to participants who worsened or remained frail, those who improved or remained non-frail had a 45.4% lower risk of all-cause mortality. Figure 2 depicts the risk of all-cause death associated with the cumulative proportion of pre-frail or frail individuals during follow-up using restricted cubic spline analysis (with a cumulative proportion of 0% as a reference, indicating maintained non-frailty during follow-up). The cumulative proportion of participants with FI scores of &gt; 1 during follow-up significantly associated with the risk of death (HR [95% CI] per 10% increase, 1.08 [1.02–1.14]). Compared with maintained non-frailty during follow-up, a cumulative proportion &gt; 63% of pre-frailty or frailty during follow-up significantly increased the risk of death by &gt; 60%. Discussion In this prospective cohort study of community-dwelling rural Korean adults aged 50 years, we assessed the risk of all-cause mortality according to FI at the initial and last visits, transition of frailty, and burden of pre-frailty or frailty. This is the first study to investigate the transition to, and burden of, frailty and mortality in South Korea. The main findings are summarized as follows: First, the prevalence of frailty was higher with increasing age in women than men, and in those participants with a lower educational level, underweight, and lower HGS. Second, the risk of mortality increased with worsening frailty status using the SOF index at the initial and last visits in both men and women. Third, those participants who maintained or improved their robustness had a significantly reduced risk of mortality. Furthermore, a higher burden of frailty or pre-frailty associated with a progressively higher risk of mortality. These findings highlight the potential population- and individual-level effects of interventions on mortality risk. The prevalence of frailty estimates exhibited substantial variability (ranging from 4 to 59%) due to differences in the study population’s characteristics, variations in the criteria for participant exclusion, and utilization of diverse frailty measurement approaches across studies 19 – 21 . Nevertheless, many previous studies have consistently reported that the prevalence of frailty is higher in women than men, increases with age, and higher among individuals with lower educational levels and underweight status 21 – 23 . Our results concurred with these findings. Over the past 20 years, global life expectancy at birth increased from 67.2 years in 2000 to 73.5 years in 2019, and healthy life expectancy (HALE) 58.6 years in 2000 to 63.5 years in 2019 24 . Although both life expectancy and HALE improved during the same period, many countries have seen an increase in the number of years spent in poor health. Additionally, owing to the rapid expansion of the aging population, the number of older adults with frailty is expected to increase rapidly. Therefore, frailty has become one of the most serious global public health problems of the coming century, and effective strategies to prevent and delay onset, slow progression, and reverse frailty are paramount 5 , 7 . Shorter follow-up periods predict the risk of mortality according to FI more accurately than long-term follow-up periods 12 , 25 . Considering the dynamic nature of frailty, a more recent frailty assessment is likely to provide the best information about older adults’ health status and their current health status, including frailty status and improved mortality predictions 26 , 27 . Consistent with previous studies, our results showed that frailty and pre-frailty status using FI had a higher risk of mortality than non-frailty status in both short- and long-term follow-up periods. Contrastingly, the predictive ability of FI at the last visit was higher than that of the initial visit; however, the difference in predictive strength was negligible. Our findings suggest that frailty influences the risk of death regardless of the duration of follow-up. Other studies have also reported an association between frailty transition and mortality risk 13 , 15 , 27 – 29 . The Taiwan Longitudinal Study on Aging showed that the worsened frailty group (decrease in frailty phenotype during four years) had significantly higher all-cause mortality than maintained robustness or prefrailty group 28 . In another cohort study of community-dwelling Mexican Americans, older adults who transitioned from pre-frail to frail, frail to pre-frail, and remained frail for over three years had an increased 15-year mortality risk 29 . However, these studies focused on predicting the risk of death according to frailty transition. Studies reporting on the risk reduction of mortality are limited. Our results showed that improved or maintained robustness associated with a decreased risk of mortality compared to worsened or maintained frailty status, after adjusting for age, sex, education level, lifestyle factors (smoking, drinking, regular exercise, and sleep time), BMI, hand grip strength, and diseases, with a 54.6% reduction in mortality risk. This study also provides the cumulative effects of frailty and pre-frailty on the risk of mortality during the follow-up period. This approach has not been adopted by any previous study assessing the association between frailty status and mortality in the general population. During the follow-up period, &gt; 63% of pre-frail or frail participants had a significantly higher risk of mortality. Therefore, minimizing the burden of pre-frailty or frailty through early intervention and control could reduce the risk of mortality. However, this approach has not been reported to date. Therefore, efforts to delay frailty or pre-frailty as much as possible or return to robustness after becoming frail or pre-frail are important for reducing the risk of mortality. Strengths and limitations This study had several limitations. First, the study population comprised healthy and middle-aged Korean participants recruited from a rural area (Ansung) within the KoGES cohort study with a low death rate. Therefore, our results may not be applicable to the general Korean population. Second, the SOF index, which consists of two short questions and one simple physical test, offers the advantages of simplicity and applicability in clinical settings 17 . Various comprehensive frailty assessment tools incorporating measures of physical function, capacity in daily living, cognitive function, comorbidities, and other health-related aspects have been proposed 1 , 8 – 11 . We were unable to apply these alternative frailty indicators to the cohort data. Instead, we provided findings adjusted for various factors including demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, body composition, and diseases. Third, in the frailty transition and burden analyses, participants who visited only once (dead or lost to follow-up) were excluded. The exclusion of participants may have influenced the results. Therefore, our results may have been an underestimation. Fourth, the comparison of frailty transition among the nine groups was limited by small sample sizes, resulting in low statistical power for assessing mortality risk. However, when comparing the nine groups, individuals who transitioned from non-frail to frail, pre-frail to pre-frail, and pre-frail to frail had a significantly increased mortality risk compared with those who remained non-frail ( Table S1 ). However, this study had several strengths. First, a large homogeneous population based on a well-designed 6-year longitudinal database was used. Second, this is the first study to investigate the association between frailty transition and burden of mortality in Korea. Conclusions In a prospective longitudinal cohort study in rural areas of Korea, the SOF index measured at both the initial and last visits (i.e., long- and short-term effects) was a simple and useful tool for predicting the risk of all-cause death. Improved or maintained robustness is associated with decreased risk of mortality. Minimizing the burden of pre-frailty or frailty through early intervention and control can reduce the risk of mortality. Therefore, efforts to delay frailty or pre-frailty as much as possible or to return to robustness after becoming frail or pre-frail are important to reduce mortality risk.
