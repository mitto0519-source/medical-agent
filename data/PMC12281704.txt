Objective This study aimed to examine whether loneliness mediate associations of family support with depression and the extent of interaction of family support and loneliness with depression in children and adolescents. Methods There were 2755 children and adolescents aged 18 and below from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) that participated in our study. Depression in children and adolescents was assessed by the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). Data were analyzed using logistic regression models, mediation analysis models and the interaction effect models. Results Of the 2,755 children and adolescents, 848 (30.8%) reported feelings of loneliness and 2,452 (88.7%) reported feelings of depression. The results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that, in comparison to children and adolescents who reported having family support, those who lacked such support exhibited a higher probability of experiencing loneliness (odds ratio (OR) = 1.668, 95% confidence interval (CI): (1.318–2.111) and depression (OR, 95%CI = 2.267, 1.402–3.664). The results of the mediation analysis indicated that loneliness exerted a partial mediating effect on the relationship between family support and depression ( β =-0.087, P &lt; 0.001), with a mediation proportion of 33.21%. No significant multiplicative or additive interactions were observed between family support and loneliness on depression. Conclusion The findings indicate that family support exerts a direct and indirect influence on depression, mediated by loneliness. The results suggest that parents should provide more support to children and adolescents to mitigate the risk of loneliness and underlying depression. This change is reflected not only in the increasing independence of individual living spaces in the process of urbanisation, but also in the popularity of digital media and Internet technology, which, while providing people with unprecedented ease of access to maintain interpersonal relationships, may inadvertently increase the sense of distance and social isolation between people [ 1 , 2 ]. This is particularly true of the youth population, which is at an important stage of physical and mental development and is very sensitive to changes in the external environment. This generation of young people communicates more through virtual platforms, reducing opportunities for face-to-face interactions, which challenges the development of their social skills and emotional well-being [ 3 , 4 ]. Prolonged social isolation has been shown to have significant and enduring impacts on the mental health of children and adolescents. Research indicates that such isolation can lead to a range of adverse effects, including increased feelings of loneliness [ 5 – 7 ] and depression [ 8 – 10 ]. Loneliness is defined as a subjective feeling of social isolation resulting from a discrepancy between the desired and perceived state of social integration [ 11 , 12 ]. In addition, depression is characterised by a persistent dysregulation of emotional and mood states, manifesting as low mood, anxiety, irritability, etc [ 13 ]. It is an undeniable fact that the prevalence of loneliness and depression in adults and children has increased significantly in recent years [ 14 , 15 ]. It is important to note that social isolation and loneliness are not synonymous. However, There is a well-established link between loneliness and mental health, particularly in relation to depression and autism [ 16 ]. A significant number of theorists in the field of depression have postulated that social exclusion, limited social networks and reduced desire for social interaction in children and adolescents are strongly associated with the experience of loneliness [ 17 , 18 ]. They have also suggested that the presence of depressive symptoms may contribute to interpersonal difficulties and impair intimacy, thereby increasing the risk of experiencing loneliness [ 19 , 20 ]. Children and adolescents are more dependent on the family system as they grow up, and family support has a significant impact on their mental health. Family support has been shown to significantly reduce loneliness and depressive symptoms in children and adolescents [ 21 , 22 ]. Moreover, loneliness may also play a key role as a mediating variable in the relationship between family support and depressive symptoms [ 23 , 24 ]. This choice is based on social support theory [ 25 ] and the stress-buffering model [ 26 ]which suggest that loneliness plays a key mediating role in the deterioration of an individual’s mental health. Based on the above statements, we use the data of the CFPS to explore the role of family support in the occurrence of loneliness and depression in children and adolescents, and further analyze the mechanism of action among family support, loneliness and depression. It is hoped that this study can provide reference for future related research. Materials and methods Sample and procedure Data were drawn from the CFPS, a national and comprehensive social survey conducted by the Institute of Social Science Survey at Peking University in collaboration with the Survey Research Center at the University of Michigan [ 27 ]. In our analysis, we used data from the 2020 CFPS, which was the latest survey data available for us. The survey was administered via telephone using computer assisted telephone interviewing and conducted in March. All participants were approved by Peking University Biomedical Ethics Committee (IRB00001052-14010). Since the target population of this study is children and adolescents, data including individuals aged younger or equal to 18 years old, others were excluded from the analysis. In this study population, 28,590 participants were surveyed in 2020. We excluded participants who were over 18 years of age ( n = 25,489), and those whose information was incomplete ( n = 346). The final analytic sample comprised 2755 eligible study subjects. Measurements Depression Depressive symptoms were assessed by the 8-item Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-8) in the CFPS 2020. And it was the only measure of depressive symptoms in the CFPS. The CES-D-8 includes 8 items that explore the frequency of feelings or behaviors during the past week. The 8 items were: question N406 “I feel depressed”, question N407 “I feel it takes a lot of effort to do anything”, question N411“My sleep is not good”, question N412 “I feel happy”, question N414 “I feel lonely”, question N416 “I live happily”, question N418 “I feel sad”, and question N420 “I feel that life can’t go on”. The four options of the eight questions were hard, sometimes, often, and most of the time. The answer options were recorded from 0 to 3, respectively. Among them, two questions (N412 and N416) that reflected positive emotion were reversely scored, resulting in a depression score of 0–24. Depression score was used in mediation analysis. To accurately identify clinically significant depressive symptoms, a depression score of 9 was used as a cut-off value [ 28 ]. The CES-D has been widely used in epidemiologic studies and has been shown to have good consistency and validity [ 29 ]. In our sample, the CES-D-8 had high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α varying from = 0.741). Loneliness Loneliness was measured using question N414 “I feel lonely”, and the four response options included ‘hardly’, ‘sometimes’, ‘often’, and ‘most of the time’. An individual was not defined as lonely if he or she selected ‘hardly’. Family support Family support was measured by five items as below, namely (1) Parents encourage you to try to do something, (2) Parents encourage you to think independently, (3) Parents enjoy talking and conversing with you, (4) Parents like to have fun with you, such as playing chess and playing games, (5) Parents praise you. Response options included ‘never’, ‘seldom’, ‘sometimes’, ‘often’, and ‘always’. The answer options were recorded from 1 to 5, resulting in a family support score of 5–25 and this score was used in mediation analysis. Only those who selected ‘never’ or ‘seldom’ or ‘sometimes’ to all those items were considered to be without family support, otherwise, they were considered to have family support. In our sample, the measure of family support showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α varying from = 0.749). Covariates To control for potential confounding variables, we examined other characteristics as well as depression-related variables. The following variables were used in this study: Age (median age = 13), Gender (female, male), School (during the semester, during the holiday), Stage (elementary school and below, junior high school, senior high school), Exercise (yes, no), Meat intake (yes, no), Smoking (yes, no). For exercise, participants were defined as ‘no’ if they did not participated in physical fitness activities in the past 12 months, otherwise ‘yes’ (; For meat intake, participants were defined as ‘no’ if they did not eat meat in the past one week, otherwise ‘yes’; For smoking, whether the participant had smoked in the past one month, if not, they were defined as ‘no’, otherwise ‘yes’. Statistical analyses Categorical variables for the included population were described as percentages, and differences between groups were detected using the chi-square test. Logistic regression models were used to assess the association of family support with loneliness and depressive symptoms. Our results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). For all outcomes, we constructed models without any adjusted covariates (model 1), models adjusted only for age, gender, school, and stage (model 2) and models further adjusted for exercise, meat intake and smoking (model 3). In addition, correlations between variables were tested using correlation analysis. To assess the mediating effect of loneliness between family support and depression, we constructed two mediation models, model 4 with no adjustment for any variable, and model 5 with adjustment for variables that were statistically significant in the correlation analysis. To quantify the additive and multiplicative interactions, we additionally included a product term of family support (yes and no) and loneliness (yes and no) in the models. The hazard ratio with its 95% confidence interval of the product term was the measure of interaction on the multiplicative scale. We used the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP) the synergy index (S) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals as the measure of interaction on the additive scale, calculated using the coefficients and corresponding standard errors of the product term, family support, and loneliness, as well as covariance matrix. All P values in this study were taken on both sides and P &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Excel 2019, R software (version 4.1.1) and Mplus Editor 8. Results Characteristics of the study population Of the 2755 children and adolescents, it turns out that 848 (30.8%) felt lonely and 2452 (88.7%) felt depressed. Among those who suffered from loneliness, 16.4% of children and adolescents without any family support, and the proportion of those who once experienced depression, was 13.0%. Among children and adolescents under the age of 13, 44.3% felt lonely and 49.8% caught depression. Most target population of this study experience loneliness (87.7%) and depression (87.1%) during the holidays due to Covid-19 control policies. The elementary (37.7% and 42.4%) and junior high school (38.4% and 36.2%) students are more likely to experience loneliness and depression than high school (23.8% and 21.5%) students. We observed that 61.7% of children and adolescents who enjoyed exercise reported feeling lonely frequently and 62.7% reported symptoms of depression. However, there was no statistically significant difference ( p -values of 0.559 and 0.615, respectively)(Table 1 ). Relationship between family support with loneliness and depression Our study found that children and adolescents without family support are more likely to experience loneliness and depression than those with family support (Table 2 ). In the unadjusted analysis, family support was negatively associated with an increased probability of loneliness (OR: 1.692, 95%CI: 1.339–2.138) and depression (OR: 2.227, 95%CI: 1.379–3.597). Upon adjusting for age, gender, school and stage, the ORs and 95% CIs were slightly reduced (1.672(1.322–2.115) for loneliness and 2.261 (1.399–3.652) for depression). In multivariable-adjusted analysis, the association between family support and loneliness and depression risk had a similar trend, but with a slightly diminished magnitude for loneliness (OR: 1.668, 95% CI: 1.318–2.111) and a slight rise for depression (OR: 2.267, 95% CI: 1.402–3.664). Figure 1 illustrates the associations between family support with loneliness and depression in the different subgroups of variables. The univariate logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between family support with loneliness and depression. In age and gender subgroups, the associations of family support with loneliness and depression were significant. And in school subgroup, the associations of family support with loneliness (OR:0.560, 95% CI: 0.438–0.716) and depression (OR: 0.398, 95% CI: 0.236–0.669) were significant only in the holiday subgroup. As for stage subgroups, the relationship between family and depression was significant in elementary (OR: 0.508, 95% CI: 0.252–1.025) and junior high school (OR: 0.169, 95% CI: 0.053–0.542) groups, similarly, the relationship between family and loneliness was also significant only in elementary (OR: 0.550, 95% CI: 0.379–0.796) and junior high school (OR: 0.464, 95% CI: 0.319–0.675) groups. In exercise and meat intake subgroups, the associations of family support with loneliness and depression were significant only in group ‘yes’. In meat intake subgroups, the associations of family support with loneliness were significant in group ‘yes’ and ‘no’, and the associations of family support with depression was significant only in group ‘yes’. However, in smoking subgroup, these relationships were significant only in group ‘no’, (OR: 0.588, 95%CI: 0.465–0.744) for loneliness and (OR: 0.450, 95%CI: 0.279–0.727) for depression. Relationship between loneliness, depression, and other variables We conducted a correlation analysis among the variables which were all categorical, and correlation significance was calculated using Spearman correlation coefficient (Table 3 ). The results showed that in addition to family support, age ( r = 0.082, P &lt; 0.001), stage ( r = 0.067, P &lt; 0.001) were positively correlated with loneliness. Therefore, we controlled for these variables in the mediation model. We also found a significant positive correlation between depression and loneliness ( r = 0.234, P &lt; 0.001). Mediation analysis of loneliness on associations of family support with depression Table 4 showed two mediation models, which revealed a mediation role of loneliness in the relationship between family support and depression. Model 4 was not adjusted for any variable and model 5 controlled for age and stage. The mediating effects of loneliness were − 0.113 (95%CI: −0.143 - −0.082) and − 0.087 (95%CI: −0.117 - −0.057), and the mediating effects accounted for 39.24% and 33.21% of the total effects, respectively. Both direct and indirect effects were significant ( P &lt; 0.001). Figure 2 depicts the two mediation models and parameters. Discussion The aim of this study was to examine the associations between family support, loneliness and depression, and to further examine whether loneliness mediates the association between family support and depression, and the extent to which family support and loneliness interact on depression in Chinese children and adolescents. After adjusting for potential confounders, the results showed that family support had a significant effect on loneliness and depressive symptoms in children and adolescents, and that loneliness partially moderated the association between family support and depression. This study explored the relationship between family support and depression and showed that lack of family support significantly increased depression. We also analysed the differences in this relationship across gender and age subgroups and found that the relationship was stable across strata. This finding is consistent with many existing studies [ 30 – 32 ]. In addition, a two-year longitudinal study of 100 school-aged children found [ 33 ] that depression was negatively associated with changes in family social support over time.The study by Elliot et al. [ 32 ] also noted a negative association between depression and family and paternal support. There is also a meta-analysis [ 34 ] that suggests that parental relationships and family support reduce the risk of depression in children and adolescents, which is consistent with our findings. However it was also shown that parental relationships and family support had a greater effect on girls than boys, which is different from our findings as we did not find a gender difference. The present study further explored the mediating role of loneliness in the relationship between family support and depression and showed that family support, by reducing loneliness, in turn reduces levels of depression in children and adolescents. This finding is consistent with the study by Jones and Brown, who found that loneliness played a significant mediating role in the relationship between family support and depression in children [ 35 ] and noted that the effect of family support on reducing loneliness varied among children of different ages. The results of the present study suggest that higher levels of loneliness are significantly associated with depression in children and adolescents, even in the presence of high levels of family support. This suggests that while the family, as an important source of social support, plays a key role in preventing depression in adolescents, lifestyle changes in modern societies, such as the trend towards isolation and de-socialisation, should not be ignored. In particular, the widespread use of digital media may be changing the way adolescents socialise and making them feel more isolated in real life. Future research could further explore the impact of other factors such as social media use habits, online gaming time and screen time on depression. These factors may be key variables in current adolescent lifestyles that contribute to or exacerbate loneliness and depressive symptoms. Understanding these complex relationships may help to develop more comprehensive interventions to promote the mental health of children and adolescents in modern society. Despite our great efforts, our study still has a number of limitations: First, our research was a cross-sectional study that could not explore the association between family support and depression and the mediating effect of loneliness over time, this requires longitudinal statistics to be studied. Moreover, due to the different definitions and measurements of family support, it is necessary to compare our results with those of other studies in a certain limited context. Third, this study used only one item to evaluate participants’ loneliness, instead of a multidimensional scale with good reliability. Fourth, our study was a cross-sectional study that could only explore the association between family support and child and adolescent loneliness and depressive performance, but could not confirm the causal association. Finally, although we have already adjusted many confounding factors, we still cannot exclude residual confounding or the effect of unmeasured confounders. Conclusion In summary, our data indicated that higher levels of family support were linked to a reduced likelihood of loneliness and depression among children and adolescents. Additionally, loneliness was found to have a moderating effect on the relationship between family support and depression. Hence, to alleviate feelings of loneliness and depressive symptoms in this population, it is crucial that parents offer increased family support. Our findings have implications for the development of targeted depression prevention strategies that focus on mitigating loneliness among youth, and they also inform healthcare policies in China during the current pandemic situation. As the pandemic continues to affect communities, future research efforts should identify other vulnerable groups at risk of poor mental health to facilitate effective public health interventions.
