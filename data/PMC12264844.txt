ABSTRACT Objective This paper aimed to review and synthesize screen time research among children and youth with disabilities. Methods Seven online databases were searched, and a machine learning–assisted systematic review model was used to identify relevant studies. English and French papers reporting on screen time among children and youth with a disability were eligible. Extracted data were synthesized by participant age, followed by type of screen time reporting. Meta‐analyses were conducted to estimate daily screen time and adherence to screen time guidelines using random effects meta‐analysis. Results Eighty‐one studies were included. Screen time ranged from 0.5 to 7.27 h/day and varied widely based on disability type. Pooled average screen time was 3.70, 3.28 and 3.39 h/day for children and youth with ASD, ADHD and CP, respectively. Conclusion Screen use is prominent among children and youth with disabilities. Limiting screen time in this group is critical in preventing numerous related consequences of excessive, prolonged use. The sample size of the included articles ranged from 9 to 65 680 children and had a collective total of 1 209 961 participants across the 81 studies included. In terms of study design, most studies were cross‐sectional ( n = 90) followed by cohort studies ( n = 5), longitudinal ( n = 2), case report ( n = 1), retrospective cohort ( n = 1) and mixed‐methods ( n = 1). Most studies assessed screen time using parent‐reported measures ( n = 95), followed by self‐reported ( n = 18) and teacher‐reported ( n = 2) measures. Televisions (TV, DVD; n = 135), followed by computers (internet, computer use, laptop; n = 70), smartphones ( n = 56), video games (video games, console; n = 43), tablets (iPad; n = 25) and other devices (not specified, ‘other electronic device’; n = 11) were the most common types of devices used. Some papers reported screen time across more than one device type, therefore resulting in a total screen type number exceeding the total number of papers included in this review. Most papers in this review included children and youth with autism spectrum disorder (ASD; n = 59), followed by attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; n = 21) and intellectual disabilities ( n = 11). Approximately 35% of papers reported on multiple disabilities, whereas 65% focused on a single type of disability. 3.3 Screen Time Among Children and Youth With Disabilities 3.3.1 Daily Screen Time Duration A total of 62 studies reported daily rates of screen time. Rates of screen time ranged from 0.5 (Halladay 2021 ) to 7.27 h/day (Sadeghi et al. 2021 ). In general, children in the early years (0–4 years) reported less screen time compared with older children (5 years and up). Based on disability type, screen time was highest among children and youth with ASD (0.5–7.27 h/day), followed by children and youth with ADHD (1.34–5.29 h/day). A total of 24 studies, which reported on 4857 children, reported on total screen time in children and youth with ASD. Meta‐analyses revealed that the pooled average screen time reported by these studies was 3.70 h/day (95% confidence interval = 3.15–4.25, I 2 = 97.9%). Levels of screen time did not differ significantly between younger children and school‐aged children/adolescents with ASD (Figure 3a ). Six studies reporting on 2321 children and adolescents reported that children and youth with ASD watch an average of 2.32 h/day of TV (95% confidence interval = 1.58–3.06, I 2 = 95.2%), five studies including 779 children reported that children and adolescents with ASD play video games for an average of 2.01 h per day (95% confidence interval = 1.57–2.44, I 2 = 95.7%), and three studies that included 2001 children found that children and youth with ASD use the computer for an average of 1.14 h each day (95% confidence interval = 0.72–1.56, I 2 = 93%). A total of 10 studies reporting on 10 933 children and youth with ADHD reported on total screen time. The average daily screen time among children and adolescents with ADHD was 3.28 h (95% confidence interval = 2.58–3.97, I 2 = 96.6%). Levels of screen time did not differ significantly between younger children and school‐aged children/adolescents with ADHD (Figure 3b ). Three studies including 4319 participants reported that, on average, children and youth with ADHD watch TV for 2.04 h each day (95% confidence interval = 1.78–2.30, I 2 = 54.5%). Three studies including 174 participants reported on screen time in children and adolescents with CP (Figure 3c ). On average, children and adolescents with CP engaged in 3.39 h of screen time each day (95% confidence interval = 1.53–5.25, I 2 = 97.3%). Results from papers examining less commonly studied disabilities (e.g., sensory disabilities, Down syndrome and intellectual disabilities) that were excluded from the meta‐analyses are presented in Table 2 . 3.3.2 Adherence to Screen Time Guidelines Twelve studies assessed participant screen time by assessing whether they were meeting or exceeding sedentary behaviour recommendations based on population (rather than providing absolute screen times values in‐text) (Burghard et al. 2018 ; Kushima et al. 2022 ; Brown et al. 2021 ; Healy and Garcia 2019 ; Li et al. 2022 ; Wilson et al. 2016 , 2023 ; Xu and Wang 2023 ; Xu and Qi 2022 ). As defined by WHO international movement behaviour recommendations (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology 2021a , 2021b ; World Health Organization 2020 ), screen time guidelines are defined as ≤ 1 and ≤ 2 h of recreation screen time per day, based on respective age groups. Two studies were included for ages 0–4 years, six studies were included for ages 5–17 years, and two studies were included for all ages (0–17 years). Among the studies focused on 5‐ to 17‐year‐olds, participants reported at least 40% adherence to the guidelines (42.5%–58.3%). See Table 2 for additional details. Six studies that included 2480 participants were included in a meta‐analysis for the proportion of children and adolescents with ASD achieving the screen time guidelines (Figure 4 ). Results demonstrated that 45.3% of children and adolescents with ASD were achieving the screen time guidelines (95% confidence interval = 29.6%–62.1%, I 2 = 92.6%). See Table 2 for additional information regarding adherence to screen time guidelines among children and youth with less commonly investigated disabilities (e.g., Down syndrome, intellectual disabilities). 3.3.3 Observation of ‘Excessive Screen Exposure’ Thirteen papers described screen use as ‘excessive’ or ‘high’ among participants. These proxy observations were provided by parents/guardians, educators or researchers. Based on disability type, screen time exposure was highest among children and youth with ASD (30.0%–70.9% excessive screen time exposure), followed by children and youth with ADHD (12.9%–47.6% excessive screen time exposure). Albeit only examined in single papers, excessive screen time was reported in 27.7%–29.7% and 77% of participants with Down syndrome ( n = 50 191; mean age = 12.5 [3.3] years) and development coordination disorder ( n = 129 278; mean age = 3.95 [0.79] years), respectively. See Table 2 for additional details. 3.4 Risk of bias of Included Studies The risk of bias for each individual study can be found in Supporting Information S2 . Overall, 77% of the included studies ( n = 62) were rated as having a high risk of bias, 22% rated as moderate ( n = 18) and 1% rated as a low risk of bias ( n = 1) (Hutzler et al. 2021 ). This demonstrates that most studies assessing screen time rates of children and youth with disabilities had a high potential for bias, primarily related to study design. The majority of studies used an acceptable definition of screen time (e.g., covered a range of screen‐based devices; n = 59, 73%), used the same mode of data collection across participants ( n = 79, 98%), had a reasonable prevalence period for screen time rate assessment (e.g., average/typical screen use, past week or past month; n = 75, 93%) and presented screen time statistics in an appropriate format (e.g., mean hours of daily screen time; n = 54, 66%). However, most studies did not recruit a nationally representative sample (only n = 24, 30% identified as recruiting a nationally representative sample), were generally based on a convenience sample sourced from a limited recruitment strategy (e.g., one hospital department) rather than random selection or census ( n = 56, 69%), relied on proxy report from a parent or caregiver ( n = 73, 90%) or used tools with unknown validity/reliability to assess screen time ( n = 74, 91%). 4 Discussion Screens are highly integrated into modern living and especially present in the lives of children. Specific to screen time among children and youth with disabilities, an influx of research has been noted since 2020. This systematic review captured a large dataset and synthesized the existing literature regarding the amount of recreational screen time in which children and youth with disabilities engage. The amount of screen time reported among children and youth with disabilities varied widely across included papers, ranging from 0.5 (Halladay 2021 ) to 7.27 h/day (Sadeghi et al. 2021 ), with a pooled average exceeding hours/day. Our study also found that older children spent more time engaged in recreational screen time than younger children, consistent with previous studies examining recreational screen time in children who did not have disabilities (Friel et al. 2020 ). It is interesting to note that, albeit varied, the reported average screen time among children and youth with disabilities may exceed the rates of screen time observed among children and youth without disabilities. For comparative purposes, a review by Qi and colleagues (Qi et al. 2023 ) found that average screen time among school‐aged children (6–14 years) was 2.77 h/day, and 46.4% of them had an average screen time ≥ 2 h/day. However, what is clear across both reviews is that regardless of the presence or absence of disability, children and youth are routinely using screens more than the recommended global public health guidelines. The secondary goal of this paper was to explore differences in screen time by disability type. Unsurprisingly, given the differences in participation abilities across disability types (e.g., individual differences and environmental factors) (Imms et al. 2016 ), the findings of this review suggest that screen time varies widely both within and between disability types. Generally, we found that children and youth with ASD had the highest levels of screen time, followed by children and youth with ADHD. Other researchers have found similar trends of high screen time in children and youth with these specific disabilities, making these populations a priority for intervention and support around reduced screen time (Slobodin et al. 2019 ; Thorell et al. 2022 ). While the present review was limited in its ability to present pooled averages for understudied disability groups (&lt; 3 studies), we were able to ascertain that ‘excessive’ screen time was reported (albeit qualitatively) among large samples of school‐aged children with Down syndrome and young children with developmental coordination disorder. This review addresses a significant gap in the research by providing an overview of screen time prevalence of children and youth with disabilities, allowing us to conceptualize more accurately the prevalence of screen time among children. However, extrapolating the reasons, type, content and context of high levels of recreational screen time in this population is beyond the scope of this work. Disability is experienced in unique ways both across and within conditions, as children age and in relation to intersectional and socio‐ecological forces experienced by individuals. As a result, the paediatric disability population is an extremely heterogeneous group, and these findings only provide a glimpse of screen time prevalence based on the currently available literature at this moment in time. To better support tailored health promotion messaging and interventions, and to maximize their reach and uptake, additional work is needed to unpack nontherapeutic screen time among children with other disabilities beyond ADHD and ASD (notably the most commonly studied). This continues to persist as a major gap in the literature. An interesting finding of this study was the global representation observed across the literature. This study located a total of 81 articles, capturing over a million participants in 24 countries across five continents. The uptake in surveillance and research, particularly in the last 4 years ( n = 57 articles) demonstrates the increasing attention to recreational screen time pursuits and their impact on children and youth with disabilities as an important global health priority. Moreover, the 2020 WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behavior (World Health Organization 2020 ) prioritized children and youth with disabilities as an important group where research into sedentary pursuits including recreational screen time require more attention, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries (a recommendation reinforced by the findings of this review). Ongoing surveillance of these behaviours is important to continue exploring the impact of recreational screen time on the health and development of children, addressing the need for global health interventions and relevant health outcomes across the life course for this equity‐deserving group. Another unanticipated finding of this review was that two studies ( n = 2) examined the use of screens (television, computers, videogames) in children's bedrooms, and how this impacted recreational screen time use. These studies (Engelhardt et al. 2013 ; Lo 2013 ) both reported higher levels of recreational screen time use by children and youth with disabilities who had screens in their rooms compared with participants who did not. This is a noteworthy finding because a systematic review by Hale and Guan (Hale and Guan 2015 ) found that screen time was negatively associated with sleep outcomes, and they recommended that children and youth aged 5–17 years (nonspecific to disability status) limit screen time exposure before bed to prevent harmful effects of screen viewing on sleep. This is especially relevant, as research indicates that irrespective of the underlying diagnosis, children and youth with disabilities frequently experience sleep disturbances that significantly impact the quality of their sleep, and this may have negative impacts on their wellbeing and overall development (Tietze et al. 2012 ). Moreover, medical interventions to minimize sleep disturbances in children and youth with disabilities are unlikely to be effective without addressing sleep hygiene (e.g., sleep‐supporting environments) (Jan et al. 2008 ). More research is recommended to examine implications of screen viewing on the sleep hygiene of children and youth with disabilities and to provide recommendations for medical practitioners and families on best practices for recreational screen viewing and bedtime routines. Finally, while the literature overwhelmingly suggests detrimental effects associated with excessive recreational screen time on children's health (Madigan et al. 2019 ), it is also critical to acknowledge potential benefits that children and youth with disabilities and their families may experience from engaging in healthy levels of appropriate screen time. Screens integrated through therapeutic settings (not captured in this review) have been linked with positive outcomes for children and youth with disabilities. For example, gamification has been used to support gross and fine motor activities, social skill development and social connectedness via videogames and tablet‐based apps (Beaumont et al. 2021 ; Coutinho et al. 2017 ; Demers et al. 2020 ; Salem et al. 2012 ; Smart et al. 2023 ). As well, parents of children and youth with disabilities have discussed the use of recreational screen time as an opportunity for their respite (Taylor et al. 2025 ; Blum‐Ross and Livingstone 2018 ). For these reasons, it is important not to demonize recreational screen time, or fault parents and children for their engagement in this leisure‐based activity. Given the high levels of average daily recreational screen time identified in the present study, future surveillance research should explore the type, context, content and reasons for screen time and understand both positive and negative outcomes, to support updated recommendations which balance user needs and health outcomes. 4.1 Strengths and Limitations This systematic review was the first to investigate the prevalence of screen time among children and youth with disabilities. The use of a machine learning classifier to assist in title and abstract screening allowed for the expansion of search terms, enabling the identification of more potentially relevant articles without overwhelming the reviewers. Additionally, using the machine learning classifier displayed articles that are more relevant first, potentially decreasing the error rate observed in traditional reviews (Wang et al. 2020 ). However, this systematic review has important limitations worth noting. First, almost all included studies scored lower in terms of study quality (i.e., high risk of bias) as most papers relied on parent/proxy‐reported screen time due to the young age of the participants and relied on convenience samples. While higher quality studies and rigour would strengthen our understanding of screen time among children and youth with disabilities, it could prove to be incredibly challenging given the population of focus. Second, through this review, we were unable to confidently parse out or comment on screen time use in children with multiple disabilities. However, based on the plausibility that children with multiple disabilities may engage in even higher rates of screen use, and therefore potentially placing them at even greater risk, we would recommend this research question as a future line of inquiry. Third, because we did not include unpublished studies or studies that were published in languages other than English or French, it is possible that several relevant papers may have been excluded. Lastly, the use of ‘screen’ as 1 of our search terms resulted in a plethora of hits on our electronic database searches referring to therapeutic screening of children for various disabilities. This resulted in a less‐than‐refined output from our electronic article searches. 5 Conclusion In summary, the findings of this systematic review assert that recreational screen time is prominent among children and youth with disabilities. Although results varied, many studies captured the need for future research involving interventions to decrease screen time among children and youth with disabilities; this should nonetheless be emphasized. Limiting screen time in children and youth with disabilities is critical to preventing the numerous related dangers of excessive and prolonged screen time.
