Abstract Background: Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) causes impairment in social and academic functionality. Intensive treatment methods such as day treatments are recommended in resistant cases. Aims: The aim of this study is to examine the changes in treatment responses according to gender in children and adolescents with OCD who are followed up in the day clinic and to evaluate the factors that predict treatment. Methods: Adolescents with OCD (n = 50) who were followed up in the day clinic between 2007 and 2021 were included in this study. Scales evaluating symptom severity and treatment responses were administered to the patients at admission and discharge. Results: The mean age of the adolescents included in the study was 14.24 (SD: 2.1). The mean length of stay in the day clinic was 8.3 (SD: 0.1) weeks. When the factors related to the treatment response were evaluated, a significant correlation was found between the length of stay in the day clinic and improvement. Conclusions: This study provides evidence for the importance of intensive treatment in adolescents experiencing severe OCD symptoms. Ensuring that intensive treatments such as day clinics become widespread and standardizing these treatments will provide important developments in the treatment of OCD. INTRODUCTION Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) is one of the most common neuropsychiatric disorders with a lifetime prevalence of 1–3%.[ 1 ] OCD is frequently seen in childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood and leads to impairments in social and academic functioning. It is accepted that many genetic, neurochemical, structural, environmental, and psychodynamic factors play a role in the etiology of OCD. Obsessions and compulsions usually occur together in childhood.[ 2 ] Studies have established that OCD is highly comorbid with anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).[ 3 ] Current recommended evidence-based treatments for OCD in children and adolescents are cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. CBT is recommended as first-line therapy, especially for mild to moderate cases.[ 4 ] Different variations of CBT, including exposure and response prevention (ERP) techniques, have proven successful in OCD treatment.[ 5 ] Apart from ERP and CBT, there are studies showing that mindfulness-based cognitive therapies, family inclusive treatment approaches, and stress management-relaxation training are also effective in the treatment of OCD.[ 6 , 7 ] Intensive treatment models such as day treatment or inpatient treatment are appropriate if patients with OCD do not respond to outpatient treatment. Day treatment is a treatment model created with the approach of providing treatment in the least restrictive environment in accordance with the needs of the child. The day treatment enables the child/adolescent to receive treatment without leaving the family environment.[ 8 ] In day treatment, group therapy, milieu therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy methods, and pharmacological approaches are used together. In studies conducted with adult patients with various psychiatric disorders, it has been shown that day treatment is effective and provides improvement in functionality and quality of life.[ 9 , 10 ] However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no study in the literature investigating the effectiveness of day clinic treatment in which multimodal treatment methods are applied for children and adolescents with OCD. In a study evaluating the efficacy of 5-day ERP treatment in 22 children/adolescents with OCD between the ages of 7 and 18, it was shown that this treatment model was effective on OCD.[ 11 ] In a Canadian study, adult cases with OCD were admitted to the day hospital treatment and CBT, including the elements of ERP was applied to the cases. It was determined that the severity of OCD decreased with the treatment and the functionality and quality of life of the patients improved. This study is the first study to show the effectiveness of the day treatment model that includes intensive therapy in the adults with OCD.[ 12 ] Our study has come to the fore with the increase in OCD cases followed in our university child and adolescent psychiatry day clinic, which is the only day clinic serving children and adolescents in Turkey. The aim of this study is to examine the changes in treatment responses according to gender in adolescents with OCD who are followed up in the day clinic and to evaluate the factors that predict treatment. MATERIALS AND METHODS The cases diagnosed with OCD who were followed up in Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Day Clinic were included in this study. The sociodemographic data and clinical characteristics of these cases as assessed on various scales were reviewed retrospectively. The inclusion criteria were those volunteering to participate in day clinic treatment, being between the ages of 12 and 18, having a primary diagnosis of OCD according to DSM-IV and DSM-5, causing significant impairment in functionality (Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale score ≥16), and with no significant improvement in symptom severity despite regular outpatient follow-up for 3 months or more and regular attendance at the day clinic for at least 3 weeks. Cases with comorbid substance use, with diagnosis of bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, and who did not complete their rating scales during discharge were excluded from the study. Of the 345 cases followed up in the day clinic between 2007 and 2021, 50 cases meeting the criteria were included in the study. Treatment response The response of the patient to the day clinic treatment was evaluated by by the child and adolescent psychiatrist in charge of the day clinic and two consultant child and adolescent psychiatrists. In case of disagreement between the psychiatrists, the scoring made by the senior consultant child and adolescent psychiatrist was accepted. A decrease in the scale score below the cutoff (at discharge CGI-S&lt;5, Y-BOCS≤15, and MOCI&lt;18) and improvement in functionality (CGAS&gt;60 at discharge according to at least two clinicians) were considered response to treatment. Treatment The treatment program is still active, and the clinic was closed for about 20 months during the COVID-19 pandemic. Patients with mental retardation and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) who can not adapt to the day treatment, who are at risk of self-harming and suicide, are not admitted to the day clinic. Patients who do not meet the stated exclusion criteria and accept day clinic treatment are included in the program. Patients are directed to the day clinic by their doctors who follow up them in the outpatient clinic. For patients and their families, both verbal and written consents are obtained for day clinic treatment. Individual treatment based on CBT and including the family are planned for each patient. In addition, group therapy, art therapy, occupational therapy, stress management, and relaxation techniques are applied to all patients within the framework of the day clinic program. Patients are in the clinic between 9:00 h and 16:00 h on weekdays. Individual interviews are held with each patient on a daily basis. Families (mother, father, sibling, and family members they live with) are interviewed at least once a week, and attitudes are worked on with families. CBT is applied by child and adolescent psychiatrists, and the program is accompanied by a multidisciplinary team. The team includes full-time specialist psychologists, nurses, teachers and part-time social workers, nutritionists, and sports trainers. Pharmacological treatments are determined by the doctor and administered by the psychiatric nurse in the clinic. Procedure In this study, patients with OCD who did not show adequate treatment response during outpatient follow-up and were referred to the day clinic were included. Diagnostic assesments were made, and pretreatment scales were applied to each patient on the first day of the day clinic treatment. The ongoing pharmacological treatments of each patient were reviewed, and medicines were adjusted wherever required. After the treatment was terminated, scales were readministered to the patients and they were discharged. Measures Childhood Depression Inventory (CDI): It is a self-assessment scale prepared by Kovacs based on the Beck Depression Scale.[ 13 ] A cutoff score of 19 is recommended. The validity and reliability study of the scale for Turkey was conducted by Öy.[ 14 ] State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI): The scale was developed by Spielberger et al. [ 15 ] The validity and reliability study of the scale for Turkey was conducted Öner and Le Compte.[ 16 ] The scale consists of two parts, the state anxiety scale and the trait anxiety scale. Each scale contains two types of statements with 20 items. Higher scores from the scale indicate a higher level of anxiety. The Coopersmith Self-esteem Inventory-Child Form (CSEI): It is a 57-item self-report scale developed by Coopersmith.[ 17 ] The validity and reliability study in Turkey was conducted by Güçray. Although validity and reliability are established for children aged 8–10, they are used until the 11 th grade.[ 18 ] The Clinical Global Impression Scale (CGI): It was developed by Guy to evaluate the course of all psychiatric disorders at all ages for clinical research purposes.[ 19 ] The CGI is a three-dimensional scale and is filled in during a semistructured interview to evaluate the treatment response of patients with psychiatric disorders. Children’s Global Assesment Scale (CGAS): It evaluates functionality based on the five-axis diagnostic system of the DSM-IV. It is scored from 1 to 100. The scale is widely used in treatment studies and sensitive to the effectiveness of treatment. It measures the clinician’s estimation by considering the condition that most severely impairs the functionality of the child or adolescent.[ 20 ] Maudsley Obsessive Compulsive Inventory (MOCI): MOCI, which aims to investigate the type of OCD symptoms, was developed by Hodgson and Rachman,[ 21 ] and it was validated in Turkey by Erol and Savaşır.[ 22 ] The scale consists of checking, cleaning, slowness, doubting, and rumination subscales and is used in children aged 9 and over. Yale Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS): It is a scale developed to evaluate the severity of OCD symptoms and scored by the administer. It consists of 19 items, and the scores of the first 10 items are taken into consideration. Subtotal scores are obtained for obsession (sum of items 1 through 5), compulsion (sum of items 6 through 10), and obsession/compulsion (sum of items 1 through 10).[ 23 ] The validity and reliability of the scale in Turkey were determined by Karamustafalıoğlu et al. [ 24 ] Statıstıcal analysis All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro–Wilk’s test was used to assess the normality of data. Continuous variables were presented depending on normal distribution with either mean ± standard deviation or (in the case of non-normal distribution) median (interquartile range). Comparisons between the groups were performed using independent sample t -test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Chi-square test was used to determine relationships between categorical variables. Associations between continuous variables were examined using Spearman’s correlation analysis. A P value &lt; 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. RESULTS The mean age of the children and adolescents included in the study was 14.24 (SD = 2.1) years. The mean length of stay in the day clinic was 8.3 (SD = 4.1) weeks. Fifty six percent (n = 28) of the patients who received treatment in the clinic were males. According to treatment response criteria, 62% (n = 31) of the sample responded to treatment. Table 1 shows the sociodemographic characteristics of the patients. When the data in Table 1 were examined in terms of gender, no significant differences were found ( P &gt; 0.05). In terms of psychiatric diagnoses, 94% (n = 47) of the patients were found to have a psychiatric disorder comorbid to OCD. The most common comorbid psychiatric diagnosis (n = 70%) was ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder). Apart from these data, no significant differences were found between the gender and presence of OCD in mothers, fathers, and siblings ( P = 0.753). Seventy two percent (n = 36) of the patients were discharged after the completion of the treatment. Of the remaining patients, 4% (n = 2) were discharged because they violated the day clinic rules, and 10% (n = 5) were discharged because of their family and their desire to terminate the treatment. 14% (n = 7) of the patients were referred for inpatient treatment. Significant differences were found between admission and discharge in all scales ( P &lt; 0.05). In addition to the positive changes in OCD symptomatology, a significant decrease in depression and anxiety scores and a significant increase in self-esteem scores were found. There were also positive changes (decrease in the scale score) in MOCI and Y-BOCS, which are used to evaluate OCD. The comparison of the scale scores of the patients during admission and discharge is given in Table 2 . When the changes in the scale scores were evaluated in terms of gender, a significant difference was found only in the STAI-Trait subscale ( P &lt; 0.05). The scale score changes of the patients according to gender are given in Table 3 . On Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient analysis, a positive and significant relationship was found between the reduction in OCD symptom severity (decreased scale scores in YBOCS and MOCI) and the length of stay in the day clinic, respectively (r = 0.585, P &lt; 0.001; r = 0.459, P &lt; 0.001). No correlation was found between other clinical characteristics and YBOCS scores. The correlation analysis of clinical characteristics with scores on YBOCS and MOCI are given in Tables 4 and 5 . All patients were receiving psychopharmacological treatment. 70% (n = 35) of the patients were using sertraline, and 30% (n = 15) were using fluoxetine. In addition to SSRI treatment, 50% (n = 25) of the patients had antipsychotic use. The most commonly used antipsychotic (32%) was aripiprazole apart from risperidone (18%). Of the patients diagnosed with comorbid ADHD, 50% (n = 25) were using methylphenidate, 10% (n = 5) were using atomoxetine, and the remaining (10%) were using combined therapy (methylphenidate + atomoxetine). DISCUSSION The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of day clinic treatment in children and adolescents with OCD. In terms of treatment response, in addition to subthreshold improvements in most of the patients, response was obtained in 62% of the sample. In addition to the changes in the OCD scale scores of the patients included in the study, significant positive changes (decrease in depression and anxiety scores, increase in self-esteem and functionality) were also detected in other scales. However, the decrease in trait anxiety scores of female adolescents was more pronounced compared to male adolescents. When the factors influencing the treatment were examined, the length of stay in the day clinic was found to be the only factor influencing the treatment response. In studies comparing outpatient treatment with inpatient treatment, it was stated that day clinic treatment was mostly beneficial.[ 24 ] As daytime treatments became widespread, the need for day clinics specific to mental mental health disorders emerged. Day clinic treatment models have been found in the literature for many disorders such as ASD, ADHD, and eating disorders for children and adolescents. A day clinic program for adults in Canada has been reported to be beneficial in OCD. Similar to our study, this program included acceptance-commitment therapy, dialectical behavioral therapy, mindfulness techniques, art therapy, and pharmacotherapy in addition to ERP-based CBT.[ 25 ] In a study evaluating an intensive inpatient treatment program for children and adolescents that included CBT, pharmacotherapy, and milieu therapy, it was reported that the severity of OCD symptoms was significantly reduced.[ 26 ] In a study in which milieu therapy, family involved therapy, response prevention, and pharmacotherapy were used, it was found that 15 adolescents diagnosed with OCD benefited significantly from treatment and their gains maintained at follow-up.[ 27 ] In terms of treatment response, the results (62%) were found to be compatible with the literature. In the studies of Bjorgvinson, Hogjard, and Rudy, the treatment response was found to be 65.6%, 62.8%, and 70%, respectively.[ 26 , 28 , 29 ] When treatment responses were compared in terms of gender, no significant difference was found between female and male adolescents. When the factors associated with the decrease in OCD scale scores were examined, gender was not one of these factors. In the literature, two studies investigating potential predictors and predictive factors of OCD treatment found that gender did not predict treatment.[ 28 , 30 ] In contrast, in Rudy’s study, gender was identified as a predictive factor for remission, along with symptom severity and family adjustment.[ 29 ] In our study, the only factor associated with the decrease in the OCD scale scores of the patients was the length of stay in the day clinic. In a study evaluating the treatment process of adult OCD patients in an inpatient center, a significant improvement was found in those who continued treatment for 12 weeks. In addition, it was stated that there was a greater improvement in the treatment period between 12 and 24 weeks.[ 31 ] In another study in which intensive 5-day ERP treatment was applied, it was found that the severity of OCD symptoms in children and adolescents decreased significantly and this improvement was independent of age.[ 10 ] Obtaining different results in different studies may be due to the heterogeneity of the patient groups included in the study and the application of different treatment protocols. Future studies examining treatment response and treatment-related factors are needed for disorders such as OCD in adolescence with a complex clinical profile. ERP-based CBT was the main treatment we used in our day clinic. Randomized controlled studies have shown that CBT is effective in pediatric OCD and increases the functionality level of the patient.[ 4 , 32 , 33 ] A more intense, individualized CBT model was applied daily in our clinic. In addition to OCD, depression and anxiety symptoms of the patients decreased significantly and their functionality level increased. In the study of Björgvınsson et al .,[ 26 ] it was reported that the severity of OCD symptoms decreased with intensive CBT, but there was no significant improvement in depression scores. This was explained by focusing on OCD symptoms in therapy and withdrawing the patient from the social environment. In the day clinic model we applied, patients were evaluated holistically and depression and anxiety disorders were also addressed. In addition, the fact that the patient’s contact with his social environment is not completely cut off is considered as a positive factor for the treatment of depression. The relationship of anxiety and depression comorbidity with treatment outcomes is mixed, but it has been anecdotally observed that OCD patients with intense depression and anxiety symptoms are resistant to treatment and have difficulty in adapting to the clinic. Whether the low self-esteem in relation to OCD is a prodromal symptom or consequence of disease is still controversial issue. However, it has been shown that self-esteem in OCD patients is lower than in healthy individuals.[ 34 ] In a study evaluating OCD patients receiving inpatient treatment and CBT, it was reported that self-esteem was one of the factors that predicted symptom reduction and it increased with treatment.[ 35 ] In our study, a significant increase was found in the self-esteem of the patients after the treatment. However, no correlation was found between the CSEI scores of patients at admission and improvement. When the changes in the scale scores of the patients were evaluated in terms of gender, the reducing in trait anxiety in females was significantly higher than in males. Trait anxiety levels decreased in both gender groups after treatment. However, it was suggested that the significant decrease in anxiety scores in the female patients may be related to the initial high scores. In addition, higher comorbidity of OCD with anxiety in women than in men is consistent with our result.[ 36 ] It was found that the mean anxiety scores of the female patients we followed up were higher, and these scores decreased more than the male patients after the treatment. The combination of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and ERP-based CBT has been shown to be better than single treatment modalities in the treatment of OCD.[ 37 ] In our study, it was determined that the use of fluoxetine and sertraline with previously proven efficacy was preferred in children and adolescents in day clinic treatment. There is not enough evidence in the literature for the use of aripiprazole and risperidone in resistant cases. All patients had SSRI use. Therefore, it was not included in the analysis as a treatment-related variable. When we evaluated the relationship between antipsychotic use and treatment response, no significant relationship was found. There were some limitations in our study. Considering the conditions such as comorbidities, treatment compliance, and family characteristics of the patients, an individualized treatment plan was chosen instead of a standard treatment. Therefore, it was not possible to talk about a completely standard treatment. Long-term follow-up data were not available. Although anecdotal evidence suggests that most patients continue with treatment after discharge, future studies will show whether children and adolescents’ gains are maintained after discharge. Besides, the absence of a waiting list for comparison was also considered a limitation. In this context, further comparative studies are needed. CONCLUSIONS Although this study has various limitations, it is the first study to investigate the effect of day clinic treatment for children and adolescents with OCD. The study proves that day clinic treatment is an option other than outpatient and inpatient treatment, especially in difficult and resistant pediatric OCD cases.
