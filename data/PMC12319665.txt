Abstract Background and aims Amid questions about a perceived association between low‐to‐moderate alcohol consumption and reduced cardiovascular risk from recent studies, research examining the impact of alcohol consumption on cardiometabolic risk markers has reported inconsistent results. We measured the association between long‐term alcohol consumption and incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers. Design, setting and participants A prospective cohort analysis was conducted using data from a population‐based cohort (2001–2020) of 4708 Koreans aged 40–69 years who were free of chronic diseases, including metabolic syndrome, at baseline. Measurements Alcohol consumption and incident cases related to cardiometabolic risk markers were assessed biennially using interviewer‐administered questionnaires or health examinations. The average alcohol intake was calculated to reflect long‐term consumption. Alcohol consumption was categorized as none or rarely (0–2 g/day), light (2 &lt; −16 g/day, within guideline limits), medium (16 &lt; −32 g/day) and heavy (&gt;32 g/day). Findings After full adjustment for covariates, light, medium or heavy alcohol consumption was positively associated with metabolic syndrome [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.55, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.32–1.83], abdominal obesity (HR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.17–1.70), hyperglycemia (HR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.62–2.26), high blood pressure (HR = 2.04, 95% CI = 1.72–2.41) and hypertriglyceridemia (HR = 1.59, 95% CI = 1.30–1.93), with the results presented for heavy alcohol consumption only ( p ‐values for trend &lt;0.001 for all cases). By sex, positive associations between light alcohol intake and hyperglycemia and high blood pressure were observed only in women ( p ‐values for interaction and p ‐values for trend &lt;0.05 for all cases). Conclusions Long‐term light (within guideline limits), medium or heavy alcohol consumption appears to be positively and linearly associated with incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers. Participants were asked whether they were never‐drinkers who had not consumed alcoholic beverages in their lifetime (e.g. for religious reasons), former drinkers or current drinkers. Subsequent questionnaires were assigned to each of the seven types of alcoholic beverages: beer, wine, hard liquor, three Korean traditional alcoholic beverages (rice wine, cheongju/jeongjong and soju), and other alcoholic beverages. The questionnaires inquired about the average drinking frequency over the past year (7 categorized responses ranging from none, once a month, 2–3 times a month, to once or more a day) and the average total amount consumed per occasion (open‐ended; number of drinks per occasion). The size of each beverage container was specified: beer 220 mL, wine 90 mL, hard liquor 30 mL, rice wine 240 mL and soju 50 mL. Alcohol intake for each type of alcoholic beverage was calculated using the formula: amount consumed per occasion (drinks/occasion) × container size (mL/drink) × frequency (occasions/day) × alcohol by volume × specific gravity of alcohol (0.7893). The alcohol by volume (%) for each beverage type was as follows: beer 4.5%, wine 13%, hard liquor 40%, rice wine 6%, cheongju/jeongjong 15%, soju 22% and others. More details are available in the Supporting information and the NIH guidelines for the follow‐up integrated KoGES dataset [ 31 ]. We calculated total alcohol intake at each survey by summing the intake of all alcoholic beverage types. For the analysis of each cardiometabolic risk marker, average alcohol intake was determined by summing the alcohol consumption from all surveys participated in up to the first incident case and dividing it by the total number of surveys completed during that period. We categorized alcohol consumption based on the United Kingdom (UK) alcohol intake guideline [ 32 ], which defines low‐risk drinking thresholds as 16 g or less of alcohol per day for adults (calculated from ‘no more than 14 units of alcohol per week’) and specifies a standard drink as containing 8 g of pure alcohol. A similar standard drink is reported in Korea [ 33 , 34 ], where no official national alcohol consumption guideline currently exists. This guideline facilitates the examination of effect modification by sex. When categorizing alcohol consumption, we also considered the proportional hazards assumption by assessing Schoenfeld residuals and the characteristics of the average alcohol intake over 20 years. Alcohol consumption was categorized into none or rarely (0–2 g/day), light (2 &lt; − 16 g/day, within guideline limits), medium (16 &lt; − 32 g/day) and heavy (&gt;32 g/day), with a standard drink defined as containing 8 g of pure alcohol. As shown in Figure 1 , former drinkers were excluded from the study population to minimize the risk of reverse causality. For the analysis by type of alcoholic beverage, we categorized alcohol drinkers into liquor drinkers (e.g. hard liquor, soju), non‐liquor drinkers (e.g. beer, wine, rice wine and cheongju/jeongjong) and mixed drinkers. Participants were classified as liquor drinkers if they exclusively consumed liquor, non‐liquor drinkers if they exclusively consumed non‐liquor and mixed drinkers if they consumed both, during all previous surveys up to the first incident case. Assessment of cardiometabolic risk markers Surveys for anthropometric measurements, clinical examinations and disease history and treatment were conducted by trained staff. The measurement methods for height, weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, HDL‐C, LDL‐C and hsCRP levels are detailed in the Supporting information. MetS was defined based on the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III criteria, with an adjusted waist circumference threshold for the Asian population [ 35 ]. Participants were defined as having MetS if they met three or more of the following conditions: (1) abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥90 cm for men and ≥80 cm for women); (2) hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL); (3) low HDL‐C (HDL‐C &lt; 40 mg/dL for men and &lt;50 mg/dL for women); (4) hyperglycemia (fasting blood glucose ≥100 mg/dL, current treatment for diabetes, or a diagnosis of diabetes); and (5) high blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥85 mm Hg, current treatment for hypertension, or a diagnosis of hypertension). High LDL‐C was defined as &gt;130 mg/dL based on a prior study [ 36 ] and the Korean Guidelines for the Management of Dyslipidemia [ 37 ]. Elevated hsCRP was defined as ≥2.2 mg/L based on a previous study [ 38 ]. Covariates Demographic and lifestyle factors were obtained using a self‐reported questionnaire or through personal interviews conducted by trained staff. Education level was classified into three categories: elementary school or less, middle or high school and college or higher. Household monthly income was categorized into three groups: less than approximately $1100 (1.5 million KRW), approximately $1100 to less than $2900 (4 million KRW) and approximately $2900 or more. Marital status was classified as married or unmarried. Smoking status was categorized as never smoker, former smoker or current smoker. Sleep duration was classified into three categories: less than 6 hours, 6 to less than 8 hours and 8 hours or more. Dietary quality was assessed using the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet‐Korean quartile (KQ) [ 39 , 40 ] with data from the validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [ 41 ]. The DASH‐KQ categorized daily intake of protein, total fat, sodium, potassium, dietary fiber and calcium into quartiles. Higher points (1–4) were assigned for lower intake of total fat and sodium and higher intake of the other components. The total DASH‐KQ score (maximum of 24) was calculated by summing these points and categorized by the median value of the study population: good diet quality (15–24 DASH‐KQ score) and poor diet quality (0–&lt;15 DASH‐KQ score). Daily total energy intake was calculated using the FFQ. Total physical activity (metabolic equivalent of task, MET, per day) was calculated using the intensity and duration of each physical activity, as detailed in the NIH guidelines for the follow‐up integrated KoGES dataset [ 31 ]. Statistical analysis For demographic and lifestyle factors, means and SD were calculated for continuous variables, and prevalence (number, percentage) was determined for categorical variables. Characteristics by alcohol consumption categories were compared using analysis of variance (F‐test) for continuous variables and χ 2 test for categorical variables. The follow‐up time for the analysis of each cardiometabolic risk marker was calculated as the interval from the baseline date to either the date of the health examination where the first incident case was identified, or the date of the last health examination conducted through 2020. To examine the associations between alcohol consumption and the incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers, we used Cox proportional hazards regression models [ 42 ] and obtained HR and 95% CI using the PROC PHREG procedure. We verified that the proportional hazards assumption was met by assessing Schoenfeld residuals and examining log–log survival plots. Model 1 was adjusted for age (years) and sex. Model 2 included additional adjustments for body mass index (BMI) (kg/ m 2 ), education level, household monthly income, marital status, smoking status, sleep duration, diet quality, daily total energy intake (kcal/day) and total physical activity (MET/day). When selecting covariates for the Cox proportional hazards regression models, we consulted previous studies related to this topic [ 15 , 20 , 21 , 23 , 24 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 ] and considered the characteristics of our study population. We tested for effect modification by sex and smoking status, as a previous study on the association between alcohol consumption and MetS suggested that the association may vary depending on these factors [ 22 ]. Cross‐product terms between categorized alcohol consumption and each potential modifier were added to the fully adjusted model. Since we found evidence of interaction with sex, but not with smoking status, we performed stratified analysis only by sex. To test for a linear trend across categories, we modeled each alcohol intake as a continuous variable in the fully adjusted model by assigning the median value to each exposure category to minimize the influence of outliers. We conducted a sensitivity analysis by introducing a lag between alcohol consumption and each follow‐up period to account for the possibility that a diagnosis or perceived physical changes immediately before the follow‐up session of the first incident case might have influenced the assessment of alcohol consumption. For example, if participants attended the baseline to the fourth follow‐up and their first incident case was identified at the fourth follow‐up (2009–2010), we calculated the average alcohol intake using data from the baseline to the third follow‐up (2007–2008). Additionally, we evaluated the trajectories of total alcohol consumption over 20 years using a group‐based trajectory model, with the PROC TRAJ procedure in SAS. The trajectory analysis method is detailed in the Supporting information. To perform the subsequent Cox proportional hazards regression models with the observed trajectories, participants were assigned to the group with the highest posterior probability calculated from the final model [ 47 ]. The study design was not pre‐registered; therefore, our findings are exploratory. We conducted all statistical analyses using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute). A two‐tailed P ‐value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. RESULTS Over a median follow‐up of 12 years, the following numbers of individuals developed health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers: 2116 (44.9%) for MetS, 1774 (46.8%) for abdominal obesity, 1774 (39.4%) for hyperglycemia, 1957 (53.8%) for high blood pressure, 1554 (48.3%) for hypertriglyceridemia, 1440 (57.9%) for low HDL‐C, 1647 (69.9%) for high LDL‐C and 375 (35.9%) for high hsCRP. Table 1 presents the general characteristics of Korean adults categorized by their total alcohol consumption. Compared to individuals in the lowest consumption group (none or rarely), those in the highest consumption group (heavy) were more likely to be younger, have a higher education level and exhibit poor diet quality. Frequent alcohol consumers were also more likely to be men, married and smokers. Additionally, people who consumed more alcohol tended to have higher household monthly income, longer sleep duration, greater daily total energy intake and higher physical activity. Table 2 presents the results of Cox proportional hazards regression analyses on the associations between long‐term alcohol consumption and incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers in Korean adults. We found that adults who consumed light (2 &lt; −16 g/day, within the guideline limits), medium (16 &lt; −32 g/day), or heavy (&gt;32 g/day) alcohol had significantly higher risks of developing various health risk conditions compared to those who drank none or rarely. After full adjustment for covariates, adults who consumed medium and heavy alcohol had a 29% and 55% higher risk of incident MetS, respectively, compared to those who consumed none or rarely ( P ≤ 0.002; P ‐trend &lt; 0.001). Light, medium and heavy alcohol intake were positively associated with developing hyperglycemia and high blood pressure ( P ≤ 0.019; P ‐trend &lt; 0.001 for all cases). Additionally, medium and heavy alcohol consumption were positively associated with developing abdominal obesity and hypertriglyceridemia ( P ≤ 0.024; P ‐trend &lt; 0.001 for all cases). In contrast, alcohol consumption at all levels was inversely associated with developing low HDL‐C ( P &lt; 0.001 for all levels; P ‐trend &lt; 0.001). There was no significant association between alcohol consumption and developing high LDL‐C or high hsCRP. When we performed a sensitivity analysis introducing a lag between alcohol consumption and each follow‐up period, the results remained consistent with the findings from the main analyses. The fully adjusted HRs for medium and heavy alcohol consumption in relation to MetS were 1.21 and 1.47, respectively ( P ≤ 0.023; P ‐trend &lt; 0.001). Table 3 shows the associations between alcohol consumption and incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers in Korean adults by sex. Significant interactions by sex were observed in hyperglycemia and high blood pressure analyses ( P ‐interaction = 0.049 and 0.026, respectively). After full adjustment for covariates, medium or heavy alcohol consumption was positively associated with developing hyperglycemia and high blood pressure in men ( P &lt; 0.001; P ‐trend &lt; 0.001 for all cases). Women who consumed light alcohol had a 54% higher risk of developing hyperglycemia and a 44% higher risk of developing high blood pressure ( P &lt; 0.001; P ‐trend ≤ 0.004 for all cases). As shown in Figure S1 , we identified four to five alcohol consumption trajectories over 20 years for each cardiometabolic risk marker analysis. Overall, the observed alcohol intake trajectories were: none or rarely, consistently light or decreasing, consistently medium or decreasing and consistently heavy or decreasing. The results of the Cox proportional hazards regression analyses on the associations between alcohol intake trajectories and incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers are presented in Table S1 . After full adjustment for covariates, the results were similar to our main findings. For the high blood pressure analysis, which included five trajectories, all alcohol consumption trajectory groups showed positive associations ( P ≤ 0.002 for all levels), except for the medium and decreasing group, which showed no significant association. The associations between alcohol consumption and incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers in Korean adults by type of alcoholic beverage are presented in Table S2 . After full adjustment for covariates, medium or heavy alcohol consumption in both liquor drinkers and mixed drinkers was positively associated with developing MetS, abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, high blood pressure and hypertriglyceridemia ( P ≤ 0.038; P ‐trend ≤ 0.002 for all cases). Light alcohol consumption showed inconsistent associations with incident cases related to cardiometabolic risk markers, depending on the type of alcoholic beverage. Results for non‐liquor drinkers were not presented because of the limited sample size ( n = 52 for light to heavy alcohol intake categories). DISCUSSION In this prospective cohort study over a 20‐year follow‐up, we found that long‐term light (2 &lt; −16 g/day, within guideline limits), medium (16 &lt; −32 g/day), or heavy (&gt;32 g/day) alcohol consumption was positively and linearly associated with incident health risk conditions related to five of eight cardiometabolic risk markers: MetS, abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, high blood pressure and hypertriglyceridemia. By sex, positive associations between light alcohol intake and hyperglycemia and high blood pressure were observed only in women. In contrast, higher alcohol consumption was associated with a lower risk of developing low HDL‐C. The positive associations between light, medium or heavy alcohol intake and various incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers observed in our study are partially consistent with findings from previous cohort research [ 21 , 23 , 25 ]. For instance, a cohort study of 8103 university graduates in Spain found that individuals consuming seven or more drinks per week had a higher risk of developing MetS, hypertriglyceridemia and impaired fasting glucose, while having a lower risk of low HDL‐C compared to non‐drinkers [ 25 ]. The mechanisms underlying the associations between alcohol consumption and several health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers can be explained as follows: first, two equivalents of reduced NADH are produced for each equivalent of ethanol oxidized. This metabolism elevates the intracellular NADH:NAD + ratio, consequently inhibiting fatty acid β‐oxidation in the liver. Furthermore, ethanol metabolism enhances the rate of fatty acid esterification, resulting in increased triglyceride accumulation in hepatocytes [ 48 ]. Alcohol consumption also activates the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system and the sympathetic nervous system, potentially increasing blood pressure [ 49 ]. Chronic ethanol exposure induces hepatic insulin resistance, primarily because of impaired insulin receptor binding [ 50 ]. In contrast, the inverse association between alcohol consumption and low HDL‐C observed in our study and previous research may indicate that alcohol intake partially decreases cardiometabolic risk. This finding is likely caused by increased transport rates of the HDL apolipoproteins apoA‐I and apoA‐II [ 25 , 51 ]. However, evidence from randomized controlled trials and genetic studies has questioned whether the inverse association between HDL‐C and CVD risk is causal. Functional measures of HDL, such as cholesterol efflux capacity, have been suggested as better predictors of CVD risk than HDL‐C levels [ 52 ]. Moreover, a meta‐analysis reported a J‐shaped association between alcohol consumption and MetS [ 18 ]. The authors speculated that the observed J‐shaped relationship might be attributable to the inverse association between alcohol consumption and low HDL‐C, potentially masking positive associations between alcohol intake and other MetS components. They also suggested that the J‐shaped association might result from a higher proportion of wine drinkers among light drinkers [ 18 ]. A low‐to‐moderate intake of red wine, which contains various polyphenols such as anthocyanins, may confer cardioprotective benefits [ 53 ]. Additionally, the lowest drinking category (0.1–5 g/day) in the meta‐analysis included only three studies: two involving Caucasian participants and one involving Korean participants, the latter of which reported a non‐significant positive association [ 18 ]. Another cohort study involving 5629 Korean men and women reported a J‐shaped relationship between alcohol consumption and MetS [ 22 ]. Light drinkers (0–15 g/day for men and 0–10 g/day for women) or moderate drinkers (15–40 g/day for men and 10–20 g/day for women) who were non‐smokers had a lower risk of developing MetS compared to never drinkers. The discrepancy with our findings may be attributed to the fact that this study presented only stratified results by sex or smoking status and categorized alcohol consumption based on the low‐risk consumption criteria (≤40 g/day for men and ≤20 g/day for women) published by the WHO in 2000 [ 54 ], which have recently been updated [ 5 ]. Furthermore, these criteria did not meet the proportional hazards assumption in our study. Moreover, a cohort study by Bell et al . [ 24 ] reported a J‐shaped association between alcohol consumption and inflammatory markers, including CRP levels. In our study, there was no significant association between alcohol intake and high hsCRP levels overall. Although men consuming light alcohol had a lower risk of high hsCRP levels, the interaction between alcohol consumption and sex was not significant. The previous cohort study excluded participants with CRP levels over 10 mg/L, possibly indicating bacterial infection or acute inflammation, but did not exclude those with chronic diseases such as cancer or CVD. These individuals may have consumed little or no alcohol and had higher CRP levels than healthy individuals, potentially overestimating the protective effects of moderate drinking. Moreover, moderate alcohol consumption may induce specific heat shock proteins and suppress the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, contributing to endotoxin tolerance [ 24 ]. In men, medium or heavy alcohol consumption was positively associated with developing hyperglycemia and high blood pressure, but light alcohol consumption was not. In women, positive associations between light alcohol intake (2 &lt; −16 g/day of alcohol) and hyperglycemia and high blood pressure were observed, but not with medium‐to‐heavy alcohol intake, possibly because of the limited sample size. Nevertheless, significant linear trends between alcohol intake and hyperglycemia and high blood pressure were observed in women. The lower threshold dose of alcohol for developing hyperglycemia and high blood pressure in women is likely because of their lower proportion of body water compared to men of similar body weight, as well as men generally having higher alcohol dehydrogenase activity [ 55 , 56 ]. Our findings for men align with the low‐risk drinking thresholds (≤16 g/day of alcohol for adults) suggested by the UK alcohol intake guideline [ 32 ], but those for women did not. Additionally, our findings were inconsistent with the 2020 to 2025 United States (US) dietary guidelines, which define moderate drinking as &lt;28 g/day of alcohol for men and &lt;14 g/day for women [ 57 ]. The discrepancy between our findings and these guidelines may be attributed to the low prevalence of wine drinkers or the lower capacity for alcohol metabolism in Northeast Asians [ 58 ]. However, a cohort study conducted in Spain indicated that individuals consuming seven or more drinks per week (~10 g/day or more of alcohol) had an 80% higher risk of developing MetS compared to non‐drinkers. The study also reported a positive association between wine consumption and hyperglycemia [ 25 ]. A recent review of clinical trials on wine intake and metabolic effects indicated that long‐term wine consumption significantly increased HDL‐C levels in healthy individuals. However, the effects of long‐term wine intake on triglycerides, LDL, insulin and markers of inflammation and endothelial function remain unclear [ 59 ]. Taken together, the limits for drinking in moderation may require further careful consideration, accounting for potential biological differences by sex. By type of alcoholic beverage, medium or heavy alcohol consumption in both liquor drinkers and mixed drinkers was positively associated with developing MetS, abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, high blood pressure and hypertriglyceridemia. This finding is consistent with previous studies that reported similar adverse effects of alcohol intake on cardiovascular outcomes across alcohol types [ 7 , 26 ]. Moreover, light alcohol intake showed inconsistent associations between mixed drinkers and liquor drinkers. For example, at light alcohol levels, mixed drinkers had a lower risk of incident MetS, whereas no significant association was observed in liquor drinkers. These varying associations may be partially explained by the masking effect of the strong inverse association with HDL‐C observed in mixed drinkers [ 18 ]. Additionally, differences in mean alcohol consumption within the light intake category (mixed drinkers: 2.37 ± 2.35 g/day; liquor drinkers: 7.45 ± 4.39 g/day) and residual confounding from more favorable lifestyle profiles in mixed drinkers, such as higher education levels (Table S3 ), may have contributed to the inconsistent associations between light alcohol intake and incident cases related to cardiometabolic risk markers by alcohol type. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study using a 20‐year population‐based Korean prospective cohort to investigate the association between long‐term alcohol consumption and incident health risk conditions related to cardiometabolic risk markers. We used recent alcohol intake guidelines and conducted comprehensive analyses, including subgroup analyses by sex and type of alcoholic beverage, as well as trajectory analyses of alcohol consumption. The average alcohol intake categories effectively capture and reflect longitudinal drinking patterns, as supported by the stable alcohol consumption levels within each trajectory over the 20‐year period. We used repeated measures of cardiometabolic risk markers and alcohol consumption to calculate the long‐term average alcohol intake, thereby reducing the misclassification bias commonly found in studies relying on a single measure of alcohol consumption. Additionally, we used strict criteria for selecting the study population, including the exclusion of participants with chronic diseases and those with missing data on covariates. Our study also has some limitations. Random measurement error in self‐reported questionnaires, including those for alcohol consumption and disease diagnoses, may have driven the associations toward the null. However, self‐administered questionnaires can provide meaningful estimates of alcohol intake over long periods in participants of prospective epidemiologic studies [ 60 ]. Moreover, we could not examine the association with wine consumption because of the limited sample size. Additionally, drinkers are likely to underestimate their alcohol consumption. However, in Korea, where drinking is part of a tolerant culture [ 61 ], this bias is likely to have minimal impact on our study. To mitigate the influence of potential confounding factors, we conducted analyses adjusting for various covariates, including dietary quality using DASH‐KQ. In conclusion, long‐term light, medium or heavy alcohol consumption was positively and linearly associated with higher risks of incident health risk conditions related to five of eight cardiometabolic risk markers: MetS, abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, high blood pressure and hypertriglyceridemia. By sex, positive associations between light alcohol intake and hyperglycemia and high blood pressure were observed only in women. In contrast, higher alcohol consumption was associated with a lower risk of developing low HDL‐C. Further large‐scale prospective studies that consider exclusive wine intake or potential modifiers such as sex are warranted. Our findings suggest that long‐term alcohol intake within guideline limits may still increase the risk of developing chronic diseases, including CVD.
