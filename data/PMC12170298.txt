Abstract Rapid population ageing, the growing burden of chronic diseases, and evolving healthcare demands have heightened the need for robust longitudinal data to support evidence-based health policy and interventions. Longitudinal panel surveys, which repeatedly collect data from the same individuals over extended periods, offer detailed insights into dynamic health-related changes and their determinants. South Korea has established numerous national panel surveys over recent decades; however, systematic comparative assessments across these surveys—particularly concerning their health-related variables and adaptations during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic—are lacking. We systematically reviewed eleven major Korean longitudinal panel surveys, focusing on health-related variables and COVID-19 modules. Key variables included health status, chronic diseases, lifestyle behaviors, healthcare utilization, and mental health measures. COVID-19 adaptations such as infection history, vaccination status, and socioeconomic impacts were also examined using official documentation. The findings revealed considerable variability among the surveys in the range and depth of health variables captured, reflecting distinct target populations and research objectives. Surveys focused on specific demographic groups (older adults, children, women, and people with disabilities) tended to provide more comprehensive coverage of health indicators and incorporated specialized instruments (e.g., CES-D, EQ-5D). Conversely, general population-based panels demonstrated substantial variability. COVID-19-specific adaptations varied significantly: while certain surveys (Korean Labor and Income Panel Study, Korea Health Panel Survey, Korean Longitudinal Survey of Women and Families, Korean Children and Youth Panel Survey) included explicit pandemic-related modules capturing infection histories, vaccinations, and changes in work and family dynamics, others relied on indirect reflections through existing measures. This comprehensive comparative analysis identifies notable strengths and gaps among Korea’s major longitudinal panel surveys in health data collection and COVID-19 responsiveness. Enhanced standardization of survey instruments and targeted data harmonization efforts are recommended to optimize these resources for future health policy development, epidemiological research, and effective public health interventions. INTRODUCTION Rapid population ageing worldwide—driven by declining birth rates and longer life expectancies—is placing unprecedented strain on healthcare systems. In many countries, older adults account for a growing share of chronic disease burden, as age-related conditions accumulate and multimorbidity becomes common. 1 Consequently, healthcare expenditures are rising, with ageing populations exerting upward pressure on costs and resource needs. These demographic shifts pose significant challenges for modern health systems, underscoring the need for data that can effectively track and inform responses to an ageing society. Reliable longitudinal data are therefore essential, as they allow accurate monitoring and management of these demographic changes. Longitudinal surveys, which repeatedly collect data from the same individuals or households over extended periods, offer valuable insights into both current population status and ongoing trends. 2 In particular, panel surveys offer distinct advantages over cross-sectional surveys by providing detailed insights into behavioral changes at the individual level, thereby enabling more precise causal analysis and avoiding ecological fallacies resulting from aggregated data. 3 , 4 In health research, panel survey data are increasingly vital because they track the same individuals over extended periods, enabling the analysis of causal relationships and dynamic trends. Unlike cross-sectional data, which capture only static associations at a single point in time, panel data facilitate the examination of dynamic relationships, enhancing the robustness of causal inference. 5 By periodically following the same set of respondents selected at baseline, panel surveys can effectively monitor changes within representative population groups over the long term. 2 These panel studies cover various populations – from children and youth to working-age adults, older adults, and special groups – providing rich data for health and social research. Unlike one-time cross-sectional surveys, panel datasets allow researchers to observe changes and causal relationships in health, economic, and social outcomes over the life course. 6 Since the late 1990s, the construction of longitudinal panel datasets has been actively pursued across various fields, resulting in a continuous increase in research utilizing accumulated panel data. As of 2023, studies based on panel data in Korea alone have exceeded 9,000 publications. 7 The ultimate aim of national health policy is “Health for All,” yet numerous epidemiological studies have demonstrated that population health cannot be achieved solely through an ideal healthcare system. External factors such as poverty and working conditions also play crucial roles. However, within the domain of healthcare policy specifically, it is essential to continuously and systematically monitor how health-related resources (such as pharmaceuticals) and healthcare services are distributed within society, and how these distribution patterns evolve over time. 5 Public health surveillance systems that employ systematic, ongoing collection and analysis of data are critical for empowering decision-makers with timely, useful evidence to target interventions and evaluate their effectiveness on population health. 8 These panel datasets have informed national health insurance reforms, evaluations of welfare programs, and strategies to reduce health disparities, firmly establishing panel surveys as indispensable tools for public health surveillance and policy assessment. In light of this, a comparative review of the major panel studies is both necessary and timely to understand their scope and contributions. Thus, this study aims to identify key differences and commonalities among these eleven panel surveys, highlight their strengths and limitations, and provide recommendations to enhance their utility and impact on public health policy and research in South Korea. METHODS Study design and selection criteria This study employed a systematic comparative review approach to analyze health-related variables and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) supplementary survey items collected in major longitudinal panel surveys conducted in South Korea. We selected 11 nationally representative longitudinal panel surveys based on predefined eligibility criteria: 1) national representativeness, 2) ongoing longitudinal design, 3) inclusion of health-related variables, and 4) publicly available data and methodological documentation. The selected surveys included the Korean Labor and Income Panel Study (KLIPS), Youth Panel (YP), Korean Welfare Panel Study (KOWEPS), Korean Longitudinal Study of Ageing (KLoSA), Korean Longitudinal Survey of Women and Families (KLoWF), Korea Health Panel Survey (KHP), Panel Study on Korean Children (PSKC), Korean Children and Youth Panel Survey (KCYPS), Multicultural Adolescents Panel Study (MAPS), Korean Youth Health Behavior Panel Survey (KYHBPS), and Disability and Life Dynamics Panel (DLDP). Data sources and collection Data were systematically extracted from each survey’s official documentation, including technical reports, annual methodological guidelines, official websites, and published peer-reviewed literature. We reviewed survey instruments, questionnaires, and methodological notes to identify key health-related variables regularly measured across these panel surveys. The extracted data included variables such as self-rated health status, presence of chronic diseases, disability status, health behaviors (smoking, alcohol use, physical activity, dietary habits), sleep patterns, healthcare utilization, private health insurance coverage, and preventive care practices. COVID-19 supplementary data collection We specifically investigated the inclusion and implementation of COVID-19-related supplementary survey items or dedicated COVID-19 modules across each panel survey. Variables of interest included COVID-19 infection experiences, vaccination status, employment changes, remote working or learning arrangements, mental health and psychosocial impacts, and other related socioeconomic disruptions during the pandemic period. The timing, scope, and integration methods of COVID-19-related items were recorded and systematically compared. Data analysis and comparative approach Comparative analysis was conducted through descriptive methods using structured comparative tables, allowing clear visualization of differences and similarities across surveys. Each health-related variable was categorized by its measurement frequency, type of items included, and survey-specific methodological details. Similarly, COVID-19 supplementary survey variables were tabulated according to inclusion criteria, specific topics addressed, and data collection methods. Ensuring data accuracy and reliability The reliability and validity of the collected data were ensured through a multi-step verification process involving cross-checking of survey instruments and official methodological documentation. Discrepancies identified during data extraction were resolved through consensus among the authors. Data accuracy was further validated by reviewing published studies utilizing these panel datasets. This rigorous verification process enhanced the robustness and credibility of the comparative findings presented. RESULTS Table 1 summarizes key panel surveys in South Korea, including the KLIPS, KOWEPS, YP, PSKC, KHP, KYHBPS, DLDP, KLoSA, KLoWF, KCYPS, and MAPS. Several surveys conduct annual data collection, including the KLIPS, initiated by the Korea Labor Institute in 1998. KLIPS tracks urban households and individuals aged 15 and above, examining economic activities, education, and household dynamics, with sample updates in 2009 and 2018. The KOWEPS, run by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs (KIHASA) and Seoul National University since 2006, annually surveys households across all age groups to assess welfare needs, poverty, and living conditions, introducing new samples in 2012 and 2022. Youth-focused surveys also frequently adopt an annual approach. The YP, administered by the Korea Employment Information Service (KEIS), tracks young adults aged 15–28 in various cohorts (2001, 2007, 2021), emphasizing education, socioeconomic activities, and family backgrounds. Similarly, the PSKC, conducted by the Korea Institute of Child Care and Education since 2008, annually monitors the developmental trajectory of children from birth. Health and specialized surveys, such as the KHP, a collaboration between KIHASA and the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), annually examine healthcare usage, expenditures, and health behaviors since 2008. Other annual health-focused surveys include the KYHBPS, initiated by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency in 2019, and the DLDP, managed by the Korea Disabled People’s Development Institute since 2018, tracking quality of life and social participation among individuals with disabilities. Biennial surveys include the KLoSA, conducted by KEIS since 2006, targeting adults aged 45 and older. This survey explores aging, retirement, health status, and economic stability, with sample replenishment in 2014. Another biennial survey, the KLoWF, administered by the Korean Women’s Development Institute since 2007, focuses on women aged 19–64, investigating economic participation, family dynamics, and health, with additional sampling in 2017. Child and youth-focused surveys, such as the KCYPS, run annually by the National Youth Policy Institute (NYPI) since 2010, track academic and psychological development among school-age children. The MAPS, also conducted annually by NYPI since 2011, investigates multicultural youths’ identity formation, academic progress, and adaptation processes. Data collection methods and COVID-19 era adaptations Over the past two decades, Korea’s major longitudinal panel surveys have progressively modernized their data collection methods, transitioning from traditional paper-and-pencil interviewing (PAPI) to computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI). The KLIPS, launched in 1998 as an annual survey targeting approximately 5,000 urban households, initially utilized PAPI methods. KLIPS became the first among Korea’s major panel surveys to fully adopt CAPI, implementing it from its 11th wave in 2008. 21 Specifically, the YP (2007), KOWEPS (2010), KHP (2010), KLoSA (2005), KLoWF (2007), PSKC (2008), KCYPS (2010), MAPS (2011), DLDP (2008), and KYHBPS (2019) all adopted CAPI data collection methodologies. As shown in Table 2 , during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023), Korea’s major panel surveys adapted their methodologies to ensure continuity and safety. 10 KLIPS, KOWEPS, KHP, KCYPS, MAPS, KYHBPS, and DLDP delayed or extended their fieldwork schedules and adopted hybrid modes, integrating telephone, mail, or web-based methods with face-to-face CAPI. KLoSA temporarily utilized telephone-assisted interviewing, while KLoWF maintained its original face-to-face CAPI methodology without significant adjustments. International panel surveys demonstrated similar adaptive strategies. The UK Household Longitudinal Study suspended all face-to-face interviews on March 15, 2020, promptly transitioning to online and telephone modes. Despite this significant change, the overall response rate declined by only 1.5 percentage points compared to the previous year (2019), indicating effective adaptation during the COVID-19 pandemic. 25 , 26 The US Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) transitioned its transition into adulthood supplement from face-to-face to telephone interviewing and incorporated COVID-19 modules into its 2021 wave. 27 For the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) in the United States, face-to-face interviews were completely halted in March 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic, prompting an immediate transition to telephone-based data collection. Prior to the pandemic, telephone interviews accounted for only about 5–8% of all annual interviews; however, by the fall of 2021, the proportion significantly increased to between 33.8% and 39.7%. During this period, computer-assisted video interviews were also implemented as part of data collection efforts. 28 , 29 , 30 Sample retention rates in major Korean panel surveys Longitudinal panel surveys are invaluable for studying individual and household dynamics over time, but their value depends on retaining participants. 31 High retention preserves sample size and representativeness, whereas attrition (cumulative loss of respondents) poses a major threat to validity. 32 , 33 Table 3 summarizes retention rates from 11 major Korean panel surveys, encompassing general household studies and age-specific cohorts. Most longitudinal panel surveys in Korea experience significant attrition between the second and third waves, followed by a stabilization in retention rates in subsequent waves. 7 To counteract attrition and maintain representativeness, several Korean panel surveys periodically added new samples. This strategic supplementation was intended to address issues arising from survey refusals and natural attrition, ensuring continued representativeness and reliability in longitudinal analyses. 34 KLIPS, launched in 1998, tracks urban Korean households’ economic outcomes and has experienced steady attrition: the Wave 2 re-interview in 1999 retained about 87.6% of the original households, dropping to roughly 70.6% by Wave 11 (2008) and 74.2% by Wave 18 (2015). To counter sample erosion, an additional sample was introduced in 2009 when retention dipped to 68.4%, resulting in a retention rate of 60.6% by Wave 26 in 2023. Internationally, analogous surveys like the U.S. PSID and ‘the Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia (HILDA) show broadly similar patterns: PSID’s first-year retention is high (over 90%) and HILDA retained 86.9% of respondents by its second wave, with about 70.8% of the original sample still participating by Wave 10. By 22 waves, HILDA’s original sample retention was 64.4%. 35 To maintain representativeness, the KOWEPS incorporated new samples at the 7th (2012) and 17th (2022) waves. Similarly, studies targeting specific populations, such as the KLoSA and the KLoWF, strategically added new samples mid-study to enhance retention. The KHP has operated in two distinct phases; Phase 1 ran from 2008 to 2018, incorporating additional samples in its 8th wave, while Phase 2 began in 2021, showing an 81% retention rate at its 5th wave in 2024. Additionally, the KCYPS initiated new cohorts in 2018 after completing earlier cycles, ensuring sustained high retention and panel stability. Additionally, the KCYPS, MAPS, KYHBPS, and Panel Survey of Employment for the Disabled (PSED) also demonstrated similar retention patterns, with periodic replenishment of samples and varying retention rates to sustain longitudinal research integrity. Additionally, the KCYPS was conducted in two distinct phases. In KCYPS 2018, retention was higher among students in fourth grade compared to those in the seventh grade. The MAPS, currently in its second phase since 2018, showed an 81.1% retention rate as of 2023. The KYHBPS and DLDP also exhibited high retention rates of 78.9% and 78.3%, respectively, as of 2023, highlighting effective longitudinal survey strategies in Korea. Mental health and psychosocial measures across Korean panel surveys A comparative analysis of mental health and psychosocial variables assessed by major Korean panel surveys is presented in Table 4 . Validated depression scales were included consistently in several surveys, notably the CES-D (Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale) used annually in KOWEPS and biennially in KLoSA and KLoWF. Additionally, the PHQ (Patient Health Questionnaire)-2 or PHQ-9 depression screening tools were utilized annually by the KHP, KYHBPS, and DLDP. The PSKC uniquely measured maternal depression (EPDS and CES-D) during initial waves. The KHP includes the PHQ-2 to screen for depression annually and assesses stress on a 4-point scale. Life satisfaction is measured along with health-related quality of life through the EQ-5D tool, aligning with its primary focus on health service utilization. Anxiety-specific instruments were not explicitly reported across these surveys. However, stress measures were widely included, albeit through diverse approaches. For example, KLoWF employed a specific measure assessing stress related to work-family conflicts, while MAPS focused on acculturation-related stress among multicultural adolescents. PSKC regularly assessed parenting stress annually through the Parenting Stress Index, highlighting its distinctive demographic focus. Life satisfaction or quality of life indicators were broadly measured, typically through single-item global ratings or brief scales. KLIPS and YP recorded general life satisfaction, whereas KHP applied the EQ-5D index annually, integrating physical and mental health dimensions. Additionally, specialized psychosocial assessments were administered by several panels: cognitive function was measured biennially using the K-MMSE by KLoSA and KLoWF, while child development surveys (PSKC) utilized instruments like the K-WPPSI and KEDI-WISC regularly to track cognitive development. Self-esteem was explicitly assessed via the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale in KOWEPS, KCYPS, KYHBPS, and DLDP, demonstrating a cross-survey interest in self-concept and psychological resources. Furthermore, suicidal ideation was consistently included in annual surveys targeting vulnerable groups such as adolescents (KCYPS, MAPS, KYHBPS) and economically disadvantaged populations (KOWEPS, DLDP). Table 5 systematically compares core health-related variables collected by eleven major panel surveys in South Korea. Self-rated health, a widely accepted indicator of subjective health, was consistently assessed in all panels reviewed (KLIPS, YP, KOWEPS, KLoSA, KLoWF, KHP, PSKC, KCYPS, MAPS, KYHBPS, and DLDP). Chronic disease presence was regularly recorded in most surveys, except for KLIPS, YP, and MAPS. Disability status or type was routinely documented by most surveys, excluding YP and MAPS, demonstrating an increasing emphasis on health disparities associated with disability. Particularly, panels targeting vulnerable populations (e.g., DLDP) provided comprehensive disability profiling to facilitate targeted health interventions. There was considerable variation in the collection of health behavior data. Smoking status and alcohol consumption were commonly included across most surveys. Conversely, information on physical activity and dietary habits was less uniformly captured, with surveys like KHP, PSKC, KCYPS, KYHBPS, and DLDP providing more comprehensive lifestyle data. Sleep patterns, increasingly recognized as significant determinants of overall health, were monitored consistently by KLoSA, KHP, PSKC, KYHBPS, and DLDP. Access to healthcare indicators, such as private health insurance coverage, participation in health screenings, and preventive care utilization, were systematically recorded in KHP, DLDP, KLoSA, and PSKC. Medication adherence, critical for chronic disease management, was explicitly included only in KHP and DLDP. Table 6 summarizes the implementation of COVID-19-related supplemental surveys across major panel studies during the pandemic period. The KLIPS conducted a specialized supplemental survey focusing on COVID-19 from the 23rd wave (2020) through the 25th wave (2022), systematically examining changes in daily life and perceptions related to the pandemic. Similarly, the KLoWF incorporated additional modules specifically addressing COVID-19-related shifts in family dynamics and women's lives during the same period. The KHP, in its 2023 survey, collected detailed data on household members' experiences with COVID-19 infections, vaccination status, and specific vaccine types administered. Additionally, the KCYPS incorporated items regarding pandemic experiences and perceptions into their 2020 and 2021 waves, including remote learning experiences, stress arising from restrictions on face-to-face activities, and psychological and emotional changes compared to pre-pandemic conditions. Conversely, while specialized COVID-19 modules were not explicitly added to the YP, KOWEPS, KLoSA, PSKC, MAPS, KYHBPS, and DLDP, changes related to COVID-19 were nevertheless indirectly captured and reflected within the standard survey data, effectively illustrating pandemic-related impacts on participants' daily lives and behaviors. DISCUSSION Korea’s health-related panel surveys collectively provide valuable longitudinal data resources that significantly enhance understanding of health dynamics over time. These panel datasets have several notable strengths. First, their longitudinal design enables detailed analysis of temporal sequences and within-person changes, substantially strengthening causal inference compared to cross-sectional studies. By tracking the same individuals or households across multiple waves, researchers can employ analytical techniques such as fixed-effects models and lagged-outcome analyses, effectively controlling for unobserved confounders and establishing clearer temporal relationships. 36 For example, panel analyses have been used to examine how unmet healthcare needs predict subsequent health declines, controlling for baseline health status. Second, these surveys offer direct utility for policy formulation and evaluation. Many Korean panel studies were initiated explicitly by government institutions to inform and refine social policy frameworks. For example, the KOWEPS, operational since 2006, provides extensive data on poverty dynamics and the impact of welfare programs, thereby directly informing social policy interventions. Similarly, the KHP conducted by the National Health Insurance Service and the KIHASA offers comprehensive insights into healthcare utilization patterns and expenditures. 37 This has allowed health economists and policymakers to study the burden of out-of-pocket payments, the impact of insurance benefit changes, and cost drivers in the healthcare system – information essential for evidence-based health policy. In Health Affairs, the MEPS in the United States has been lauded as “a critical asset to health policymakers,” and Korea’s KHP serves a similar role domestically in guiding healthcare policy and evaluating service utilization trends. The panel surveys focusing on older adults (KLoSA) and women (KLoWF) have likewise informed policy, retirement age debates, and gender equity programs by elucidating long-term trends in employment, caregiving, and health among their constituencies. Third, the breadth of vulnerable and special populations covered by Korean panels is a strength for health equity monitoring. Health equity requires detailed data on how different subgroups fare over time. 38 Third, Korean panel studies effectively monitor health equity by encompassing diverse and often marginalized population subgroups such as low-income families, rural communities, individuals with disabilities, and multicultural households. Longitudinal data from the DLDP, for instance, provides critical insights into whether quality-of-life indicators for disabled persons improve or deteriorate over time, revealing disparities often obscured in aggregated statistics. Such longitudinal perspectives are essential for understanding how health inequities evolve with aging populations and shifting policy contexts. Korea’s panels collectively encompass low-income households, rural vs. urban residents, the disabled, children in multicultural families, and more. This allows tracking of disparities; for example, the DLDP can reveal whether people with disabilities see improvements or stagnation in quality-of-life indicators over time, relative to general population trends. Findings can highlight gaps that might be missed in aggregate statistics. Moreover, because the data are longitudinal, one can observe whether inequalities are widening or narrowing as individuals age or as policies change. In theory, this is ideal for equity monitoring. Another significant limitation is sample representativeness and attrition. Long-term panels invariably suffer attrition as participants drop out over time. The review documented that Korean panels are no exception: for example, KLIPS (started 1998) retained 87.6% of households in its second wave but only ~70% by the 10th year, prompting a refresh sample in 2009. By 2018 (wave 21), original sample retention in KLIPS had fallen below three-quarters, despite replenishments. Despite these significant strengths, our review identified notable limitations, primarily related to sample representativeness and attrition. Like other global longitudinal surveys, Korean panels face considerable challenges in retaining participants over extended periods. For instance, the KLIPS, initiated in 1998, retained approximately 88% of households by the second wave, but this retention rate declined to about 70% by its tenth year, prompting the addition of a refresher sample in 2009. By the 21st wave in 2018, less than three-quarters of the original participants remained, despite periodic sample replenishments. Similar patterns of attrition are evident in other major surveys, including KOWEPS and KLoSA. Although such retention rates are consistent with those observed internationally—like the U.S. PSID or HILDA—the attrition remains a significant concern. 32 Differential dropout, particularly among disadvantaged or less healthy individuals, can introduce bias, potentially undermining the validity and representativeness of the results. Therefore, implementing effective participant engagement strategies, including regular communication, appropriate incentives, and sophisticated statistical corrections, remains essential to sustain data quality and representativeness over time. Furthermore, an often-overlooked issue in Korean panel research is fieldworker attrition, an operational challenge that can significantly affect data quality. While international practices increasingly recognize the importance of interviewer continuity for maintaining data consistency and reducing measurement error, 39 Korean panel reports rarely document or address interviewer turnover explicitly. A prevailing implicit assumption seems to be that trained fieldworkers are readily interchangeable without substantial data quality implications. International experiences, however, suggest otherwise. For example, a recent national mental health prevalence study in the United States anticipated significant interviewer turnover during the COVID-19 pandemic and proactively incorporated recruitment and training strategies into their fieldwork plan to ensure continuity and data integrity. Such transparency and foresight remain uncommon in Korean surveys. Given that high interviewer turnover may cause variability in survey responses or contribute to attrition spikes, a shift in the Korean approach is warranted. Korean survey organizations should systematically monitor interviewer retention, include continuity metrics in their quality assurance processes, and treat interviewer stability and morale as critical components of overall survey success. In summary, Korea’s longitudinal panel surveys are indispensable for longitudinal health research and evidence-based policy formulation. Yet, their effectiveness depends heavily on improving methodological rigor, enhancing operational transparency, and addressing sample attrition comprehensively. By proactively addressing these areas, Korea can further strengthen its health data infrastructure, align with international best practices, and ensure robust support for policies promoting health equity and population well-being over the long term. CONCLUSION This study was conducted to systematically compare and analyze health-related variables and COVID-19-specific survey modules across eleven major longitudinal panel surveys in South Korea. Specifically, the study aimed to clarify variations in survey coverage concerning health status, chronic diseases, health behaviors, healthcare utilization, and responses to the COVID-19 pandemic. By highlighting each survey’s unique strengths and limitations, this comparative analysis provides valuable foundational evidence for designing future panel studies and improving data integration. Ultimately, these insights will enhance the effectiveness of national health policy-making and health equity monitoring and strengthen capacity for timely decision-making during public health crises.
