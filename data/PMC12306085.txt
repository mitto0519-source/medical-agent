Background Anemia is a global public health concern, particularly among adolescent girls in rural Bangladesh, owing to inadequate knowledge and insufficient nutritional intake. Mobile health (mHealth) education has the potential to solve these limitations. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of mHealth education on raising hemoglobin levels and reducing anemia among adolescent girls in rural Bangladesh. Methods This open-label, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial was conducted at two schools in Chandpur district, Bangladesh from late May 2022 to January 2023. We randomly assigned one school to the intervention group and another to the control group and then performed simple random sampling to recruit 138 adolescent girls with anemia from the intervention ( n = 69) and control ( n = 69) groups. The intervention group received online counseling sessions and health education via mHealth technology, whereas the control group received usual care, and both groups were observed for 8 months. We performed mixed-effects model and generalized estimating equations (GEE) to evaluate the outcomes. Results The study analyzed 121 participants (intervention group, 62; control group, 59). After adjusting for covariates, mixed-effects model observed that body mass index (BMI) [coefficient: 1.10, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.44–1.76, P = 0.001], mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) (coefficient: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.27–1.26, P = 0.002), hip circumference (coefficient: 2.24, 95% CI: 0.62–3.87, P = 0.007), and waist circumference (coefficient: 2.20, 95% CI: 0.74–3.66, P = 0.003) were significantly improved in intervention group. Although hemoglobin levels improved in the intervention group, the change was not statistically significant. However, the intervention group experienced a higher anemia recovery than the control group (25.8% vs. 13.6%). The GEE showed improvement in knowledge (coefficient: 4.11, 95% CI: 3.63–4.58, p = &lt; 0.001), attitude (coefficient: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.24–1.96, p = &lt; 0.001), and practice (coefficient: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.13–0.66, p = 0.004) in the intervention group compared to the control group after adjusting for relevant covariates. Conclusions The mHealth education led to improvements in knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) scores related to anemia among adolescent girls. Furthermore, the intervention resulted in improvements in BMI, MUAC, and hip and waist circumference measurements. Additionally, there were positive trends indicating a reduction in anemia prevalence, accompanied by an upward trend in hemoglobin levels. Therefore, implementing this strategy could enhance KAP and contribute to reducing adolescent girls’ anemia in low-resource settings. Adolescence, defined as the period between 10 and 19 years of age, is a critical phase for growth and development; however, anemia can drastically affect this process [ 2 ]. Anemia among adolescents is more prevalent in developing countries than in developed countries, and its impact is exacerbated by a high disease burden, food scarcity, poor hygiene, and economic challenges [ 3 ]. Furthermore, anemia among adolescent girls is prevalent in 6% of developed countries and 27% in developing countries, which differs significantly from the global average of 15% [ 3 ]. Iron deficiency is the primary cause of anemia in adolescent girls and can lead to other micronutrient deficiencies, including vitamin B12 and folic acid [ 4 ]. Other important causes of anemia include parasite infections, thalassemia, and chronic diseases [ 5 ]. Adolescent girls require high iron intake during puberty [ 6 ]; however, owing to menstrual blood loss and poor nutritional intake, iron storage may diminish and contribute to anemia [ 7 ]. Anemia impairs school-aged girls’ daily activities, academic performance, and overall well-being [ 8 , 9 ]. Furthermore, anemia in adolescent girls increases the likelihood of giving birth to low-birth-weight infants, which perpetuates the cycle of malnutrition over generations and has long-term implications for population health and well-being [ 10 ]. Moreover, anemia increases their susceptibility to infection and can cause complications during pregnancy [ 11 ]. The prevalence of anemia among adolescent girls varies across different developing countries, with rates reported at 26.7% in Southwest Ethiopia [ 12 ], 53.3% in Tanzania [ 13 ], 50.3% in Ghana [ 14 ], and 36.2% in Burkina Faso [ 15 ]. Anemia is a long-standing concern in Bangladesh and other South Asian nations. According to the literature, anemia rates among adolescent girls in Bangladesh range from 23 to 51.6% [ 16 , 17 ]. This high prevalence can be attributed to diverse risk factors, including insufficient nutrition, inadequate dietary intake, malnutrition, eating habits, blood loss during menses, and socioeconomic conditions [ 16 , 17 ]. There remains a significant journey ahead to meet the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) targets for reducing anemia in adolescent girls. Overall, anemia among adolescent girls imposes a considerable burden on local communities and healthcare systems, exacerbating health inequalities [ 18 ]. Regionally, anemia prevention requires tailored interventions that consider sociocultural factors. Globally, anemia undermines endeavors to achieve economic empowerment, and health equity emphasizing the need for coordinated action and awareness of nutrition programs [ 19 ]. Adolescents with anemia who receive adequate information and knowledge regarding anemia and its associated risk factors tend to adopt healthy behaviors and make lifestyle changes. Several high-income countries have implemented anemia prevention awareness campaigns and early diagnostic strategies to address this condition. Increasing awareness of anemia and its risk factors is essential to prevent and control it. Mobile phone use is widespread in rural areas of Bangladesh, and mobile phone technology, a cutting-edge advancement in information and communication technology, plays a critical role in healthcare delivery. mHealth initiatives have emerged as a valuable tool in public health [ 20 ]. Health education via phone calls and SMS text messaging has considerable potential for raising awareness about anemia and promoting iron-rich diets (Fig. 1 ), especially in populations with low literacy levels [ 21 ]. Furthermore, community health workers (CHWs) working in public-sector health facilities are now expanding their responsibilities to include noncommunicable disease services, such as health education and counseling [ 22 ]. Authorizing CHWs to deliver health education and monitoring interventions has proven effective in managing noncommunicable diseases, including lowering blood pressure, and has shown promise for expanding in resource-constrained settings [ 23 ]. However, the impact of mHealth on the health outcomes of adolescents with anemia remains unclear in terms of its effectiveness in reducing anemia. Research indicates that nutrition education effectively improves adolescents’ nutritional knowledge, attitudes, hygiene practices, and eating habits across countries. Adherence to such programs substantially improves nutritional status and hemoglobin levels [ 24 ]. Implementing nutritional health education counseling sessions led by public health physicians also hold promises for increasing the knowledge and awareness of adolescents with anemia [ 24 ]. Several studies indicate that mHealth education interventions delivered via mobile phone technology significantly improve anemia-related KAP and hemoglobin levels among various populations affected by anemia [ 21 , 25 ]. Nevertheless, most of the interventions in low-resource settings focus on the provision of iron, folic acid, and nutrient supplementation to reduce anemia, primarily targeting pregnant and reproductive age (15–45 years) women [ 26 – 28 ]. The traditional anemia interventions have limitations, including poor adherence, limited access to supplements, lack of dietary diversity, and inadequate health awareness, which can reduce the effectiveness of iron supplementation. However, technology-driven solutions like mHealth provide a sustainable approach by promoting behavior change and increasing knowledge to help reduce anemia. In Bangladesh, 98% of households have mobile access [ 29 ], and mHealth education has been successfully implemented for reproductive health education [ 30 ], chronic kidney disease awareness [ 22 ], and blood pressure control [ 23 ], demonstrating positive effectiveness. But in Bangladesh, particularly in rural areas, there have been no specific studies of mHealth nutrition interventions to enhance hemoglobin levels and reduce anemia. Incorporating anemia prevention group counseling sessions and mHealth-based health education could play a crucial role in mitigating anemia and its associated risk factors among adolescent girls in rural areas. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the impact of mHealth nutrition education on increasing hemoglobin levels among school-going adolescent girls with anemia in rural Bangladesh. Methods Design and recruitment This study employed a school–based approach and applied an open-label, parallel-group, two-arm (1:1) randomized controlled trial (RCT) design [ 31 – 33 ]. The participants were adolescent girls with anemia recruited from two schools located in a rural area of Bangladesh. To ensure adherence to the established standards, the study followed the guidelines of Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) [ 34 ] and Standard Protocol Items Recommendation for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) [ 35 ]. A flowchart illustrating the progress of this study is shown in Fig. 2 . The intervention period was 8 months, from the end of May 2022 to the end of January 2023. Study population and sampling This study was conducted in two secondary schools: Baburhat High School and Uttar Shahatali Zobaida Girls High School located in two separate subdistricts of the Chandpur district in Bangladesh’s Chittagong division. Baburhat High School has 1,676 students, 62% (1,032) of whom are girls. Another school, Uttar Shahatali Zobaida Girls High School, has 310 girls. The Chandpur district covers a total area of 1740.6 sq. km and has a population of 2,416,018, with a literacy rate of 69.8% [ 33 ]. These schools were selected for the convenience of laboratory investigation. Before this RCT, the research team evaluated the prevalence of anemia and its associated factors. Among 207 participants at Baburhat High School, 75 (36.2%) were diagnosed with anemia. Similarly, among 207 participants at Zobaida Girls High School, 81 (39.1%) were diagnosed with anemia. In total, 156 (37.7%) out of 414 participants were found anemic in a prior study and were eligible for this RCT [ 36 ]. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows: Inclusion criteria Being an adolescent girl aged 10–19 years at the schools selected for this study. Diagnosis of mild or moderate anemia in the prior prevalence study. The participants or families who had their own cell phone. Those who provided informed consent along with their legal guardians. Exclusion criteria Individuals diagnosed with hemoglobin level &lt; 7.0 g/dl considered with severe anemia and excluded them. Adolescent girls who were regularly taking iron supplements for treatment. Adolescents with mental illness and physical disability. Adolescent girls who were pregnant. Refusal to participate in this study. The assumption of a 34% decrease in anemia within the intervention group was based on a similar nutritional health education intervention study in a low- and middle-income country which showed significant enhancements in health outcomes, including anemia reduction, behavioral and nutritional changes [ 38 ]. We adopted a significance level (α) of 5% and power (1-β) of 90%. These values correspond to a Z-alpha value of 1.96, and a Z-beta value of 1.3. Using this formula, the sample size was calculated to be 106. Assuming a 30% dropout rate owing to reasons such as coronavirus disease (COVID-19), absence, or reluctance to provide blood samples. Owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, Bangladesh schools were closed, and student absence rates were much higher, which directly influenced our study’s 30% dropout rate. The study team followed up with participants and their guardians at regular intervals to minimize attrition. They also worked collaboratively with community health workers and school officials. The final estimated sample size was adjusted to 138. Consequently, the intervention and control groups were assigned sample sizes of 69. Randomization This RCT included two schools, each of which was considered a randomization unit. A statistician who was not engaged in the trial employed a simple coin-toss randomization to assign a school as the control group and another as the intervention group. School-level randomization was chosen in this study to account for the potential contamination health education effect that could occur if individual students were randomized within the same school. Since the intervention involved health education delivered to the students, there was a risk that students within the same school might share information, discuss the intervention, or influence each other’s behaviors. By randomizing at the school level, we ensured that the intervention effects were not confounded by peer interactions within the same school. Subsequently, 69 participants with anemia from each school were selected using a random number generated table to ensure unbiased participant selection within each school. Data contamination across groups was reduced because the schools were located in distinct unions of the Chandpur subdistrict. The list of each school’s participants was placed in separate envelopes and securely stored in the office lockers. After obtaining written informed consent from the participants and their guardians, they were enrolled and assigned a study number. Definition Anemia: Anemia was defined based on hemoglobin levels when hemoglobin levels were below 12.0 g/dL with hemoglobin levels between 10.0 and 11.9 g/dL, 7.0–9.9 g/dL, and &lt; 7.0 g/dL considered mild, moderate, and severe forms, respectively [ 11 , 17 , 39 ]. Guardians: Guardians were defined as individuals responsible for supervising and caring for adolescent girls, such as fathers, mothers, elder siblings, or other relatives [ 40 ]. Usual care: Study participants received the standard healthcare practices available at the community level as part of their regular routine. These practices did not involve any specific interventions for anemia, such as iron supplementation, unless a severe case was identified [ 31 ]. Study activities Data collection of the participants The community health workers (CHWs) collected sociodemographic data, such as age, family structure, religion, parents’ occupation, parents’ education, monthly family income, and KAP information, using field-tested validated survey questionnaires during school visits. The KAP questionnaire was developed and adopted by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [ 41 ]. The KAP questionnaire has 22 questions, with 9 focusing on knowledge, 6 on attitude, and 7 on practice. Subsequently, the CHWs examined anthropometric data on height (cm), weight (kg), mid-upper arm circumference (cm), hip circumference (cm), and waist circumference (cm). To ensure data consistency and minimize self-reported bias trained CHWs performed data collection, and two individuals did the data entry on two separate computers. Additionally, blood samples were collected by a certified medical laboratory technologist during the school visits. An auto hematology analyzer was used to measure hemoglobin levels, ensuring precise and reliable data on anemia status and its severity. After assessing the eligibility of the study participants, a statistician randomized the schools for the intervention group and control group; and performed a simple random sampling to enroll participants in the intervention and control groups. Afterward, the research personnel in charge opened the participant lists for each school, which had been placed in separate envelopes, to reveal the randomly assigned intervention and control group participants and then shared their IDs with the CHWs. Although the CHWs involved in the trial were not blinded to the intervention or control group, they were blinded to baseline interviews. The CHWs then contacted the trial participants in the intervention group, inviting them to participate in the mHealth education program and counseling sessions to improve hemoglobin levels and thereby reduce anemia. In addition to the baseline assessment, the CHWs conducted physical examinations at the end of the 4th month and 8th month and collected data during the intervention at their school visits. Laboratory technologists collected blood samples during these visits. At the end of the 8th month, the same KAP questionnaire was used for the assessment (Table 1 ). KAP assessment The KAP questionnaire consisted of 22 questions. The knowledge part comprised nine variables: (1) knowing about anemia, (2) symptoms of anemia, (3) health risks for adolescents owing to a lack of iron in the diet, (4) health risks for pregnant women lack of iron in the diet, (5) know about causes of anemia, (6) prevention of anemia (7) know the iron-rich foods, (8) foods that increase the iron absorption, and (9) beverages that reduce iron absorption. The attitude part included six variables: (1) how likely think to be anemic, (2) how serious is anemia, (3) prepare meals with iron-rich foods, (4) difficult to eat or prepare meals with iron-rich foods (5) taste of iron-rich food, and (6) confidence in preparing meals with iron-rich foods. The practice part included seven variables: (1) consumption of iron-rich foods the last week, (2) usually intake of vitamin C/citrus fruits or juice, (3) every day eating citrus fruits or juice, (4) timing of eating citrus fruits, (5), usually tea or coffee consumption, (6) everyday drink coffee or tea, and (7) timing of drinking tea or coffee. Each variable was scored as either 0 or 1, with 1 indicating a favorable response. In total, all variables’ scores were combined for the mean average. Questionnaire validity Following the forward-backward translation procedure, the English questionnaire was translated into the local language, Bangla. This process involved two native Bangla speakers fluent in English: a public health specialist and a Ph.D. student. Each translated the English questionnaire into Bangla independently. Their translations were then combined to create a single forward-translated Bangla version. Next, this Bangla version was translated back into English by a professional medical translator, followed by a second translation by a medical doctor not involved in the previous steps. The researcher compiled and compared these back-translated versions. To ensure validity, all translated versions were reviewed by an expert committee. The finalized questionnaire was then pretested with 5% of school-going adolescent girls not included in the main study. Feedback from the pretest was collected and incorporated into the final version of the questionnaire [ 31 ]. Questionnaire reliability The KAP questionnaire’s reliability was measured through the test-retest method and Cronbach alpha. Laboratory investigation Blood samples were obtained to measure hemoglobin levels and white blood cell (WBC) and red blood cell (RBC) counts. Laboratory tests were conducted at the Lab Aid Diagnostic Center in Comilla, Bangladesh, using a Sysmex automated hematological analyzer (Model: XN-550). Blood hemoglobin levels were measured using a spectrophotometric method for whole blood samples. Quality control, in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines, involved running control samples with known hemoglobin levels to confirm the perfection and accuracy of the analyzer and ensure reliable results. Intervention group Online group counseling sessions and mHealth technology were used to provide health education to the intervention participants. Online group counseling session: The online group counseling session was designed to promote behavioral change and create awareness regarding dietary habits, nutrition, and hygiene management, all of which can contribute to anemia. The health education counseling session was conducted for the intervention group via Skype for approximately 90 min. A public health physician conducted two sessions (baseline and midline) with adolescent girls and their guardians. All 69 participants and their guardians attended the same online session, which was conducted in a large classroom in the school. The session was live and interactive. These sessions used online videos, PowerPoint presentations, and discussions to address the causes, consequences, prevention, and recovery from anemia. The sessions covered topics such as iron-rich foods, functions and sources of iron, dietary strategies, food habits and plans to improve iron status, personal hygiene management, and healthy lifestyle practices. At the end of the session, there was a Question and Answer part, and participants asked several questions regarding anemia and recovery strategy, the public health physician gave detailed answers and suggested follow-up health education guidelines (Supplementary material 1). Following the online education sessions, the participants were provided with health education materials in the form of notebooks containing information on anemia prevention and a list of local iron-containing foods. Modified health education materials (notebooks) were provided monthly to each participant throughout the intervention period (Supplementary materials 2). mHealth education: During the 8-month intervention period, trained female CHWs delivered mHealth education through mobile phone calls and SMS text messaging to enhance participants’ nutritional knowledge, improve behaviors, dietary habits, and raise awareness about anemia. Participants received phone calls and mobile text messages four times each month in the initial 4 months, followed by three times during the 5th and 6th months, and finally twice during the 7th and 8th months. The mobile messages delivered targeted health education on anemia prevention, iron-rich dietary practices, strategies to improve iron absorption, hygiene management, and healthy lifestyle behaviors. Additionally, the CHWs provided information to the participants about locally available iron-rich foods and their benefits for iron absorption. They also guided avoiding beverages that may hinder iron absorption when consumed with meals, as well as limiting fatty and oily foods, and emphasized the importance of thorough hand washing before and after eating. Additional instructions included washing fruits carefully before consumption, wearing shoes while using the toilet, and thoroughly washing hands after using the toilet. Participants were also advised on the importance of taking anti-helminth medication every 6 months and using sanitary napkins during menses. Individual participants, along with their guardians, engaged in a 5-minute health education conversation with CHWs over the phone. Additionally, they received regular informative health education messages that were updated weekly (Table 2 ). Control group The control group received usual care and was observed without any specific intervention throughout the trial. Physical examinations and blood sample investigations were performed on these participants at the end of the 4th and 8th months. The KAP questionnaire interviews were conducted at the end of the 8th month. However, after each blood test, we provided hard copy reports with information on their anemia stage and advised them to consult with physicians if they wished. To ensure ethical fairness, control group received all health education materials after the intervention period. Outcomes The primary outcome was improved hemoglobin levels to reduce anemia in school-going adolescent girls. The secondary outcomes were as follows: An increase in the scores of KAP related to anemia, measured using the KAP questionnaire. Improvement in anthropometric indicators such as BMI, MUAC, hip circumference, waist circumference. Outcome measures An automated analyzer measured blood hemoglobin levels to assess anemia categories (non-anemia, mild, moderate, and severe) in adolescent girls. Adolescent girls aged 10–11 years with hemoglobin levels below 11.5 g/dL were classified as having anemia. Within this age group, hemoglobin levels falling within the ranges of 10–11.4 g/dL, 7.0–9.9 g/dL, and &lt; 7.0 g/dL were identified as mild, moderate, and severe anemia, respectively. Similarly, nonpregnant adolescent girls aged 12–19 years with hemoglobin levels &lt; 12 g/dL were considered to have any form of anemia. For this older age group, hemoglobin levels ranging from 10.0 to 11.9 g/dL, 7.0–9.9 g/dL, and &lt; 7.0 g/dL were categorized as mild, moderate, and severe anemia, respectively [ 11 , 17 , 39 ]. After the mHealth education intervention, changes in hemoglobin levels associated with anemia were examined. These changes, including those from anemic to non-anemic, mild to non-anemic/moderate/severe, and moderate to mild/non-anemic/severe, were assessed at baseline, midline, and endline in the intervention and control group. The KAP measurement involves a total of 22 variables, including 9 related to knowledge, 6 related to attitude, and 7 related to practice. Each variable is scored as 1 for a favorable response and 0 for an unfavorable response. Finally, all variable scores are summed up and analyzed for the mean average. Statistical analysis Initially, the data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2019 and subsequently imported into the Stata Software (Version 18.5). Our study was an RCT, allowing three times data collection at the baseline, midline, and endline. Those who provided incomplete data during any time point of data collection were considered missing data. After excluding missing data participants ( n = 17), a per-protocol analysis was used to ensure comparability between the control and intervention groups. Descriptive statistics were analyzed as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. We performed a Box Plot analysis to check the outlier data, but it was not detected. However, in the baseline assessment, continuous variables between groups were evaluated using either the independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test after checking the normality of variables, whereas chi-square tests were employed to compare categorical variables. To evaluate potential violations of normality assumptions, we conducted the Shapiro–Wilk test to assess the data distribution. A p-value &gt; 0.05 indicated normally distributed data, while highly skewed data were analyzed using non-parametric tests. Mixed-effects model was conducted to assess various outcome variables, including hemoglobin levels and physical measurements of hip–waist circumference, MUAC, and BMI. Changes in KAP scores were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data and the independent t-test for normally distributed data. In addition, we performed a generalized estimating equations (GEE) model with a Gaussian family and identity link function to analyze our study outcomes. The exchangeable correlation structure was selected based on the study design and prior literature. To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by refitting the GEE model using different correlation structures (independent and autoregressive). The GEE model was applied to estimate the effect of health education over time after adjusting for age, student grade, father’s education and occupation, mother’s education and occupation, and family income covariates. Statistical significance was set at p &lt; 0.05 in all analyses. Quality assessment To ensure the quality of the mHealth education during the intervention, we employed several key measures. Before the intervention was implemented CHWs received comprehensive training through a public health physician and principal investigator regarding the standardized data collection, health education delivery, and follow-up process. The principal investigator’s weekly supervision and reviews of SMS and phone call content were conducted following the study protocol. CHWs maintained a Microsoft Excel for documenting all the interactions with the participants including SMS, phone calls, educational materials distribution, planning, and feedback of the participants. Furthermore, CHWs took assistance from school teachers when they encountered difficulty reaching participants. The feedback was collected monthly from participants during the in-person delivery of health education notebooks at the school, allowing for real-time adjustments to the intervention. Additionally, maintaining standard procedures during online counselling sessions and phone interactions confirmed that all educational content was delivered as planned, thereby ensuring high quality and consistency throughout the study intervention period. Digital divide To minimize the digital divide, this intervention was specifically designed to be delivered via phone calls and SMS, which were accessible on feature phones and smartphones. This approach ensures that adolescent girls and their families can engage with health education content without needing internet access or advanced technology. Results Study population A total of 156 individuals met the eligibility criteria, of whom 138 (69 each in the control intervention groups) were enrolled. During the intervention period, 7 participants from the intervention group and 10 from the control group declined to continue with the study. In the intervention group, the primary reasons for dropout were refusal to provide follow-up blood samples ( n = 1), absence during follow-up assessments ( n = 4), and school transfers ( n = 2). Similarly, in the control group, participants withdrew due to refusal to provide follow-up blood samples ( n = 5), absence during follow-up assessments ( n = 4), and school transfers ( n = 1). Therefore, the analyzable sample size was 121 (62 in the intervention group and 59 in the control group) (Fig. 2 ). Sociodemographic characteristics The mean (SD) age of the participants was 14.0 (1.3) years in the intervention group and 14.2 (1.5) years in the control group. Overall, 11 (17.7%) and 16 (27.1%) participants completed the tenth grade in the intervention and control groups, respectively. Additionally, in the intervention group, 55 of the participants’ mothers (88.7%) were housewives, compared to 57 (96.6%) in the control group. Similarly, 7 (11.3%) and 13 (22.0%) of the participants’ fathers were farmers in the intervention and control groups, respectively. Additional comparisons between the two groups in three other categories showed the following: participants’ mothers with no formal education were 8 (12.9%) versus 10 (16.9%); fathers with no formal education were 18 (29.0%) versus 18 (30.5%,); and families with a monthly income of US ≤$85.86 were 22 (35.5%,) versus 38 (64.4%). Except for the monthly income, none of these characteristics showed significant differences between the intervention and control groups (Table 3 ). Linear mixed-effects model was performed to examine the outcomes. The adjusted model increases in BMI (coefficient: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.44–1.76, P = 0.001), MUAC (coefficient: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.27–1.26, P = 0.002), hip circumference (coefficient: 2.24, 95% CI: 0.62–3.87, P = 0.007), and waist circumference (coefficient: 2.20, 95% CI: 0.74–3.66, P = 0.003) in the intervention group compared to the control group over the study period. However, improvements were observed in hemoglobin, WBC in the intervention group but these were not statistically significant (Table 4 ). Post-Hoc power analysis of according to primary outcome of hemoglobin At endline, the mean hemoglobin level in the intervention group was 11.40 g/dL; 95% CI: 11.16–11.64, and in the control group was 11.08 g/dL; 95% CI:10.81–11.36. The pooled standard deviation was 0.99, producing a Cohen’s d value of 0.32, indicating a small effect. A post-hoc power analysis performed using G*Power based on this effect size (0.32) and sample size ( n = 121) showed an estimated power of 42%, indicating that the study was likely underpowered to detect small effects (Supplementary materials 4). At baseline, 91.9% of the participants in the intervention group had mild anemia, whereas 8.1% had moderate anemia, compared to 88.1% with mild anemia and 11.9% with moderate anemia in the control group. By the midline assessment, 29% of participants in the intervention group and 35.6% in the control group recovered from anemia. However, by the endline assessment, the recovery rate in the intervention group was 25.8%, compared to 13.6% in the control group. This reflects a 12.2% higher recovery rate from anemia in the intervention group than in the control group (Fig. 3 ). At baseline, there was no significant difference between the intervention and control groups in terms of overall KAP, attitude, and practice; however, the knowledge score differed considerably ( p = 0.017). Following an 8-month intervention, the intervention group showed significant improvements in knowledge ( p = &lt; 0.001), attitude ( p = &lt; 0.001), practice ( p = &lt; 0.001), and overall KAP scores ( p = &lt; 0.001) compared to the control group (Table 5 ; Fig. 4 ). Using a GEE model, the intervention group showed a significant improvement in BMI (coefficient: 1.41, 95% CI: 0.30–2.52, p = 0.013), MUAC (coefficient: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.14–1.83, p = 0.022), hip circumference (coefficient: 3.19, 95% CI: 0.43–5.94, p = 0.024), knowledge (coefficient: 4.11, 95% CI: 3.62–4.59, p = &lt; 0.001), attitude (coefficient: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.31–1.98, p = &lt; 0.001), practice (coefficient: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.15–0.65, p = 0.001), and overall KAP (coefficient: 6.15, 95% CI: 5.38–6.93, p = &lt; 0.001). After adjusting the covariates, the intervention group showed a significant improvement in BMI (coefficient: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.01–2.20, p = 0.049), knowledge (coefficient: 4.11, 95% CI: 3.63–4.58, p = &lt; 0.001), attitude (coefficient: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.24–1.96, p = &lt; 0.001), practice (coefficient: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.13–0.66, p = 0.004), and overall KAP (coefficient: 6.10, 95% CI: 5.31–6.89, p = &lt; 0.001) compared to the control group. Moreover, improvements were observed in other outcomes, including hemoglobin in the intervention group and WBC, although these were not statistically significant (Table 6 ). Discussion To our knowledge, this is the first school-based trial to evaluate the impact of the mHealth nutrition education intervention among adolescent girls with anemia in rural Bangladesh to improve the hemoglobin level, reduce anemia, and increase KAP and related outcomes. The mHealth education intervention demonstrated a small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.32) in improving hemoglobin levels compared to the control group; however, this effect was not statistically significant. A post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study was underpowered to detect a meaningful difference, suggesting that the sample size may have been inadequate to confirm a clinically relevant effect of the intervention. Furthermore, the interpretation of these findings should account for potential unmeasured confounders, such as dietary factors, menstrual history, and parasitic infections, which can independently influence hemoglobin levels and may have obscured the true impact of mHealth education. For instance, variations in iron intake or menstrual blood loss could contribute to fluctuations in hemoglobin concentrations, whereas parasitic infections are common causes of anemia in similar contexts. Future research should incorporate these biological and behavioral factors to provide a clearer understanding of the effectiveness of the intervention. Increasing the sample size and controlling for confounders would enhance evidence regarding the clinical significance of mHealth education for anemia reduction. However, studies from developed and developing nations have revealed that nutritional education including mHealth-based instruction on dietary plans with iron-containing foods contributes to increased hemoglobin levels among adolescent girls [ 42 – 44 ]. Similar interventions among pregnant women in other low- and middle-income countries have shown positive effects. For instance, a cluster RCT in Nepal evidenced that a mobile phone-based text messaging intervention significantly improved gestational weights and hemoglobin levels among pregnant women. The Nepalese intervention utilized weekly SMS messages to provide education on iron-rich diets and antenatal care information, resulting in increased hemoglobin [ 45 ]. Similarly, a study in Ethiopia showed that mHealth interventions, particularly those involving SMS reminders and nutritional counseling, increased compliance with iron supplementation and dietary intake, ultimately improving hemoglobin levels in pregnant women [ 46 ]. However, the effectiveness of mHealth interventions is not universal. A study conducted in Indonesia among adolescents found that although mHealth and ePoster education improved knowledge and nutritional intake, there was no significant effect on hemoglobin levels [ 47 ]. The authors attributed this to other underlying factors such as food insecurity and access to healthcare services, which were not addressed by the SMS intervention. This highlights the importance of considering the broader socio-economic context when designing mHealth interventions, as barriers such as poverty and food availability may limit their effectiveness. To address these barriers, future mHealth interventions should integrate food security support, healthcare resource access, partnerships with local service providers, and real-time nutrition and access guidance to address food insecurity and limited healthcare access. However, this intervention was useful, given that the participants in the intervention group recovered from anemia by 25.8% compared with 13.6% in the control group. This intervention entailed online group counseling sessions with participants and guardians, which were performed by public health physicians through health campaigns, while CHWs provided health education using mHealth technology. Additionally, mHealth education on iron-containing foods and healthy living choices may have contributed to the increase in hemoglobin levels observed in this study. There are several natural sources of iron-containing foods in rural Bangladesh; however, because of illiteracy, they are unknown [ 48 ]. Health education, combined with the implementation of a healthy dietary plan, may serve as a promising strategy for increasing awareness and identifying these dietary sources [ 24 ]. Targeted nutritional interventions for adolescent girls focus on several dietary options, including the increased consumption of iron-rich foods and diets that promote iron absorption and ultimately prevent anemia. Research from Bangladesh [ 49 ] and its surrounding countries, India [ 50 ], Pakistan [ 51 ], and Nepal [ 52 ] shows that iron supplements alone are sufficient to alleviate anemia. However, our findings were consistent with those of similar studies conducted in impoverished countries, which revealed that dietary health education interventions were effective in reducing anemia among participants. Furthermore, educating adolescents about the complications and consequences of anemia and the health benefits of iron intake may motivate them to adhere to the dietary guidelines. This study aimed to educate adolescent girls and their guardians on health issues to improve their dietary choices. The guardians, who play a key role in food preparation and decision-making, received detailed instructions on incorporating iron-rich foods along with meal ideas [ 36 ]. Health education provided essential insights to guardians, guiding them on the appropriate nutrition for their daughters and recommending the optimal frequency of servings to prevent anemia. Along with these physiological improvements, the intervention also led to significant changes in the KAP, which are key for sustaining long-term health benefits. Our findings showed that adolescent girls in the intervention group had significantly higher total KAP scores than those in the control group. Individual comparisons of knowledge, attitudes, and practices also revealed substantial differences across groups. Phone calls and SMS text messaging, along with a health education notebook, can encourage adolescent girls to seek information about nutritious foods and healthy lifestyles, which may lead to behavioral changes and increase their motivation, knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Several studies have revealed that mHealth and physical health education are beneficial for improving KAP scores for anemia among adolescent girls [ 21 , 42 , 53 , 54 ]. Currently, the mHealth technology is a promising tool for public health education to prevent and control anemia in adolescents. However, increasing knowledge about anemia leads to changes in attitudes, such as perceived severity, social stigma, and health-seeking behaviors. Moreover, this heightened awareness leads to the increased adoption of iron-rich meals, improved hygiene practices, and a healthy lifestyle among adolescents, resulting in combatting anemia. Finally increased KAP scores can have a positive impact on participant’s long-term benefits in their quality of life and overall health. The CHWs have demonstrated their efficiency in delivering health education via mHealth in rural areas of developing countries, where the majority of the population lacks literacy skills [ 22 ]. Therefore, it would be advantageous to foster them in schools, especially in rural areas where there are either insufficient or no registered physicians or nurses. These improvements in knowledge and behavior likely contributed to the significant changes observed in the participants’ anthropometric indices. This study found that adolescent girls in the intervention group had significant improvements in anthropometric indices, such as BMI, MUAC, and hip and waist measurements, compared with the control group. The comprehensive strategy of public health physicians and CHW, which informed all participants about the long-term repercussions and risks of being underweight and malnutrition associated with anemia, would help them maintain a healthy weight. A similar conclusion was reached in India [ 55 ] and Ethiopia [ 56 ], where dietary education helped improve adolescent girls’ BMI and MUAC [ 57 ]. Moreover, malnutrition and being underweight are major risk factors, and nutritional deprivation has consistently been recognized as the primary cause of anemia, especially among adolescent girls in developing countries [ 9 ]. Malnutrition during adolescence is associated with delayed maturation and reduced muscle strength, leading to physical capacity constraints and subsequent decline in bone density later in life. Adopting a healthy lifestyle, combined with pertinent health education, could mitigate malnutrition and preserve healthy anthropometric indicators in school-going adolescents. Strength and limitations A notable aspect of this research was the innovative utilization of mobile phone technology to educate individuals on nutrition, aiming to increase hemoglobin levels and facilitate recovery from anemia. The results of these studies indicate that mHealth education can improve learning experience, inspire and boost self-assurance, and focus on preventing anemia. This study had some limitations. The first limitation of our study was the use of a simple coin-toss randomization approach for selecting control and intervention groups at the school level, which may have introduced bias. To mitigate the possibility of bias in health education within the same school, randomization was conducted at the school level. Although a mixed-effects model was used to account for potential intra-cluster correlation due to school-level clustering. However, the intra-cluster correlation (ICC) is not appropriately estimated and is statistically invalid, likely because only two schools were included, an insufficient number for reliable intra-cluster correlation estimation. As a result, the findings were interpreted in a manner similar to an individual-level analysis. This study randomized only two schools, which limits its generalizability and statistical power. Future research should incorporate multi-site studies with at least 4 to 6 clusters per group to facilitate valid cluster-level inferences. A larger number of clusters is necessary to validate and extend these findings across diverse contexts and to enable the appropriate modeling of ICC and school-level effects. Second, we measured hemoglobin levels, which prevented us from evaluating the differentiation of anemia types, including iron deficiency and nutritional, genetic, and infectious anemia. The decision to use hemoglobin levels was driven by practical constraints, including the primary focus on evaluating the impact of mHealth education on overall hemoglobin levels and resource limitations. Third, a key limitation of this study is the absence of a direct dietary intake assessment, such as a 24-hour dietary recall or a food frequency questionnaire, to account for confounding dietary factors. Instead, we assessed dietary knowledge and self-reported practices through the KAP survey, which provided insights into participants’ awareness and behavior related to iron-rich foods. Future studies should incorporate objective dietary intake assessments alongside KAP evaluations to better understand the impact of dietary changes on hemoglobin outcomes. Furthermore, the presence of unmeasured confounders, such as dietary habits, menstrual history, and parasitic infections, which can independently affect hemoglobin levels, may have obscured the true impact of mHealth education. Fourth, this study lacked blinding due to the nature of the mHealth educational intervention. Participants and CHWs were aware of group assignments, which may have introduced performance and observer bias. Although we aimed for objective assessments, the lack of blinding among participants, and CHWs could have influenced the results. To minimize these biases, we trained data collectors to ensure consistent and objective data collection using standardized protocols. Additionally, we utilized an automated hematology analyzer for key outcomes, such as hemoglobin levels, to objectively assess anemia. Despite these precautions, we recognize that completely eliminating bias in field-based studies is challenging, and future research should aim to fully separate intervention delivery from outcome assessment to enhance methodological rigor. Fifth, the study’s sample size was a narrow population, it may not be generalizable to other populations, such as urban adolescent girls or boys. Moreover, our research was confined to a single subdistrict in rural Bangladesh, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to broader contexts. The findings can be generalized to broader rural contexts because the selected subdistrict for this research has many sociodemographic and health-related aspects similar to the other rural areas across the country, such as income level, education attainment, healthcare facilities, dietary habits, similar cultural attitudes toward health, and cultural practices. Public health implications This study shows the potential impact of using mHealth education to address anemia with knowledge, attitude, and practice among adolescent girls in low-resource settings. Given the positive outcome of the intervention, there is a significant opportunity to scale up mHealth education through integration with school health programs and addressing barriers like limited digital literacy and low-bandwidth communication methods at the national level in Bangladesh. The use of SMS and phone calls enhances accessibility, particularly for individuals in remote areas with limited internet access, making this intervention feasible in rural settings. To achieve national implementation, collaboration with non-governmental organizations and government health programs is crucial. Integrating mHealth education into existing public health strategies, such as nutrition initiatives and adolescent health programs, could boost efforts to reduce anemia rates across the country. However, successful implementation would require overcoming potential challenges, including resource allocation, securing funding for infrastructure and human resources, and integrating the intervention into existing health systems. The intervention was designed to address local health challenges, but its core components mHealth technology, health education, and CHWs engagement can be adapted for similar low-resource settings globally. Additionally, the intervention’s minimal infrastructure requirements, cost-effectiveness, and adaptability to diverse geographic and socioeconomic settings highlight its potential for broader implementation, particularly among adolescents, reproductive-age women, and pregnant women, ensuring long-term sustainability and effectiveness in reducing anemia and other nutritional deficiencies at scale. Conclusions Our study concluded that nutritional education provided by public health physicians and CHWs using mHealth technology showed an association in improving KAP scores among adolescent girls. In addition, there was a potential impact on BMI, MUAC, and hip and waist circumference measurements. However, this intervention also showed a promising trend toward improvement in hemoglobin levels and anemia recovery. These outcomes were influenced by various factors including nutritional knowledge, healthy lifestyle, behavioral change, anemia prevention awareness, and levels of engagement. However, although the study outcomes are encouraging, it is important to approach the findings with a nuanced aspect. Our study finding revealed a significant outcome in a specific area; however, future research with larger sample sizes across diverse communities is needed to validate these effects and assess the long-term sustainability of the improvements. Incorporating nutritional mHealth education into current public health strategies, such as nutrition initiatives and adolescent health programs, can enhance efforts to reduce anemia rates nationwide. This study recommends adopting mHealth nutrition policies in school settings for adolescents and encourages the implementation of nutritional interventions to prevent and control anemia at the national level in Bangladesh and other low-middle-income countries, especially in rural settings. Additionally, before national adoption, further large-scale trials are needed to confirm the findings and ensure the effectiveness of the intervention on a broader scale. However, to scale the mHealth intervention at a national level, it could be integrated into existing government health programs and implemented in collaboration with NGOs. Moreover, its successful implementation will require overcoming key challenges, including securing infrastructure support and ensuring adequate resource allocation.
