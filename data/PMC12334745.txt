Abstract Adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) is critical for HIV treatment success, yet the impact of tuberculosis preventive therapy (TPT) remains inadequately understood. Using observational data from 4152 HIV patients in Ethiopia (2005–2024), we applied causal inference methods, including Adjusted Logistic Regression, Propensity Score Matching, and Causal Forest Double Machine Learning (DML), to estimate TPT’s effect on ART adherence. The DML approach (leveraging Random Forests and orthogonalization) provided the most precise estimates after model comparison. We found TPT initiation reduced adherence probability by 3.14 percentage points on average (ATE = − 0.0314; 95% CI − 0.0373, − 0.0254; p &lt; 0.001). While most patients experienced negligible effects, substantial heterogeneity existed: individuals with advanced WHO stage, longer ART duration, higher BMI, or older age showed better adherence responses, whereas those with higher CD4 counts, functional impairment, or cotrimoxazole prophylaxis use faced greater risks. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent effects across clinical strata but greater variability among non-TPT initiators. These findings support personalized TPT deployment, prioritizing patients with advanced disease while monitoring vulnerable subgroups and highlighting the need for adherence support. Future research should validate results in multi-site cohorts using longitudinal and psychosocial data. Introduction The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) remains one of the largest global public health threats, particularly among low and middle-income countries (LMICs), where healthcare accessibility, resource limitations, and stigma still hamper prevention and treatment 1 – 3 . ART has revolutionized HIV treatment by turning HIV into a chronic, manageable disease 4 – 6 . However, the success of ART relies heavily on high and strict adherence 7 . ART adherence is essential to viral suppression, immune restoration, reduction of HIV-related morbidity and mortality, and prevention of drug-resistant strains of the virus 8 – 10 . Optimal rates of adherence remain a formidable challenge for HIV care despite the availability and effectiveness of ART 11 , 12 . Various studies have reported that a high proportion of patients are not adherent to their prescribed ART regimens. This non-adherence results from several factors, including but not limited to socioeconomic status, educational level, mental illness, drug use, stigma, side effects, pill burden, forgetfulness, and lack of social support 13 . Clinical factors, including the stage of the patient’s HIV, co-infections (e.g., tuberculosis), and the ART regimen prescribed, play an important role as well. Hence, there is a growing need for a greater understanding of the determinants underlying adherence to designing efficient and specific interventions 14 , 15 . One of them is TPT, which the World Health Organization (WHO) has promoted as an additive preventive intervention for PLHIV 16 , 17 . TPT has been proven to reduce the incidence of tuberculosis, a leading cause of death among people infected with HIV 16 , 18 . Although TPT is generally well tolerated and has clear public health benefits, treatment with TPT can have the potential to raise the treatment burden in the patient, and this may have a bearing on ART adherence 19 , 20 . Alternatively, patients receiving enhanced care alongside TPT may feel more supported and motivated, which could lead to improved adherence to ART 21 . Because of these conflicting possibilities, the net effect of TPT on ART adherence is not so straightforward and requires exact empirical investigation 22 , 23 . Previous studies investigating the impact of TPT on adherence to ART have traditionally employed standard statistical methods like logistic regression and the Cox proportional hazards model 24 – 26 . While these are well-worked-out and extremely widely used methods, they suffer from several disadvantages. They are typically restricted to assuming linear relationships, struggle with handling high-dimensional data, and are susceptible to model misspecification. Moreover, they do not work well in capturing complex interactions and non-linear associations that are common in actual healthcare data. Traditional methods also rely heavily on accurate model form specification, and their violation could lead to biased estimation and erroneous inferences 27 , 28 . Similarly, standard machine learning (ML) algorithms such as decision trees, support vector machines, and random forests, while incredibly powerful for predictive work, are not causality-effect-estimation models in themselves 29 – 32 . Such algorithms are optimized to maximize predictive accuracy and are not causality-concerned by design to determine cause-and-effect relationships 27 – 30 . They are therefore not concerned with confounding biases and cannot distinguish between correlation and causation 33 , 34 . Classical ML can, by itself, take advantage of spurious data relationships to overfit and provide incorrect policy recommendations when used to make estimates of treatment effects. Furthermore, their metrics of choice (e.g., AUC, accuracy) do not assess causal validity 35 – 37 . To better manage these limitations, methods of causal inference have come to be the core of health data science. Among these, causal machine learning (CML) represents a powerful and generalizable group of methods combining the power of causal inference with the predictive ability and flexibility of existing machine learning methods 38 . CML is highly appropriate for observational healthcare data in which randomized controlled trials are often impossible due to ethical, logistical, or cost considerations 39 – 41 . This study applies the DML method, a state-of-the-art CML model, to estimate the individualized causal treatment effects of TPT on ART adherence. This method allows both ATE and CATE estimation, thereby making it possible to identify subgroups that benefit more or less from the treatment 42 , 43 . The ability to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects is especially relevant in HIV care contexts, wherein patient response to treatment interventions can be expected to be extremely variable. This study aims to provide actionable insights into the effect of TPT on ART adherence based on real-world clinical evidence from a clinical cohort of HIV-positive patients. In doing so, it is intended to enable evidence-informed decision-making in clinical and public health settings to produce more personalized and effective HIV care strategies. This work also shows the practical validity of causal machine learning methods to tackle difficult health issues in real life and highlights the importance of their use in advancing precision medicine initiatives. Methodology Data source and study population This research employed the University of Gondar Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia’s electronic medical record system (EMR-ART). Routine secondary data on PLHIV-initiated ART at the hospital between March 2005 and December 2024 were employed. The source population was all patients who were HIV-positive and attended the hospital’s ART clinic between March 2005 and December 2024. Inclusion factors were: age ≥ 15 years at the moment of starting ART, confirmed HIV-positive diagnosis, and full baseline clinical and laboratory information at the start of treatment. The patients were excluded if they had missing data at baseline, were referred from another facility with an incomplete clinical record, or were lost to follow-up in the first month after starting ART. After the application of these, 4152 patients were transferred to the final analytic cohort in total. Follow-up was continued up to the date of death, loss to follow-up, or administrative study clo. Variable definitions For this causal analysis, the variables were assigned to three basic roles: treatment, outcome, and covariates. These were selected based on clinical significance, theoretical justification, and availability in the electronic medical record system. Treatment variable TPT Started: The treatment variable of interest was TPT initiation, indicating whether or not a patient had been prescribed TPT as part of their HIV care. It was coded as:1 = Yes (TPT initiated) and 0 = No (TPT not initiated). TPT initiation is as per physician orders recorded in the ART clinic database and reflects the exposure of the patient to preventive TB treatment during the study. Outcome variable ART Adherence: The outcome variable of the present study was adherence to antiretroviral therapy. It was operationalized as a binary variable derived from adherence data routinely collected during clinical follow-up visits = Good adherence (according to ≥ 95% of doses taken or clinicians’ “good” marking), and 0 = Poor adherence (fair, poor, or missed medication included). This variable measures the patient’s ability to sustain adherence to the prescribed ART regimen over time and is a proxy for treatment success and the likelihood of viral suppression in the long term. Covariates (potential confounders) The following covariates were included in the analysis as potential confounders that may influence both TPT initiation and ART adherence. These variables were selected based on clinical practice and the results of prior research on HIV treatment processes: Demographic characteristics Age: Continuous variable in years at ART initiation. Sex: Male or Female. Marital status: Categorical variable (Single, Married, Divorced, Widowed). Education level: Ordinal variable for the highest level of education completed. Residence: Urban or Rural. Religion: Self-reported religious affiliation. Clinical characteristics WHO clinical stage: Categorical variable (Stages I–IV) based on WHO guidelines at baseline. Duration on ART: Measured in months from ART start to final follow-up. Functional Status: Working, Ambulatory, or Bedridden, indicating patient capacity at baseline. BMI (body mass index): Calculated from documented weight and height measurements. Immunological and Laboratory Data: Baseline CD4 Count: Measured in cells/µL at ART start. Recent CD4 Count: Most recent CD4 result on follow-up. Treatment-related variables Regimen line: Categorical variable for whether the patient was on a first-line or second-line ART regimen. CPT use: Binary indicator for whether Cotrimoxazole Preventive Therapy was initiated (1 = Yes, 0 = No). These covariates were included to adjust for potential selection bias in TPT initiation and to be in a position to estimate unbiased treatment effects using causal machine learning methods. The broad span of the covariates across sociodemographic, clinical, immunological, and treatment categories ensures a very wide control for observable confounding in the estimation of the causal effect of TPT on ART adherence. Analytical approach To estimate the causal effect of tuberculosis preventive therapy (TPT) initiation on patient adherence, we implemented and compared three causal inference models: Adjusted Logistic Regression, Propensity Score Matching, and Causal Forest Double Machine Learning (DML). Each model was applied to the same dataset, and the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) was estimated along with its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). The comparison focused on the precision and reliability of the estimated effects, as reflected in the width of the confidence intervals. This multi-model approach allowed us to assess the robustness of the findings and identify the most suitable method for both average and potentially heterogeneous treatment effect estimation. The first step in any causal inference analysis is the explicit definition of the hypothesized causal relationships between variables. This is generally defined in terms of a causal graph, or Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), that visually defines the relationship between treatment, outcome, and confounders 44 . In this work, a causal graph was constructed to guide the selection of covariates to control for in the estimation of the effect of TPT on adherence to ART (Fig. 1 ). A causal graph has various applications. One, it makes it possible to systematize the integration of knowledge in a given field, primarily from clinicians, into modeling. By identifying which variables are likely to causally affect treatment assignment and outcome, we can determine the minimum sufficient adjustment set required to block backdoor paths and obtain an unbiased estimate of treatment effect. This is especially crucial in observational studies since treatment is not randomly received. Second, the graph facilitates the explanation of assumptions and avoidance of standard pitfalls such as conditioning on colliders, control for mediators, or omission of informative confounders. The graph used in this study was developed in collaboration with domain experts from the clinical field and represented well-documented relationships in HIV care, such as the impact of WHO clinical stage, CD4 count, and duration of ART on the chance of receiving TPT and taking medication. Our graph had demographic, clinical, immunological, and treatment-related variables as confounders. Arrows were drawn from the covariates to both treatment (initiation of TPT) and outcome (ART adherence), indicating their assumed causal effect. An arrow was also drawn from TPT to ART adherence, representing the causal effect that we wished to estimate. This graph provided a free and interpretable framework to explain our variable selection for causal modeling with the DML method. Upon confirmation of the covariate adjustment set, we performed thorough data preprocessing. This included label encoding of categorical features and missing value processing by appropriate imputation or exclusion. This method relies on several key assumptions, including unconfoundedness, positivity, and Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) 45 . We adjusted for a comprehensive set of demographic and clinical covariates to satisfy the ignorability condition, though unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Positivity was assessed by examining the distribution of treatment probabilities. The treatment (TPT initiation) was consistently coded across patients, and we assume no interference between individuals (SUTVA) (Table 1 ). Finally, the DML framework’s use of non-parametric models and orthogonalization ensures robustness to model misspecification and correlated residuals. Lastly, we split the dataset into training and test subsets in an 80/20 ratio to allow for genuine estimation, an important double machine learning requirement. The Causal Forest DML model was trained on single-base learners: A Random Forest Regressor to train the outcome as a function of the covariates, and a Random Forest Classifier to train the treatment assignment as a function of the covariates. These were trained separately, and their residuals were orthogonalized, i.e., the treatment and outcome models were residualized against the covariates, to satisfy the Neyman orthogonality condition. This is important to reduce the sensitivity of the final estimator of the treatment effect to the nuisance model errors. To ensure robust model performance and prevent overfitting, hyperparameters for the Random Forest base learners were optimized using cross-validation on the training set. Key parameters such as the number of trees, maximum tree depth, and minimum samples per leaf were selected based on minimizing out-of-sample prediction error. For enhanced interpretability, we supplemented feature importance analysis with SHAP value estimation, allowing us to visualize and quantify the marginal effect of each covariate on the individualized treatment effect predictions. This approach provided a more nuanced understanding of how specific patient characteristics modulate the impact of TPT on ART adherence. After fitting the models, we estimated the ATE for the overall causal effect of TPT on ART adherence in the population, and the CATEs to adjust for individualized treatment effects in different patient profiles. Additionally, this causal estimation pipeline combines domain knowledge, causal inference, and modern machine learning to glean interpretable and tailored causal estimates from real-world clinical data. With these methods, we aimed to create long-lasting and actionable knowledge of the effect of TPT on ART adherence for PLHIV. Mathematical modeling of the causal forest DML To formally describe the estimation framework used in this study, we present the mathematical formulation of the DML approach. The objective is to estimate the causal effect of TPT on adherence to ART, adjusting for observed covariates using modern causal inference techniques. Results Descriptive statistics Key demographic characteristics of the 4152 participants showed a predominantly female (59.5%), married (65.0%), and urban (76.5%) population. Most attained secondary education (51.7%), and Orthodox Christianity was the near-universal religion (94.2%) (Table 2 ). Key clinical characteristics revealed excellent viral suppression (96.5% combined suppressed/undetectable), high adherence (87.0% good), and predominantly functional patients (93.0% working). Most patients had normal BMI (62.8%), were on first-line ART (91.8%), and used CPT (79.8%), though TPT initiation was lower (71.2%). Notably, 15.2% were underweight (Table 3 ). Model comparison results After conducting a comparative analysis of three causal inference methods, Adjusted Logistic Regression, Propensity Score Matching, and Causal Forest DML, the results indicate that Causal Forest DML is the most robust and reliable model (Table 4 ). While all methods estimated a negative Average Treatment Effect (ATE), suggesting that TPT initiation is associated with reduced adherence, the Causal Forest DML model produced the narrowest confidence interval (ATE = − 0.0314, 95% CI [− 0.0373, − 0.0254]), indicating high precision and statistical significance. In contrast, Adjusted Logistic Regression and Propensity Score Matching yielded wider intervals, limiting their inferential strength. Moreover, Causal Forest DML is uniquely suited for exploring heterogeneous treatment effects, enabling deeper insights into how treatment impacts vary across patient subgroups, an essential capability for personalized healthcare interventions. Based on both statistical rigor and analytical flexibility, Causal Forest DML is selected as the preferred model for this study. Assumption checking results As an initial step in the diagnostic process, we checked the balance of treatment assignment by estimating the proportion of individuals who had received TPT and those who had not received it. The check showed that approximately 71.2% of patients had initiated TPT and 28.8% had not, which indicates a moderate imbalance in treatment group sizes. To address causal effect estimation, we therefore examined the overlap and positivity assumptions by estimating and plotting the propensity score distribution. The resulting histogram indicated that there was little overlap between the untreated and treated groups, particularly in the range of propensity score extremes (Fig. 2 ). This lack of shared support means that certain covariate profiles are very strongly associated with treatment or control, which can lead to biased effect estimates. To adjust for this, we used a trimming strategy by excluding observations with extreme propensity score values (&lt; 0.20 or &gt; 0.80), around 594 datas are removed, restricting the analysis to regions where there was sufficient overlap between treatment groups (Fig. 3 ). This approach strengthens the validity of our causal effects by targeting the same populations for whom both treatment assignments are plausible. Estimated treatment effects of TPT on adherence The ATE (causal risk difference) of TPT on adherence was − 0.0314(95% CI − 0.0373 to − 0.0254, p &lt; 0.001), equivalent to a 3.14% decrease in the probability of good adherence relative to non-recipients. While the overall effect is small and negative, one has to ask if this means conceals variation between people. To analyze such heterogeneity, Fig. 4 presents the estimated CATE. The CATE pattern is skewed towards zero, and most individual-level effects cluster around zero, with fewer individuals having large positive or negative effects. This suggests that the impact of TPT on adherence varies across individuals, but for most, it is small. The symmetrical design of the distribution also suggests that while some may benefit from TPT in conformity, others may lose, further highlighting the potential role of subgroup or individual analyses. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore effect modification by key demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, ART regimen line, and baseline CD4 count. These analyses were pre-specified based on clinical relevance. Formal statistical tests for interaction were performed by including interaction terms in the Causal Forest model, revealing that the effect of TPT on ART adherence was substantially modified by ART duration and WHO clinical stage ( p &lt; 0.05 for interaction). Heterogeneous effects TPT’s heterogeneous treatment effects on adherence are stratified by whether or not individuals initiated TPT. The box plots compare the distribution of estimated treatment effects between two groups: individuals who did not initiate TPT (TPT = 0) and individuals who initiated it (TPT = 1). In both panels, the median effect of treatment is near zero, suggesting that the middle of the effect of TPT on compliance is low regardless of whether or not they are treated. However, heterogeneity of treatment effects differs between the groups (Fig. 5 ). Among non-initiators of TPT, the interquartile range is wider, about − 0.1 to 0.1, and holds more extreme outliers, indicating greater heterogeneity in the manner in which these individuals would have responded to TPT had they initiated. In contrast, the treatment group that initiated TPT has a narrower interquartile range, about − 0.05 to 0.05, with fewer extremes, indicating greater homogeneity of the treatment effect. These findings emphasize the presence of individual-level variability in TPT, particularly in non-treatment initiators, and indicate the possible value of individualized or subgroup-based approaches to the assessment and implementation of TPT interventions. The estimated treatment effects of TPT on adherence by WHO clinical stage. The box plots show that the distribution of effects is slightly different by stage but that the median effect is close to zero in all groups (Fig. 6 ). This suggests that the effect of TPT on adherence is comparable across WHO stages and indicates little heterogeneity in response to treatment by severity of disease. The estimated treatment effects of TPT on adherence by CD4 cell count at baseline. The box plots show that median treatment effects are close to zero in all CD4 groups (&lt; 200, 200–350, 350–500, and 500 + cells/μL) with some heterogeneity in spread and outliers (Fig. 7 ). This suggests that the impact of TPT on adherence is not substantially different by immune status at baseline. Feature importance The most prominent predictors of the estimated treatment effect of TPT on adherence are presented in Fig. 8 . Age, BMI, time on ART, and WHO stage were affirmative predictors of the treatment effect and suggest that advanced clinical profiles or longer treatment duration would benefit more from TPT through adherence. Conversely, CD4 count squared, functional status, and use of CPT made a negative contribution, implying that perhaps there are subgroups to which TPT might be less beneficial or even harmful (Fig. 8 ). These findings point to the importance of examining individual-level variables in evaluating the efficacy of TPT. Discussion This study applied a DML approach to estimate the causal effect of TPT on adherence to ART among PLHIV. The key finding was ATE of TPT on ART adherence was − 0.0314, indicating a small average decrease in adherence among those who received TPT. Yet, this average effect masked substantial heterogeneity in individuals’ responses, as revealed by the CATE. Most of the individual-level effects were concentrated around zero, while some patients experienced positive or negative deviations. Subgroup analyses by TPT initiation status, WHO clinical stage, and CD4 count strata also showed that the treatment effect was generally consistent across clinical strata, though with more variability among non-TPT initiators. Feature importance analysis also identified WHO stage, ART duration, BMI, and age as positive contributors to treatment effect, and CD4 count squared, functional status, and CPT use as negative contributors. The modestly negative ATE suggests that, on average, TPT may have a small negative effect on ART adherence. This could be due to pill burden, side effects, or treatment fatigue from the addition of TPT to an already onerous ART regimen. However, the near-zero median CATE and the symmetric shape of the distribution of covariate-specific causal treatment effect suggest that for most patients, TPT does not substantially enhance or worsen adherence. The greater heterogeneity of treatment effects among TPT non-initiators suggests that there are some in this group who would have reacted differently if they were randomized to the intervention, highlighting how counterfactual outcomes matter. Our binary adherence measure may obscure nuanced behaviors. Future studies should use continuous metrics (e.g., pharmacy refill adherence) to mitigate attenuation bias. The importance of features provides further insight into which patient factors influence the treatment effect. For instance, patients with more advanced WHO stages or longer ART duration may be more accustomed to complex treatment regimens and thus more able to accommodate TPT without compromising adherence. Patients with poorer functional status or higher CD4 counts, however, may experience less immediate TPT benefit and hence have less motivation to adhere. The findings from this study are in line with and extend current evidence on the relationship between TPT and ART adherence 46 . Current studies using standard statistical models have had mixed results, some showing that TPT improves adherence through increased care and support, and others cautioning about treatment fatigue and reduced adherence due to additional pill burden 45 , 46 . These studies, however, often used linear models and did not account for treatment effect heterogeneity. In contrast, the current research utilized a causal machine learning framework, enabling flexible modeling of non-linear relationships and interactions. This approach offered a more nuanced understanding of how TPT influences adherence across diverse patient profiles. That both positive and negative effect contributors to treatment effects were found is consistent with the personalized medicine literature, which emphasizes the need to adapt interventions to individual-level characteristics rather than exclusively average effects. This study demonstrates the applicability of Causal Forest DML to health services research, and more generally, to the estimation of interventions in observational data when randomized controlled trials are not feasible. Compared to default regression or traditional machine learning specifications, the DML method explicitly addresses confounding, model misspecification, and heterogeneity of treatment effects 47 , 48 . The use of orthogonalization and non-parametric learners renders the estimates robust to complex data structures and enhances the validity of causal estimates 49 , 50 . Moreover, the use of feature importance analysis under the causal framework allows researchers to move beyond overall average effects and examine the determinants of treatment effect heterogeneity. This is particularly applicable to HIV care, where populations are heterogeneous and treatment responses are highly individualized. The ability to identify subgroups that will benefit more or less from TPT can inform targeted interventions and resource allocation. From a clinical perspective, the findings from this study suggest that TPT can be safely implemented in HIV care without compromising ART adherence in the majority of patients. However, the small negative average effect and the presence of variability at an individual level both underscore the need for individualized care approaches. Clinicians would need to consider patient-level factors such as functional status, ART duration, and clinical stage when deciding to initiate TPT. At the level of public health, these results support the continued use of TPT as a tuberculosis prevention strategy among PLHIV, yet call for monitoring and support for adherence. Programs need to be put in place to identify patients at risk of poor adherence due to TPT and provide individual counseling or adherence support. Finally, this study highlights the importance of data-driven decision-making in HIV care. By leveraging real-world clinical data and advanced causal inference methods, health systems can generate actionable evidence that improves patient outcomes and optimizes the distribution of limited resources. Conclusion This study investigated the causal impact of TPT on adherence to ART among PLHIV using a DML approach. The findings reported a tiny mean negative impact of TPT on ART adherence with a point estimate of ATE = − 0.0314. However, this means concealed a great deal of heterogeneity of response at the individual level was concealed, in that most patients had small changes in adherence, while some had greater positive or negative effects. Subgroup analyses by status of TPT initiation, WHO clinical stage, and CD4 count category revealed that the treatment effect was generally fairly consistent across clinical profiles but with more heterogeneity in those who did not initiate TPT. Feature importance analysis again ranked that clinical and demographic variables such as WHO stage, duration of ART, BMI, and age positively affected treatment effect, while CD4 count squared, functional status, and use of CPT had negative effects. Such findings underscore the importance of transcending average treatment effects to realize individual-level responses, particularly in complex and heterogeneous populations like PLHIV. Using causal machine learning approaches enabled more precise and comprehensive estimation of treatment effects, yielding informative input for both public health policy and clinical practice decision-making. In conclusion, although TPT remains a valuable intervention for HIV-positive groups’ prevention of tuberculosis, its application should be accompanied by scrutiny of ART adherence, especially in high-risk subgroups vulnerable to reduced adherence. Individualized care practices and evidence-based interventions are required to maximize the benefit of TPT while reducing unintended impacts on ART adherence. Limitations of the study Despite the strength of this study, limitations must be recognized. First, the analysis made use of observational data from one clinical facility, which may limit extrapolation to broader populations or different healthcare environments. Although the DML approach minimizes confounding, residual confounding by unmeasured variables, such as patient motivation, clinician behavior, or psychosocial support, remains impossible to eliminate. While DML minimizes parametric assumptions, it remains susceptible to unmeasured confounding (e.g., psychosocial factors) and covariate omission. Future work should incorporate sensitivity analyses (e.g., E-values) to quantify residual bias. Second, longitudinal trajectories of adherence and time-varying effects of TPT were not studied, which would yield a more nuanced understanding of how adherence varies over time. Third, the use of a binary adherence endpoint could be too reductionist to describe the richness in adherence behavior and could overlook important variations in intensity or consistency of adherence. Last but not least, our study was conducted at a single tertiary care center in Ethiopia, and the findings may not be fully generalizable to other settings with different patient populations, healthcare systems, or TPT implementation strategies. Future work Based on the findings of this study, some potential avenues for future research are offered. First, future studies would benefit from using multi-site or nationally representative datasets to maximize the external validity of results across different types of healthcare settings and populations. Extending the geographic and institutional base would help to provide evidence to corroborate the observed treatment effects and to determine contextual influences that would impact adherence. Second, longitudinal patterns of adherence need to be investigated over time with the use of time series or survival analysis. Such methods are capable of further illuminating how patterns of adherence evolve after receiving TPT, both during and after the therapy. Third, additional studies need to have more accurate measures of adherence, including pharmacy refill records, electronic medication monitoring systems, or valid self-report measures of adherence. These measures would allow for a more nuanced measurement of adherence behavior beyond binary classification and could be employed to identify important thresholds for intervention. Fourth, the incorporation of psychosocial and behavioral measures into causal models may improve the explanatory capability of treatment effect heterogeneity. Such variables, while often unobserved in standard clinical data, are known to have a powerful influence on adherence outcomes. Finally, future research could explore adaptive treatment strategies using reinforcement learning or dynamic treatment regimens to personalize TPT delivery based on real-time patient data. Such interventions would align with the goals of precision medicine and perhaps best optimize outcomes in different patient subgroups.
