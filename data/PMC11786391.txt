Background This study aimed to compare the response to growth hormone (GH) therapy according to the presence of GH deficiency (GHD) in short-stature children born small for gestational age (SGA) in Korea and to present appropriate GH dose criteria. Methods We evaluated 27 children born SGA with short stature and GHD (GHD group) and 23 without GHD (non-GHD group) registered in the LG Growth Study. Growth responses and changes in GH dose over a 2-year GH therapy period were compared, and the factors affecting growth response were investigated. Results The standard deviation scores (SDSs) for baseline weight and body mass index (BMI) were significantly lower in boys without GHD than in boys with GHD. The SDS for insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) was lower among boys without GHD than among boys with GHD, while the SDS for insulin-like growth factor-binding protein-3 (IGFBP-3) was higher among girls without GHD than among girls with GHD; however, there was no significant difference when comparing all children with GHD to those without GHD. Regardless of the presence of GHD, the difference between chronological age and bone age decreased annually. Notably, there was significantly rapid bone age progression among patients without GHD. The findings showed differences in GH dose according to GHD starting from the 2nd year of therapy, with the non-GHD group receiving a significantly higher dose. Regarding the factors affecting growth response, younger age and bone age, higher height SDS, BMI SDS and MPH SDS were related to higher growth response (Δheight SDS and Δgrowth velocity), but there was no statistically significant correlation. Conclusion GHD is rare among children born SGA. Nonetheless, if there are any signs of decreased growth velocity or hypopituitarism, the presence of GHD should be assessed before GH therapy, and personalized therapy based on the results is required. The indications for GH therapy include GH deficiency (GHD), children small for gestational age (SGA) without catch-up growth, chromosomal abnormalities, and chronic renal failure [ 1 – 5 ]. SGA is defined as a weight and/or length less than − 2 standard deviation score (SDS) for the gestational age, compared to a gender-specific reference population. However, by the health insurance system in Korea, SGA is defined as a birth weight or height below the 3rd percentile at each gestational age. Children who fail to achieve catch-up growth by the age of 4 years are eligible for GH therapy, regardless of GHD [ 6 ]. While classic GHD in children born SGA is rare, there are differences in expected height gain, GH dose, and medical expenses between children born SGA and those with GHD [ 4 ]. Children born SGA have an increased risk of endocrine disorders, such as obesity and sexual precocity, and those on GH therapy have a tendency for increased bone age relative to chronological age [ 4 , 7 ]. There has been reported that in children born SGA with growth retardation, GH secretion is lower than in children born appropriate for gestational age [ 8 ]. In contrast, children with GHD characteristically have a decrease in growth velocity and lower bone age relative to chronological age; hence, they have the most favorable height gain during GH therapy [ 9 , 10 ]. Based on the current Korean insurance standards, the therapeutic dose of GH is 0.245–0.490 mg/kg/week (35–70 µg/kg/day) for children born SGA and 0.165–0.234 mg/kg/week (23.6–33.5 µg/kg/day) for children with GHD; hence, the therapeutic dose administered to children born SGA can be up to twice as high as that in children with GHD [ 6 ]. Therefore, we speculated that children born SGA would show differences in the appropriate GH dose and therapy response depending on the presence or absence of GHD. Hence, this study aimed to compare the GH therapy response according to the presence of GHD in children born SGA with short-stature in Korea and present appropriate GH dose criteria. Materials and methods Study population The LG Growth Study (LGS) is a multicenter (97 sites), observational study that analyzed the long-term efficacy and safety of GH (Eutropin injection, Eutropin AQ injection, Eutropin Pen injection, and Eutropin Plus injection, Eutropin S Pen injection; LG Chem, Ltd., Korea) in Korean children receiving GH therapy (ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT01604395 ) [ 11 , 12 ]. Among the patients registered in the LGS between 2001 and 2021, the clinical data of 773 prepubertal children born SGA who received GH therapy were reviewed. Among them, GH stimulation tests were performed in 383 patients. The SGA diagnosis was based on birth weight below the 3rd percentile at each gestational age [ 4 , 6 ]. The diagnosis of GHD was based on delayed bone age relative to chronological age and peak GH levels of less than 10 ng/mL in at least two GH stimulation tests (stimuli: arginine, clonidine, dopamine, and insulin) [ 13 – 15 ]. The patients diagnosed with GHD were those diagnosed with the isolated GHD or GHD with multiple pituitary deficiencies. To eliminate the influence of puberty, only boys aged between 2 and 11 years and girls aged between 2 and 10 years who were in a prepubertal state were selected. Prepubertal status was defined as no breast development in girls and testicular volume less than 4 mL in boys according to the Tanner and Marshall criteria [ 16 ]. Moreover, the exclusion criteria were patients with: insufficient data; incomplete treatment (recombinant human growth hormone therapy for &lt; 2 years); or medical conditions such as pituitary or hypothalamic lesions (structural abnormalities), chromosomal anomalies, chronic disease, and endocrinologic or nutritional abnormalities (such as diabetes mellitus and hypercalcemia, not abnormalities of pituitary hormones). Ultimately, only 50 of the 773 patients were included in the study population: 27 children born SGA with GHD and short-stature (GHD group) and 23 children born SGA with short-stature but without GHD (non-GHD group) (Fig. 1 ). Auxological and clinical data Auxological and clinical data were obtained from the LGS database. The patients’ sex, chronological age, bone age, gestational age, birthweight, height, weight, pubertal status, parental height, GH response to GH stimulation tests, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), insulin-like growth factor-binding protein-3 (IGFBP-3), and GH dose were determined from their medical records. Annual changes in bone age, height, weight, IGF-1, IGFBP-3, and GH dose were assessed every 12 months for 2 years. The SDSs for height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and mid-parental height (MPH) were calculated using LMS parameters (Lambda for skew, Mu for median, and Sigma) in the 2017 Korean national growth charts for children and adolescents [ 17 ]. The SDS for MPH was derived by calculating male MPH = [paternal height + maternal height + 13]/2 and female MPH = [paternal height + maternal height − 13]/2 and converting the value to SDS. Bone age was calculated by the treating physician using the Greulich-Pyle method [ 18 ]. All laboratory analyses were performed at each participating center (97 sites). For the SDSs of IGF-1 and IGFBP-3, normal IGF-1 and IGFBP-3 levels in Korean children were referenced [ 19 ]. Statistical analysis Data are presented as means ± standard deviations or medians (interquartile ranges). Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality. If the Shapiro-Wilk test shows that the data was not normally distributed, the data distribution was presented as median (Interquartile ranges) and non-parametric methods were used (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Spearman’s correlation). The independent t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used to compare auxological and biochemical data between the GHD and non-GHD groups. Pearson’s correlation or Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to identify factors associated with growth response. Multivariate linear regression was used to identify the growth response according to the GH dosage. A two-sided P-value of &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Results Comparison of baseline characteristics The characteristics of the 50 children diagnosed with GHD or non-GHD among children born SGA with short-stature are shown in Table 1 . There was no significant difference between GHD group and non-GHD group for gestational age and birth weightage, bone age, and for age and bone age at GH therapy onset. The SDSs for weight, BMI, and IGF-1 were significantly lower among boys in the non-GHD group than among boys in the GHD group, while the SDS for IGFBP-3 was higher among girls in the non-GHD group than among girls in the GHD group. However, comparison of all children with and without GHD showed no significant differences. Moreover, there were no differences in the initial GH dose, regardless of the presence or absence of GHD. Growth responses after GH therapy and changes in GH dose Growth responses after GH therapy and changes in GH dose are shown in Table 2 ; Fig. 2 . The findings showed that the difference between chronological age and bone age (CA-BA) decreased, regardless of GHD. In both groups, the CA-BA difference was significantly reduced in the second year of treatment compared to the start of treatment. The difference was greater in the non-GHD group than in the GHD group, but there was no significant difference. The SDS for height showed a significant increase in both groups ( P &lt; 0.0001), whereas the differences in the SDS for height and MPH also showed a significant annual decrease ( P &lt; 0.0001). Differences in GH dose according to GHD were found in the second year of GH therapy, with the non-GHD group using significantly higher doses (non-GHD vs. GHD group: 0.31 ± 0.11 vs. 0.27 ± 0.1, P = 0.0466). Correlation with growth response during GH therapy The effects of baseline characteristics on height SDS and growth velocity over 2 years is shown in Supplementary Table 1 . In the correlation analysis, younger age and bone age, higher height SDS, BMI SDS and MPH SDS were related to higher growth response (Δheight SDS and Δgrowth velocity), but there was no statistically significant correlation. The regression analysis of the effects of GH dose on height SDS and growth velocity is shown in Supplementary Table 2. This showed that height SDS and growth velocity increased with higher GH doses; however, the interaction between the GHD and non-GHD groups showed no statistically significant differences. Discussion Most children born SGA achieve catch-up growth within 4 or more years. Up to 90% of children born SGA experiences acceleration of linear growth during the first 12 months of life, reaching a height of -2 SDS or more within 2 years of birth; however, those who fail to achieve catch-up growth may be eligible for GH therapy regardless of GHD. Factors associated with response to GH therapy include dose, MPH, and age and height SDS at the start of treatment; of these, the dose is very important for treatment response in the first 2–3 years [ 4 , 20 , 21 ]. In the present study, we found that Δgrowth velocity increased with higher BMI SDS, in addition to MPH and age and height SDS at start of treatment, in children born SGA regardless of GHD. A 2-year study by Lee et al. [ 22 ] on children with GHD also reported better height gain with a higher BMI. However, a study by Himes et al. [ 23 ] on height growth patterns according to obesity status in young adults reported that overweight to obese adults were relatively taller when they were younger; however, they had relatively less height growth at 10–19 years of age. Hence, further long-term studies on children born SGA with GHD may be needed; however, considering the disease burden of obesity, maintaining an appropriate body weight is recommended. In general, the evaluation of growth hormone secretion is based on stimulation tests. However, there are limitations in the interpretation of test results, and there are reports that GH and IGF-1 secretion are different in certain patients [ 24 – 28 ]. In our study, we could not explain the fact that boys with GHD had higher IGF-1 SDS than non-GHD ones and non-GHD girls had higher IGFBP-3 SDS than GHD ones in the data at the start of treatment. In addition to GH stimulation tests with a cut-off value at a level of 10 ng/mL and IGF-I values corrected for the age, sex, and puberty, efforts should be made to find other methods of qualifying short children for GH therapy. In this study, IGF-1 SDS in both GHD and non-GHD groups increased after the 2 years of GH treatment, and especially in the GHD group. The initial GH dose in the GHD group was 0.29 mg/kg/week, which was higher than the domestically approved GH treatment dose of 0.165–0.234 mg/kg/week for GHD children. It seems that the initial dose was determined considering that the patients were children born SGA. Considering that the IGF-1 SDS value was relatively high despite the dose reduction in the second year of treatment, it seems necessary to further reduce the GH dose in the future. Nevertheless, according to Kim JH et al. [ 29 ], it would be better to use the IGF-1 concentration as an indicator for monitoring treatment compliance rather than an indicator for determining the treatment effect. There was also a difference in the IGFBP-3 SDS value before and after treatment, and some IGFBP-3 SDS values in the non-GHD group are extremely high. Since this was a high value even at the start of treatment, it is considered a secondary increase due to an increase in GH dose. According to Kim M et al. [ 30 ], measuring the change in IGFBP-3 level during GH treatment could predict the therapeutic response in long-term treatment, so it would be recommended to refer to it for long-term GH dose adjustment. Bone age is a poor predictor of puberty onset or adult height in children born SGA as it can accelerate faster than in children without SGA [ 4 ]. In 2017, a study in Japan divided GH therapy for children born SGA into low- and high-dose groups and compared the therapeutic efficacy. While there was no difference in puberty onset between GH doses, bone age accelerated starting from the 3rd year of GH therapy in both dose groups; moreover, bone age was significantly accelerated in the high-dose group. Additionally, starting from the 5th year of treatment, more than half of the children showed much more advanced bone age than chronological age regardless of dose [ 7 ]. In our study, the difference in CA-BA decreased each year, regardless of GHD. Starting from the 2nd year of treatment, the non-GHD group showed a decrease in CA-BA difference, although not significantly, compared with the GHD group, while the GH dose was also significantly increased. In a 2022 study on European children born SGA, there was no difference in adult height according to GH dose; however, the onset of puberty was significantly earlier in boys born SGA, regardless of dose [ 31 ]. In our study, bone age progression was especially rapid in the non-GHD group. Therefore, bone age progression should be considered when increasing GH dose; moreover, the timing of puberty onset should be closely monitored. Regarding height gain in response to GH dose in children born SGA, a study by Hokken-Koelega et al. [ 32 ] reported no difference in pubertal height gain according to GH dose; additionally, a study by Upners et al. [ 31 ] assessed adult height and peak height velocity and found a significant increase in adult height but no difference according to GH dose. In contrast, in our study, the GHD group showed relatively slower bone age progression and better height gain than the non-GHD group, even at lower GH doses. In patients with GHD, treatment with a relatively low dose may be attempted; however, personalized treatment may be needed as each patient responds differently to treatment. This study has a few limitations. This study is a multicenter study and the most of the medical centers are general hospitals, there may be limitations in generalizing the study results. Since the laboratory test and bone age measurements were conducted according to the clinical process of each medical center and it is not clear whether the same laboratory equipment and kits for all tests were used, measurement variance between centers may exit. Although this study is an exploratory study and does not assume appropriate sample size and statistical power, the statistical power may be low due to relatively small sample size ( N = 50). Further investigation on larger sample with long-term follow-up data, including growth velocity before and during treatment of children born SGA with GHD, may be needed to interpret our findings. In addition, if the final height attachment is confirmed through continuous follow-up as an extension of this study, the importance of such study may increase. In conclusion, GHD in children born SGA is rare. Nonetheless, if there is any indication of decreased growth velocity or hypopituitarism, the presence of GHD should be checked using GH stimulation tests before GH therapy, and personalized therapy based on the results may be warranted. Bone age progression should be considered when increasing GH dose; moreover, the timing of puberty onset should be closely monitored. Further long-term studies on children born SGA with GHD may be needed to validate our findings. Electronic supplementary material Below is the link to the electronic supplementary material.
