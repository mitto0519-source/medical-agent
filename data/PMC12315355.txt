Background Low levels of physical activity (PA) and high levels of sedentary behavior (SB) are major modifiable risk factors for the prevention of non-communicable diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and diabetes. However, there is insufficient information on the barriers and facilitators of PA and SB in Ethiopia, particularly at multiple socioecological levels, to inform behavior change interventions that promote active living among office-based working adults. This qualitative formative study aimed to explore the barriers and facilitators to increasing PA and reducing SB in Ethiopian office employees using the social-ecological model (SEM) as a conceptual framework. Methods A total of twenty-six office workers (19 physically inactive/sedentary, 7 physically active) and seven key informants participated in in-depth interviews conducted between April and June 2023 in Hawassa, southern Ethiopia. The interviews were audio-recorded, fully transcribed, translated into English, and iteratively coded. Data were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach, and the barriers and facilitators generated were mapped onto the SEM categories. Results The findings were organized into thirteen themes that belong to the five levels of the SEM. Six themes were categorized to the intrapersonal level (sociodemographic and economic status; knowledge, experience and misperceptions; attitudes, beliefs and values; motivational challenges; sitting habit; and physical competence/skill); one theme belonged to the interpersonal level (social support); two themes were classified to the organizational level (work-related factors; organizational support); three themes were categorized to the environmental/community level (physical environment; social norm associated with walking; and PA promotional initiatives); and one theme belonged to the policy level (workplace health promotion policies). Conclusions A complex interplay of multiple factors contributes to office workers’ PA and SB engagement. Interventions should consider multi-component behavior change strategies that target barriers and facilitators at multiple levels, including individually tailored approaches, in order to effectively influence these physical behaviors. NCDs are currently estimated to cause 41 million deaths globally each year, with more than 85% of premature adult NCD deaths in LMICs [ 2 ]. According to global burden of disease estimates, the proportion of NCDs increased from 18.6 to 29.8% of the total disease burden in Sub-Saharan Africa between 1990 and 2017 [ 3 ]. In Ethiopia, NCDs contribute to more than one-third of the disease burden [ 4 ], and an estimated 43% of total mortalities in 2019 were caused by NCDs [ 5 ]. Moreover, research evidence showed that modifiable/behavioral risk factors accounted for 52% of all NCD-caused mortality [ 6 ]. Urbanization, epidemiological and demographic transitions, which are characterized by significant lifestyle transitions, particularly the adoption of low levels of physical activity (PA) and high levels of sedentary behavior (SB), are key contributors to this growing NCDs burden [ 7 , 8 ]. Low physical activity (LPA), defined as not achieving 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per week, is a known risk factor for premature death and a number of NCDs, such as CVDs (e.g., heart attacks, stroke), several cancers, respiratory diseases, and diabetes [ 9 – 11 ]. LPA can also increase the risk of all-cause mortality by 20 to 30% compared to sufficiently active individuals [ 12 ]. The health benefits of regular PA, performed at a moderate- or high-intensity for 150 min or more per week, have been widely recognized [ 9 , 13 ], and evidence shows that it can reduce the odds of developing over 25 chronic health conditions, with relative risk reduction ranging from 20 to 40% [ 14 ]. Yet, 31.3% of adults globally do not meet the recommended levels of PA in 2022 [ 15 ]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that accumulating SB, defined as “waking behavior characterized by an energy expenditure of 1.5 metabolic equivalents or less, while in a sitting, reclining, or lying posture” [ 16 ], is associated with increased risk of CVD, cancer, type 2 diabetes, and all-cause mortality [ 17 – 19 ]. As a result of this emerging evidence, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends not only regular PA, but also reducing sedentary time and replacing it with PA of any intensity (regular standing/walking or ‘activity breaks’) [ 9 ]. Goals of increasing PA and decreasing SB are both important. While epidemiological studies have shown that the negative health effects of excessive sitting can be eliminated by engaging in PA, the level of activity needed to offset the risks of SB is very high (60–75 min/day or 420–525 min/week of MVPA) [ 20 , 21 ]. This amount of PA is not attained by the majority of the general population of adults [ 22 ]. Targeting both MVPA (participation in a regular leisure-time PA) and SB by promoting ‘sitting less and moving more’ throughout the day is therefore an important strategy for improving health outcomes [ 23 , 24 ]. Office workers are exposed to prolonged sitting due to the sedentary nature of the office-work setting, which has a significant health concern [ 25 ]. They spend a substantial amount of time predominantly sitting at a desk, which significantly contributes to total daily sedentary time, increasing the risk of disease associated with SB [ 26 ]. Most importantly, research has shown that workers who spend a greater proportion of their time sitting at work also spend more time sitting outside of work hours and do not engage in leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) [ 25 , 27 ]. Limited research in Ethiopian urban centers have also indicated that LPA and SB are prevalent among office workers [ 28 – 30 ]. Given the substantial amount of time office workers spend at work being inactive and sedentary, it is important to target them with workplace-based health-promoting interventions that increase PA and reduce SB as a public health priority. However, there is significant evidence gap in LMICs, with limited research on workplace PA and SB interventions in regions other than high-income countries, particularly in Africa [ 31 ]. Several factors are likely to influence an individual’s movement behaviors, and contextual insight into the barriers and facilitators that influence PA and SB is essential for developing effective and appropriate interventions. In view of that, social-ecological models (SEM) provide conceptual approaches to better understand how multiple determinants likely influence PA and SB across various hierarchical levels of influence that are nested within each other (e.g. intrapersonal, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy levels) [ 32 ]. In contrast to intra-individual models or theories of behavior change, SEM takes a holistic approach and acknowledges that a comprehensive understanding of multi-level factors is likely to be required to achieve the greatest changes in behavior. There is limited information on the barriers and facilitators of PA and SB in Ethiopian office-based working adults. Previous studies have examined the factors related to PA and SB separately and from a narrow individual-focused perspective [ 8 , 28 , 33 ]. This qualitative formative research, therefore, explores barriers and facilitators to increasing PA and reducing SB in Ethiopian office workers, specifically office workers in Hawassa, southern Ethiopia, to inform the development of a locally appropriate behavior change intervention, and uses the SEM as a framework to organize and synthesize the findings. Methods Study design An exploratory, descriptive qualitative research design [ 34 ] was employed. Since PA and SB are mostly unexplored phenomena in Ethiopian office workers, this qualitative formative research was conducted to investigate the different barriers and facilitators, which will inform the development of a behavior change intervention. Furthermore, motivational factors for participating in LTPA (regular exercise or sports) were examined between inactive and active office workers using a comparative inquiry approach. Setting The setting for this study was office-based workplaces in Hawassa, the capital city of Sidama National Regional State, Ethiopia. Hawassa, located in southern Ethiopia, has a population of 429,697, of which 223,013 (51.9%) are adults aged 18–64. There are 89 public sector organizations in the city, with an estimated total number of 13,510 employees. The study setting was conveniently selected to understand the barriers and facilitators to increasing PA and reducing SB among office-based working adults living in Ethiopian urban areas, and the sampled workplaces reflect typical office-based features and are generally expected to represent Ethiopian office environments. Study participants In-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted with office employees, as well as key informants (exercise coaches and NCD program coordinators). Physically inactive and sedentary office employees working in public sector organizations were recruited from five workplaces by approaching them directly to participate in the study. Study samples were theoretically generated using convenience and purposive sampling techniques in order to include participants with different sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, income status, marital status, etc.) and gather a diverse range of perspectives. The following criteria were considered while selecting inactive and sedentary office workers: full-time desk-based office worker, at least two years of service, age 25–64 years, apparently healthy, self-report of sitting ≥ 6 h per day at work, and self-report of &lt; 3 days or less than 60 min of MVPA per week in the preceding six months. Exclusion criteria included a service year of less than two years, contract staffs, and self-reporting of any chronic disease condition/s. Likewise, physically active office workers who engaged in regular LTPA were purposively included to allow for comparisons of motivational factors to participate in regular LTPA by PA level. Furthermore, NCD program coordinators and physical exercise coaches were involved in the study as key informants using purposive and snowball-referral techniques. Multiple data sources, including IDIs with office workers and key informants, were used with the goal of obtaining a wide range of comprehensive information and insights from multiple sources, as well as to provide triangulation of the phenomenon under study. Data collection procedure IDIs took place between April and June 2023 in participants’ natural settings, such as workplaces, community outdoor exercise places, and team sport settings, depending on their location of access. Office workers initially filled out a brief demographic and socioeconomic status form. An interview guide adapted from relevant literature [ 35 , 36 ] (Additional file 1) was used, which offered participants to discuss their own experiences and practices, as well as detailed descriptions of existing barriers and opportunities for increasing PA and reducing SB. The interview questions served as a topic guide, and they were modified by asking probing questions based on each respondent’s responses to encourage participants to share their personal experiences in depth. Self-reported MVPA status, specifically participation in LTPA, was primarily considered to classify office workers based on PA level (active vs. inactive) by asking a general question (i.e., do you participate in regular exercise or sports during your leisure time?) for a comparative analysis of motivational factors; however, both active and inactive participants’ general PA status and time spent sitting during the day were explored. All IDIs were conducted by the principal investigator (AN) in Amharic, which were subsequently transcribed and translated into English. Interviews with office workers lasted 32 to 76 min, whereas interviews with key informants lasted between 53 min and 1 h and 34 min. During the interviews, reflective field notes and memos were taken that describe body language, attitude and behavior, which helped to express implicit meanings of participants’ statements and contextualize the analysis process. Also, a sample of participants received a summary of their interview transcript to check that it accurately reflected their shared experiences and that the words matched their intended meaning. Definition of PA and SB terms For the purpose of this study, PA is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that result in energy expenditure and it includes exercise, sport (organized, club, and social), walking (for recreation and transport) and unstructured/incidental PA (such as home activities that need MVPA). LPA was defined as not meeting PA recommendations, i.e. &lt; 150 min of MVPA per week. Additionally, LTPA was defined as participating in regular exercise or sports during leisure time (spending more than 30 min per day in MVPA in bouts of at least 10 min) for at least six months. Prolonged sitting was defined as sitting bouts ≥ 30 min in length, and short PA breaks was defined as efforts to interrupt prolonged sitting or take frequent short breaks (“activity snacks”) during work and outside of work hours by standing up and/or walking around in bouts of 2–5 min duration. Data analysis All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using a thematic analysis framework [ 37 ]. A line-by-line coding was conducted using the online coding tool Taguette [ 38 ], and a codebook was generated. Data were transcribed and coded by AN, and the codebook was shared with the research team for review. Exploratory codes were first discussed by AN and EG in order to refine them and generate higher-level codes. The higher-level codes were then grouped together to form emerging themes or categories through an iterative, systematic process until the final themes emerged. Finally, the identified overarching themes and sub-themes were categorized as “barriers” and “facilitators” and mapped onto the social-ecological domains of intrapersonal, interpersonal, organizational, environmental/community, and policy-level factors. Furthermore, rich descriptions of each theme and subtheme were made using textual information as well as selected informative quotes that support the study’s findings. To ensure rigor, sound methodologies and principles, namely credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, were considered under the framework suggested by Lincoln and Guba [ 39 ]. An immersion into the participants’ experiences and perspectives was achieved through reading and re-reading transcription data, comparing and contrasting data with similar and different participant categories, including comparisons by PA status (i.e., physically active vs. inactive), and extrapolating rich information relating to participant experiences pertaining to PA and SB. The data analysis was constantly evolved by comparing emerging patterns and themes from the analysis to data from subsequent interviews. In addition, the field notes and memos assisted in explaining and interpreting any implicit (latent) participant’s views and descriptions of their knowledge, attitude, beliefs, emotions, and practices related to PA and SB. Results Participants’ sociodemographic characteristics A total of 33 participants (26 office workers, 7 key informants) were involved in the study. Of the office workers, seven (4 male) engaged in regular LTPA and 19 (12 male) were physically inactive. Of the seven key informants, three were NCDs program coordinators and four were physical exercise coaches involved in the study. Detailed description of the study participants is presented in Table 1 . Description of participants’ PA and SB profile PA and SB characteristics of office-employee participants were analyzed in terms of the four physical activity domains, i.e. work, leisure, home, and transportation. They reported that they spend most of their working hours sitting in the office, with no activity breaks. Although they mentioned that they have a habit of taking tea/coffee breaks during the workday, it may not be sufficient to benefit from sit-stand transitions since they commonly prefer taking tea/coffee breaks while sitting. At home, female office workers stated that they participate in household tasks. This implies that women may be relatively active at home, performing routine household chores. However, most participants, both women and men, reported that they spend a lot of time sitting at home while watching TV and using social media. Most office workers consider walking as a viable way to increase PA, and they practice good walking habits, particularly on weekends and after work evenings. In terms of means of transportation, most office workers commute by motorcycle. The physically inactive participants did not engage in regular exercise or sports during their leisure time, and they consider themselves as inadequately physically active. Although most of them have a regular walking habit, the type and intensity of their walking are insufficient to qualify as MVPA. Barriers and facilitators to increase PA and reduce SB After data analysis, thirteen main themes regarding the barriers and facilitators for increasing PA and reducing SB were identified: (1) sociodemographic and economic status, (2) knowledge, experience and misperceptions, (3) attitudes, beliefs and values, (4) motivational challenges, (5) sitting habit, (6) physical competence/skill, (7) social support, (8) work-related factors, (9) organizational support, (10) physical environment, (11) social norm associated with walking, (12) PA promotional initiatives, and (13) workplace health promotion policies/regulations. Themes 1 to 6 were intrapersonal, theme 7 was interpersonal, themes 8 and 9 were organizational, themes 10, 11 and 12 were community/environmental, and theme 13 was the single policy-level theme. Further descriptions of each theme and subtheme are shown in Table 2 . Intrapersonal level barriers and facilitators Sociodemographic and economic status Some relevant sociodemographic factors, particularly age, gender, and income status, were reported as potential barriers to engage in LTPA by office workers. During the interviews, all inactive women participants stated that family and household commitments, including child care responsibilities, were important challenges to participating in regular LTPA. “I think the challenge is related to family duties. In particular, participating in regular morning physical exercise may be difficult. I need to make breakfast for my children and also get them ready for school” [Participant 5, office worker, physically inactive] Similarly, participants who engaged in regular LTPA indicated that normative gender roles and responsibilities make it difficult for women to participate in LTPA. For example, two of the active male participants mentioned that their spouses exercised with them but stopped after childbirth and subsequent child care responsibilities, and that they were unable to resume exercising afterwards. Some participants highlighted that older adults may fear injury, limiting their participation in LTPA, and that long-held habits of inactivity may deter their efforts to adopt active lifestyles, particularly participating in LTPA. Another important sociodemographic factor reported as a barrier is financial inability to pay for gym membership or physical exercise facility fees. During the interviews, most participants revealed that, while it was possible to engage in LTPA outdoors, exercising at well-equipped gym facilities, which they couldn’t afford to subscribe to, was preferable for better PA outcomes due to quality exercise facilities and coaching assistance. “-----I can’t afford gym fees due to the current high cost of living------you may see more people exercising outdoors in the morning than in the gyms. This is because people may not want or have the financial capacity to pay for gyms” [Participant 7, office worker, physically inactive] “-----I think the cost of standard gym centers is currently rising and may be expensive for most adults” [Participant 2, key informant, physical exercise coach 2] Knowledge, experience and misperceptions General awareness about the health benefits of PA was identified as a facilitator to increase PA among office workers. All inactive participants were able to describe the health benefits of increasing one’s daily PA levels and participating in LTPA in general terms, yet they failed to describe the appropriate level of PA recommended for adults that is beneficial to health. However, most office workers (both active and inactive) were unaware of the health risks associated with SB, as well as the daily opportunities in the workplace to interrupt sitting time and replace it with light-intensity physical activity. “------I think the main problem is that most office workers are not aware of the issue, and they are not conscious of their daily sitting time” [Participant 5, office worker, physically inactive] “Yes, I spend a little more time sitting when I’m at work and working on a task, especially if there is a deadline involved. I don’t think it has negative health effects though-----So far, I haven’t noticed any negative health effects associated with sitting” [Participant 33, office worker, physically active] When comparing inactive and active office workers, having experience of participating in regular physical exercise or sports as an adolescent or youth was recognized as the most common feature among active office workers. Six of the seven participants who engaged in regular LTPA had prior experience with physical exercise or sports, which influenced their knowledge about the health benefits of regular physical exercise as well as their interest to actively participate in sport clubs established in their workplaces. Furthermore, low perceived risk of insufficient levels of PA potentially leading to chronic diseases was appreciated among inactive office workers, which was also supported by key informant views: “-----Because of longer prognosis and latent nature of chronic conditions, people may be unconcerned about the risks-----They [adult office workers] only initiate some positive actions to increase their physical activity when they experience some severe health consequences” [Participant 25, key informant, NCD program coordinator 3] Importantly, certain misperceptions related to PA have been identified from the interviews with inactive office workers and key informant descriptions. Considering routine daily activities as an adequate level of PA was common among inactive female participants. They perceive themselves as being active as they spend most of their waking time performing multiple tasks, mainly related to household chores. Misperceptions that PA is only important for overweight or obese people, and that exercise is only beneficial for weight loss, were also indicated. “-----Most people, assume that physical activity is only important for those who are overweight. If you ask a normal-weight person, particularly an adult, why he/she don’t exercise, he/she may ask you why-----Although obese and overweight people need to exercise to lose weight, physical exercise is important for all adults, regardless of weight. However, such a knowledge gap exists-----” [Participant 30, office worker, physically active] Attitudes, beliefs and values Factors related to attitudes, beliefs, and values that could influence office workers’ motivation to increase PA and reduce SB were explored. The study participants stressed that early disease-preventive practices, such as physical exercise, are generally rare and less practiced, due to potential competing personal, financial, and social constraints. They stated that an active lifestyle is not valued or prioritized as important preventive or health promoting practice among office workers and the general public. “-----It is a matter of valuing and prioritizing health. Despite the various social and economic challenges, health should be prioritized. Even it is possible to think about or overcome those life issues only if one’s health is maintained-----what I see is a lack of priority and concern for one’s health” [Participant 16, office worker, physically inactive] Participants discussed the issues of prioritizing PA in terms of allocating the necessary time and resources to engage in LTPA and not integrating PA into daily life routines. According to the views of participants, adopting an active lifestyle requires appropriate planning and allocation of available personal resources, which is usually lacking among office workers due to less priority and attention given to active living. Some key informants also shared important insights on how'diseases risk' is perceived, which may reflect beliefs and value systems that disfavor prioritizing health-promoting practices, including adopting an active lifestyle. “-----Sometimes I feel that how we see ‘disease risk’ as important. Since people are already busy with different life tasks, they may not be concerned of the disease-risk that they may assume will not happen to them-----During screening campaigns, though we advise people to engage in regular physical activity and accept the advice, they don’t actually practice it” [Participant 25, key informant, NCD program coordinator 3] During interviews with inactive office workers, while time constraints due to work and social duties were indicated as reasons for not engaging in LTPA, attitude-related issues perhaps play a role as a subtle factor in the participants’ descriptions of not participating in regular LTPA. Perceived views that “shortage of time” or being “busy with life routines” as limiting their participation in LTPA was commonly reported among inactive office employees. Motivational challenges Participants reported motivational issues as the main reason for not adopting an active lifestyle among office workers, specifically to participate in LTPA. They stated that most adults, including office employees, have weak PA intention (i.e., motivational decision to engage in regular LTPA) and become triggered to improve their PA levels merely when signs or symptoms of chronic disease conditions (such as prediabetes or prehypertension) are warned or advised by a doctor to engage in regular LTPA. Correspondingly, two of the seven active participants were alerted to change their PA behavior and decided to participate in regular LTPA after signs of chronic disease conditions or following medical advice. A comparison of the motivation-related barriers to engaging in and maintaining regular LTPA among active vs. inactive office workers revealed that different factors can make it difficult for office workers to be sufficiently motivated to increase PA levels. One common constraint was a lack of self-confidence in one’s ability to participate in and/or maintain LTPA in the face of potential personal and social challenges (self-efficacy), despite having good intention and, in some cases, even appreciating the benefits of physical exercise or sports from one’s own experience. “If possible, it is good to do some physical exercise early in the morning. However, it needs a strong commitment and decision-----until you get used to it, it is not something pleasant” [Participant 16, office worker, physically inactive] “-----I understand the health benefits of exercise since I’ve had personal experience with it. I had a severe cough, similar to pneumonia that went away while I was exercising. But, once I get better, I can’t continue to exercise” [Participant 1, office worker, physically inactive] As such, most inactive office workers have a history of starting but then stopping exercising, indicating a struggle to maintain LTPA status and adopt it as a regular habit. Maintaining one’s participation in regular exercise was reported as more challenging than initiating it. “-----I have tried and failed several times to continue exercising. What I realized is that adjusting to it is a difficult task. Despite paying for and joining gyms on multiple occasions, I eventually dropped out due to a lack of motivation-----” [Participant 14, office worker, physically inactive] “-----Above all, the main challenge is to maintain the lifestyle changes. I see many colleagues start exercising but stop after one or two months” [Participant 9, office worker, physically inactive] Our comparative investigation of motivation-related factors also indicated that active participants’ knowledge and experience on PA may influence their motivation to participate in LTPA compared to inactive participants. Participants who engaged in regular LTPA had more experience and knowledge about PA, which may have helped them overcome the motivational challenges of maintaining regular LTPA. Furthermore, among inactive participants, the difficulty of resuming to exercise after a break or short withdrawal from an exercise regimen, as well as fatigue and exhaustion during the initial sessions of routine workouts, were described as reasons for discontinuing exercising or participating in routine LTPA. And these were supported by key informant views, and exercise coaches mentioned similar motivation-related issues: “When people start to exercise and encounter some social issue and withdraw schedules for 2–3 days, they find it difficult to maintain their previous status-----once they withdraw it, they might view exercise as a suffering activity” [Participant 20, key informant, physically exercise coach 1] “There may be some physical fatigue at first, until it becomes a habit, and most adults do not resist these early stage discomforts and usually withdraw from continuing to do physical exercise” [Participant 22, key informant, physical exercise coach 3] Likewise, some exercise trainers stated that unrealistic expectations related to losing weight in a short period of time may discourage office workers, particularly some middle-aged or older adults, from continuing to exercise. “Some adults expect to lose weight quickly after starting to exercise and become easily discouraged when they do not lose weight as quickly as they expected. Even there were some guys who weighed themselves before and after each exercise session. However, it is unrealistic to expect to lose weight after just a single day of exercise” [Participant 21, key informant, physical exercise coach 2] Despite having interest in increasing PA levels, the responses of some inactive participants highlighted challenges in planning and time management skills to balance and integrate PA with other personal, family, social, or work-related tasks and duties. They reported that they used to participate in various sports and/or physical exercise activities as adolescents or youths, but were unable to continue their active participation in PA after starting job and leading one’s own family as adults. Additionally, potential motivators for engaging in LTPA among active office workers that could be considered facilitators of PA were explored. As a result, various reasons for participating in LTPA were recognized, including an interest to reduce weight for good physical appearance (especially for women), having fun/enjoyment, making new friends, and for better physical fitness (among men). Physical competence/skill The other important individual-level theme identified based on participant reports was a lack of exercise skill. According to some participants’ descriptions, office workers lack the appropriate skills to engage in LTPA on their own. They stated that due to financial constraints, office workers may prefer to exercise outdoors on their own rather than in gyms or exercise facilities where they can get necessary training support from exercise/sport specialists, but their exercise skills may be insufficient to achieve beneficial health or fitness outcomes. “I have seen some friends try to do regular aerobics exercise, but they don’t do it consistently, and the way they do physical exercise isn’t scientific in my view. Running one day per week may not be enough or beneficial to one’s health. The type and intensity of exercise are also important” [Participant 16, office worker, physically inactive] “-----When I see adults, including office workers, their outdoor physical exercise lacks scientific etiquette. For example, they instantly begin running without even recording how many minutes they have run or checking whether their sporting clothing and shoes are appropriate. Such practices may expose them to physical harm” [Participant 20, key informant, physically exercise coach 1] Participants also highlighted that a lack of exercise skills can affect expected outcomes (e.g., weight reduction), discouraging adults from exercising. Sitting habit In terms of barriers to reducing SB, participants indicated the difficulty of self-controlling sitting, noting that it occurs mostly unconsciously. Participants’ expressions imply that sitting is difficult to self-regulate. One participant stated: “I think sitting is more of a habit, and people may be unconscious of how much time they spend sitting-----it can be more difficult to deal with it [prolonged sitting], as it occurs mostly unconsciously-----” [Participant 16, office worker, physically inactive] Such participant reports were common, and most of them reporting a lack of self-control to reduce sedentary time and form active habits. Some participants also emphasized that adopting an active lifestyle and replacing old habits with new ones, particularly forming new habits to reduce daily sedentary time, requires a high level of commitment and habit formation, which many office workers may find difficult to attain. “I think forming an active living habit is primarily a psychological issue, and it is difficult to train oneself until such a habit is formed, such as remembering to stand every some period of sitting” [Participant 4, office worker, physically inactive] “It is difficult for most office workers to change their routines-----the human mind needs some mandatory requirements in order to consistently adhere to some practices and form habits” [Participant 2, office worker, physically inactive] Interpersonal level barriers and facilitators Social support Women office workers reported a lack of family and/or partner support as a barrier to participate in LTPA. They underlined that, due to family and household responsibilities, women may seek family and partner support to participate in LTPA, which is usually lacking. “----If there is support from family members, it is possible to engage in regular exercise----while regular exercise is possible, I think women may require additional family support due to the burdens of household activities” [Participant 8, office worker, physically inactive] “Partner support is crucial. Women are especially motivated to continue exercising when they have the support of their husband” [Participant 20, key informant, physically exercise coach 1] In addition, some active participants reported receiving unfavorable feedback from coworkers, colleagues, or other community members about their participation in LTPA, which could indicate societal-level knowledge and perceptions of PA and its benefits. “----Even some of my coworkers at the office have asked me, ‘When do you finish your exercise?’ as if it was something that is completed after some time” [Participant 32, office worker, physically active] “----Physical exercise may be seen as a luxury by some people. When I tell some of my friends that I exercise, they’re surprised that I’m paying for it despite my financial situation” [Participant 28, office worker, physically active] Meanwhile, most participants described that their neighborhood social environment is supportive to increase PA levels, particularly walking ‒ i.e., they have good social ties with their neighbors and have a habit of regular walking with them after work. Correspondingly, most of the office workers who participated in the study had a good walking habit. Also, some participants pointed out that the presence of social clubs/networks at workplaces could be good opportunities for expanding support from coworkers/peers in order to reduce workplace sedentary time. Organizational level barriers and facilitators Work-related factors Expressions highlighting how working in an office setting exposes employees to sitting for prolonged periods of time and limits their chance to be active were noted from the descriptions of inactive participants. Perceived characteristics of the office-work setting, in that there is less opportunity to be active at work due to prolonged sitting, were reiterated in discussions regarding the barriers to reducing sedentary time, particularly in the workplace. “----The problem is that the nature of my work favors sitting and there is less chance to be active at work” [Participant 1, office worker, physically inactive] “----In the office setting, even going to coworker’s offices, what you get is a chair, and it can be difficult to recognize extended sitting times” [Participant 7, office worker, physically inactive] In addition, some office workers described work overload and busy work schedules as major obstacles to reducing sedentary time at work. “----There is a cyclical work overflow that requires me to sit for longer periods of time in order to complete work assignments----Even working hard, I may not be able to complete the tasks. As a result, I may sit longer hours at work” [Participant 3, office worker, physically inactive] “One possible challenge is the job burden. Many office workers are likely to be busy with job tasks, and as their attention is on completing the required job assignments, they may not even be conscious of their long sitting habits” [Participant 26, key informant, NCD program coordinator 4] While the habit of having tea/coffee breaks has been identified as a facilitator for increasing daily PA in office workers as a normative workplace behavior, they are usually unintentional, and the sit-stand transitions may be insufficient to attain the expected health benefits. On the other hand, the use of motorbikes in daily commuting as well as for any work-related activities during the workday may considerably contribute to daily sedentary time and it can be a missed opportunity for walking to increase daily PA levels. Furthermore, views on certain workplace norms, such as long office meetings, and managers’ expectations and attitudes toward working hours, that spending more time at one’s desk indicates an employees’ greater work efficiency or productivity, were expressed as barriers, which could favor prolonged sitting or make frequent breaks difficult. “----There are some cultures in workplace environments, such as perceptions that an employee is considered active in his/her work if he/she sits at his/her desk whether or not he/she is actively engaged in work tasks. Such perception and attitude is common among most managers” [Participant 17, office worker, physically inactive] “---In government offices, long meetings are common, which exposes employees to sitting for long periods of time with no physical activity. When there are meetings, it becomes necessary to sit for long periods of time…” [Participant 13, office worker, physically inactive] Organizational support Office workers raised a lack of organizational support as a factor influencing their motivation to participate in LTPA and reduce sedentary time at work. This was primarily described in terms of managers’ insufficient knowledge about the importance of increasing PA and reducing SB in the workplace and absence of organizational policies and regulations to support employees. They mentioned that, while managers may be willing and cooperative for health promotion initiatives targeted at increasing PA and reducing SB in the workplace, they are unaware of the issue and lack understanding of what kind of support can be provided to employees. Indeed, middle and senior-level managers who participated in the study had positive views on taking frequent sitting breaks in the workplace, describing that frequent sitting breaks are acceptable and do not affect job productivity. “----I don’t think this would be a problem. Short activity breaks might even boost productivity” [Participant 11, office worker, physically inactive] “I think managers would accept that such practices would benefit employees----these short bouts of physical activity have no effect on work productivity, and even employees may become more energetic at the job after the breaks” [Participant 3, office worker, physically inactive] The availability of “sport-for-health” groups (sport teams, generally soccer teams, established by volunteer employees interested in promoting and maintaining their health and wellbeing) at some organizations was highlighted by office workers as a good opportunity to participate in team sports during their leisure-time. However, according to their reports, these sport teams do not exist uniformly across all organizations, and current teams receive inadequate support. Community and environmental level barriers and facilitators Physical environment Some inactive office workers reported issues related to the availability and accessibility of green spaces and exercise or sport facilities in their neighborhoods as barriers to regular LTPA participation, while others stressed the potential challenges in affording gym membership fees. Exercise trainers also indicated that, although open green spaces are available in Hawassa, they are being used for other social activities rather than promoting physical activity. Further, one exercise coach suggested the need for a comprehensive, free, public physical activity center with various kinds of sport playing spaces for adults, which is currently unavailable. “----Another problem is the availability of physical activity facilities. In Hawassa, for example, there is no single comprehensive physical activity center with various types of sport playing spaces” [Participant 21, key informant, physical exercise coach 2] Interestingly, participants mentioned the availability of walking trails and neighborhood walkability as an opportunity to promote walking among office workers and the general public. Social norms Even though participants and key informants reported a generally weak culture of embracing PA at the community level, particularly in terms of participation in LTPA, they stated that there are no significant cultural or societal norms that hinder PA. Nevertheless, some participants revealed that there are societal-level normative expectations and perceptions related to regular walking that may discourage walking or active commuting, as office workers and adults may be afraid of being judged by others for their regular walking practice (proscriptive injunctive norm). “I consider that there are some perceived societal barriers to walking instead of taking a taxi. They [office workers] may be embarrassed to walk, especially during midday, because people may negatively associate their walking with issues such as aversion to pay transportation fees or financial savings. Such thinking is common and may discourage people from walking” [Participant 22, key informant, physical exercise coach 3] Physical activity promotional initiatives The study participants mentioned different community-wide initiatives and educational campaigns to promote PA, such as “mass sport” campaigns, the “Great Ethiopian Run” event in Hawassa, and “vehicle free” bi-monthly cycling campaigns that were previously implemented. However, they indicated that, while these educational campaigns were influential in raising awareness, they lacked consistency and a target audience in their implementation. Moreover, some key informants expressed concerns about the quality and adequacy of public health messages transmitted during educational campaigns, particularly gaps in the integration of PA and SB messages. They emphasized that SB is an overlooked topic in PA promotion activities. Policy level barriers Lack of workplace health promotion policies and regulations Regarding policy-level barriers, office workers and key informants reported a lack of workplace health promotion policies and regulations that support office workers to adopt active lifestyles both during work and non-work hours, as well as improve employee well-being in general. “-----Organizations primarily lack operational regulations and policies for promoting physical activity among their employees in the workplace” [Participant 27, office worker, physically active] Some participants indicated the absence of incentives as a barrier to supporting office workers to participate in sport-for-health teams. They stated that when organizations provide incentives to support sport-for-health teams (for example, sports clothing), employees are more likely to join sport teams or participate in LTPA. Theoretical framework and mapping of interactions In an effort to summarize the key findings of our formative research as a theoretical framework that would inform our intervention targeting PA and SB, interactions between the different socioecological levels and their corresponding themes were mapped (Fig. 1 ). Based on the findings of the exploration of factors, the broad interaction of community or environmental-level factors with intrapersonal, interpersonal, and organizational levels in affecting individual interests to engage in practices that increase PA and reduce SB was outlined. For instance, while perceived availability and proximity of green public spaces, as well as the cost of exercise facilities/gyms influence office workers’ intentions and motivations to participate in LTPA, positive neighborhood walkability and a social environment may have contributed to good walking habits. Furthermore, insufficient public health messages in PA promotional campaigns may favor misperceptions and inadequate knowledge about PA and SB, negative social feedback that some participants encountered in relation to their participation in regular exercise, and motorized transport habits as missed opportunities to increase daily PA through active commuting. Meanwhile, the absence of workplace health promotion policies and incentives were found to influence the other socioecological barriers, in terms of shortages in informing employees to reduce workplace sedentary time, motivating employees to participate in LTPA to increase daily PA levels, and creating and facilitating enabling environments for employees to adopt active lifestyles during work and leisure times. A socioecological bidirectional link between policy- and organizational-level barriers, such as work overload and workplace culture, was also included in the theoretical framework. In addition, the theoretical framework illustrated the importance of social support (interpersonal level factors) in influencing personal intentions and motivations related to PA and SB, more specifically participation in LTPA. Discussion The development of complex public health interventions to increase PA and reduce SB requires a good understanding of the different barriers and facilitators for behavior change. Accordingly, this study was the first phase of a larger study which will be used to inform the development of a behavior change intervention, targeting both LPA and SB. To the best of the author’s knowledge, this is the first socioecological investigation of Ethiopian office employees to provide valuable insight into the facilitators and barriers to increasing PA and decreasing SB in this region. By using a qualitative design, our formative research identified multiple factors that hinder or facilitate office workers’ PA and SB, and thematically grouped these into thirteen themes mapped onto SEM levels. It is important to acknowledge that there is a likely interaction between these factors over the day and, perhaps, across the different PA and SB domains (work, leisure, home, and transport). Based on the findings, the identified barriers and facilitators to increasing PA and reducing SB, as well as their implications for developing a behavior change intervention in the local context, are discussed below by each socioecological level. Intrapersonal barriers and facilitators Barriers and opportunities to be physically active are not equal for every person, and some people are disproportionately impacted, resulting in disparities in levels of PA [ 40 ]. Our findings indicated that different sociodemographic factors could influence office employees’ PA and SB status. Particularly, age, gender roles (for women), and income status were found to influence participation in LTPA. Although these factors cannot be feasibly modified to increase PA levels, it is important to recognize that these groups may require more targeted and tailored interventions. Concerning psychological factors, barriers related to knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, values, motivations, and habits were identified. Different misperceptions related to PA, such as considering busy daily activities, mostly household chores, as adequate forms of PA were identified as knowledge-related barriers. A similar finding of misperceptions related to PA was reported among Nepalese adults, who considered their hectic household routines were adequate for meeting the required levels of PA, affecting their interest and engagement in physical activities during leisure [ 41 ]. Such misperceptions about PA, combined with low perceived risk-consciousness among inactive office employees in the current study, justify the importance of raising awareness about the importance of regular LTPA at a moderate level or greater in future interventions. According to participant views, not valuing or prioritizing active lifestyles and disease-preventive practices in general in the face of other competing personal, family, social, or work-related life priorities was appreciated. This was somewhat reflected in office workers’ responses, which stated “shortage of time” or “busy life routines” as reasons for not engaging in regular LTPA. Although perceived lack of time is a well-recognized barrier in the PA literature [ 42 ], it often reflects one’s value to regular physical activity as the key issue is not whether an individual has time, but how he/she chooses to spend his/her time and personal resources [ 43 ]. Personal values serve as guiding principles in a person’s life, and the pursuit of multiple values (e.g., health, family, work) by individuals may lead to conflicts between values [ 44 ]. For instance, pursuing PA and maintaining good health may conflict with family and work related values because of time constraints. Thus, since health is generally considered an important value, individuals’ unhealthy choices, such as inactive and sedentary lifestyles, may indicate a “value disconnect” rather than the absence of a value [ 45 ]. In the current study, we noted a disconnect between the value assigned to health and the relatively little time and effort dedicated to LTPA. In this case, Motivational Interviewing [ 46 ] and the Disconnected Values Model [ 45 ] offer useful behavior change approaches for linking the long-term health risks of LPA habits to health and the person’s other core life values and beliefs (e.g., family, work, pleasure) so that conflicting values are viewed as being congruent, ultimately supporting more successful and enduring PA behavior change. Through comparing physically active vs. inactive participants, our study has also revealed important motivation-related factors to engaging in regular LTPA among inactive office workers, which were expressed in terms of weak intention, low self-efficacy, difficulty to maintain regular exercise, unrealistic outcome expectations related to losing weight in a short period of time, and lack of planning and self-monitoring skills to balance PA with daily life tensions. Given the well-recognized psychological/emotional “costs” of PA (e.g., uncomfortable feelings) [ 47 ], especially for adults with no or little PA experience, behavioral support strategies focusing on intention and motivation appears important to increase PA levels among office workers. Intention translation, habit, and action control were also found as imperative in the current study in explaining office workers’ PA motivation, particularly in maintaining regular LTPA status. As such, it makes sense to consider addressing psychological determinants including habits and affect-related factors in addition to knowledge. The use of a combination of appropriate behavior change strategies (e.g., habit formation, self-regulatory techniques such as action/coping planning) and changes in other socioecological levels (e.g., social or environmental influences) can indeed be relevant in translating intentions into action (i.e., bridging the “intention-behavior gap”) [ 14 , 48 ]. Notably, a lack of exercise skills was highlighted as another possible reason for not participating in regular LTPA. This finding agrees with previous studies that reported a lack of skills as a barrier to performing regular LTPA [ 41 , 49 ]. Despite the need for further research, the lack of exercise skills in our study could be related to the culture of PA and the societal-level promotion of PA across the lifespan. From an intervention development perspective, however, including group-based exercise skill training as one intervention component seems beneficial in the specific setting to improve office workers’ physical competence to increase LTPA participation rates. Pertaining to barriers to reducing SB, the habitual disposition of sitting was identified as one major theme based on the expressions of the study participants. According to office employees’ descriptions, controlling one’s sitting behavior is difficult as sitting decisions are mostly unconscious. Indeed, it has been argued that the motivational processes of LTPA and SB are distinct in that the decision to exercise tend to be planned and involves conscious decision-making, whereas the choice to sit is often spontaneous and habitual [ 50 ]. This suggests that interventions targeting PA and SB in combination should include behavior-specific intervention strategies. Inter-personal barriers and facilitators Lack of social support from a partner and/or family members was reported by women office workers as a barrier to participating in LTPA. Prior studies have also reported an association between perceived social support and regular PA [ 41 , 51 ]. This means that the role of family and partner support should be emphasized in the design of interventions to address lack of time, which is a common barrier to regular LTPA participation, particularly among women [ 52 ]. Some physically active office workers reported receiving negative feedback from colleagues and friends about their regular exercise, which could be indicative of wider community misperceptions and knowledge gaps around PA. On top of that, such unfavorable reactions from close social networks may influence behavioral decisions and the effort needed to maintain regular LTPA [ 53 ], necessitating proper consideration in behavior change interventions. Aside from that, different opportunities to promote active lifestyles among Ethiopian office workers by utilizing available social ties and structures at both the workplace (e.g., using social clubs) and community (e.g., a supportive neighborhood social environment for promoting walking) levels were identified as potential facilitators. Organizational-level barriers and facilitators Health behavior is a result of the interaction between the individual and the environment, and promoting a change in SB at the workplace should therefore include changes in workplace’s physical, social, and organizational/policy environments [ 54 ]. The physical environment of seated desk-based working and computer based tasks may expose office workers to prolonged occupational sitting [ 55 , 56 ], as also indicated by the study participants in the current study. Modifying the “built environment” for workers to facilitate behavior change has been successfully achieved in high-income Western settings such as through use of sit-stand desks [ 57 ]. Although these sit-stand workstations may not be accessible in LMIC workplace settings, behavioral approaches that support office workers to regularly break their sitting time may be successful to reduce occupational SB. Additionally, changes in organizational structure (e.g. workplace policies) and work environments to'active design' principles (e.g., flexible space, open floor plans) [ 58 ], can suitably be applied in office workplaces of LMIC settings to encourage more movement and less sitting at work. In this study, high workload was identified as a barrier to reducing SB in the workplace, which is consistent with other study findings [ 59 – 61 ]. Evidence also suggests that, as workers prioritize work tasks over sitting or standing for the purpose of completing discrete tasks, workplace interventions need to be compatible with productive work and should encourage workers to identify personally meaningful “opportune moments” for standing or postural change transitions in order to effectively promote movement behaviors at work [ 62 , 63 ]. Another important barrier identified in this study is related to workplace norms and organizational culture, such as managers’ expectations of working hours and productivity (i.e., the belief that spending more time at one’s desk indicates an employees’ greater work efficiency) and long office meetings. Although managers’ normative beliefs about employees’ regular presence at their desks and work productivity may be common, evidence shows that a physically active lifestyle during and after working hours is associated with improved work-related outcomes such as work productivity, reduced sickness absence among employees, and positive financial return for employers [ 64 ]. Consequently, in order to bring a cultural shift in office norms, research has suggested that targeting senior management is crucial [ 56 , 65 ]. Intervention strategies to break or reduce prolonged sitting should, therefore, engage senior organization management through adequate education/communication training; and multilevel approaches incorporating both individual- (e.g., self-monitoring using activity logbooks) and organizational-level strategies (e.g., prompting messages in offices) can increase the chance of designing practical worksite interventions, with a potential to be integrated into organizational routines [ 65 , 66 ]. Moreover, recruiting workplace champions as peer-support groups can serve as role models and support their coworkers [ 67 ]. Community/environmental-level barriers and facilitators Regarding community- or environmental-level factors, barriers and facilitators related to the availability and proximity of green public places, costs of exercise facilities, social norms associated with walking, and some community-level PA promotional initiatives were identified. Behavior change requires enabling conditions for a certain behavioral intention to be realized, and some participants mentioned the availability and proximity to green spaces or exercise/sport facilities as a barrier to regular LTPA participation. Similarly, studies conducted in high-resource settings showed that environmental barriers (e.g., a lack of public space for outdoor exercise) and facilitators (e.g., open access to a gym facility) can influence PA levels [ 68 , 69 ]. Congruently, research has demonstrated that perceptions of the physical environment (the built environment) interact with psychological factors such as intention and motivation in determining LTPA practice, indicating the importance of supportive neighborhood characteristics [ 70 , 71 ]. Likewise, the issue of affordability of standard exercise facilities was mentioned as a challenge by some office workers, and availing of free public exercise facilities (“open gyms”) in Hawassa and other Ethiopian cities, as suggested by one exercise trainer, may have a greater impact in motivating adults, including office workers, for regular LTPA participation [ 40 ]. Accordingly, for such community-level initiatives to modify the built environment, multi-sectoral collaboration between the health sector and other government sectors and policymakers, such as sport, urban planning, and municipalities, is needed. The study participants generally perceived the neighborhood walkability as positive for leisure walking. Most of the office workers also reported good walking habits. Previous studies and reviews have shown that environmental and neighborhood characteristics are linked to increased SB [ 72 – 74 ]. For example, in a review of SB correlates by Prince et al. [ 73 ], walkability was found to be negatively associated with transportation SB and suggested that enhanced walkability in neighborhoods and cities may offer an intervention focus for reducing transportation-related SB. It is recommended that national and regional urban planning and transportation systems/policies should prioritize investment in supportive or “physical activity friendly” infrastructures to promote active travel in cities [ 40 ]. In this regard, the current study identified a positive perception of neighborhood walkability as a potential facilitator for promoting walking among office workers, which could help them meet their daily PA requirements. Importantly, though, a social norm that may discourage walking or active commuting was recognized as regular walking practice may be perceived by others as reflecting financial concerns or as an effort to save transportation costs. In line with this finding, Paudel and colleagues [ 41 ] reported a similar social norm in their study on socioecological influences of LTPA among Nepalese adults. Indeed, such a subjective norm or perception of social approval (injunctive norm) might influence behavioral choices and intentions for walking, and should be addressed in future behavior change interventions and community-wide PA promotion activities in the specific setting. Policy-level barriers A few study participants mentioned a lack of workplace health promotion policies and regulations as a barrier to increasing PA both at work and in their non-work/leisure time. They emphasized that if organizations have a policy framework in place to provide necessary support, including incentives, employees may be more motivated to participate in LTPA. Previous research has shown that workplace policies are strongly associated with PA [ 51 , 75 ]. The WHO encourages the promotion of PA in the workplace within the Global Action Plan on PA [ 76 ], and existing evidence indicates that workplace PA promotion and worksite PA programs can improve workers’ health and productivity while reducing healthcare costs [ 77 , 78 ]. As such, national NCD prevention policy and PA guidelines allow countries to develop detailed operational policies and action plans in key areas, including the workplace [ 40 ]. While Ethiopia is currently implementing the second national strategic plan for the prevention and control of major NCDs [ 7 ], there is a lack of comprehensive policies and guidelines, as well as inadequate multi-sectoral collaboration, to promote PA and reduce SB [ 79 ]. Thus, besides the need for national workplace policies to promote PA among Ethiopian office employees, future multi-level interventions would better include organizational-level strategies to influence existing workplace policies to promote active living not only during work but also outside of the workplace (e.g., transport-related and leisure-time activities) [ 80 ]. Strengths and limitations of the study This study has provided in-depth contextual information on the potential barriers and facilitators to increasing PA and reducing SB in Ethiopian office-based workers that can be targeted in future intervention designs. The strength of this study is that it investigated the topic from a socio-ecological perspective, which allowed for a more comprehensive and multi-level examination of the various barriers and facilitators. Furthermore, we recruited office workers from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds to include more nuanced views and insights, as well as to support transferability of the findings to other similar settings. Similarly, triangulation of multiple data sources (i.e., office workers and key informants) can contribute to the trustworthiness of the findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the barriers and facilitators to increasing PA and reducing SB in the Ethiopian context. The study has also benefited from a comparative analysis of motivational aspects between physically active and inactive office workers, which was particularly useful in identifying important motivation-related factors for LTPA. On the other hand, this study had some limitations. For feasibility reasons, office workers’ PA and SB status was determined based on general self-report, and we acknowledge that collecting detailed domain-specific data using standard questionnaires (e.g., IPAQ) may provide a more reliable and comprehensive characteristics of their movement behaviors. Nonetheless, we tried to seek domain-specific PA and SB information by asking general interview questions. Furthermore, while we included participants with multiple sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, and socioeconomic status) in our study, the study topic merits further systematic study using mixed-method approaches, particularly for certain specific sociodemographic groups with unique barriers to participating in LTPA, such as women and older adults. Finally, we consider that the public policy domain explored in our study has a lesser depth and breadth of information, perhaps due to the participants’ limited knowledge and expertise specific to policy-related matters, and that the succinct reporting of the policy-related barriers in our study shouldn’t be interpreted as an absence of other policy-level factors. Conclusion Developing effective and contextually feasible behavior change interventions, particularly for the most risky urban population groups, such as office workers, is an important public health priority for LMICs with a rising trend of NCDs, including Ethiopia. This study provides contextual information on barriers and facilitators that may influence the PA and SB of Ethiopian office workers; ultimately, the study’s findings will be used to inform the development of a combined PA and SB behavior change intervention. Considering the multiple influences on PA and SB over the SEM and targeting them through multi-component behavior change strategies that consider barriers and facilitators at multiple levels (e.g., intrapersonal-, interpersonal-, and organizational-level) is recommended to maximize increases in PA and reductions in sedentary time. In addition, the wide range of the employees’ barriers and facilitators suggests that PA and SB interventions should offer tailored or personalized approaches that are specifically targeted to individual constraints and opportunities.
