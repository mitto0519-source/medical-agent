Abstract Background To date, the clinical equivalence between branded and generic medications remains debate and may sometimes be a reason why psychiatrists are hesitant to prescribe generic medications. Depression is recognized to exacerbate suicide risk globally and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, such as fluoxetine are common treatment options. Therefore, this study aimed to explores differences in suicidal risks between users of branded and generic fluoxetine in Taiwan. Methods This cohort study used Taiwan Longitudinal Health Insurance Database, encompassing 2 million individuals covered by National Health Insurance (NHI) program. The full cohort consisted of 32,298 fluoxetine new users. Then, 7,380 branded and 7,380 propensity score matched (PSM) generic fluoxetine new users were identified. The study further utilized Cox proportional hazards models to assess risk of 5-year suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality. Results The study revealed that the adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for suicidal ideation, suicide mortality and all-cause mortality in branded users were 0.766 (95% CI, 0.497 − 1.181), 0.660 (95% CI, 0.447 − 0.975), and 0.942 (95% CI, 0.849 − 1.045), respectively, when compared with matched generic fluoxetine users. Stratified and sensitivity analyses showed the lower risk of suicide mortality in specific subgroups, such as male (adjusted HRs = 0.536, 95% CI = 0.306–0.939) and young branded users (adjusted HRs = 0.549, 95% CI = 0.334–0.904). Conclusion This study observed trends in the prevention effects of suicide-related risks. However, only suicide mortality was statistically significant, especially in males and those aged &lt; 40 years. These insights may assist clinicians and policymakers in decision-making. Introduction Depression is a highly prevalent mental disorder and a growing public health concern worldwide [ 1 ], with lifetime prevalence rates varying from 6 to 21% [ 2 ]. Depression increases the risk of suicide [ 3 ], with more than two-thirds of suicide attempts occurring in individuals with depression [ 4 ] whose lifetime suicide risk is between 2% and 8% [ 5 ], which is approximately 20 times higher than that of the general population [ 6 ]. In 2010, depression accounted for the largest proportion (46.1%) of the 36.2 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributed to suicide [ 7 ]. Depression treatment options include the use of antidepressants [ 8 ]. A network meta-analysis in 2018 showed that the 21 different antidepressants studied were more effective than placebo [ 9 ]. Since the 1950s, various antidepressants have been successively developed [ 10 ]. Based on the monoamine hypothesis of depression in 1965 [ 11 ], fluoxetine was the first medication approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1987 as a treatment for depression. It was named Prozac, the first selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI). SSRIs are commonly used as first-line antidepressants and represent the most widely used class of antidepressants [ 12 – 14 ]. Since the early 20th century, various branded antidepressants have been introduced into the market, followed by numerous generic drugs as their patents expired. Generic drugs provide patients with better access to medicines. It was reported that FDA-approved generic drugs account for approximately 90% of prescriptions filled in the United States [ 15 ]. Generic psychotropic medications, particularly generic antidepressants, constituted more than 70% of all psychiatric prescriptions [ 16 ]. According to U.S. and Taiwan FDA regulations, generic drugs should pass bioavailability and bioequivalence tests to demonstrate their efficacy, quality, and safety [ 17 ]. Additionally, they should have the same active ingredients and not be different from the branded drugs [ 18 ]. However, passing a bioequivalence test does not imply identical bioavailability between the two drugs. The FDA requires that the 90% confidence interval (CI) for the area under the curve (AUC)/Cmax ratio between the two drugs be between 80% and 125% to be considered bioequivalent. The bioequivalence limits may indicate a potential variance of 25% between branded and generic drugs, which may impact efficacy, particularly for drugs with poor solubility in water, a narrow therapeutic index, and nonlinear pharmacokinetics [ 19 ]. Generic drugs are economical and reduce drug shortage risks. However, they are associated with negative clinical perceptions, such as being less effective and having more side effects [ 20 ]. A systematic review reported impaired efficacy and occurrence of adverse reactions after fluoxetine, mirtazapine, and venlafaxine were switched from branded to generic drugs [ 21 ]. In 2018, Hsu et al. noted the prescription of higher daily doses of some generic antipsychotics compared with branded drugs [ 22 ]. Moreover, patients who use several generic antidepressants had significantly higher risks of psychiatric hospitalization compared with those receiving their branded counterparts [ 23 ]. However, the evaluation of efficacy is limited, and its impact on other depression indicators, such as suicide, remains unclear. Because of the undetermined bioequivalence between branded and generic drugs, we conducted a comprehensive cohort study to assess their treatment outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the risk of suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality between branded (Prozac) and generic fluoxetine users in Taiwan. We further employed propensity score matching (PSM) to eliminate any potential bias. Methods Data source We collected data for this cohort study from the Taiwan Longitudinal Health Insurance Database 2005 (LHID 2005), part of Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI) research database. This database has nationwide representation, encompassing claims data from 2 million individuals randomly selected from the Taiwan NHI program in 2005. The database spans from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2018. The NHI program started in 1995 and included &gt; 99.9% of Taiwan’s population. This database has been used in numerous studies of significant academic value. The Institutional Review Board of Tri-Service General Hospital, Taiwan, approved this study (C202205001). Study design and participants This cohort study involved patients using generic and branded fluoxetine as the study and comparison cohorts, respectively (Fig. 1 ). First, we included patients prescribed with fluoxetine between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2013. We used the code N06AB03 from the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System (ATC) to identify those using fluoxetine. We initially identified 63,837 fluoxetine users. The index date of this cohort study was the prescription date of fluoxetine. Furthermore, we excluded those prescribed with fluoxetine before the index date, aged &lt; 20 years to limit the selected cohort to adults, with a diagnosis of suicidal ideation or traumatic brain injury (a significant risk factor for suicide) [ 24 ], and prescribed both branded and generic fluoxetine during the study period. Ultimately, the final full cohort included 7380 and 24,918 patients newly using branded and generic fluoxetine, respectively. To mitigate the impact of confounding factors on our results and address the biases frequently encountered in observational studies, we implemented a PSM strategy (using OneToManyMTCH macro) to achieve a balance between the groups. The PSM cohort pairs were matched in a 1:1 ratio based on their calculated propensity scores by demographics and potential confounders. Finally, the PSM cohort included 7,380 and 7,380 branded fluoxetine new users and matched generic fluoxetine new users, respectively. Outcome measurement and confounders This study investigated the 5-year suicide-related risk between patients using branded and generic fluoxetine. Suicide-related outcomes included suicidal ideation (International classification of diseases, ninth revision, clinical modification [ICD-9-CM] codes E950, E951, E952, E953, E954, E955, E956, E957, E958, and E959), suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality. All selected fluoxetine users were monitored from the index date up to an additional 5 years. Moreover, we considered various potential confounding factors, such as sex, age, cohort entry year, medical institution, insured amount, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, depression, schizophrenia, mania, bipolar disorder, anxiety, sleep disorder, nicotine dependence, alcohol dependence, drug dependence, eating disorder, obsessive compulsive behavior, and lithium use, which were inputted into the adjustment and PSM models. We conducted several subgroup and sensitivity analyses to enhance the credibility of the study and provide a more comprehensive perspective. In the subgroup and sensitivity analyses, we considered the impact of different populations (such as sex and age strata) and varying follow-up periods on the risks of suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality. Statistical analysis We used SAS 9.4 and SPSS 21 software for data cleaning and statistical analysis, respectively. We used descriptive statistics to display patient characteristics and outcome measurements in patients using branded and generic fluoxetine. Subsequently, we used chi-square and independent t-tests to compare the patient characteristics between the two groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were also performed to plot suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality-free survival rates for both groups, which were compared using log-rank tests ( p &lt; 0.05). Furthermore, the Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the risk of suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality between the two groups. The analysis results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. A two-sided p &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results Table 1 shows the distribution of characteristics between patients using branded and generic fluoxetine. In the full cohort, 7380 and 24,918 patients used branded and generic fluoxetine, respectively. Sex, age, monthly insurance salary, medical institution, and cohort entry year were significantly different between the two groups. Additionally, patients using branded fluoxetine had a higher prevalence of depression, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder and a lower prevalence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and sleep disorder than their generic counterparts (all p &lt; 0.05). After PSM, most demographics and comorbidities were balanced between the two groups ( p &gt; 0.05). Table 2 shows the incidence and HRs for suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality during the 5-year follow-up period among the study patients. The Cox proportional hazards model showed that the adjusted HR of suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality for branded fluoxetine users was 0.780 (95% CI, 0.522–1.165), 0.699 (95% CI, 0.490 − 0.998), and 0.948 (95% CI, 0.868 − 1.036), respectively. In the PSM cohort, the adjusted HR of suicidal ideation for branded users was 0.766 (95% CI, 0.497 − 1.181), suicide mortality for branded users was 0.660 (95% CI, 0.447 − 0.975), and all-cause mortality for branded users was 0.942 (95% CI, 0.849 − 1.045). In this study, we observed trends in the prevention effects of suicide-related risks. However, only suicide mortality rates were statistically significant. Figure 2 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves of the 5-year suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality-free survival rates for the full and PSM cohorts. Notably, branded fluoxetine users had lower suicide mortality rates than matched generic users after PSM (log-rank test, p = 0.032). In addition, we performed stratified analyses by sex, age groups, and medical institutions to evaluate the risk of suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality between branded and generic fluoxetine users across different subgroups (Table 3 ). After PSM, branded fluoxetine exhibited a lower risk of suicide mortality in male patients, the young population (age &lt; 40 years), and hospitalized patients. Furthermore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to estimate the risk of suicidal ideation, suicide mortality, and all-cause mortality at study intervals of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years between the two groups (Table 4 ). The results showed that the suicide mortality rate in branded fluoxetine users was lower than that in generic users in the fourth year. Discussion To the best of our knowledge, this is the first propensity-score‐matched, new‐user study to compare suicide-related risk between branded and generic fluoxetine users in Taiwan using real-world evidence. In this study, we observed trends in the prevention effects of 5-year suicide-related risks. However, only suicide mortality was statistically significant in the full and PSM cohorts. Notably, this association was significant in male patients, the young population (age &lt; 40 years), and long-term follow-up (&gt; 4 years). To date, there have been very few studies focusing on relevant issues comparing the differences between branded and generic fluoxetine users. A study using data from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System [ 25 ] reported a significantly lower suicide risk associated with branded sertraline compared with its generic counterpart (aHR, 0.58). These findings indicate that within the class of SSRIs, observational research evidence already exists for lower suicide rates associated with branded fluoxetine and sertraline compared with their generic counterparts. However, ruling out the possibility of suicidal ideation and actions as rare adverse reactions to certain antidepressants is challenging, and the determination of whether suicides in individuals with depression who take antidepressants result from depression or are influenced by antidepressant use is difficult [ 26 ]. An observational study following patients with depression for up to 38 years indicated that long-term antidepressant therapy was associated with a lower suicide mortality rate [ 27 ]. Although the association between antidepressant use and suicide risk remains debatable, untreated depression significantly increases suicide risk [ 28 ]. Accordingly, the conclusive determination of this inference necessitates further research involving more countries, larger databases, and additional investigations into other drugs. By contrast, one NHI study focusing on antidepressants as a treatment of depression found that generic fluoxetine users had a lower psychiatric hospitalization risk compared with branded users (aHR, 0.9), and the mean daily dose of the generic drug was higher than that of the branded drug [ 23 ]. This indicates that generic fluoxetine may require a higher dosage to achieve therapeutic efficacy comparable to that of branded fluoxetine. We found that branded fluoxetine was associated with lower suicide risk than generic fluoxetine. The inconsistent viewpoints between the two studies might be related to patient selection and healthcare resource availability in Taiwan, where patients with severe depressive symptoms and higher suicide risks tend to visit tertiary referral hospitals that use branded drugs more. Thus, we considered the medical institution as a factor in the matching models to eliminate this bias. Moreover, the subgroup analysis results indicate that in male and younger populations, branded fluoxetine shows a lower suicide mortality risk than their generic counterparts. This outcome may be due to two possible factors. First, the suicide rate is higher in males than in females [ 29 ], evidenced by the 2019 data from the United States, where the male suicide rate is 3.73 times that of females [ 30 ]. Second, suicide constitutes a higher proportion of deaths in younger populations than in older populations, with this rate increasing annually among younger populations in Taiwan [Taiwan association against 31 ]. Suicide is the second leading cause of death in the 15–29-year age group [ 32 ] and the primary cause of death among females aged 15–19 years [ 33 ]. In the United States, suicidal ideation in the 18–25-year age group is 3–5 times higher than that in older populations [ 34 ]. We speculate that in populations with higher suicide rates, the slight variances between branded and generic fluoxetine led to the potential differences in our study. Other potential explanations based on pharmacology and pharmacokinetics are as follows: First, young populations have higher densities of 5HT1A and 5HT2A serotonin receptors [ 35 ], which are associated with increased suicidal ideation and suicide mortality [ 36 ], and 5HT1A receptors are less responsive to SSRIs [ 37 ]. A higher density of 5HT2A receptors is associated with impulsivity [ 38 ], a significant risk factor for adolescent suicide [ 39 ]. In addition, young populations metabolize drugs faster than older populations. A study investigating seven antidepressants used to treat adolescent depression showed a highly negative association between drug half-life and the occurrence of suicide events [ 40 ]. Based on the bioequivalence test, branded and generic drugs can differ by up to 25% in their AUC and Cmax. This potential variance may affect drug efficacy, particularly for drugs with poor solubility in water, a narrow therapeutic index, and nonlinear pharmacokinetics [ 19 , 41 ]. Because CYP2D6 can be inhibited by fluoxetine and its active metabolite norfluoxetine [ 42 ], fluoxetine can inhibit its metabolic pathway, leading to its nonlinear pharmacokinetics [ 43 ]. Our study has some advantages. First, compared with clinical trials, this study is an observational research analysis using a national population-based database in Taiwan, which has a large sample size and national representativeness, eliminating selection bias and volunteer effects. Second, the follow-up period was extended up to 5 years, allowing for the effective detection of rare outcomes. Third, two methods were used, including a full cohort with adjustments and a PSM cohort, which resulted in a loss of some sample size. However, confounders and biases could be eliminated. Fourth, this was the first and largest cohort study to investigate branded and generic fluoxetine and their association with suicide risk. Our study also has some limitations. First, this study was unable to determine patients’ medication adherence. Second, psychological therapy did not consider in this study due to time and cost limitations within the NHI system in Taiwan. Finally, although the follow-up period was extended to 5 years, the study didn’t statistically assess the subsequent use of the fluoxetine within this timeframe. Conclusion This study evaluated the suicide-related risk between patients using branded and generic fluoxetine in Taiwan using real-world evidence. The overall findings indicated trends in the prevention effects of suicide mortality. Branded fluoxetine showed a lower risk of suicide mortality than its generic counterpart, particularly in long-term use, male patients, and those &lt; 40 years old. The results offer insight for clinical practitioners and policymakers in making treatment selections for different patient groups and in making cost-effective decisions.
